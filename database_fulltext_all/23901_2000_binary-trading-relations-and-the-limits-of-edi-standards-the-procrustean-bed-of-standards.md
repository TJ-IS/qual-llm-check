# Binary trading relations and the limits of EDI standards: the Procrustean bed of standards

J Damsgaard<sup>1</sup> and D Truex<sup>2,3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Computer Science, Aalborg University, Aalborg, Denmark; <sup>2</sup>CIS Department, J Mack Robinson College of Business, Georgia State University, 33 Broad Street, Atlanta GA, USA; <sup>3</sup>Information Systems Institute, The University of Salford, Salford M5 4WT, UK

This paper provides a critical examination of electronic data interchange (EDI) standards and their application in different types of trading relationships. It argues that EDI standards are not directly comparable to more stable sets of technical standards in that they are dynamically tested and negotiated in use with each trading exchange. It takes the position that EDI standards are an emergent language form and must mean different things at the institutional and local levels. Using the lens of emergent linguistic analysis i shows how the institutional and local levels must always be distinct and yet can coexist. EDI standards can never represent the creation of an ‘Esperanto of institutional communication’. Instead we believe that standards must be developed such that they support and accommodate general basic grammatical forms that can be customised to individual needs. The analysis is supported by a set of exemplary cases. European Journal of Information Systems (2000) 9, 173–188.

## Introduction

In Greek mythology the bandit Procrustes would invite people to spend a night in his iron bed. If the unsuspecting victim was shorter than the bed, his body was stretched, hammered or racked to fit. Alternatively, if the victim was longer than the bed, his legs were cut off to fit the bed’s length<sup>1</sup>. In either event the victim died. Over time the term ‘Procrustean bed’ has become synonymous with arbitrarily—and perhaps ruthlessly—forcing someone or something to fit an unnatural scheme or pattern.

The international standards governing interorganizational computer-to-computer electronic data interchange (EDI) are like the Procrustean bed. If accepted at face value they force organizations to truncate richness in their interorganizational communications while obliging a stretch to the manner by which they participate in interorganizational communications with members of a given industry. Standards like any ‘one-size-fits-all solution’ represent approximations, rather similar to that of a population mean. We rarely find real situations that exactly fit a theoretical norm, eg a perfect circle or a straight line. Like the Procrustean bed, a given standard, at any moment, may require the extension or truncation of the unfortunate ‘guest’ of the standard. So, we are convinced that the notion of a standard, whilst typically celebrated as an advance over a ‘non-standard’ (Farrell & Saloner, 1985; Katz & Shapiro, 1986; Farrell & Saloner, 1988; Quintas, 1994; Katz et al, 1995), represents a problem in interorganizational communication.

We illustrate our concern. Two dominant international EDI standards, UN/EDIFACT and ANSI X. 12, exist. These two EDI standards have been developed and advocated as technical problem solutions in interorganizational communication by two powerful international institutions; namely the United Nations Centre for the Facilitation of Procedures and Practices for Administration, Commerce and Transport (UN/CEFACT-http:// www.unece.org/cefact/) and the American National Standard Institute (ANSI-http://web.ansi.org/). They are also backed by numerous national governments, and by many other powerful institutions and trade and industry associations. Many companies believed that UN/EDIF ACT or ANSI X. 12 was a ‘neutral’ technical standard comparable to 220 V 50 Hz or 110 V 60 Hz or the ANSI/NISO Z39.50 library standard (Moen, 1998). Such standards may be the result of an emergent and path dependent process (David & Bunn, 1988; David, 1994; Moen, 1998) but the final result of the process is a fixed and invariant standard that is not open to negotiation. Yet companies that adopt any of the EDI standards are often surprised and disappointed when they discover that the standards do not exhibit characteristics of ordinary technical standards. To complicate the matter further, institutions that belong to the EDI movement advocate EDI as a general and invariant solution to the challenges of interorganizational communication. For example the Danish EDI council advocates that:

With the widespread diffusion of the EDIFACT-standard in the future it will only be necessary to deal with one format. EDIFACT functions as a kind of Esperanto of communication . . . The result will be independence in the choice of business partners when communicating electronically (http://www.edi.dk, our translation).

We will argue that existing EDI standards do not resolve problems with interorganizational communication. This is because these exchanges are not simply highly standardised technical exchanges. Rather they are a part of an ongoing and continuously evolving conversation or discourse between complex human organizations about a portion of their business relationship. These EDI exchanges are therefore part of embedded, goal-driven and dynamic business practice. But let us begin by illustrating a common misconception associated with EDI standards. Try to imagine what an individual buying a VCR that is based on the VHS standard would expect? This person rightfully expects that accessories are available, that tapes rented or purchased are playable, and that exchanging tapes with others will be non-problematic. Furthermore, the buyer expects the VHS standard to persist such that investment in tapes and equipment are not made obsolete overnight. Finally, he also expects a measure of stability in the standard and that future releases will be compliant with the previous ones.

In a similar vein, companies that build EDI transactional systems based on one of the two prevailing international EDI standards might expect these internationally endorsed standards to address and solve a majority of situations related to establishing, managing, and routinising interorganizational communication with business partners using the same standard. As in the VCR illustration, the company may expect the EDI standard to provide interoperability, stability, and persistence such that its investment is protected (Bailey et al, 1995; Katz et al, 1995). Indeed, much of the rhetoric found in consulting, management and software vendor literature suggests this outcome. Instead, and quite unexpectedly for some firms, EDI standards, to be of practical use, are not fixed, but require constant adjustment and maintenance (Brousseau, 1994). Rather than focusing on the dynamic nature of the EDI in use, the research literature ignores the fluidity of EDI standards in practice and chooses instead to discuss the stability of ‘ideal’ and relatively stable standards (Kremar et al, 1995; O’Callaghan & Turner, 1995; Riggins & Mukhopadhyay, 1999).

In contrast, this paper uses the lens of an emergent language analysis to address and provide an explanation to this apparent fluidity of EDI standards. To frame the discussion we provide a critical analysis of the underlying assumptions about technical standards that have been applied erroneously to EDI standards. We then argue that EDI standards are in fact not comparable with ‘neutral’ technical standards, nor are they merely rules governing the structure and format for interorganizational communication. On the contrary, they constitute emerging grammars and syntax, a language, for interorganizational communication. As Brousseau (1994) correctly notes EDI is both a telecommunications technology and a language technology. We see it as a kind of ‘work language’ or code, which is framed by, and must constantly adjust to, the ongoing particulars of the types of exchanges in which the business partners are engaging (Andersen, 1991; Andersen & Holmqvist, 1991). This is because within EDI users the EDI messages ‘. . . are strongly related to specific work practices in the sense they embody these practices . . . rather than being implemented only to automate existing coordination processes, EDI is mainly used to metamorphose them (Brousseau, 1994, p 321). The work practices, coordination practices and the language used to convey them and talk about them is in constant transition. They are all emergent. We offer recommendations as to how to understand, use, and manage interorganizational communication standards at the local organizational level, in the form of an alternative frame of reference that suggests a flexible compliance with ‘emergent standards’.

By emergent standards we mean real time, never fixed, always negotiable, but flexible frameworks, which guide rather than govern EDI transactions between trading partners. To avoid the dangers of a ‘fixed’ standard, the notion of an emergent standard always accepts that the particulars of a standard are in play and that we keep the discourse open and adaptive. Thus, emergent standards are not given. Rather, they are a continuing discourse about language formation, and language use, which must remain open and in constant negotiation. They are rooted in the practice of doing intraorganizational exchanges and in the requirement that those exchanges succeed. As Brousseau points out, the more general top-down generated standards are not designed to guarantee understanding but to construct unambiguous messages and avoid misunderstanding. So trading partners may appeal to the standard for guidance in the raw form of the message, in the same manner we use standardised and habitual forms from our spoken language of choice. But in the conduct of the actual exchange and the particulars of that exchange, it is the conveyance of the negotiated business practices from one partner to others that shape the meaning of the exchange. And the standard will necessarily be shaped or even abandoned at the service of completing a successful exchange. Hence, as Brousseau (1994, p 327) argues: ‘EDI standards are not usable per se. They have to make message interpretation rule explicit in order to suppress ambiguity’.

Brousseau’s analysis examines work practices, and we interpret Brousseau’s comments as an attempt to address a general misconception that EDI structures, by being standardised, can be fixed and invariant. Our view differs in that we see this misconception about structure and standards as arising, in large part, from a structural view of language itself. That is, as if language was a highly structured and rule driven system which was not shaped by practice. As will be explained in the section ‘EDI as emergent grammars’ following the discourse in contemporary linguistics, the emergent view of language does not accept this position.

This paper proceeds as follows. ‘Electronic data interchange’ defines and provides a linguistic view of EDI and associated standards and provides a formal definition of the notion of emergent grammars, which underlie our analysis. ‘EDI standards are good—myth or reality’ deconstructs the assumptions arising from technical standards as applied to the work of EDI use. This section also presents and discusses the framework used to analyse to a number of organizations studied in Europe and Hong Kong. ‘Illustrative cases’ provides recommendations based on this analysis. Finally, the ‘conclusions summarise our analysis and outline a proposal for further research.

## Electronic data interchange

## The nature of electronic data interchange

EDI provides trading partners with a tool for the automatic transmission of commercial data from one computer system residing in one organization directly to another (see Figure 1).

In a typical EDI set-up, business partners communicate by sending and receiving EDI messages (purchase orders, invoices and other business data). The sender’s computer system translates internal data into an intermediate standard language agreed between the partners and sends it as an EDI message to the receiver. The receiver’s computer does the reverse. It receives and interprets the EDI message and updates the appropriate information systems (IS) with the data. The structure, the rules governing the form of the messages, and the agreed-upon meanings of the message elements, are critical to a successful EDI interchange. It is important to notice that the receiving part is expected to act according to a received EDI message. So, if the receiver gets an invoice, the sender expects the receiver to then pay it (Hørlu¨ck, 1994).

For the purposes of this discussion, we define an EDI standard as a set of rules, procedures, and associated know-how, which constitute a form of grammar and syntax that guides interorganizational computer-to-computer communication. Accordingly, with its grammar, syntax, semantics and pragmatics providing a framework governing the conduct of EDI exchanges, EDI is a kind of language (Dietz, 1991; Brousseau, 1994; Hørlu¨ck, 1994). But like any language it is enacted in use, that is at the individual exchange level. As such, the standard, like any other language, is subject to the pressures of use.

Let us illustrate. Suppose that an academic colleague sends an EDI message stating that he will come to visit your department 10-03-99, at 12 o’clock. The syntax of the message may be clear, in fact the commitment, the date and the time may all reside in the proper message segment and field. The semantics governing the common notions of ‘visit’, ‘department’ and ‘arrival’ may also be clear. But does 10-03-99 mean the third of October or March tenth? And when should we expect our visitor to actually show up; when should we be concerned that something unusual has happened? Semantics describes how to interpret the date. The pragmatics will vary with culture and tradition, such that if our visitor is late more than ten minutes in Denmark one might worry; whereas, in other cultures, one may not become concerned before the next day. Moreover, there are local contingencies and power relationships to consider (David, 1994). A person being interviewed for a job might be expected to be more prompt than usual, whereas other persons—based on earlier experience—are expected to be late.

In this example, the structure of such an exchange might be governed by the EDI standard, but to be actionable the meanings are governed by the particulars of the interaction and the participants to the exchange. As these participants engage in many such exchanges they may discover that the structure is not sufficient or precise enough for their particular practical need. They must then alter the rules of a given exchange to accommodate their specific and local needs. This is how the EDI grammar is continuously emergent. It is under constant negotiation and adjustment as a consequence of the application of the grammar in interorganizational practice. Accordingly, whereas some authors may see an epistemological separation between a theory and the practice of such communications, we take a situated action view. Such a view suggests that standards only arise and emerge because of the continued interaction between theory and practice. This interaction defines the discourse about how these agreements map to interorganizational communications.

## EDI as emergent grammars

Our position is therefore one which characterises organizations themselves and the ongoing transactional discourses among trading partners, including those supported by EDI, as emergent. Hence, further explanation of our origin and use of the concept of emergence and its relationship to organizational language and EDI is in order.

We use the terms ‘emergent’ and ‘emergence’ rather than ‘emerging’ because ‘emergent’ refers to the state of being in continual process, never arriving but always in transition. ‘Emerging’ differs from ‘emergent’ because it gives rise to the possibility of a current state being a stage to a possible outcome and is always arising from its previous history and context. This distinction between the notions of ‘emergent’ versus ‘emerging’ can be described more formally by distinguishing between two general approaches to the study of systems. One of them accepts units, rules and meanings as prerequisites to the construction of a stable, bonded system that function as the basis for communication between users. This approach assumes a priori structures that precede communicative uses of the system between users. The alternative approach is to think of the system as being in constant flux as it is used in practice. In this view of a system, structure does not precede actual individual uses of the system, but is constantly being re-negotiated by individual users of the system and modifications found to be necessary in practice are added to the cumulative structure of the system in a constant process of feedback. Because structure can be viewed in this sense as constantly in change, structure is therefore an emergent property of the interaction rather than a structural prerequisite to acts of communication. It is an emergent property rather than a structural property that is emergent.

Organizational emergence refers to a theory of social organization that does not assume that stable structures underpin organizations or language (von Foerster, 1984; Truex et al, 2000). It is a process view of organizations which does not see them as stable and fixed entities but as entities that are ever in formation, constantly adapting to shifting environments. These organizations can be said to be in a state of constantly seeking stability, while never achieving it. Such organizations are said to be emergent (Truex & Klein, 1991; Truex, 1993; Truex & Baskerville, 1998; Truex et al, 1999). This notion is both poignant and relevant to the topic of EDI standards because as organizations change so do the types and nuances of their business interactions (Brousseau, 1994). Organizational forms, partnerships, and the nature of business relationships are all evolving in real time. So the way these activities are described, supported and linguistically represented is under constant pressure. This implies that the discourse about these evolving business processes, forms of organization and business relationships is itself an evolving property of those continuous adjustments. Hence we can say that the discourse is emergent. They are continually emergent, following no predefined pattern, the products of constant social negotiation and consensus building. They may exhibit temporal regularities, but are always in process; they are never fully formed.

Our characterisation of EDI as a type of emergent language form borrows liberally from linguist Paul Hopper’s formalisation of emergent grammars (Hopper, 1987, 1988, 1990, 1992; Hopper & Thompson, 1993). Hopper’s notion of emergence shares some systems science concepts of emergence. However, Hopper’s characterisation of emergent grammars embodies two important distinctions from the systems science contexts.<sup>2</sup> First, linguistic emergence assumes that unexpected behaviour entails no underlying structure, no matter how deeply embedded, that might be expressed as a set of structural equations in system simulations. Second, emergence is not anchored in any equilibrium-seeking behaviour or any ideal of progress. The focus of an emergent grammar is truly local, temporally anchored in the exchange of the moment, and interaction-oriented with little regard for, or awareness of, how it may advance the language system. The theory of emergent grammars is a viewpoint, which eliminates structure as a serious basis for linguistics. Emergent language theorists worry that structural linguists abstract only a trivial part of the communicative process and reify it, thereby making language an autonomous entity. Those favoring the emergent perspective see grammar as an evolving response to language environment, or to discourse. For them grammar is not based on categories of structure or of fixed rules and standards but rather on the human ability to use previous utterances as a basis of new utterances (Hopper, 1990, 1992; Hopper & Thompson, 1993). It is the similar attributes of EDI interaction that draw our attention to emergent grammars as a frame of reference.

Emergent linguists are principally concerned with how speech (or language as communication) happens, that is the performative aspects of language. Any appearance of structure in grammar arises from certain regularities in the reference to previous utterances. Language is in constant flux as it is used in practice. In this view structure does not precede actual individual uses of the language system but is constantly being renegotiated by individual users of the language system. Modifications found to be necessary in the real-time use of the language are added to the cumulative structure of the system in a constant process of feedback. In this view, grammars are not structures which are given or innate to the speaker, but arise from the product of speech. Emergent theory sees structure as fragmented and distributed and language as moving towards systematicity without ever reaching stasis. The appearance of structure is momentary regularity, distributed over time, and is not simultaneously present for all users or for study. The development of language is, therefore, a continuous, real-time process. As noted earlier, EDI standards are subject to the same kinds of processes, which render any grammar emergent. EDI, like any language, is a form of discourse in which the grammar is ever evolving through situated use.

Thus standards present a dilemma. On the one hand they serve as templates to insure coherence and preserve negotiated agreements and knowledge. On the other hand they may confine the discourse such that certain trading transactions may be seen as ‘radical’ from a standards perspective. Since many trading relationships must reflect unique interactions and relationships there must necessarily emerge expressions of those relationships not covered by the standard. In order to participate meaningfully in the discourse means the participants must necessarily be ‘out of bounds’ with the standard. Thus any notion of an EDI standard as stable and robust is inherently flawed. A ‘one-size-fits-all’ approach can never work.<sup>3</sup>

EDI standards represent efforts to codify the discourse by structuring the formal message through which the discourse is conducted. And since about 1985, two dominant EDI standards have evolved.<sup>4</sup> It is the existence of a myriad of local implementation guidelines (and subsets) of these standards, however, which reflects the dilemma. That is, how do we define the standards broadly enough to accommodate a large variety of business needs, while at the same time trying to keep the standards simple and useful (Hørlu¨ck, 1994). To further complicate the matter new versions of the international standards are issued at an increasing pace (Salminen, 1994, 1995). Given the previous discussion this should come as little or no surprise. The standards themselves are shaped by the wider discourse on standardising combined with the practice of conducting intraorganizational computer mediated exchanges. Thus any attempt to ‘fix’ a standard will not succeed. Although we may provide a framework for an exchange, the framework is devoid of meaning arising from the particulars of an exchange. At worst, it will be wholly ignored, thus losing any of the benefits arising from the existence of a standard. Thus there is a constant tension arising from forces needing to seek stability and uniformity and the forces needing to deal at the level of performance of given exchanges.

Next, having examined the issue of emergent ‘standardising’ forces, let us continue by examining the rationale and challenges associated with the creation and use of such standards governing business-to-business electronic interchanges.

## Benefits of EDI usage

Despite the challenges described in the preceding section, there are significant advantages to adopting an EDI standard as a stand-alone intermediary that automates the exchange of transactions between a pair of trading partners. The immediate benefits of this automation, called first order benefits, are relatively predictable and measurable. A more important class of benefits is referred to as second order benefits (O’Callaghan & Turner, 1995). These second order benefits are less predictable and may only emerge over time.

First order benefits are generally associated with a reduction in labour and handling costs, typical of automation, whereas second order benefits help define a change in supplier/customer relationships and organizational workflow. The first order benefits only increase the speed and reliability of interorganizational connections. The second order benefits can only be fully realised if EDI adoption is followed by radical process reengineering initiatives such as Just-In-Time production and delivery, Zero-Inventory, and Every-Day-Low-Cost initiatives (Clark & Croson, 1994; Clark & Hammond, 1995; Clark & Lee, 1996). Typical first and second order benefits, as reported in pertinent literature, are given in the table below (Pfeiffer, 1992; O’Callaghan & Turner, 1995; Kremar et al, 1995). Most would concur that the ‘real’ advantage of EDI is related to achieving the second order benefits (Brousseau, 1994).

## A typology of EDI usage

EDI relationships can be classified in a number of ways. O’Callaghan and Turner (1995) suggest three types of configurations, 1:1, 1:M, and M:M. Our similar typology of EDI use is depicted in Figure 2.

In Figure 2(b) below the two companies connected are engaged in a binary trading relationship. The business relationship is highly developed and based on mutual trust. Both companies are aimed at achieving virtual business integration (Short & Venkatraman, 1992). The EDI setup is therefore highly specialised and customised to accommodate the needs of both business partners. This is often achieved by building computer-based systems that cater for the highly integrated business processes that are supported by the EDI link. The interaction is governed by a set of local and constantly evolving grammatical rules that denote the common language used between the two interacting companies. The semantics of the ‘EDI work language’ are shaped by the particular work practices of each organization and the way the partners agree to reflect these practices in the particulars of their EDI exchanges. This is like a local work language evolving through constant interaction.

Figure 2(d) depicts a setup of a number of companies engaged in market-like transactions. Here the business relationships are shorter and more general than in the previous example. Whenever a company wishes to engage in business with another company that is part of the industry network, it will send an EDI message that is based on the general grammar agreed upon by all the industry players and often backed by some common industry association (Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 2000). Of course, most companies engage in business relations with several different EDI partners. O’Callaghan and Turner (1995) do not attempt to capture this in their description. We believe that it is imperative to take these different classes of relationship into account because the differences have profound implications for the adopting unit and its use of EDI standards. In time most companies that adopt EDI will be simultaneously engaged in all three types of relationships. For some close partners with highly specialised EDI, links will be developed (binary setup). For other less frequent or less critical partners, the EDI transactions will be based on general industry or international standards (market setup), and at the same time a company may be part of a ‘hub and spoke’ relationship wherein the particular set of translations are dictated by the more powerful ‘hub’ partner. As we will demonstrate later, a single generalised or ‘universal’ grammar will not suffice in all three types of EDI relationships. We argue that these relationships and the grammars through which they are maintained, are not fixed, but constantly evolve. These are the focus of the discussion in the following section.

## EDI standards are good—myth or reality?

The use of standards is celebrated over non-standards for a number of reasons. Firstly, technical standards are generally believed to provide a common vocabulary and as such help to preserve knowledge by making tacit knowledge more explicit. Therefore, the standardisation process is also instrumental in knowledge creation and sharing, since it is conceptualised through combined efforts. Secondly, there is the promise that using standards reduces transaction costs. Thirdly, it is hoped that investing in developing systems that adhere to some standard yields economics of scale and associated network externalities. Finally, building standard-based systems is thought to reduce risks, since standard-based systems are generally believed to be useful and more stable than non-standard proprietary systems. Yet, as is suggested in the previous section, these assumptions may not hold for EDI based interorganizational communication.

## Preservation and sharing of knowledge

It is thought that developing and maintaining a common EDI grammar helps to create, preserve, and share knowledge among trading partners. This shared knowledge is embedded into rules for what constitute the foundation of a legitimate interorganizational conversation between complex human organizations. In this sense, both best practice and past experience as to what constitutes good conversations is preserved (David, 1994). When described and discussed in terms of the ‘binary’, ‘market’ and ‘hub-spoke’ scenarios, the preservation of knowledge takes surprisingly different forms, which may ultimately weigh against the development and maintenance of shared knowledge.

## Binary set-up

In the binary set-up, the preservation and sharing of knowledge is highly local and understood almost exclusively by the two trading partners. The local grammar is constantly renegotiated and adjusted during conversations. As a consequence, their grammar is highly applicable and precise for their specific binary conversations, so the two companies enjoy a high degree of virtual vertical business integration and alignment.

## Market

In the market model, grammars serve to preserve knowledge about what constitutes best practice and generic knowledge about how to perform business transactions. Often a number of market players and industry associations come together to identify and describe an EDI grammar unique to these exchanges (Boch, 1987; Sako, 1996; Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 2000). It is necessary to identify the set of grammar which is widely understood by everyone that operates within the market, therefore the agreed grammar often constitutes the lowest common denominator of common knowledge about business processes. Of course, there is the danger that the lowest common denominator is too general to be practical in any actual conversations between trading partners.

## Hub vs spoke

In the hub and spoke set-up the preservation of knowledge is almost entirely restricted to the hub. This is a power-driven relationship where the grammar is highly localised, specific and customised to support the hub’s internal processes. The spokes have little bargaining power in installing their knowledge into the common grammar. This is hardly a shared view of discourse, whereby the King’s English is the only admissible dialect.

## Providing a common vocabulary

It is a common misconception that the meaning of words is relatively fixed over time and distance; they may be captured and later referenced in a dictionary. But word meanings are always subject to negotiation, interpretation and subtle adjustment in use. The construction of ‘work languages’ (Andersen, 1991; Andersen & Holmqvist, 1991) is in part described by the way in which people sharing common tasks arrive at vocabulary agreements while using the language. Work languages or codes exist at all levels of work activity and levels of work abstraction and may vary between different groups of people performing the same work. So it is not at all unusual that an individual may hold membership in many work language units. Language evolves through use; thus it is that the meaning of various EDI activities, as forms of language, may also evolve in their use.

As we have already noted there are differences between the transactional interactions between established trading partners and in the nature of the rules establishing the business relations that allow these transactions. In the linguistic arena this is equivalent to institutional languages which frame and guide the use of language and the actual conversations in which the rules are adjusted, redefined and made up as the conversation occurs. The conversation may need to reference the institutional form of language initially, but, in the conversation in the act of communicating, the form of the institutional language is itself amended, at least temporarily, and perhaps even ultimately extended. The analog to EDI standards is, of course, that the transactions are part of continuous, long-standing and on-going ‘conversations’ where the extant EDI standard may be viewed as the institutional language. Learning and adapting one’s systems to a particular EDI code is a significant investment and represents ‘sunk’ organizational capital (Arrow, 1974). The investment tends to lock firms into the way they frame and wish to share transactions. Because of this, different organizations rarely share the same language (Schelling, 1960; David, 1994).

## Binary

This relationship is heavily transaction-based and relies on the history of previous successful transactions (conversations) to serve as a backdrop for current interaction. It is customised, comfortable, unique and constantly being adjusted to the needs of a given transaction. Because it is a kind of personal or family language, it matters little if the vocabulary is a standard and the rules are followed exactly because the two companies have customised them for their own use.

## Market

Here we see an attempt to rely on the institutional form of the language. The EDI standard is a lowest common denominator in both a transactional and business relation sense. So it becomes necessary to limit the discourse to the rather sparse set of general and more narrowly defined grammatical elements. As such the interaction is more rigid, limited and less rich than in other settings.

## Hub vs spoke

The opportunity for customisation is less democratised in this power-based interaction. On the one hand, the interactions are customised to the needs of the hub, on the other hand, the spokes find that, to the extent they are participants in numerous hub-spoke relationships, they must adopt the custom grammars of many ‘hub trading partners. The benefits of relative customisation inure to the hub at the cost of the spokes. To the degree the spokes can exert pressure it is likely that they will pressure for a more general set of transactional rules (eg, industry standards) to reduce the overhead of managing multiple custom languages.

## Transaction costs

There is the general belief that standards reduce transaction costs. Because standards exist any transaction between trading partners need not start from scratch. The standard and associated grammar defines the rules of the transaction and that which is and is not admissible to the discourse (ie, reduces the cost of creating the contract), and it ensures procedures for monitoring the transaction. The standards help us to know when the relationship has broken down and thus reduces the cost of reconciliation and arbitration.

## Binary set-up

In the binary relationship the two business partners will have invested considerable resources in developing a common vocabulary and grammar to precisely describe and define the intended transactions. The common vocabulary and grammar are regularly renegotiated and adjusted by the trading partners. Therefore the asset of specific investments when developing and maintaining the mutual set of grammars are high. In daily conversations the benefit of precisely and accurately being able to describe transactions keeps the costs per transaction low. The invested capital in having low transaction costs has a lock-in effect in the relationship. Thus, even if more attractive partners are available in the market (offering cheaper or better products or services), the trading partner in question will be reluctant to change trading partner because the switching costs are so high. But overall transaction costs are low.

## Market

In the market relation relatively few resources have been invested in developing a precise and common grammar, and therefore initiating a transaction with trading partners constitutes a significant investment (compared to the above example). Consequently the transaction costs remain high for market-like transactions (for each new transaction the market is scanned for potential partners). However the industry-wide standard defines a basic set of grammatical rules on which further negotiations and local interpretations can take place (essentially resulting in a binary EDI setup). So, market model transactions at a rather general and nonspecific level may evolve to more binary-like transactions over time and as trust and semantic specificity are negotiated into the particulars of the given partners relationships. This evolution serves to keep transaction costs low.

## Hub vs spoke

In the hub and spoke model little effort is put into developing a mutual beneficial grammar. Instead the hub defines the grammar according to its own needs and pushes its satellite spokes to adopt its dialect for all conversations. Since essentially all spokes must adopt its grammar, it is the hub that enjoys low transaction costs. The spokes on the other hand must carry the costs of adjusting its conversations to the grammar dictated by the hub and thus their transaction costs remain high. This is especially true for the spokes that serve several masters. They must be able to speak several local dialects fluently to please the demanding hub. Thus the overall transaction costs are high.

## Economies of scale

Traditionally economies of scale refer to an industrial production setting wherein long-run ‘technological efficiencies’ are gained by increasing plant size and labour specialisation such that the costs of production are driven to a minimum. The downside, resulting in diseconomies of scale, occurs when plant size and the specialisation of labour is so great that the transaction costs associated with oversight and management of systems components exceeds the savings of specialisation and size.

For the purposes of this paper we distinguish between the concept of ‘the transaction’ and the concept of the business relationship. There may be one, or many, transactions between business partners. But because subtle differences in the character of a transaction may require adjustments in the trading rules governing the transaction, it is important to be able to distinguish individual transactions. The business relationship is a necessary condition that must be established before a transaction may be undertaken. Economies of scale (as well as the analog diseconomies of scale) may therefore be realised at both the transactional and business relationship levels.

At the transactional level, an economy of scale is realised when the EDI grammar strikes a balance between precision and generality. It must be sufficiently precise to allow for a normal degree of complexity within a class of transactions (eg, a purchase order) and yet remain sufficiently general to represent a class of transactions such that each need not be treated as unique entities.

By economies of scale of business relationships we refer to the relative ease and cost of establishing a business relationship sufficient to support transactions. This must necessarily consider the balance between the precision of a class of business communications versus the generalizability of those communications. That is, there is a trade-off between general rule governed interactions with a larger number of trading partners versus more precise and customised interactions with a smaller number of partners. In EDI terms this is illustrated when a firm must choose whether to relinquish a local, perhaps market specific, set of EDI rules to move to the more general international EDI/EDIFACT standard. In the exchange a degree of precision is relinquished for greater generalisability. Thus economies of scale at the business relationship level are achieved when trading partners are willing/able to accept a world view and set of grammatical conventions as a backdrop to the relationship.

## Binary set-up

In the binary set-up the investment made to develop the local and highly customised grammar allows the two business partners to achieve economics of scale on a transaction basis because it allows the partners to exchange complex information accurately. However the local/customised grammar cannot easily be used for communication with other partners. Hence it is not possible to capture economics of scale on a business relation level (the local dialect is not widely spoken and understood). Diseconomies of scale occur in this set-up when the investment in customising and managing the local grammar exceeds the savings of specialisation.

## Market

In the market a large number of companies have agreed on a general grammar. This means that all adopters of this grammar constitute potential EDI business partners. Therefore the market relation gives economics of scale on a business relation level (widely spoken and understood but lacks local precision and flavour). Because of the very general grammar it is not sufficiently precise enough to allow for economics of scale on a transaction level (too general). Diseconomies of scale happen when the grammar is too general to support any meaningful transactions between companies. This means that meaningful transactions can only be initiated after local interpretation to recreate meaning.

## Hub vs spoke

Here the hubs take all benefits from both types of economics of scale. The hub enforces its own local and customised grammar to be accepted by the less powerful spokes. Thereby it becomes both a common, widespread and customised grammar from the point of view of the hub. The spokes do not benefit from any of the economics of scale. They must accept the special grammar dictated by the hub, and furthermore they usually must learn several local dialects (one from each hub they serve). The hub and spoke kind of relationship can be supported only insofar that the spokes are willing to submit to the unequal relationship. In time they will seek more consensual structures or relations that provide greater benefit. Hence the costs of maintaining such a relationship are high. The burden of the spokes in learning multiple changing grammars increases the incentive to build intermediating institutions to provide a more balanced grammar (Attewell, 1992; Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 2000).

## Network externalities

For many standards the concept of network externalities is applicable (Katz & Shapiro, 1986; Oliva, 1994). Standards with high network externalities such as the telephone system, the ZIP code system and the internet and world wide web where impact and value of the technology increases as more people embrace the technology. However high externalities also mean, in contrast to many other innovations, that the benefits of being an early adopter can be relatively low compared to be being a ‘laggard’. Simply put, if you are the first one who has a telephone, who are you going to call? The network externalities make it beneficial to postpone adoption of a standard until most potential partners have adopted and the prevailing grammar can be identified.

## Binary set-up

In the binary set-up the local and volatile nature of the grammar makes the network externalities limited. However, insofar as a widely accepted grammar is used as the basis for the local conversations, there are some network externalities in play.

## Market

In the market the concept of network externalities is highly applicable. The more firms that adopt a given standard the more lucrative it becomes for others to follow suit resulting in ‘herd effects’ of simultaneous adoption decisions. However, local dialects as well as competing standards or coexisting implementation guidelines that are in play deplete the network externalities.

## Hub vs spoke

In the hub vs spoke configuration the network externalities are limited to the hub only. From the point of view of the hub it seems that its standard is widely accepted and thus it enjoys high network externalities. From the point of view of the spokes it is quite different. Because of lack of coordination between hubs each spoke must adopt and learn several standards and implementation guidelines. This leads to ineffective adoptions of incompatible local grammars.

## Reduces risk

As standards-based technologies are generally useable longer than non standards-based technologies, systems built to accommodate EDI standards are therefore assumed to reduce risk because of an inherent stability in the standard. Both the assumption of stability and the notion that stability lowers risk are however, subject to challenge. First, any stability that does exist is relative, short-lived, and is achieved at a cost. Secondly, stability itself limits the evolution of the EDI as a form of discourse. Williamson argues that networks are inherently unstable, and under environmental stress tend to break down (Williamson, 1979, 1989). We will illustrate how in the world of EDI linguistics the opposite holds true, that is, the networked interacting web of transactions between binary trading partners is the underlying source of stability. Moreover, it is the generalised standard that fails in favour of the network when the system is stressed.

## Binary

Here the nature of the business relationship is familiarity and trust, and thus risks are low. The discourse is not limited but is driven by the needs of a given transaction. The discourse is open, flexible and allows for the complexity and uniqueness of the particular transaction.

## Market

In the market model, risk is thought to be reduced because the standard sets the structure of the interactions at both the transactional and the business relationship levels. This structure is achieved at a cost. The cost is in the loss of precision and ability to deal with the particulars of individual relationships. Risk is actually introduced into the system because the standard restricts the elements that may be introduced into the discourse. The underlying pressures of emergence do not cease. At the transactional level trading partners are forced to adjust or find ‘work-around’ solutions to address needs that are not part of a given standard. These pressures build, eventually forcing an adjustment in the standard itself. This is illustrative of how the transaction is basic ‘linguistic’ interaction, which, while guided by the standard, also shapes and makes it impossible for an emergent standard to ever become fixed or frozen. So to the extent a standard does not adapt to the transactional pressures for change and adaptation it actually introduces risk into the system. When the standards do not meet the transactional needs the trading partners, who must after all make the system work for them, retreat to a binary, or network-based environment, abandoning the standard in favour of a local solution. Hence it is that in environments of great change and instability, it is the network model, which is more stable than the market model (Cunningham & Tynan, 1993).

## Hub vs spoke

Risk is reduced for the hub partner to the extent it may control the relationship and discourse. For the spoke the reality is somewhat different. Any investment in EDI to serve a hub is subjected to change at the will of the hub, since the spokes possess little bargaining power in installing their preferences into the grammar and its evolution.

## Illustrative cases

In this section we illustrate the emergent nature of EDI standards application. The focus is to illustrate by reference to practice how EDI standards are constantly modified under the pressure of use. The three cases are drawn from five years of fieldwork in Hong Kong and northern Europe in which managers from more than 50 companies were interviewed about their implementation of EDI (Damsgaard, 1996).

The set of informants used in the study covered companies that are involved in EDI standardisation and use: government agencies, semi public organizations, trade and industry associations, EDI service providers, and individual user organizations. An interpretive case study approach as recommended by Walsham (1993), and Klein and Myers (1999) was performed to collect and analyse information about the phenomena. Detailed data were collected about EDI use and its diffusion and standardisation process through semi-structured interviews and review of documents. Interviewees spanned both horizontal and vertical organizational structures and included CEOs, senior government officers, vice presidents, corporate EDI managers, and EDI consultants. Each interview lasted between one to two hours; they were tape-recorded and summaries were written and subsequently approved by each interviewee.

Each of the three illustrations below was selected from the sample because they are illustrative of EDI typology depicted in Figure 2. We have chosen the illustrations from different cultural and legal settings to underpin the striking similarities in the types of problems faced and concerns voiced by management when implementing EDI standards. As such these are not presented strictly as an empirical test of the theory but as exemplars representing the structures and emergent forms discussed above. Coupled with further work they provide a backdrop against which additional tests may be made.

## The binary EDI model: the case of a Finnish supplier and a wholesaler

This case is organized and managed through a joint approach to develop a mutually beneficiary EDI service. It involves a binary relationship between a supplier and a wholesaler (Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 1998). The two companies take both dominant positions in the retail sector in Finland. Together they are moving towards an increasingly complex and mutual beneficial use of EDI by aligning and supporting their interorganizational business processes through it.

The business relationship has been long-term and builds on mutual trust. This is reflected in the ordering and delivery process, which is quite complicated and built around highly integrated business processes within ordering, replenishment and production cycles. This way both companies enjoy a high degree of virtual vertical integration and business process alignment. There are of course huge operational first order benefits to be gained from automating the ordering process, but the real benefits are in optimising inventories and production schedules (second order benefits). In extending the virtual integration the wholesaler will soon be sending data, which will allow the supplier to implement EDI even further into its operation and production scheduling procedures.

In this binary set-up the preservation and sharing of knowledge and establishing of a common vocabulary between the two companies are clear. Transaction costs are minimised and business processes are aligned in a highly precise and local grammar. Thus the two companies have achieved economies of scale on a transaction basis, however, due to the local and customised dialect there are few economies of scale on a business relation level, as well as there are few network externalities. The long-term relationship and mutual trust make the risks associated with investments in developing the local dialect low and it justifies the large investment made in building the tailor-made system. The experiences of this binary EDI set-up are summarised in Table 3.

In this particular case it led to an innovative but legitimate solution. The solution was that EDIFACT standards all contain a ‘free text’ segment, which is intended for a short format-free message. In this case the segment was over-utilised to accommodate local bilateral agreements and practices that were processable by a receiving computer. So the segment became the carrier for various non-standard pieces of information like conditions for delivery, notes about discounts etc, thus allowing the discourse and refinement of the institutional grammar to stay open.

In this case the point of departure was a sound desire to avoid the pitfalls of earlier adoption failures elsewhere. The involved trade associations therefore sought to preserve and share existing knowledge by having competitors and business partners alike define and implement one single set of grammatical rules and syntax (ie, an institutional or market language). However, what the industry representatives could compromise on was the lowest common denominator to describe the business processes of their industry, and as such they provided a common, but not very precise vocabulary. The widespread adoption of this institutional language allows the companies to achieve economies of scale on a business level, so relations can be initiated relatively easily by using the common language. On a transaction level the language was not sufficiently precise, which the overuse of the free text segment clearly demonstrates. In this case the network externalities are high since the institutional language becomes increasingly attractive with the number of adopters, however, the extensive free text segment’s usage increases knowledge barriers and stands as an obstacle that partly depletes the positive externalities. The active backing of the trading association should increase trust in the standard, however the extensive use of the free text segments actually increases the risks associated when investing in it. ## The hub vs spoke EDI model: the case of international shippers and a Hong Kong container terminal

The next case is an example of a hub vs spoke set-up and illustrates the difficulties that spokes realise when being forced to set up several customised EDI links with powerful hubs (Damsgaard & Lyytinen, 1997).

In Hong Kong large international shipping lines persuaded a Hong Kong container terminal operator to start to use EDI. The terminal operator recognises that most shipping lines are eager to shift from paper to EDI use, but it does not believe that it benefits much from EDI. On the contrary, the EDI system has increased its workload. The terminal now must capture more information about each container movement, which it did not earlier have to do and which it does not use itself. Moreover, each shipping line requires different types of messages to be sent. Though each shipping line argued that they followed the EDIFACT standard, the problem was, however, that this standard was too broad to specify accurately all the details needed to define a transportation transaction. The terminal’s operations manager explained:

Different companies have different operational procedures. It is impossible to match all these needs. Everybody should be more flexible and seek to compromise more. The shipping lines receive the same format but different contents. We have different set-ups for different shipping lines. That is a hell of a job actually. The shipping lines also change their requirements from time to time and we have to cope with this also.

This means that the container terminal has to learn and sustain a number of different grammars and local dialects for sending and formatting EDI messages for each of its hub partners. Before EDI the same paper form was used for communication with all the customers, but now each shipping line with sufficient power imposes its own dialect and ever changing content.

In this case few of the celebrations of standards were achieved for the container terminal. The terminal did not influence the communication at all. So there was no preservation or sharing of knowledge from its point of view. The dedicated EDI link did not reduce transaction costs, on the contrary it increased them, and there was no common vocabulary—as in the past with the paper-based form. The container terminal did not achieve economies of scale in its communication with the hub shipping lines, and there were few network externalities since each shipping line requires its de facto EDIFACT-flavoured syntax and grammar to be used. Finally investing in the EDI link did not reduce risks since the grammatical rules for the conversion were constantly changing at the will of the hubs. However using the EDIFACT as the basis for the hubs preferred grammar did allow the terminal to create knowledge and experience in using the grammars.

The terminal was obviously displeased with the current EDI setup, it was therefore active in forums aimed to define a common Hong Kong EDI standard for the whole transportation sector, and thus the terminal was also a player in a market set-up (Figure 2(d)).

## Discussions and recommendations

There are profound differences between pure technical standards and applying EDI standards for interorganizational communication. There are different types of EDI standards, and each must be studied at the appropriate level. And while there exist domains in which the application of a given type may be appropriate, the mismatching of one type to the wrong domain virtually guarantees failure. Thus, in the world of EDI, the term itself has multiple meanings with each meaning referencing different levels of abstraction, or use. These different meanings are not given sufficient credence or attention. A substantial factor in the dissatisfaction with EDI arises from the application of technical standards to what is essentially human activity even though a purely technical standard generally does not involve human intervention. However, EDI is typically promoted as a means to model organizations, their human actors and their activities as if it were a technical standard.

We have used the lens of linguistic analysis, to provide an explanation as to why standards must mean different things at the local and institutional levels of analysis and to each different type of trading relationship. Our analysis shows how the institutional and local levels must always be distinct and yet can coexist. We believe that ANSI and EDIFACT standards are justifiable at an institutional level. But the EDI movement tries to enforce the adoption and ‘sell’ use of these standards at local levels of interaction as well.

The standard setting bodies have the goal to create universal, general, highly regulated and ultimately stable ‘institutional’ languages. We find this goal to be misguided and unattainable. For, as we have argued, it is the local conversations in the form of specific binary interactions and not the institutional language that allow for the almost infinite number of possible combinations of possible interactions. To be useful beyond the most basic level, trading partners must adapt a standard to the particulars of a given interchange. The process of making reference to an institutional language, then adapting and subtly altering the grammar to fit specific binary transactions describes how the standard evolves beyond all but the lowest levels of institutional relevance. Only when adapted to the specifics of the local conversation can it accommodate the business process needs of transacting organizations, and second order benefits are reached. Our analysis therefore leads us to make recommendations targeted at the institutional and the organizational meanings of standards.

At the individual company level we recommend a careful requirement analysis of how the standards should be applied. Each interorganizational communication process can be supported either as a blunt technical instrument to automate bureaucratic routines where the pure institutional universal language is highly applicable, but this application will only yield first order benefits. Or, on the other hand, EDI can be applied as a business tool that can completely reengineer the value chain, where more substantial second order benefits come into reach. In the latter instance the institutional language may be limiting this possibility, and EDI standards are more in compliance with emergent grammars. In the former case, EDI as governed by a strict technical standard is justifiable.

At the institutional, standard-setting level we recommend that institutions abandon the notion that we will ever achieve an institutional language that is stable, complete and precise across the whole spectrum from syntax to pragmatics for all businesses and business processes worldwide. Instead we encourage institutions to deal with a general syntax, and semantics, and avoid the temptation to build pragmatics into the standard. The key is to focus less on standards and more on standardising.

## Conclusions

In this paper we have argued that EDI standards are not directly comparable to that of technical standards. Instead EDI grammars are emergent in nature and rigid institutional EDI standards are often challenged by practical use. At the local organizational level institutional standards should therefore be used merely as a frame of reference, flexibly interpreted and applied, ie, as ‘emergent standards’. At the institutional level we call for a simplification by abandoning the quest for universal and complete grammars. The intent should never be the creation of an ‘Esperanto of institutional communication’. For a way forward, institutions should recognise the distinction between what David (1994) calls human organizations and ‘machine organizations’<sup>5</sup>, where sensate and volitional actors in the former do not populate the latter.

Instead we believe that standards must be developed such that they support and accommodate general basic grammatical forms that can be customised to individual needs. Variety is accommodated through customisation and not selecting specific segments or adding/layering segment upon segment with the rules of use. One way forward is meta-standards and dynamic flexible institutional language standards such as XML (eXtensible Markup Language, see Harvey (1998) or http://www.xmledi.net/), which has a more suitable and customisable grammer. However, there are signs that institutions are building Procrustean beds out of the XML standard too.

In essence, institutions should not try to define an entire encyclopedia that covers all possible conversations that may be imagined between business partners, as it will collapse of its own weight and in actual conversations, as we have argued in this paper from both a theoretical and empirical point of view. Instead, institutions should acknowledge the usefulness of ambiguity, and put a halt to efforts to define and promote a complete and exhaustive standard for all possible interorganizational conversations. Therefore the discourse is the game. We must preserve the continuing binary interaction with the possibility of an out-of-standard interaction. The tension between having the self-referential nature of the standard, which serves as a springboard for further experimentation and the restrictive nature of the boundaries defined by the standard is useful and a desirable feature of interorganizational communication of business information. After all when Procrustes himself was put to the test of his own bed, he did not fit.
