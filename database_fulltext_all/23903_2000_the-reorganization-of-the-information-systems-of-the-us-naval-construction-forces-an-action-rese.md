# The reorganization of the information systems of the US Naval Construction Forces: an action research project

SJ Simon

Decision Sciences and Information Systems, Florida International University, University Park, Miami, FL, USA

Organization change is a difficult task under the best of circumstances. Change dictated from outside the organization makes a reorganization project even more difficult. This study follows the information systems reorganization of the US Naval Construction Forces whose restructuring was directed as a result of budget cuts and military ‘right-sizing’. The process was conducted through the use of action research, an intensive investigative method seeing limited use in information system’s research. This method directly involves the researcher in the organizational change effort while placing organization members in close collaboration with the researcher. The study describes the action research methodology while illustrating the research strategy in phases and recounts the successful migration from a highly centralised to a decentralised organi zation. European Journal of Information Systems (2000) 9, 148–162

## Introduction

As the critical revelations of information systems (IS) research problems continue, interest grows in alternative methods of discovery (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1996). The victory in the Cold War has led to profound changes and expectations in the armed forces of the United States and her allies. Downsizing and consolidation have been the watchwords guiding the congressionally mandated reorganization. As part of that reorganization, the US navy has fostered the creation of a one Navy concept which integrates reserve forces with their active duty counterparts. This study follows the process which aided the Naval Construction Forces (NCF) through the reorganization of their IS infrastructure. The study used an intensive research strategy, action research. This technique, utilised in organization development and other social sciences, has seen limited use in the IS area.

The aspects of the reorganization in which this study is interested are the changes in the IS organization. At the time of the reorganization, the NCF was in the process of upgrading IS assets, installing local area networks in all their units, and implementing a new IS to improve connectivity at home or abroad while facilitating project management and enhancing productivity. Unfortunately, the IS support organization for the NCF was centralised in the reserve brigade that was slated to be absorbed into the other brigades (see Appendix A for description of the NCF, its mission, and organization structure). As a result of the reorganization, the staff of the reserve brigade was to be greatly reduced and the majority of their facilities (located in Mississippi) turned over to other commands. Despite the problems and inconveniences associated with the mandated reorganization, the IS reorganization provided the NCF with a remarkable opportunity to examine and make needed improvements in the existing structure.

This paper chronicles the use of the action research method while illustrating the IS reorganization project. Section one provides some background information on organization development and the organization setting of the project. The methodology of the study is briefly examined. The cycles and outcome of the research project are chronicled in section three. Section four discusses the project, key action research characteristics used, and its execution. The final section draws conclusions from the study.

## Background and organizational setting

The NCF with its systems and personnel are organised in a rigid, hierarchical manner. Personnel are very knowledgeable within their assigned technical areas, but lack broad overall knowledge especially in the management area. Further, officers and senior enlisted, the equivalent of an organization’s managers, are rotated to a different command after only three years. Hierarchical organizations are known for their top-down orientation and the NCF fits this stereotype. The reorganization project of the NCF’s IS infrastructure was impacted by the transition to a new IS designed to expedite the flow, handling, and tracking of material while providing project management. The system and its uninterrupted operation are critical to the execution of the NCF’s mission; once installed, system failure could result in the stoppage or slowdown of construction projects. Further, the topdown nature of the organization created a situation where the highest levels of the command structure (equivalent to senior executive management) were included in the change process. These factors provided unique challenges to the change and research processes.

For any reorganization effort to be successful, the structure of the organization must be understood because ultimately, during the reorganization effort that structure will change. Additionally, researchers and organization participants must comprehend the structures they seek to modify (Cunningham, 1993). Henderson and Clark (1990) provide a scheme which they call architectural knowledge for the basis of that understanding, and with that scheme a tool for affecting change. Architectural knowledge is knowledge about the ways in which an organization is integrated and linked together into a coherent whole. Henderson and Clark further indicate that architectural innovation is the reconfiguration of an established system to link together existing components in a new way. The challenge for any reorganization effort is to recognise what is useful and what is not, and to acquire and apply new knowledge to affect change when necessary.

The procedures that managers use, their channels for communication, and their information filters emerge in an organization to help it cope with complexity (Henderson & Clark, 1990). An organization’s communication channels, both those that are formal and those that are informal, develop around interactions within the organization that are critical to its task (Galbraith, 1973; Arrow, 1974). These are also the interactions that are considered critical to effective design. Thus an organization’s communications channels will come to embody its architectural channels (Henderson & Clark, 1990). Within the NCF organization, the communication channels reflect the hierarchical nature of the organization’s structure. It is critical to the norms of the NCF to exercise ‘the chain of command’, the act of submitting requests or suggestions to one’s immediate superior with that procedure repeated until the item reaches the highest levels of the organization. Given this structure, communication and information is directed and filtered as it progresses through the command structure. This could lead to a reduction in innovation, as ideas are suppressed or modified before they reach decision making levels.

## Method

Action research has been used in the field of social science since its inception in the 1940s as a research strategy that integrates theory and practice through change and reflection (Lewin, 1947; Argyris et al, 1985). The long-term intent of action research is to transfer ownership and control of the research process from the researcher alone first to a team, referred to as the action research team, comprised of the researcher and members of the organization being studied and ultimately to the organization alone. Action research enhances diffusion of new ideas, changes in individual behaviors and in organizational practices, and long-term program acceptance (Israel et al, 1992). Greenwood et al (1993) suggest that action research enhances problem formulation, hypothesis formulation, data acquisition, data analysis, synthesis, and application. In action research, good research is determined by the degree to which the results are useful in solving practical problems while contributing to a general knowledge of organizations, and is based on the idea that the interpretation of the problem depends on a combination of factors including the perspectives and histories of various participants.

Action research (AR) can be described as the process of systematically collecting research data about an organization relative to some objective, goal, or need of that organization; feeding these data back into the organization; taking actions by altering selected variables within the organization based both on the data and on hypotheses; and evaluating the results of the actions by collecting more data (Susman & Evered, 1978). Although action research follows steps in a logical sequence, the process itself is iterative; the receipt of new information may make it necessary to repeat earlier steps and to revise conclusions that have been found inadequate or to discard material determined to be irrelevant. Action research is a process as well as a strategy; it is a sequence of events and activities within each cycle (data collection, feedback working the data, and taking action based on the data) and it is a cycle of iterations of these activities, sometimes treating the same problem through several repetitions and sometimes moving to different problems in each cycle. Knowledge in the action cycle begins with experience, with ‘a priori’ expectations witheld till later in the process. Hult and Lennung (1980) provide one of the most comprehensive definitions:

Action research simultaneously assists in practical problemsolving and expands scientific, as well as enhances the competencies of respective actors, being performed collectively in an immediate situation using data feedback in a cyclical process aimed at an increased understanding of a given social situation, primarily applicable for the understanding of a given process in social systems and undertaken within a mutually acceptable ethical framework.

Embodying the ideas of sociotechnical systems, the Scandinavian approach, and hermeneutics, action research has been used by social scientists to understand changes in organizations and complex social interactions since its development at the end of Word War II. Projects conducted using action research include but are not limited to the Tavistock Institute’s work on social behaviour (Trist & Bamforth, 1951; Rice, 1958), work related injuries (Pasmore & Friedlander, 1982), cost and job savings at Xerox (Pace & Argona, 1991), facilitation of quality circles in the US and Japan (Cole, 1991), studies of organizational restructuring in the Norwegian shipping industry (Roggema & Smith, 1983; Walton & Gaffney, 1989), effecting change in the culture of a factory in England (Jaques, 1952), leadership changes in industrial plants (Seashore & Bowers, 1963), community relations projects (Lippitt & Radke, 1946; Lippitt, 1951), overcoming resistance to change (Coch & French, 1948), relation of organization to its environment (Miller & Rice, 1967), careers and changing work roles (Rapoport, 1970), consumer behaviour (Emery et al, 1968), large scale institution building (Trist, 1967), and planned change in a large oil refinery (Morse & Reimer, 1956). Additionally, a special issue of Human Relations highlighted action research and provides several exemplary cases while Bennett and Oliver (1988) furnish an excellent ‘How to’ guide. It is suggested that only a process like action research which combines both research and practical problem solving can produce extremely relevant research findings. Such relevance is an important measure of significance in IS research (Keen, 1991).

The discipline of IS seems to be a very appropriate field for the use of action research (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1996). Action research (AR) aims to contribute both to the practical concerns of people in an immediate problematic situation and to the goals of social science by joint collaboration within a mutually acceptable ethical framework (Rapoport, 1970). This technique merges research with real-time problem solving in an organization. However, action research articles in major North American publications are extremely rare (Baskerville & Wood-Harper, 1996). Only one research article was uncovered by Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991) between January 1983 and May 1988. Richard Baskerville (1993, 1996) has contributed to the development of the methodology in both theory and practice, including its use for the development of semantic databases. Checkland and Scholes (1990) linked action research and software development. Lau (1997) reviewed the literature for information technology related action research studies. His work initially uncovered 600 works from which he culled 70 articles to develop an action research framework. Examples include implementation (Gibson, 1975), systems analysis, development, and satisfaction (Mirvis & Lawler, 1983; Wood-Harper & Flynn, 1983; Liu et al, 1990; Jonsson, 1991; Ngwenyama, 1993; Crowther, 1994; Fox, 1995), and IS social change (Rickards, 1985; Sandberg, 1985; Pava, 1986; Checkland, 1991).

## Steps and application

The iterative action research process is viewed as a continuous feedback loop (see Figure 1), much like the hermeneutic spiral. It is critical to the success of the action research cycle to include a wide sample of the organization’s population. Participants who view themselves as ‘clients’, rather than ‘subjects’ often are more highly motivated to ensure that all of the relevant data will be utilised. It is desirable to involve all the groups who will be directly affected by the change process. This will improve not only the range and validity of data available but also the likelihood that the interests of these groups will be addressed and their support forthcoming (Walton & Gaffney, 1991).

Interpretive action research is used in this study. The goals of both interpretive action research and this study are two-fold. The first and primary goal is the resolution of the organization’s problem/situation and the improvement of organization members who are participating in the process. The second goal is to provide the researcher with an understanding of the external validity of theoretical models through their application in the solution of the organization’s situation. The project, at its inception, sought only to facilitate the Naval Construction Force integration and IS function transformation but the process provided insights into other areas of the NCF as solutions were discovered to have more general applications. One critical aspect of this method is the role of the researcher. In this study, the researcher serves as an active participant—contributing to both the process and solution—yet allows the organization’s members to lead, maintain, contribute, and learn from the experience.

The complexity of information required for organizational research means that it is very difficult to attribute causes and effects in research. Data collection in this project is characterised as triangulation, where multiple methods were used to determine and then confirm results in the iterative cycles. The use of multiple methods in research helps compensate for complexity, but by its very nature is time consuming and expensive. To the manager, as to the academic researcher, the multiple method approach increases confidence because it does not rely on only one information source and thus is seen as reducing risks of reaching wrong decisions, which in turn increases rigor, which depends on getting the facts straight (Wood, 1988). Additionally, the iterative action research strategy forces researchers to go through the rigorous process of having the facts checked by those with first-hand knowledge of those facts, before any reports are written (Whyte, 1989). Serving as a validity check and self-correcting mechanism, the results and interpretations of data were analysed by the action research team with discrepancies or anomalies reinvestigated as the process advanced.

Internally, the action research team—also referred to as the Computer Configuration Board (CCB)—utilised techniques such as brainstorming, breakout sessions with investigative groups, presentations, and survey development. Externally, the CCB relied upon interviews— structured and semi-structured—and survey data as its primary methods of information gathering. Data collection for the study was accomplished through participant observation by the researcher, presentation slides and packets presented by the working groups and contractors, the quantitative and qualitative survey data, notes from each group breakout session, and transcriptions of official minutes from an appointed secretary. As an element of the participative nature of the study, all members of the CCB had complete access to information— excluding the personal observation notes taken by the researcher—and were given the opportunity to provide input into all decisions, reports, and to the study’s reflection phase—explained in the following section. A recap of data collection methods and their applications in the project are found in Appendix B.

## Chronology of the project

This section provides a description of the project presented in action research cycles. Each cycle will be decomposed into three steps—diagnosis (identification of problem or situation and consideration of alternative actions), action (executing a chosen course of action), and reflection (evaluating the consequences of action and determining contributions)—described in the method section. The entire process is illustrated in Table 1.

## Initiation (researcher’s and commanders’ preunderstanding)

Prior to a discussion of the project’s cycles is a review of the initiation of the project. The researcher was approached by a senior member of the NCF and requested to assist in an upcoming reorganization project. The researcher was already affiliated with the NCF as a Reserve Logistics Officer. Upon agreeing to participate in the project, at the request of the researcher, unstructured interviews were conducted individually with the commander and his chief of staff (CoS) (hence referred to as the command staff) of each of the three existing brigades. The goal of these interviews was to broaden the researcher’s understanding of the project from the point of view of the brigade commanders and assess each commander’s desired outcome. Interviews lasted approximately two hours and were conducted informally in the commander’s office. As a result of the interviews, the researcher reviewed the literature on organization development and began to develop a proposed format through which the research/project process would progress.

Two weeks later, the researcher met again with the command staffs. This meeting was also at the request of the researcher, but was conducted with all three command staffs simultaneously at a central location. The researcher began with a brief explanation of organization development theory and organization communication channels while soliciting input and feedback. The command staffs almost unanimously expressed a desire for a centralised infrastructure, which was the historical norm and provided the most control. The researcher supported by one CoS suggested that there were other options, although some non-traditional, and that the process should not be predetermined but more importantly should include members throughout the NCF and related commands to gather the maximum input. This led to a discussion of the benefits of including organization members other than the command staffs and the potential advantages and savings of decentralised infrastructures.

After some discussion, it was agreed by the commanders that the action research format was acceptable but they insisted upon defined parameters through which the project would be conducted. The command staffs and the researcher then agreed to the specific details of a research agreement which constituted a formal research contract. This understanding was critical to all parties, insuring that the researcher recognised the expectations of the organization and the authority and limits within which the action research team would operate. Included in the agreement was an understanding of how the project would be conducted, limitations and stipulations, and a projection of costs and time frames. Agreements/contracts between the researcher and an organization are common in action research projects.

Over the next several days, the researcher and the chiefs of staff discussed in detail the elements of the project, the location of the meetings, and began selection of the action research team. Each CoS contacted counterparts at the regiments and field units under his brigade for recommendations of knowledgeable and interested personnel to participate as team members. Additionally, related commands, for example, Naval Facilities Command (NAVFAC), and two current contractors were approached to participate. While no firm requirements were stipulated, it was recommended that candidates have computer and systems management experience. A total of 21 candidates were recommended over the next several days. From those candidates, four active duty officers (two training and two logistics [logistics officers usually are responsible for a unit’s computers as a collateral duty]), three reserve officers (all logistics officers), three representatives from related commands (two officers plus one senior enlisted), and a contractor representative were selected to participate on the action research team, now called the computer configuration board (CCB). The entire CCB was now composed of 15 members, including the researcher and the three CoS. The selectees were notified and the composition of the team was approved by the brigade commanders, as stipulated by the research agreement.

## Cycle I

## Diagnosis

At a Navy base near Los Angeles, the CCB met for the first time. The meeting began with introductions and an opening statement by the senior officer present (one of the three CoS), who informed the CCB that no predetermined outcomes had been programmed for the reorgani zation. It was their task to create the best possible struc ture for the IS reorganization while identifying and resolving existing and potential problems. He went on to describe the current situation and the importance of the new IS which was approaching implementation. The researcher was introduced and provided background overview of various organization structures and examples, formal and informal communications structures and their importance to any organization, and centralised/decentralised IS structures. Within the researcher’s presentation, a common language of flow charting and modelling was suggested and demonstrated so that all discussions would be equally understood. The AR team was then split into three working groups with each group composed of one CoS, one active duty officer, one reserve officer, and members from related commands. This division was purposely done to achieve the greatest possible diversity and knowledge-base within each group. The groups were instructed to utilise the brainstorming technique, as explained by the researcher, to illustrate their impression of the current information infrastructure, both formal and informal lines of communication, and their vision of an optimal infrastructure with communications channels. The groups were informed that despite the wide range of ranks (from Navy lieutenant and senior enlisted—senior chief—to Navy captain), they were to work as a unit with all members free to supply input and comment at any time. To minimise the researcher’s impact and allow the researcher to ‘float’ among the groups while observing and collecting data, he was not assigned to any of the working groups. This also began the transfer process, where eventually the organization participants would take over the process themselves.

## Action

The working groups met for the balance of the first day and most of the second. On the afternoon of the second day, the groups were reassembled and each group was given 40 minutes to present their results. There was striking similarity in each group’s vision, with all groups indicating that the current infrastructure was designed to accomplish the mission but had hardly evolved from its paper-based origins. Each working group, as per instructions, used organizational diagrams and flow charts to illustrate what they interpreted as the current structure which was hierarchical and well defined. It was interesting that in each presentation, the groups referred to the current infrastructure in terms of the organization, history, and why it facilitated current operations. They were also in agreement with their descriptions of the communications channels. The formal communication channel mirrored the hierarchical organization structure utilising the chain of command, described earlier. There was some difference of opinion with regard to the informal communications channel. One group in particular discussed a web of phone and e-mail channels among logistics officers which they believed already existed and suggested that this channel was/should be used to accomplish tasks outside of the formal network.

The AR team decided that the best way to proceed was to ‘divide and conquer’; therefore each working group was assigned a topic and given two days to explore the issue. On the fifth day the AR team reconvened with each group presenting an issue and their solution. The first group discussed the informal communication channels and what could be done to expedite the flow of information among the units. Their solution was a combination of phone, e-mail, and a proposal to establish a website to act as a clearing house for information. The second group presented the issue of maintenance and repair of the IS. This group reported that they viewed the issue in much broader terms and indicated that procurement, maintenance, repair, and upgrades (hardware and software) should also be included. The ensuing discussion yielded several proposals including teams which could be dispatched to units to solve problems as required, increasing the support staff in each unit as well as in a central place—the location as yet to be determined, and outsourcing the systems to a vendor who would procure and maintain them for a fee. All these proposals were well received by the team as a whole but there was substantial concern over the costs associated with each option. The third group also viewed their issue—system policies plus perceived strengths and weaknesses—as much broader than previously defined. They queried the AR team as to the degree of IS system satisfaction at each unit and what personnel thought were the problems with the IS. There was silence among the team. No one, not even the people who worked with the systems on a daily basis could provide a good indicator of user’s ‘feelings’ toward the IS. A survey of unit personnel was suggested by a reserve officer who pointed out that the reserve command was creating a survey to address issues with the NCF and that the IS questions could be included as part of that instrument. The CCB thought that this was a particularly good idea and tasked the researcher to assist that working group in the development of the instrument.

## Reflection

The reflection phase of each cycle reviews the general findings and contributions while providing input or preunderstanding for the following iteration. This cycle, taking approximately six days, can be characterised as the organization and planning stage for the CCB and produced an understanding and agreement of the current organization structure and insights into the existing informal communication channels and their future potential. As suggested by the Heideggerian and Wittensteinian approaches, a common language—organization charts and information flow charts—was established so that all team members understood terminology and had practical knowledge of the situation; therefore, communication was facilitated. Theories introduced to the CCB included: (1) organization structures, (2) formal and informal communications channels, and (3) centralised and decentralised information infrastructures. The combination of theoretical background, brainstorming and working group investigations, and personal experiences resulted in the following issues being discovered and suggestions for actions explored: (1) procurement and repair, (2) system issues—including implementation, (3) modernisation and upgrades of technology, (4) system utilisation, (5) approval authority, (6) reliability, and (7) user perceptions/satisfaction.

No solutions were generated in this cycle but the problem situation was identified with issues discovered and discussed, forming the preunderstanding for Cycle II. As part of the project’s conditions a summary of issues and actions was prepared and forwarded to the three brigade commanders. This report was prepared by the three CoS and the researcher from the official minutes and the researcher’s notes. Once the report was compiled, it was presented to and approved by the entire AR team.

## Cycle II

## Diagnosis

Upon reconvening, the first order of business for the CCB was a review of the actions taken during Cycle I and the promulgation of the summary report with comments from one of the commanders. There was agreement that the work to date was in accordance with the commanders’ expectations and would continue as planned. The ‘procurement and maintenance’ group was first to present. Their representative suggested that two primary alternatives emerged during their investigation: (1) a combination of improved on-site staffing plus a centralised support group located at either the brigade or regimental level and (2) outsourcing the system to a contractor/vendor who would manage it for a fee. Both issues were discussed by the CCB, with the group’s representative explaining the meaning and ramifications of outsourcing (the researcher made a few comments about industry experience). Of most concern to the CCB were the additional costs to the NCF of both options (only estimated costs were provided) and the loss of control and full-time on-site support with outsourcing (contractors would have already been cleared to work on classified systems). Understanding that both options were viable, the CCB agreed to further investigate outsourcing and at the presenting group’s recommendation invited two current contractors to prepare and present proposals.

The next working group to present continued the discussion on informal communication channels. Their work expanded on their existing proposal to create a system, sponsored by the two remaining brigades, centred around a website. Their impression was that this system would be the easiest to integrate with the formal communication channels and sponsorship by the brigades would expedite implementation. Their vision integrates the use of phone and e-mail contacts by providing a list of ‘experts’ on the site. In essence, their plan would use the formal communication channels to implement and support the informal channels. The plan was quickly approved by the CCB with little discussion.

The final working group, user perceptions, presented a package of questions to each team member to be included in the survey instrument. The questions, derived in part from the issues raised in Cycle I, were reviewed and discussed by the CCB, after which some questions were dropped and others were added. The researcher suggested that the instrument be pilot tested during the next few days using the active duty battalion stationed at their location. The CCB agreed.

## Action

Over the next several days, the pilot test was conducted, approximately 130 questionnaires were completed— almost 15% of the battalion (this was a random sample and arbitrary number). Also during this time two contractors were scheduled to present proposals for system outsourcing.

As a result of the pilot test, two questions were deleted and three others were added. The working group reported that over 65% of the survey participants indicated a desire to learn more about computers and were pleased that they were included in the survey. The revised survey was once again reviewed by the CCB and approved for distribution, pending a decision by the commanders. Since the reserve force had completed its instrument and was ready to conduct its survey, the CoS faxed a copy of the survey to each commander and received approval to continue via phone. The reserve’s survey instrument was administered to all personnel within the NCF, some 10 000 officers and enlisted. The

CCB felt that a complete sample of the NCF was too costly/time consuming and that a representative random sample would be satisfactory. The researcher explained that a random sample of 20% (approximately 2000 surveys—based on the last digit of the social security number) could provide an viable sample from which to judge user perceptions and satisfaction while containing costs. The survey was conducted over the course of three weeks with the IS system sections forwarded to the CCB for analysis.

The CCB reconvened to address the first agenda item—the survey results. The most controversial finding from the survey was the difference between perceptions of the active duty and reserve forces—reserve units were making greater use of their IS but were significantly less satisfied than their active counterparts. Up to this point the CCB had been focusing on the quantitative questions from the survey; the researcher suggested a review of the qualitative, open-ended, questions. The results of the open-ended questions (conducted by Q-sort and a content analysis software package) confirmed the quantitative findings and suggested that the active duty was very uncomfortable with the use of the IS and in cases avoided its use when possible. This was unexpected and very troubling given the pending implementation of a mission critical system whose use would be required by almost every member of the NCF, yet no reason for this discomfort was discovered. As a result of these and other unanswered questions, the CCB decided to conduct site visits at the regimental and unit level using in-depth, semi-structured interviews. Several CCB members suggested that as part of each site visit an inventory of information processing equipment, its condition, training practices, and available software be conducted.

As a preface to interview script development, the researcher reviewed the sociotechnical and soft systems methodology (SSM) philosophy with the CCB. The active duty force’s reluctance to utilise the IS could be related to the organizational culture’s relationship with technology, explained the researcher. Several of the active duty officers suggested that since the NCF was in essence a military construction organization with most enlisted personnel trained only in construction and combat skills, the use of information technology might be ‘troublesome’ to them. The reserves on the other hand had a different organizational culture which was a mixture of their former active duty service and their civilian job experiences. The reserve CoS suggested that perhaps the interview script could provide additional insights into the differences in the active and reserve perceptions and focus on how systems and personnel could become more compatible. Consequently, the CCB produced an interview script which focused on user-based issues, while allowing for greater freedom for interviewees to express their views and suggestions.

The final agenda item included two presentations from outsourcing contractors. After the presentations, some members of the CCB were concerned with the problems—loss of control, contractor interests, and on-site time constraints—associated with the use of contractors and were discouraged by the contractors’ own cost estimates. Logistics officers generally felt that those funds could be best allocated to new computing resources.

## Reflection

This cycle—conducted over the course of five weeks and six days (surveying accounted for three of those weeks during which time the full CCB did not meet)—can be characterised as the end of development and planning and the beginning of the data gathering and analysis. The cycle illustrates the integration of outside information supplied by the researcher, working group’s research, and contractors and the introduction of local knowledge supplied by organization members, leading to local theory.

The development and execution of the survey instrument and its findings are of great interest, uncovering what is perhaps a major underlying problem within the NCF—the differences between the active and reserve members and how to deal with those differences. The development and implementation of the survey instrument itself is not spectacular and one might even suggest that given the omission of certain topic areas, it was less than successful. Fortunately, the survey uncovered critical differences between two components of the NCF. The discovery of significant differences led the researcher and the CCB to investigate the perception gap between the two components, failure to achieve synchronic reliability. The differences led the CCB to determine that another, more in-depth data collection was required—the semi-structured interviews. Sociotechnical and SSM approaches were introduced as a means to understand the different dynamics which could account for the active force’s reluctant system use versus the positive attitude of reserves towards the same system.

Through the iterative process, a dual communication channel was emerging, as described earlier, although this had yet to be approved by the commanders. The development of an informal information channel gains momentum by using the formal channel to support and enrich the informal channel while also providing defacto support from upper levels of the command structure. Despite its early stages, this proposal provided the opportunity to integrate not only the communications channels but other elements of the reorganization. The discovery of differences between the active and reserve components could impact the use of an information technology-based channel.

In this cycle the CCB investigated various proposals for the procurement and maintenance of their IS. One proposal, outsourcing, was initially unfamiliar to most of the team members. Through research and an informative presentation by one working group, assisted by the researcher, the concept was fully explained to the CCB. Outsourcing as a solution initially had very high appeal as an answer to the NCF’s procurement and maintenance problem despite concerns over cost as it was felt that negotiations could reduce cost to an acceptable level. It was local knowledge provided by the several logistics officers—widely dispersed mission critical assets, security, and operational issues—that led to outsourcing’s ultimate rejection. Without the contribution of local knowledge, outsourcing might have been adopted, a solution that could have severely impacted the overall readiness of the NCF’s IS.

## Cycle III

## Diagnosis

Preunderstanding from Cycle II identified and highlighted key concerns including: (1) the issues of procurement and repair of equipment, (2) age of existing computer assets, and (3) lack of training of members entering the organization. These topics dominated the ensuing discussion as it was felt that they were critical contributing factors and must be resolved before standards and guidelines could be agreed upon. The current system provided for centralised procurement and repair of computing assets. Unfortunately, it was found that this system, although widely practised in the military, was: (1) located in a section of the NCF that was slated to be integrated into other elements and (2) was considered by unit Information Resource Management (IRM) officers to be less than efficient in expediting the acquisition and repair of computing assets.

The review switched to the topic of active duty and reserve differences. The data confirmed the suspicions of the reserve officers, that reservists are more experienced in using IS as a result of their civilian occupations. Their lower levels of satisfaction were a result of several issues including: (1) computer availability versus need— reservists were always waiting to use a machine; many reservists reported bringing their personal machines on drill weekends and active duty periods, (2) reservists felt that the computers were old compared to what they were used to in their civilian jobs and that the technology and software, in particular, was outdated, and (3) systems were always failing or repairs took excessive lengths of time. The reports from the active duty units were totally different, confirming the results of the survey taken in Cycle II. Users didn’t perceive the technology or software as outdated but actually reported being ‘afraid’ to use the system because they did not understand it, although they almost universally expressed a desire to learn.

## Action

The CCB determined that the best solution concerning procurement and maintenance was to locate these functions at or close to the unit level. While agreeing, the senior CoS indicated that without some oversight and standardisation the commanders would never approve the proposal. As a result, that group was tasked with the creation of a template to guide standardised policy and to ensure compatibility of information technology across the NCF while decentralising procurement and repair. The communication channels working group suggested that the proposed website could be used as a means to promulgate information and policies for procurement and standardisation.

The entire CCB now focused on the issue of active and reserve differences. It was their perception that this was not a technology issue but a social issue. They therefore began to focus on the development of a training program which could raise the level of IS proficiency within the active force and create changes in the social fabric. Several proposals were considered with the final determination yielding a combination of: (1) improved training as sailors entered the NCF from basic training, (2) an exportable, CD-based, hands-on training program for deployed units, and (3) reservists spending their annual two week active-duty period with their activeduty counterparts to provide training and support on the existing and soon to be implemented IS. This option could also improve interactions and relations between active and reserve sailors while incurring no additional expense to the Navy.

The final issue remaining for the CCB to resolve was the structure of the new information support organization. It was consequently decided that the existing structure should be decomposed and that each of the two newly formed brigades house its own infrastructure. Instead of maintaining a highly centralised infrastructure at the brigade level, the CCB determined that the best place for the support resources was at the regimental level. This placed IS elements closer to the units which they supported and improved reaction time to problems above the authorized limits of individual units. To provide an infrastructure to facilitate the new organization structure, the CCB recommended that personnel from the existing reserve brigade IS organization be transferred to regimental staffs. The CCB also created a template of directives and policies, in part derived from existing doctrine, which both brigades consequently adopted. These policies, including parameters for contracts and contractors at the unit level, guided the regimental staffs and units in the conduct of daily operations but were flexible enough to provide adjustments as required by the rapidly changing environment. Overall responsibility was still at the brigade level, which ultimately set the standards for reporting, equipment, software, and allocated funds.

The final drafts of the standards and policies were presented to the brigade commanders for their review. Minor changes were made to the documents and the commanders agreed to accept the new structure and policies. One brigade commander was so impressed with the results of the CCB that he suggested its structure be retained as an ongoing board to maintain, review, and update IS issues. That suggestion was adopted and the CCB now meets twice a year and is responsible for updating standards and policy.

## Reflection

In this cycle—characterised as analysis and decision making and taking 20 days to complete—the CCB confirmed their assumptions from Cycle II by merging analysis of the on-site interviews, sociotechnical and SSM theories, and local knowledge gained from field unit personnel. The acquisition of information in this cycle provided support for the emerging organizational structure, training, procurement and maintenance, as well as the informal communication channel proposals. Additionally, lack of knowledge on the part of active duty unit members helped to explain the significant differences discovered in the survey taken in Cycle II. The active units were experiencing similar hardware-based problems as the reserve units but their lack of knowledge prevented them from realising the depth of the situation and could have even contributed to some apprehension to use the system. During this cycle, the researcher limited his contributions to the process, becoming more of an observer and less of a participant. This facilitated the transfer of the process to the organization participants; the researcher had almost no input in the drafting of the final document. At the conclusion of Cycle III, the CCB wrote and refined their recommendations based on feedback from the commanders and confirmation of earlier assumptions.

## Results and lessons learned

As a result of the action research process and before the mandated decommissioning, the IS assets of the reserve brigade were dispersed into the two remaining brigades and their regiments. The brigades maintained an IS staff housed in the logistics department responsible for (1) supervising standards, policy, and training, (2) administration of information technology budgets, and (3) developing and maintaining the NCF website. Members of this staff and the logistics director became permanent members of the CCB. Each regiment enhanced their IS staff responsible for supporting their units and maintaining vendor contracts. These staffs were also responsible for scheduling training with brigade offices, conducting inspections, providing on-site assistance, and participating as CCB members on a rotating basis. At the unit level, the IS staff was enlarged as logistics officers took responsibility for the procurement and maintenance of computing assets. IS proficiency was improved through the training program and a ‘crash’ program instituted as the new IS was implemented in each unit. Active duty units received additional training supplemented by reserve personnel as previously discussed. Each unit’s IS staff was the first to receive the new training and eventually served as trainers themselves.

Six months after the execution of the MOU and the creation of the new infrastructure, the researcher contacted the senior NCF officer who initiated the project to determine the success of the CCB’s proposals. At this time the reserve brigade had been decommissioned and its assets reallocated. The CCB had just met for the first time as an on-going policy board and highlights from their minutes follow:

(1) The vast majority of units were satisfied with the procurement and maintenance arrangement. Logistics officers reported that repairs had been reduced as a result of ongoing maintenance, repair times had been decreased, and procurement expedited by using the contracted local vendors. The regiments were monitoring funds and standardisation through quarterly inspections.

(2) The website had become a clearing house for information. One area even listed the best ‘deals’, quickest repairs available, and frequently asked questions (FAQ) section in addition to a list of system ‘experts’. The brigades also utilised the website to notify units of downloadable software upgrades, pending system upgrades, training opportunities, policy changes, and solicit input from the units. The informal communication channel envisioned by the CCB working group quickly became a formal communication channel.

(3) The information infrastructure was functioning without problems and no unit or regiment complained that their workload was unmanageable.

(4) The training programing was making measurable improvements in the proficiency of active duty units. Reservists were being utilised to conduct training classes and provide support while the two brigades were developing the curricula for a CD-based exportable training program.

(5) The CCB planned to conduct a follow-up survey of user satisfaction and perceptions during the next six months to measure improvements.

(6) The new IS implementation was on schedule with more than 40% of the units receiving the system. Some ‘bugs’ had been detected with the software and its connectivity with sister services but overall the implementation had been considered successful. Units receiving the system and the training reported minimal problems although measures of improved productivity were not conducted given the short duration since installation and the inexperience of new users.

(7) The two brigade commanders were also satisfied with the outcome of the reorganization.

## Discussion

This project combines theories—sociotechnical and SSM, organization structures, and communications channels—with local knowledge contributed by organization participants—to yield local theory which facilitated the NCF reorganization. Action research is the research strategy used to execute the project, develop local theory, and guide the eventual reorganization of the IS infrastructure. The process, iterative in nature, was guided by the principles of open communication, history and tradition, and cyclical learning. There were no predetermined outcomes or a priori expectations; action research studies must be flexible to assess data collected during one iteration and adapt the project in future iterations based on earlier results. These iterations allow the process to drill down and progressively explain in more detail organization issues.

The action research strategy provides for an open forum which creates conditions permitting organization members to freely share and express ideas, a concept foreign in some highly structured environments. This research strategy includes organization members as active participants in the research process, collaborating to form a team that directs and controls the investigation through agreed upon warrants and mandates, leading to self improvement of the participants. These participants were drawn from all levels of the NCF—CoS (senior executives) from the highest levels to the operational unit level. Despite the disparity in the ranks, the AR team—CCB—operated in a democratic manner with members providing equal input and voting on recommendations. Problems within the AR team were resolved through open discussion among the participants, although on several occasions arguments were terminated via the intervention of senior members. This open atmosphere encouraged junior members of the team to speak freely and contribute ideas which proved vital to key outcomes, for example, the outsourcing decision.

This inclusion of organization members promotes the meshing of theory and outside information with local knowledge resulting in co-learning, and dual use of results—theoretical contributions plus the pragmatic use by the organization. These stakeholders bring to the relationship information about the organization and their reactions to it, while researchers contribute methods for investigating issues and general theories to help make sense of the findings that emerge from the joint investigation. Additionally, the participants bring rich knowledge about the inner workings of the organization— including its history and traditions (critical in organizations such as the military and large corporations), its structures and operating environment, rules and regulations, and especially its ‘corporate culture’. The organizational and cultural information proved invaluable when the CCB developed the website and feedback mechanisms—the informal information channel. Without the explicit contributions about the NCF’s inner workings and current informal operating procedures plus the expectations supplied by the CoS, the final solution might have proved substantially less effective and perhaps never fully implemented. The solution, built upon the existing informal channel but improved and optimised to meet current requirements, proved not only successful but extremely popular and has been expanded to other command elements.

The insights gained from the action research effort allowed stakeholders to plan interventions to improve organizational effectiveness, and researchers better able to revise their theories to more fully explain the relationships observed among variables (Pasmore & Friedlander, 1982). The iterative nature of the action learning permitted both the researcher and the CCB to learn and enhance their knowledge as the project progressed. Information discovered during one cycle—the differences between the active and reserve components—led to an understanding of more complex problems, for example, the active duty’s lack of knowledge of technology systems and the consequent development of the integrated training program in latter cycles. Sociotechnical and soft systems theories provided an initial level of understanding which the CCB members, with the assistance of the researcher, were able to transfer from theory to application. These theories afforded the organization participants—most of whom had no system development and design experience—insights into the design of technology and support systems that simplify interaction with users and lead to optimum system utilisation and productivity. It was ultimately discovered through the survey data and a series of on-site interviews, using the iterative AR methodology, that the design of the systems and the lack of training inhibited many active duty members from fully using IS intended to improve their job performance. Corrective actions, particularly the reserve training program, developed from an understanding of these theories, and led to improved levels of perceptions and satisfaction and ultimately higher levels of productivity.

This project directly applied Henderson and Clark’s (1990) architectural knowledge. Their work, while not originally intended for reorganization studies, provided a distinct perspective through which to analyse and understand the organization and its culture. This understanding combined with action research assisted the research team in decomposing the hierarchical structure and assisted in the creation of a new framework which opened communication channels, reduced filtering, and identified areas requiring investigation. The formal structures of most organizations are clearly illustrated through organization charts, but the informal structures are much more difficult to uncover and provide a key into the inner workings of the organization under investigation. The success of the CCB, a formal structure that fostered new informal channels, is a testimonial to the new structures, channels, and methods that resulted from this approach. Given the success of architectural knowledge and the application of action research in this setting, one might conclude that their use in less hierarchical situations would enhance understanding of organization dynamics which could lead to clearer, more implementable reorganization plans in other organization settings.

One of the advantages of action research is the dual benefits to both subjects and science. As illustrated above, the introduction of academic theories and their application to the organization by the researcher provided insights and understanding not readily available to organization participants outside the action research project environment. On the other hand, organization participants contributed a wealth of local knowledge enhancing the application of the researcher-provided theory. This combination became local theory which was directly applied to solving the issues and problems of the organization, confirming the application and transferability of these theories in this organization domain while offering their potential application in other organization settings. ## Conclusion

This project adapted the action research strategy to an organization situation where it had not been previously used. Unlike other situations, the involvement of the highest levels of the organization—commanders and their CoS—were included on the action research team and were active participants in the research process. Additionally, the nature of the IS infrastructure, with its mission critical systems, provide a unique aspect through which the action research process was conducted. This combination of factors including the hierarchical structure and culture of the NCF provided an exceptional opportunity to use the action research strategy under conditions markedly different from most organization settings.

Academic research and teaching has been criticised for lacking relevance to the practitioner’s world. In addition to conducting rigorous studies, researchers should integrate the real world into their research and teaching (Melnyk, 1996). Melnyk suggests that researchers develop credibility in the eyes of practitioners while identifying interesting problems to study. Action research can help meet those goals while providing a platform that produces rigorous, scientifically sound results. AR mandates transfer of control to the organization, easing transition, while providing the organization with the means to continue progress from within. AR achieves results of current benefit to organizations while leading to rethinking and restructuring which can impact future processes, potentially contributing to the greater body of knowledge. Action research represents an alternative strategy for conducting organization-based research and is particularly relevant for solving complex, problematic situations routinely found in IS research. Since action research is not applicable to all research settings, for example, laboratory setting, it is not intended to wholly replace traditional methodologies, but should be added to the tools of IS researchers for dynamic organizational situations.
