# Operationalising the implementation puzzle: an argument for eclecticism in research and in practice

RP Marble

College of Business Administration, Creighton University, 2500 California Plaza, Omaha, Nebraska 68178, USA

Efforts to implement organizational information systems (IS) have evolved through the years from purely technical processes to multi-faceted organizational change activities. Largely in reaction to implementation failures, numerous research models have been developed to prescribe appropriate implementation strategies and tactics. A multiple of such contributions has made the theory quite diverse and somewhat chaotic. This paper presents a survey of implementation models, research approaches, and investigation methods that have surfaced in the literature of the field. Using an implementation framework of Swanson (1988), an eclectic perspective of IS research, and an expert knowledge integration approach, a perspective is proposed that demonstrates the possibility of a unified view of IS implementation theory. European Journal of Information Systems (2000) 9, 132–147.

## Introduction

The implementation of automated support systems for information processing has long been regarded as an important component of corporate efforts to remain competitive. The relentless advance of capabilities made possible by information technology (IT) releases very few firms from the need to conduct such implementation efforts on a regular basis. While the collective experience of information systems (IS) professionals in the implementation of systems now spans many decades, the associated challenges and opportunities continue to harbour surprises and uncertainties. Numerous theories and models have surfaced through the years, consolidating and synthesising the lessons learned in implementation projects and research. Nevertheless, no unified view of IS implementation appears to have achieved widespread acceptance as a vehicle for better understanding and guiding implementation efforts.

It is the contention of this paper, that a unified view of IS implementation already exists in the rich parquet of IS literature. This unified view must only be recognised and extracted. Unfortunately, different approaches to research and different methods of investigation appear to have obscured the unity that can be extracted from their cumulative results. Moreover, the idiographic nature of many current contributions seems to reject the outlook for comprehensiveness in the future. In this paper, it is argued that the theoretical frameworks, research approaches, and investigative methods of prior research already evince a unified path to discovery in and management of IS implementation.

A first step in this argument requires that we regard implementation theory from the perspective of expert knowledge. Incorporation of discoveries and experiences into the theory must be considered from the point of view of knowledge acquisition and representation. It is this assumption that provides the opportunity to reconcile the numerous results exposed in the IS implementation literature. The notion of expert knowledge involves many different scopes, categories, degrees of structure, and levels of uncertainty (Turban & Aronson, 1998). The well-known difficulties inherent in acquiring and representing expert knowledge can easily explain the rather disjointed profile of IS implementation theory. By the same token, however, this outlook allows us to temper our expectations with regard to the degree of algorithmic structure, with which a unified view of implementation should be characterised.

Numerous metastudies of IS implementation literature have attempted to glean commonalities from published research (Kwon & Zmud, 1987; Cooper & Zmud, 1990; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991; Alavi & Joachimsthaler, 1992). While they all provide useful looks at the field, most of their conclusions highlight the disparities between frameworks, approaches, and methods. Part of the problem evinced by these disparities appears to stem from the level of precision that is typically sought in attempts to model IS situations and processes.

While absolute precision is an understandable goal in a modelling pursuit, it is not always realistic and often is not even desirable. As long as natural language is involved, an uncompromising expectation of absolute precision in IS modelling is doomed from the start. Numerous studies have highlighted the consequences of modellers’ failures to anticipate different linguistic interpretations for model components and their interactions. (Kaplan & Duchon, 1988). More importantly, however, in some contexts, even an attempt at absolute precision is inappropriate. It is connotations, not denotations, which give poetry its rich expressiveness. Why should IS researchers or practitioners insist on negating that expressiveness in their own communications? Many years of professional interactions with practitioners have reinforced the usefulness of imprecision. In discussing a model that is too precise, one usually encounters the idiographic response, ‘Yes, that may be true for many shops, but we are different . . .’ On the other hand, a more general description of the model often elicits the reply, ‘That sounds just like the way things happen in our organization!’ One of the tenets of this paper is that, in the expression of system implementation models, the existence of various degrees of imprecision should be recognised and even utilised. If our goal is to provide expert knowledge for the management of implementation, we must acknowledge the appropriateness of less structured knowledge as part of the mix. (It should be noted that this topic is not intended to characterise a distinction between positivist and interpretive approaches toward IS research, as described, for instance, by Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991. Indeed, positivist survey research relies on the imprecise connotations of parsimonious phrases to discover human attitudes and intentions, while many interpretivist ethnographers contribute long expositions to achieve extreme precision.)

Irrevocably tied to the concept of precision is the nature of the reasoning used to incorporate implementation knowledge from multiple sources. As Turban and Aronson (1998) explain, it is the exercise of judgement, intuition, and approximate reasoning that characterises the most valuable (if elusive) trait of expert knowledge. This is what allows an expert to generalise from experience and this is what we ultimately seek for implementation theory. Since most implementation literature appears to rest on the foundation of classical logic, attempts to synthesise its results have witnessed difficulty. An ability to live with the intuitionistic (or ‘fuzzy’) logical tenets of approximate reasoning would provide a much more realistic outlook in this context. While the design of a specific expert system is beyond the scope of this paper, some remarks are in order about the logical frames available for representing knowledge about IS implementation.

A logical theory is defined as a collection of statements, called axioms, together with all statements that can be derived from the axioms. An interpretation of a theory is a well-defined mapping of those statements into a real-world structure, whose elements and relations obey all the theory’s axioms under the mapping (Enderton, 1972). In this sense, we can regard the theory of IS implementation as everything we believe to be true in the area. The targets of interpretation mappings would ideally be the collection of IS implementation situations, past, present, and future. General acceptance of an axiom in the theory correctly requires empirical validation in many individual situations, assuming that an exhaustive check would be impossible. In IS implementation theory, the importance of top management support might be seen as an example axiom, as posited by Boland (1978). A problem arises, however, as soon as we discover a counterexample to a statement in the theory. The whole theory is called into question. (This follows from the completeness theorem of Go¨del, 1930, which states that any statement in a consistent theory must be valid in all possible interpretations of that theory.) A cogent elucidation of Myers (1994) appears to represent just such a counterexample to the top management support axiom mentioned above.

Classical logical foundations lead us to adjust the axioms when an instance of apparent contradiction is found. Typically (and implicitly) axioms receive qualifications, in the form of conditional clauses, which filter out the counterexample from their domain of application. Further, discoveries from the counterexample usually are incorporated as new axioms. This is because every theory of classical logic contains a (purely) logical axiom, known as the Law of the Excluded Middle (Enderton, 1972), insisting that a statement which is not true must be false. This is not meant to imply that a formal logical theory of IS implementation is (or should be) defined and administered by some official authority. It does, however, characterise the implicit view of most writing and research in the field. In fact, with the way many current research streams are heading, it appears as if every contribution wants to be a new axiom. The theory is relatively light on derivable truths and very heavy on axioms. While they don’t necessarily contradict each other, these axioms preclude the possibility of any universal interpretation. Large sets of conditions qualify the applicability of each one. In the logical sense, each statement covers only a special case, whose premises are not satisfied by other axioms.

Of course it is not unreasonable that a statement’s universal applicability to IS implementation be hard to achieve. It is equally reasonable, however, to note that implementation experts can utilise all their discoveries and experiences, in every situation confronting them, without requiring the strict logical rules of classical foundations. They do this by avoiding an insistence on absolute ‘black and white’ precision in the definitions of terms. In addition, they allow the process of inference to accommodate fractional values for truth and certainty, covering the entire spectrum between the binary extremes of classical logic. Individual situations are simply handled with judgements about the degree of relevance that various kernels of knowledge witness at the time. Prior knowledge is integrated, not disqualified. There are formal logical systems for representing this type of approximate reasoning. They provide bases for the design of many expert systems, and their rigour would satisfy any official authority in charge of IS implementation theory. A good overview, replete with further references, of fuzzy logic and other such systems, has been provided by Turban and Aronson (1998). The goal of this paper is not to formalise an intuitionistic theory of IS implementation, but rather to suggest how such an orientation may benefit the field. It is hoped that the reader will seek to adopt the logical frame discussed above in reading that which follows.

The preceding remarks are not intended to belie the need or ability to impose some useful order on the apparent chaos of IS implementation theory. Ginzberg and Schultz (1987) have noted the dynamic nature of the environments, technologies, and cognitions of the field. However, as is currently being discovered, for instance, by new companies in the electronic commerce arena, basic principles of business do endure and cannot be denied, in spite of radical technological change. While the very definition of implementation success may defy specification (Munkvold, 1999), the ultimate success of a business enterprise does boil down to quantifiable measures. Although our explicit understanding of the phenomenon may always be a bit nebulous, we have and can continue to provide management with concrete ‘handles’ for use in influencing implementation outcomes (Ginzberg & Schultz, 1987).

The following section contains a review of prior research in IS implementation. It is intended to outline the various frameworks of consideration, which have enriched the theory over the years. Some areas of focus within it are intended to provide groundwork for later suggestions. The next section highlights one particular framework: the Implementation Puzzle of Swanson (1988). Offering guidance through the process of ‘actualising a system design concept’, this model cites key factors that must be addressed in every such process. It is argued that this framework embodies sufficient generality to form the basis for a theory of implementation. Two subsequent sections propose schemes for operationalising Swanson’s framework in the positivist and interpretive research traditions, respectively. The value of each approach in this context is embraced and a concluding section argues for an eclectic means (in the sense of Klein et al, 1991) of combining their application.

## The research context

## IS implementation

The very definition of implementation has undergone considerable transition in the research and writings of IS literature. Systems development methodologies once relegated implementation to the last step in the development life cycle, referring to it as the conversion and installation of newly developed systems. General innovation diffusion literature seemed to agree with this, indeed, perhaps to foster it. Rogers (1983) characterised implementation as the point at which a newly designed system is put to use. A survey of 75 innovation diffusion articles (Tornatzky & Klein, 1982) reinforced a clear distinction between adoption and implementation, which appears to have survived in much of the early MIS literature. Ein-Dor and Segev (1978) pointed to the emphasis on physical realisation of the system and, as Coe (1996) complained, reflected an implementation view of the time that was oriented to ‘system delivery’. This largely technical approach to IS implementation can probably be seen as an extension of the earlier research focus on the implementation of operations research and management science (MS) models in the realm of organizational decision-making. To be sure, that stream of research did give rise to theories of organizational change, which presaged many well-accepted non-technical theses in current implementation theory. The assumption usually prevailed, however, that implementation was desirable, regardless of whether its eventual beneficiaries thought so or not. Lucas (1981) provided an excellent overview of the MS implementation models and approaches taken between 1965 and 1975. His work exposed a growing sensitivity to political and behavioural issues and laid the foundation for a change in the prevailing view of IS implementation.

This change, or expanded perspective, in implementation research was partially evinced by a general dissatisfaction with the restricted view described above. Iivari (1985), for example, provided an expression of this dissatisfaction by calling it ‘too narrow’. As Coe (1996) later pointed out, numerous implementation efforts of the time represented technical successes, but organizational failures. In reaction to this circumstance, a comprehensive definition of implementation was advocated by Lucas and began to enjoy more attention. He called IS implementation ‘an ongoing process which includes the entire development of the system from the original suggestion through the feasibility study, systems analysis and design, programming, training, conversion, installation, and evaluation of the system’ (Lucas, 1981, p 14). While this definition did not necessarily coincide with the assertions of other researchers, it also did not initially incur direct dispute. Indeed, its sweeping inclusiveness provided legitimacy for the congeries of relatively disconnected implementation theories and models. The lack of a ‘directing and organising framework’ (Cooper & Zmud, 1990) for IS implementation research became partially obscured by the umbrella definition offered by Lucas. Prominent examples of theories and models of the time were forwarded by Alter and Ginzberg (1978), Ginzberg (1978), Zmud and Cox (1979), Nichols (1981), McKenney and McFarlan (1982, 1983), and Schultz et al (1984). Together, they did, however, produce an important collection of considerations and prescriptions for IS implementation.

Some researchers have characterised Lucas’s definition of IS implementation as too broad and have expressed dissatisfaction with the body of implementation research that has appeared under its umbrella. Iivari and Ervasti (1994), for instance, called the definition ‘simplistic’ and cited it as a reason for which implementation research has been neglected in the recent past. Cooper and Zmud (1990) pointed to the limited perspectives and progress of IT implementation research, due to the lack of a more specific guiding framework. Furthermore, as Kanellis et al (1999) point out, many research findings about implementation success have been established by studying implementation failures. Indeed, this normative, case-study approach to implementation research was predominantly oriented to unsuccessful implementation efforts and yielded more cautions than true guidelines for managing the implementation process. Works of Swanson (1974), Ginzberg (1981), and Guimaraes (1981) illustrate that circumstance.

In the last decade or so, researchers’ approaches to the study of IS implementation have evolved to address many of the shortcomings discussed above. In 1988, Swanson’s ‘Implementation Puzzle’ model was formulated on the basis of a new definition of the concept. This definition occasioned use of the term ‘system realisation’ and restricted the process to (but also encompassed) the systems life cycle stages between design and use. In 1994, Iivari and Ervasti used the term ‘institutionalising’ in reference to implementation and provided a model of systems ‘implementability’. They justified the use of a narrower model of implementation by citing the current trend toward software acquisition and the relative scarcity of large-scale production systems that are still being developed in-house. This changing landscape of implementation challenges was later called ‘dramatic’ enough not only to require more focus, but also to necessitate the retesting of all prior implementation research on today’s changed system characteristics (Guimaraes & Igbaria, 1997)!

The move toward more focus in implementation research has manifested itself in numerous studies of individual factors significant in the implementation process. With a tacit acceptance of evolutionary characterisation of IS implementation (Swanson, 1988), they single out organizational or technological factors that currently deserve attention and formerly may not have. Most of these works do not purport to cover a general IS implementation scenario and some (Thong & Yap, 1996), for instance, testify to the hopelessness of such an intention. Special implementation considerations pertaining to particular types of systems, for example, have been given for decision support systems (Alavi & Joachimsthaler, 1992), inter-organizational systems (Cavaye, 1996), clientserver systems (Guimaraes & Igbaria,

1997), group support systems (Munkvold, 1999), and enterprise systems (Fan et al, 2000). User-oriented perspectives on implementation have been offered by Joshi (1991), who discussed user feelings of equity in implementation, and Marakas and Hornik (1996), who treated the possibility of passive resistance by users. Saarinen and Vepsa¨la¨inen (1992) addressed the allocation of rational and experimental approaches to managing the risks of implementation and Cavaye and Christiansen (1996) treated the effects of organizational power on the implementation process.

This research trend toward focused perspectives in each contribution has truly embodied the fragmented characterisation of Kwon and Zmud (1987). They cited (and complained of) individual (and unrelated) research streams addressing the areas of implementation success factors, user-designer interactions, social change processes, behavioural motivations, and implementation risk factors. The trend has succeeded, however, in providing the field with a large spectrum of consideration that has now been shown to be relevant in the pursuit of implementation theory. Indeed, the expansion of this spectrum has coincided with the appearance of numerous in-depth investigations of individual cases. Witnessing the increasing acceptance of intensive studies in IS research (Markus & Lee, 1999), many of these expositions have elucidated significant phenomena observed in the conduct of real-world implementation projects. Some particularly interesting examples are from Markus (1983), Kaplan and Duchon (1988), Myers (1994), Walsham and Waema (1994), Cavaye and Cragg (1995), Munkvold (1999), and Fan et al (2000). Whether conducted from a positivistic, an interpretive, or a critical point of view, works like these have given a much more comprehensive definition to the accepted notion of ‘empirical.’ Their multiple contributions have lent a new urgency to the need to broaden our logical approach to axiomatising the theory of IS implementation.

An interesting tendency can be observed in new implementation research, which is worth noting here. It is a renewed interest in the definition of IS implementation success. Adding to the calls of Vitalari (1985) and Iivari (1985) for a longitudinal, continuum-oriented view, Jiang and Klein (1999) have connected it to the larger issue of IT assimilation. In concert with this, a renewed interest in implementation failure has also surfaced. In line with Bodenstab’s ‘post-mortem’ indicators of 1970, many of the case studies cited above have illustrated risk factors and contributors to the downfall of implementation projects. In fact, Alavi and Joachimsthaler (1992), Cavaye and Cragg (1995), and Munkvold (1999) have all used the distinction between voluntary and involuntary systems to debunk the logical assumption that lack of success must always be the same as failure. They reinforce the observations of Iivari (1985), that use of an involuntary system is not necessarily an indicator of success and non-use of a voluntary one is not necessarily an indication of implementation failure. As was aptly observed by Davis et al (1992), ‘The relationship between IS success and IS failure is not a straightforward one’. Diagnosis of failure does not constitute prescription for success. This has further stimulated thoughts of a deviation from classical logical reasoning, which might otherwise have misled us in that connection.

## IS effectiveness

In parallel with the work described above, a great body of related literature addressed the effectiveness of IS that already exist. The search for an acceptable definition of IS effectiveness has also witnessed numerous approaches. Value-added and various other economic outcomes have always been preferred indicators of IS effectiveness. Early formulae for IS development feasibility studies traditionally sought to match the very concrete costs of new systems with equally concrete outcomes. Lucas (1981) pointed to the desires of management for tangible evidence that IS can carry their own weight in a budgeting scenario. Saarinen (1996) summarised the traditional investment analysis approaches to measuring IS success, pointing out some of the difficulties experienced in their connection. Cooper and Mukhopadhyay (1991) advocated the use of economic production functions in this effort. In 1994, Iivari and Ervasti reviewed the expression of IS effectiveness in terms of the more general concept of organizational effectiveness. Their construct for the increased organizational effectiveness that is directly attributable to an IS includes more than strictly financial effects. The construct has been modified to accommodate the ideas of organization theory, together with their emphasis on establishing a good fit between organizational unit task requirements and the functionality provided by the system (Iivari & Ervasti, 1994; Gelderman, 1998). Further direct measures of IS success have focused on individual users, notably with the ‘improved decision effectiveness’ and ‘user efficiency’ constructs of Gatian (1994) and the ‘user performance’ measure of Etezadi-Amoli and Farhoomand (1996).

Eventually, a general shift occurred in the directions taken by researchers for evaluating IS effectiveness. This shift represented an increased emphasis on indirect measures of IS success and reflected an increased recognition of the many intangible costs and benefits associated with IS. Prominent among these effects were organizational context variables, such as were exposed by Ein-Dor and Segev (1978), and the user beliefs, attitudes, and behaviours concepts discussed by Davis (1993). Most of the early efforts at indirect IS assessment utilised positivist approaches and statistical methods, maintaining an insistence on measurement, as opposed to management (Ginzberg, 1978). Gatian (1994) offered a good synopsis of this shift. She cited the measurement difficulties encountered in attempts to capture intangible effects using only direct, objective measurements. General recognition of these difficulties also served to bolster the legitimacy of interpretive methods for investigating and reporting in IS research. As can be seen, for example, in the study of Kaplan and Duchon (1988), interpretive techniques showed an ability to capture intangibles that eluded quantitative measurement methods.

This shift has not captured the agreement of all researchers. Melone (1990) gave a sobering characterisation of this ‘preference of the literature for retrospective, perceptual measures over current objective measures’. As a partial result of this disagreement, the literature has spawned a plethora of models for evaluating IS effectiveness. DeLone and McLean (1992) offered a review of more than 100 such studies. Saarinen (1996) also provided a good discussion of the various approaches in the literature to measuring IS success and IS effectiveness. Good reviews and discussions of prior research in this area have also been produced for such focuses as technology acceptance (Straub et al, 1995), contingency theory studies (McKeen et al, 1994), and interpretive approaches to IS evaluation (Kanellis et al, 1999).

## User information satisfaction

For the problem of measuring IS success, indirect approaches evident in the literature have most commonly involved the use of surrogate measures. System quality, information quality, system usage, user satisfaction, individual impact and organizational impact all represent constructs used as substitutes for the elusive measure of IS effectiveness. The survey of DeLone and McLean (1992) provided coverage of these constructs and their use in the research to that time. Hwang and Thorn (1999) have extended that analysis of the literature to the present. Both the appropriateness and the measurability of these surrogates have been reinforced as well as disputed by research and writings in the field. Galletta and Lederer (1989), Melone (1990), Hawk and Raju (1991), Gatian (1994), Iivari and Ervasti (1994), Saarinen (1996), Thong and Yap (1996), and Gelderman (1998) have all picked up this gauntlet in one way or another and, taken together, provide a comprehensive look at the state of affairs in this connection.

One point, with which the above mentioned researchers all seem to be in agreement, is that the most popular surrogate measures of IS effectiveness have been developed using user satisfaction as their underlying concept. Representing the degree to which IS users ‘approve’ of the products and services provided by a system, user satisfaction has generally been seen to indicate the value to the firm that this approval must imply. This implication has itself been the subject of some degree of controversy in the literature, however. Various theoretical bases and numerous empirical studies have been cited in the process of addressing it. The distinction between voluntary and involuntary usage (Iivari, 1985) offers one example of aspects that have called the assumption into question. Melone (1990) and Woodroof and Kasper (1998) forwarded treatments of the degree to which research theories support a connection between user satisfaction and IS success. Some interesting summaries of the various investigations of this connection have been given by Iivari and Ervasti (1994) and by Saarinen (1996). The concept of user satisfaction has apparently survived this scrutiny and continues to enjoy application in studies of IS effectiveness. As demonstrated (Gelderman, 1998), user satisfaction ‘is the most appropriate measure for IS success available’.

In positivist approaches, the instrument most commonly used to operationalise the user satisfaction concept was originally developed by Bailey and Pearson (1983). It constituted a four-item overall measure of user information satisfaction (UIS), together with 39 items measuring various factors that may contribute to UIS in an organization. Each item was measured with four semantic differential Likert-type scales. The instrument was later tested for reliability and validity by Ives et al (1983), who used factor analysis to reduce its 39 items to a set of 24. They also were able to reduce the number of scales measuring each item to two. Their factor analysis yielded three first order factors, which they called EDP staff and services (ESS), quality of the information product (QIP), and knowledge or involvement level of users (KIL).

The UIS instrument has been tested and validated in numerous empirical studies. Baroudi et al (1986) used path analysis methods and McKeen et al (1994) used contingency theory to evaluate the efficacy of the instrument’s measurement of UIS. Confirmatory factor analyses for the instrument’s independent variables were performed by Baroudi and Orlikowski (1988), Subramanian and Nilakanta (1994), and Doll et al (1995). The UIS instrument was generally shown to offer a sound basis for testing factors that may be related to changes in the overall user approval of systems. Indeed, in commenting on their exploratory factor analysis of the instrument’s items, Iivari and Ervasti (1994) noted that ‘applied to a subset of the Bailey-Pearson instrument, [it] gave an elegant and easily interpretable factor structure that has acceptable convergent and discriminant validity’.

As later noted by Thong and Yap (1996), the exact criteria for IS effectiveness may vary from organization to organization. Hence, many applications of the UIS instrument have resulted in modified forms of the instrument, adapted for particular types of systems or circumstances. Modified instruments can be found in the literature for small business systems (Raymond, 1985), enduser computing (Doll & Torkzadeh, 1988), international systems (Lascu et al, 1995), expert systems (Guimaraes et al, 1996), computer-mediated communications (Abdul-Gader, 1996), outsourcing environments (Sengupta & Zviran, 1997), client/server systems (Guimaraes & Igbaria, 1997), and information centers (Essex et al, 1998).

A shorter form of the UIS instrument, originally suggested by Ives et al (1983), was validated by Baroudi and Orlikowski in 1988. Containing only 13 factor items, this instrument showed the same three factor first order structure as was established by Ives et al, 1983 with the original items (Bailey & Pearson, 1983) and further supported the diagnostic utility of the four item UIS construct. This and other modified forms of the UIS instrument have been applied in numerous empirical studies and, in the process, have validated it for their particular scenarios (see Joshi, 1990; Henry & Stone, 1994; Iivari & Ervasti, 1994; Kettinger & Lee, 1994; Doll et al, 1994; Saarinen, 1996; Ang & Koh, 1997; Downing, 1999).

The UIS instrument has incurred some criticism in the literature. In addition to the conceptual complaints about its theoretical grounding as a measure of IS effectiveness, the instrument has evinced comments on its constructs and their statistical properties. Telling critiques came from Galletta and Lederer (1989) and Melone (1990). Various concerns with the instrument have been addressed and, to some extent, settled, in connection with the instrument’s further use. Hawk and Raju (1991) also presented a direct defence of the instrument’s statistical reliability. Iivari and Ervasti (1994) discussed the level of analysis to which the UIS instrument has been applied. They pointed out that it has been used to evaluate the overall, organizational IS function, individual application systems, and even specific reports and queries. They express the view that the UIS instrument offers the best basis for analysing user satisfaction at the individual application system level. In comparison with other similar operationalisations, it provides the best support for regarding UIS ‘as a predictor of IS implementability and as an indicator of IS effectiveness’.

## Swanson’s implementation puzzle

In the search for a viable structure with which to characterise important systems implementation considerations, one existing model appeared to offer a good basis— Swanson’s Implementation Puzzle (Swanson, 1988). Its comprehensive nature seemed to allow the coverage, at least conceptually, of most considerations encountered in the literature. Its distinctions, in principle, also seemed to reflect the differentiations judged in the literature to be significant. While the model hasn’t ever been operationalised for statistical testing and analysis, its principles have continued to enjoy citation through the years (see, for instance, Joshi, 1991; Henry & Stone, 1994; Abdul-Gader, 1996; and Guimaraes & Igbaria, 1997).

The ‘puzzle’ characterisation was intended to reflect the variety of perspectives that must be covered by such a model and the different interrelationships they may embody in each particular situation to which the model might be applied (Swanson, 1988). It is the claim of this paper, that, given the degree of logical flexibility proposed in the introduction, Swanson’s model offers a reasonable axiomatic starting point for building a theory of IS implementation.

To begin the exposition of his model, Swanson criticised the broad definition of implementation discussed above. As a foundation for the model, he defined implementation as ‘a decision-making activity that converts a design concept into an operating reality so as to provide value to the client’ (Swanson, 1988, p 2). According to Swanson, while a design is assumed to exist, it need not necessarily be complete. Decisions and choices in IS implementation may thus also occur in the design areas.

The model itself was formulated as a collection of nine factors critical to the success or failure of an IS implementation effort. These factors are called user involvement, management commitment, value basis, mutual understanding, design quality, performance level, project management, resource adequacy, and situational stability. Swanson cited this collection of themes as being mutually exclusive and exhaustive at least for the context of the small-scale study from which they were derived. He asserted that IS implementation success involves the establishment of a fit among these factors. In his model formulation, Swanson pointed out that, while all these factors are subject to the influence of a systems implementor, none of them is entirely under the implementor’s control. He also noted that it is never possible to know everything about a particular factor in a given situation and that ideal relationships among the factors are neither constant nor unique. It is the address (and embrace) of these uncertainties, to which the work of this paper is targeted.

## User involvement

The first of Swanson’s factors embodies the importance of involving users in system implementation decisions. Swanson echoed previous testimonials to the importance of user involvement in design activities and noted that this is usually a prerequisite for user involvement in the rest of the implementation process. Recommending the participative approach of Enid Mumford and colleagues (Mumford & Henshell, 1979), Swanson noted the system familiarity and appreciation which also always follow. Since then, Barki and Hartwick (1994) have defined a distinction between user involvement in design activities, which they call ‘user participation’, and the importance and personal relevance of the system to a user (the latter was referred to by them as ‘user involvement’). This distinction was adopted by McKeen et al (1994).

They showed user participation to be a significant determinant of UIS, as was earlier shown by Baroudi et al (1986), and as was postulated even earlier (Ginzberg 1981). More recent studies also support this (Guimaraes & Igbaria, 1997). An independently formulated factor called ‘user influence’ also enjoyed prominence in the study of McKeen et al (1994). In many respects, these aspects of the user involvement concept correspond to the KIL factor discovered by Ives et al (1983).

## Management commitment

Swanson pointed to management commitment as the indicator of top-down support for an IS. He cited a considerable body of prior research in organizational power, while arguing that top management support legitimises the redistribution of power that a systems implementation effort evinces. Lucas (1981) explained how the organizational power accumulated by an IS can otherwise be very disturbing to individuals. This power involves subtle, yet undeniable, control as tasks become more and more dependent on proper functioning of the system. Management commitment publicly asserts the view from the top that this is in the best interests of the entire organization.

Literature in the field has long accepted and expounded on the importance of top management support to the success of IS. Ein-Dor and Segev (1978) cited similar considerations as variables that are under the control of top management in determining IS success. Among the priorities that they prescribed for top management was the highest possible placement of the executive to whom the MIS chief reports. This was supported by Ginzberg’s (1981) study and earned inclusion in the Bailey and Pearson instrument for UIS. Since then, numerous studies have verified the importance of visible management support in determining user satisfaction. Some of these were provided by Cooper and Zmud (1990), from a technological diffusion viewpoint, Henry and Stone (1994), with six items formulated to measure management support, Lane et al (1994), with an ‘organizational commitment’ construct, and Guimaraes and Igbaria (1997), in the context of client/server systems. In fact, with a slightly more active connotation, King and Thompson (1996) cited the importance of top management ‘guidance’.

## Value basis

The ‘value basis’ category of Swanson’s implementation model refers to the general understanding of the value of the IS to the organization. The traditional cost-benefit and risk analysis approaches of feasibility assessment can serve to influence this understanding. Swanson asserts, however, that the users’ perceptions of a system’s value often outweigh objectively established valuations in influencing the success or failure of an implementation effort. Ginzberg (1981) called this the ‘perception of the project’s importance to the organization’. In a meta-analysis of innovation literature, Tornatzky and Klein (1982) noted consistent significance reported for the ‘relative advantage’ afforded an organization, as perceived by the innovation adopters. This perceived value factor has been singled out in DeLone and McLean’s (1992) literature survey as ‘organizational impact’ and has been employed in numerous later studies of UIS. Gluck (1996) called it ‘cost-benefit satisfaction’, for example, and Saarinen (1996) defined its construct to be the benefits of the IT investment. In line with Swanson’s assertion, all have measured it with subjective user surveys.

## Mutual understanding

The desired relationship between the user and the provider of the IS represents a significant factor in the success or failure of an implementation effort. This relationship is characterised as ‘mutual understanding’ in Swanson’s model. Swanson pointed out that failures in mutual understanding could arise from either or both groups and will be strongly manifested in users’ perceptions of the relationship. If the desired state of understanding is lacking, it most commonly stems from misunderstandings in the system design phases, but only becomes evident in the latter stages of (attempted) implementation. Among the numerous literature sources that support the significance of this relationship, Cooper and Zmud (1990) called it ‘appropriate user-designer interaction and understanding’ and McKeen et al (1994) referred to it as ‘user-developer communication’. The Bailey and Pearson UIS instrument featured items addressing mutual understanding, which later were included in the higher order EDP staff and services (ESS) factor of Ives et al (1983). The confirmatory factor analysis of the instrument done by Doll et al (1995) actually found that the survey items targeting this mutual understanding embody their own significant subfactor, representing user perceptions of the IS staff.

## Design quality

Swanson cited the quality of a system’s design as obviously important to the success of the system’s implementation. Considered to be separate from the products and services that the design evinces, this factor refers to the more general characteristics of the system modelling and presentation. Swanson named design flexibility as an important aspect of this factor, stressing the ability to adapt the design to accommodate changing needs and preferences of users. DeLone and McLean (1992) also recognised the importance of a distinction between ‘system quality’ and ‘information quality’ in evaluating IS success. As was later found by Hwang and Thorn (1999), the separate aspect of general system quality is often more important to users than the products of the system.

Referred to as ‘good IT design’ by Cooper and Zmud (1990), the concept has enjoyed frequent reference in the literature. In his technology acceptance model, Davis (1993) pointed out that the ‘system design features’ help determine the system’s ‘perceived ease of use’, which, in turn, influences the user attitudes toward the system. Research of Henry and Stone (1994) also confirmed the significance of ‘ease of system use’ in explaining changes in user satisfaction.

## Performance level

According to Swanson, system designs are ultimately responsible for system performance. He maintained, however, that it is in the latter stages of implementation when the performance expectations of users are squarely faced. Even when precise objectives have not been specified by users, implicit performance expectations will always surface during implementation. Swanson named such aspects as reliability and responsiveness, in explaining the significance of user views of products and services generated by an IS. As mentioned above, this encompasses a view of the system’s results or outputs, which is separate from the more general evaluation of its design features. DeLone and McLean (1992) also stressed the distinction of this aspect, calling it ‘information quality’, from the ‘system quality’ factor discussed above. Saarinen (1996) referred to the ‘quality of the IS product and related services’ in this regard, and found that it separates in factor analysis into pure information quality and more general design-oriented factors. It appears to coincide with the higher order quality of the information product (QIP) factor derived from the Ives et al (1983) study of the Bailey and Pearson UIS instrument. This QIP factor has survived numerous other factor analyses of this and related measurement instruments for UIS (Baroudi & Orlikowski, 1988; Joshi, 1991; Doll et al, 1995).

## Project management

The component of Swanson’s model that encompasses the actual conduct of the implementation process is called the ‘project management’ component. It involves the way in which the magnitude and complexity of the project are addressed in terms of time and allocation of the project’s resources. Swanson noted the numerous tools and techniques that are available to guide the management of an implementation project. He further pointed out that many of the difficult choices in implementation surface in this connection. Lucas (1981) had already cited adherence to time schedules and resource allocations in evaluating the success of development efforts. Cooper and Zmud (1990) echoed the importance of this consideration, calling for ‘extensive project definition and planning’ in an implementation scenario. In confirmatory factor analyses of UIS short form data, Doll et al (1995) found the EDP services consistently loaded as a separate factor from factors for EDP staff and the QIP and KIL factors discussed above. The EDP services factor was characterised by items evaluating the processing of requests for changes to existing systems and time required for new systems development. These items represent the only scales of that instrument addressing project management practices in the IS context. This appears to coincide with the ‘controllability factor discovered by Saarinen (1996), which embodied the scheduling aspect of the systems development process in his study.

## Resource adequacy

Swanson stressed the importance of resource adequacy in every implementation effort. Whether it refers to personnel, equipment, or data, this must include considerations of quality, as well as quantity of resources. As an example of a category of adequacy in this regard, Swanson cited the technical skill levels of the implementation staff. They must be sufficient to match the requirements imposed by the project. Swanson went on to place responsibility for resource adequacy squarely in the hands of the implementors, who should compete for the allocations they need. In his models of equity, Joshi (1990, 1991), showed that users’ perceptions of resource allocation fairness were significant determinants of user information satisfaction. He provided interesting discussion of the conflicting interests that must be addressed with limited resources and, using the UIS construct, studied the consequences of different user views of the process. Guimaraes and colleagues found developer skills to be a telling factor in the user satisfaction with expert systems and with client/server systems (Guimaraes et al, 1996; Guimaraes & Igbaria, 1997). Saarinen (1996) also found this aspect of resource quality to be significant in explaining variation in UIS.

## Situational stability

The sensitivity of the implementation effort to situational changes that may occur represented the final component of Swanson’s model. A climate of instability can result in some degree of organizational disruption and alienation, which themselves can hinder implementation progress. Lucas (1981) discussed the risks involved in system development projects, which grow out of the natural human adversity to change. Calling the implementation process a ‘planned change activity’, he cited a large body of literature devoted to managing those risks. Nichols (1981) noted the importance of understanding the effects of an implementation on the work lives of its stakeholders. While all changes are not necessarily bad ones, expectations and perceptions can certainly cause users to act as if they are. Most researchers and practitioners concur with Swanson’s view that sensitivity to changes can go a long way toward ameliorating user concerns in that regard. Notable examples of this agreement are evident in ‘impact on work environment’ factor (Joshi, 1991) and ‘impact on end-users’ job’s construct (Guimaraes et al, 1996; Guimaraes & Igbaria, 1997).

## A positivist approach

As described by Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991), a positivist research approach assumes the existence of definable phenomena with fixed relationships that can be observed and studied using structured methods. It usually involves the testing of formal propositions on sample entities and the statistical or logical generalisation of test results to larger populations. Rooted in the traditions of natural science, this approach is embodied in much of the classical and current research in IS. Whether conducted using statistical sampling, structured case investigation, or laboratory experiment, positivist research always rests on the notion of modelling. A positivist model seeks to represent the object of study, while distilling or filtering out irrelevant (and seemingly confounding) aspects of reality. This enables a more focused look at the particular phenomena of interest. The limitations of positivist approaches to IS research have long been recognised. Important reasons for these limitations centre around the inability of positivist models to represent dynamic situations, whose significant determinants and effects include behavioural and sociological ones. Some early writings in that regard were presented, for example, by Ginzberg (1978), Ginzberg and Schultz (1987) and Swanson (1987). More recent discussion, in the context of alternative approaches, can be found in Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991), Walsham and Waema (1994), and Klein and Myers (1999). Positivism has never been disqualified from participation in IS research and practice, however, and this paper seeks to demonstrate its useful role in IS implementation theory.

## Quantitative methods

To employ the Swanson Implementation Puzzle as a positivist model, a method is required for applying it and interpreting the results of that application. For a quantitative method of operationalising Swanson’s model, it is proposed that the UIS instrument of Bailey and Pearson (1983) be used as the basis. The unit of analysis would be at a system level and the subjects of study would be participants in the implementation effort. Bailey and Pearson employed an interesting method to establish the completeness of their original 39-item instrument. It is claimed here that the instrument offers a sufficiently complete measurement domain for the purposes of this exposition.

In the process of establishing the validity of the Bailey and Pearson (1983) instrument, Ives et al (1983) eliminated six of the original 39 items, because of their ‘undesirable psychometric characteristics’. They were formulated to measure user perceptions of competition with the

EDP unit, chargeback method, vendor support, computer language used, security of data, and format of output. Since these items have escaped further testing and use in subsequent applications of the UIS instrument, they are also excluded from the questionnaire formulated here. As mentioned above, the abbreviation achieved for this instrument by Ives et al (1983) included the reduction to two of the number of semantic differential scales used to measure each item. The remaining 33 items, each measured with two scales, together with the four-item construct used to measure the dependent variable of user information satisfaction, constitute the basis for the instrument of this proposal.

The work of Ives et al (1983) was ultimately targeted at the construction of a ‘short form’ of the instrument for use ‘when time is limited and/or only an overall assessment of UIS (rather than analysis of particular symptoms of a problem) is adequate’. To this end, they used a factor analysis, together with corrected-item total correlations and correlations with the overall UIS criterion, to eliminate 20 further items. This resulted in the suggested 13-item questionnaire that has since been employed in numerous further studies (Baroudi & Orlikowski, 1988; Doll et al, 1995). The aim of the project described in this paper is to establish an instrument capable of representing Swanson’s factors in particular implementation efforts. Hence, this short form is not considered to be rich enough in coverage. Others have also argued for the need to re-expand this short form. Joshi (1990), Iivari and Ervasti (1994), Kettinger and Lee (1994), and Ang and Soh (1997), all elucidated successful studies that included items from the original Bailey and Pearson instrument, which had been eliminated from the short form. Galletta and Lederer (1989) complained specifically about eight important and ‘potentially meaningful’ items that were dropped from the instrument in spite of their high factor loadings, reliability scores, and correlations with other instruments.

Seven of the remaining 33 items in the basis questionnaire have been selected for elimination from the instrument proposed in this paper. Items measuring the means of input/output with the EDP centre, response/ turnaround time, and integration of database are considered obsolete issues because of the on-line nature of current systems and the predominance of database technology in today’s system designs. They had not produced factor loadings in the Ives et al (1983) study and were not later used in subsequent UIS studies. Items measuring documentation, correction of errors, expectations of computer support, and precision of output are also judged to be potentially confusing. Reasons include the distinction that could surface between paper documentation and on-line help, the interactive character of some application support functions, and the lack of relevance of numeric precision in non-scientific applications. Additionally, as suggested by Joshi (1990), the semantic differential pair ‘progressive . . . regressive’ for the item ‘time required for new system development’ has been replaced by an ‘improving . . . deteriorating’ pair. No further changes are recommended to the Ives et al (1983) instrument.

As noted by Swanson (1987), in an earlier work conventional research methods like this presume the situation of interest is static. They provide ‘snapshot’ views that may offer little insight into why the object of study has achieved the depicted state (Galliers, 1991). One means of overcoming this weakness may be with the type of longitudinal designs advocated by Vatalari (1985). King and Rodriguez (1978) had already lamented the lack of pre and post hoc comparisons in such survey research, noting that the original expectations, against which respondents judge the systems, are unknown and probably unrealistic. In a situation containing large numbers of participants, this survey method may still be the best way to avoid missing out on the input they might provide—particularly in view of the plurality of factors our underlying model addresses. In this regard, it is not unreasonable to plan on multiple ‘snapshots’, by administering the instrument at key milestones of the project. In this way, changes can be tracked and practices can be modified along the way.

## Qualitative methods

Kaplan and Duchon (1988) have observed that quantitative methods only accommodate ‘unidirectional assessment’ of IS impact on users or of users’ impacts on IS implementation efforts. They note that instruments like the one proposed above ‘remove the effects of context in order to produce generalisable, reproducible results’. Swanson (1988), himself, emphasized the importance of environmental factors and the uniqueness of each situation’s manifestation of the interplay between significant factors. This may indeed signal situations in which the Swanson model needs an alternative positivist application. Where more interactive methods of data collection are possible and a deeper understanding of individual factors is desired, the qualitative case-study method of Yin (1989) provides an excellent backdrop for its formulation. As is well-described by Lee (1989), a casestudy design can be produced, which follows a ‘scientific method’ for testing the nine postulates of Swanson’s model. Individual investigative designs must be customised to their target situations, but the Swanson postulates are stated with enough flexibility to allow for realistic focus and appropriate goal-setting. In addition to the above cited guides, the literature contains numerous examples of positivist case-studies that can be used as patterns for such a design. The most celebrated one is an IS implementation study of Markus (1983), whose results have often been cited for the insights they achieved through qualitative methods of scientific inquiry.

While the foregoing treatment is not intended to provide anything more than suggestions for a case-study design, it is also not intended to imply that the procedure is trivial. It must first be noted that we are no longer tied to UIS as a surrogate measure of the outcome. The propositions of a positivist case analysis do require measurable dependent variables, but the model of Swanson contains no prescription in that respect (Swanson has referred to the establishment of ‘an operating reality that provides value to the client’ as a measure of implementation success). Markus (1983) investigated resistance to IS implementation and defined it in terms of behaviours and intentions. Its use as a measurable indicator would carry over to a case study design based on the Swanson model. In fact, the only differences from the original Markus design that the Swanson model would evince involve the formulation of the three theories to be tested. Her first proposition asserts that resistance occurs because of inherent traits of the resisters. The situational stability factor is cited by Swanson as a significant consideration for implementers and addresses the internal characteristics of the system users. Analogously, the inherent system traits of the second Markus proposition would be expressed in terms of the design quality and performance level factors of Swanson. The third proposition questions whether it is the interaction of user and system traits that causes resistance. This is already addressed by the Swanson notion of fit between the puzzle pieces. The claim is not that Swanson’s framework can replace all theory in IS implementation, but rather that it can provide a reasonable basis for investigating it. (Indeed, the exhaustiveness of Swanson’s coverage is a question that could spawn a tedious tabulation of propositions from the literature and their possible restatement in the terms of Swanson’s model. Such a semantic exercise would go far beyond the point of demonstrating the existence of a framework whose precision is elucidating and whose imprecision is unifying.)

## An interpretive approach

A great many taxonomies have been produced through the years to describe and classify different types of IS research. Notable examples among them have been oriented to the domain of study (Nolan & Wetherbe, 1980) and to the strategies or methods (Benbasat, 1984; Franz & Robey, 1987). Later typologies have embraced the philosophical underpinnings that distinguish different research streams. Examples are by, for instance Galliers (1991) and Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991). As the philosophical foundations of research pursuits have become more a part of the collective consciousness in the IS research community, one epistemological distinction has achieved prominence: that between positivist and interpretive research approaches. As discussed in the previous section, positivist assumptions have guided a great plurality of IS research in the past (Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991).

Interpretive research rejects the assumption that an objective reality of verifiable facts and implications can ever be established in its domains of study. Originally stemming from the social sciences, interpretivism assumes that every situation must be studied in terms of how it is perceived and interpreted by its participants. An excellent discussion of this approach was provided by Orlikowski and Baroudi in 1991. They noted the importance of context and process in the considerations of this approach. They also qualified the notion of generalising the results of interpretive studies, by citing the process of logical induction. Baskerville (1999) characterised interpretive underpinnings as ‘post-positivist assumptions and noted that ‘complex social systems cannot be reduced for meaningful study’. Numerous works in the interpretive tradition have supplemented IS research and writing and are cited at various places below.

In the IS implementation research arena, pure factor research had already been exposed for its weaknesses (Ginzberg, 1978; Iivari, 1985) and the resulting recommendations for a process-oriented view had also found their detractors (Srinivasan & Davis, 1987). Now, with an interpretive approach, Levine and Rossmore (1993) have rejected the normative, rationality assumptions of IT implementation perspectives and Griffith and Northcraft (1996) have highlighted the significance of cognitive elements in IS implementation. In fact, the gap between research and practice in the field has even been attributed to an inadequate emphasis on interpretive methods (Harvey & Myers, 1995; Lee et al, 1999). In coincidence with the recent surge of interpretive IS research literature, several articles have appeared, which offer explanations and guidelines for conducting interpretive studies (Walsham, 1995; Klein & Myers, 1999).

Although the interpretive approach to research in IS appears to allow for considerable leeway in viewing its objects of study, the approach does not prescribe the absence of theory. Indeed, an interpretive study must always be guided by a theoretical framework. Walsham (1995) cited theory as an important initial guide to design and data collection in interpretive studies and Baskerville (1999) noted the importance of a clear theoretical framework to guide the process of deriving lessons from the research. Klein and Myers (1999), with their ‘Principle of Abstraction and Generalization,’ also pointed to the importance of theoretical concepts in the analysis of interpretive data. It is simply the case that interpretive research avoids imposing any initial theoretical assumptions on its objects of study. Klein and Myers (1999) noted the absence of predefined dependent and independent variables and Baskerville (1999) even cited the need to adjust the theoretical framework as a study progresses. This flexibility of theory is intended to manifest sensitivity to the different meanings different people may assign to a situation, as well as to the contexts and the nondeterministic aspects of these contexts (Walsham & Waema, 1994; Klein and Myers, 1999).

No attempt is made here to argue that the implementation puzzle of Swanson (1988) has specifically been shown to encompass all theoretical considerations that interpretive IS implementation literature has exposed. It is suggested, however, that Swanson’s model has the flexibility to serve this approach as a theoretical basis. In that respect, it is claimed that the model’s strength is rooted in the non-Euclidean nature of its representation. In depicting the framework as a puzzle, Swanson (1988) sought to defy the imposition of Euclidean dimensions on the way it would be visualised. Moreover, he stated that the shapes of the puzzle pieces should never be expected to be fully observable or even fixed. Euclidean geometry certainly does influence the expression of complex models on a printed page and it often drives the process of visualising models in the abstract. It is not, however, the only rigorous mathematical framework for serving the intuition. Topology, for instance, denies the use of shape and size as distinguishing characteristics of well-defined geometric entities (Willard, 1970). The fluidity and open-ended consideration that interpretive research methods give to their theoretical bases is certainly not precluded by the Swanson model.

IS implementation literature has shown the interpretive case study method to be quite elucidating. Walsham and Waema (1994), for instance, have exposed the importance of ‘contextualist research’ in the study of IS implementation. The Swanson factor of top management support was advanced to ‘active intervention’ in the realm of IS strategy. Myers (1994), on the other hand, was able to qualify the factor’s absolute sovereignty, with a hermeneutic case analysis that highlighted the dynamic nature of top management support and its influence on a situation. (This study combined the use of interpretive and critical research approaches. The critical research approach is not treated in this paper.) Walsham (1995) has provided an excellent overview of the nature and methods for interpretive case research in IS. The ethnographic method of interpretive research has also enhanced IS literature. This method seeks the deep involvement of researchers in the objects of their study, as is practiced in anthropological research. Trauth and O’Connor (1991), for example, studied numerous contextual issues using this method, while illustrating the efficacy of a reactive approach to the consideration of basis theories. Harvey and Myers (1995) have argued for this method’s utility in connecting the work of scholars and practitioners and Myers (1999) has given a helpful guide for conducting ethnographic research in IS. Action research, the other primary method used in interpretive IS research, prescribes that researchers go beyond the deep involvement of ethnographics, to participate in the system under study and to enlist the participation of its subjects in the research. This method has long been advocated and practiced by IS researchers (Mumford & Henshell, 1979). Levine and Rossmore (1993) recently illustrated the benefits that this intervention can bring to IS implementation. Baskerville (1999) has written an informative summary of the basic aspects of an action research project. As is argued in the papers cited above, each of these methods has exhibited a circumstantial utility that would be hard to deny.

## An eclectic view

The preceding sections have discussed research results that have all been instrumental to the development IS implementation theory. Their contributions, the approaches they followed, and the methods they used all have enriched the state of knowledge in the field. It is argued here that nothing cited above could have been omitted, without sacrifice. All the discoveries, together with the research strategies that achieved them, have served us. The reason for this argument is to dismiss the first two conceptions of the nature of science, which were outlined by Klein et al (1991). They characterised supremacy as the belief in the universal applicability of one research method only. Contingency was then described as the recognition that several legitimate research methodologies exist, together with well-defined conditions under which each is applicable, to the exclusion of the others. Klein et al (1991) went on define and advocate pluralism, where multiple research methods can co-exist and no prescriptions can favour one over the others in any situation. That is certainly a magnanimous view and it is shared by others. Gable (1994), for instance, published a very comprehensive treatment of different IS research directions and endorsed a pluralistic stance. Harvey and Myers (1995) argued for methodological pluralism, calling the qualitative methods ‘alternate discourse’. This paper is written in support of the fourth conception described by Klein et al (1991), eclecticism. This qualifies the pluralistic view with a stipulation that ‘it is possible to pick and choose from among different methods’ to concoct a strategy that best serves the circumstances at hand. The final conception given by Klein et al (1991) is dialectics. This advocates the perpetual competition of research methods for recognition and prominence.

Numerous IS researchers have argued for the need to combine research methods in the pursuit of discovery. Trauth and O’Connor (1991) showed profit from the combination of multiple qualitative methods, while Gable (1994) and Kaplan and Duchon (1988) illustrated the benefits of combining qualitative and quantitative methods. Lee (1991) even proposed a model for combining positivist and interpretive approaches in general. Throughout these papers, the benefits are cited of ‘triangulating’ the results of different approaches, to achieve greater certainty or deeper understanding. It is asserted here that methods and approaches can and should be combined and that there is never any strong ambiguity in the choice among them. Each has a contribution that could be brought to a particular project and its utility can be evaluated in the context of the project’s goals and resources. In line with a pluralistic view, there may not exist an algorithmic sequence of (classical) logical rules for choosing one method over another in a given circumstance. We should, however, believe that a comprehensive knowledge of the field can afford one the capability of designing optimal research strategies.

A disturbing aspect of Klein et al’s (1991) exposition is their tacit acceptance of the methodological bias that follows from the need for researchers to specialise in a limited number of research approaches. This was echoed by Gable (1994), who noted the limits this implies on the number of methods that could ever be brought to bear in one study. This would appear to preclude the possibility of ever seeing a true integration of the expert knowledge that makes up IS implementation theory. It is the contention of this paper that such a view is unduly limiting and the possibility of research teams seems to be a simple argument for that contention. We expect practitioners to benefit from the entire theory of IS implementation, with comprehensive, rather than specialist, strategies. Kwon and Zmud (1987) complained of narrow perspectives in IS implementation literature. They noted that, ‘most studies focus on small pieces of the implementation puzzle, without considering larger issues’. This can be remedied with recognition and cooperation. An expert implementor can bring the knowledge gained from much experience to bear in a new situation. An ability to integrate this knowledge is assumed, for instance, when we favour experience in reviewing resumes to fill an IT position. Why not allow IS implementation research to be viewed and pursued with a unified perspective, too? As Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991) remarked, ‘the findings of information systems research filter into the practitioner community and are used as prescriptions for action. Restricted research, thus, has far-reaching consequences’.

The foregoing discussion is intended to clear the air a bit in the realm of IS implementation research and practice. The atmosphere is charged with competition among approaches, methods, and frames of reference. It is hoped that ideas expressed here will stimulate discussion about comprehensive strategies for solving implementation problems. The Swanson framework is not necessarily the best basis for this, but it has served as a vehicle for demonstrating their plausibility. Benbasat and colleagues (1987) have made a cogent obser-

vation that IS implementation researchers may sometimes forget. They noted that, ‘researchers usually learn by studying the innovations put in place by practitioners, rather than by providing the initial wisdom for these novel ideas’. We should allow this to encompass the higher level grappling that expert practitioners have always done with the vast array of knowledge at their disposal. The best implementors find a way to ‘factor’ it all into their decision-making, without missing any opportunity to further their cause. The intuition and judgements made along the way might be difficult to explain in a strict, rule-based format, but the results are often undeniable. Researchers should hope this continues and perhaps even seek to emulate it.
