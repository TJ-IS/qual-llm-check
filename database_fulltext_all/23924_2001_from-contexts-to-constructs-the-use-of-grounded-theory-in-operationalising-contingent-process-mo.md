# From contexts to constructs: the use of grounded theory in operationalising contingent process models

GH Galal

School of Informatics and Multimedia Technology, University of North London, 166–220 Holloway Rd, London N7 8DB, UK

In software and systems engineering, process models have been suggested that partly develop artifacts and then deploy them to evaluate the degree of their success in satisfying requirements, using the results of the evaluation to inform further engineering efforts. This paper discusses the important role of evaluation in non-sequential process models, and suggests an interpretive framework for effecting such evaluation, as well as the determination of risks and constraints to operationalise such models. We outline a particular mode of the Grounded Systems Engineering Methodology (GSEM) which aims at supporting the appli cation of non-sequential process models, thus enabling them to be more responsive to the development context. GSEM utilises the philosophy and procedures of the Grounded Theory method, an interpretive research strategy which is being widely used for the inductive analysis of qualitative data in theory building. An illustrative case study is used to show how the framework can be applied in practice. European Journal of Information Systems (2001) 10, 2–14.

## Introduction

Increasingly, IS engineering professionals are aware that the most widely used systems development methodologies fail to guide the practitioner towards a fuller appreciation and incorporation of contextual factors into their artifacts. Systems development methodologies that give priority to contextual factors, such as political and psychological consideration, fail to carry the results of their investigations through to hard technical systems engineering decisions (Mingers, 1992). This chasm has been wide open for far too long. Now is perhaps the time to explore methodologies that start from a contextual viewpoint, and end in concrete constructs that are directly relevant to the engineering of informational artifacts, including the engineering process itself.

One particularly relevant aspect of contextual complexity is in establishing the functional and non-functional requirements of information systems, due to their highly ambiguous and dynamic contexts. Software engineering professionals have also recognised for some time that it is extremely difficult, if not impossible, to get software systems ‘right first time’. In the words of Brooks (1995), the question becomes whether to recognise that the first version of the system is an imperfect prototype and start again, or to deliver such a system to the client anyway. This view has led to a number of proposals for resolving this question of lack of clarity at the start of an IS project. Some such proposals focused on approaches to software and IS engineering processes that are essentially non-sequential such as Prototyping (Boar, 1984), Evolutionary Delivery (Gilb, 1988) , and the Spiral Model (Boehm, 1986). However, these models need to be more sensitive to the context, while being sufficiently rigorous and inspectable by the parties involved.

The social and human sciences have had to contend with contextual and soft, ambiguous sides to their investigations, similar to those that IS engineers now have to contend with. In this paper we will be turning our attention to one such method, Grounded Theory, to outline the extent to which some of its procedures may help in operationalising contingent, or non-sequential, process models.

In this paper we give an account of a particular aspect of a methodology that we have developed for dealing with contextual issues that we highlight above. This is the Grounded Systems Engineering Methodology, or GSEM for short. This is a methodology that has at its heart the philosophy and procedures of a widely recognised method for conducting qualitative research in the human sciences, Grounded Theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967), and aims at supporting process and artifact engineering decisions.

The paper starts with a discussion of the role of evaluation in applying non-sequential systems development process models; this discussion gives a brief overview of some of the main evaluation paradigms available. An overview is then given of the Grounded Theory method, from the social sciences, which is used in our approach to analyse qualitative data, and the potential role of its formulations in constructing frameworks for evaluating system increments. The paper then describes the precise steps in our Grounded Theorybased approach to effect in-process evaluation in a systematic manner. First we elaborate on the issues that our approach aims to tackle.

## Operationalising non-sequential process models: a question of evaluation

The traditional software engineering view of the software development process (often referred to as the ‘Waterfall Model’, Royce, 1970) assumes that full system specifications can be captured in a ‘single pass’, with validation and verification activities occurring at the end of each development phase. The purpose of such validation and verification activities is to assure the quality of the product at any of the development phases, including those aiming at specifying the system’s requirements. Such process models have been criticised for the unrealistic linearity that is assumed between the development phases, and for assuming that complete pre-construction specification is always attainable (McCracken & Jackson, 1982; Pressman, 2000).

An exploratory, experimental and contextually-oriented approach to systems development becomes more realistic. Alternative process models that rely on partial building and delivery of the software, and evaluating it, have been proposed. We refer collectively to such models as ‘contingent’ or ‘non-sequential’ process models, and regard them as the antithesis of the Waterfall model. Such process models suggest the development of a prototype or an increment on a restricted scale, experimenting with it, and using the experience gained in the design and development of further system components. As such, these models try to be sensitive to, and contingent in their detailed workings upon, both the development context and the context for which an information system is intended. Where the engineering process model is risk oriented, such as in the case of the Spiral Model, the identification of major development risks becomes critical to the operationalisation of the approach. Establishing risk types (or categories) has a nature analogous to establishing evaluation questions of the type: ‘What evaluation criteria are relevant here?’. We note here that not all evaluations need to have a distinct step that aims to establish evaluation criteria (see for example a summary of the Goal Free and Connoisseurship models of evaluation, explained in Guba & Lincoln, 1981).

It is for this latter category of process models that evaluation acquires a particularly critical nature. The aims of evaluation activities in non-sequential process models may include the assessment of aspects of IS such as the human-machine interface, functionality, and perhaps more importantly, the system’s impact on the social and organisational environment. The subjective nature of the quality of information systems (Iivari, 1988) means that their in-process evaluation frameworks need to pay attention to the subjective and contextual aspects of IS. The results of the evaluation feed into subsequent iterations, leading to changes in the detail or the architectural design of the information system, its functional or non-functional aspects. We emphasise that it is the process of evaluation, rather than mere measurement which is relevant here. This is because neither aspects to be measured, nor frameworks for interpreting the measurements, are as yet known when the evaluation is started. Evaluation should determine the aspects that need to be measured, as well as the way in which the measurements may be interpreted.

We posit, therefore, that as far as non-sequential process models and their underlying exploratory/ experimental orientation are concerned, the construction of evaluation frameworks that are grounded in the development context becomes a major aspect of operationalising such process models. A concomitant issue is that of whether such evaluation frameworks can, once more, be specified fully in advance, and the extent to which the dynamic context of the development situation should feed into the evaluation framework itself. This is especially relevant if we acknowledge that experimenting with a part of an information system may lead to the discovery of factors or criteria relevant to the evaluation, that could not have been identified without some in-context experimentation. But the subject of evaluation has occupied social researchers and policy makers for some considerable time (see for example Guba & Lincoln, 1981; Patton, 1990; Rossi & Freeman, 1993), and it seems to us foolish not to capitalise on the wealth of research and philosophical inquiries into this area. Below we glimpse what of the evaluation literature might be of use to us in the field of information systems engineering.

## Paradigms in evaluation

Before selecting a model, or a method, for evaluation we must recognise that the process of evaluation involves making knowledge claims about the informational artifact (or a part of it) that is being evaluated, and about the factors that together determine its value. This brings us to viewing the nature of the fundamental paradigms that underpin, or should underpin, our evaluation practices with some importance. This is vital if we are to justify or defend the results of our evaluation, and base further engineering decisions on these. For the purposes of this paper, we define ‘Paradigm’ as the set of basic metaphysical assumptions underlying human thinking or its products. These assumptions typically concern the nature of the world, the relationship between the observer and the observed, the nature of knowledge that can be reliably generated about the world, and the methodology appropriate for knowing about, or acting upon, the world (Boland, 1985; Hirschheim & Klein, 1989; Ngwenyama, 1991; Walsham, 1995). The contention is mainly between the traditional positivistic-scientific paradigm that is seen to dominate the traditional approaches to IS engineering (Iivari, 1991; Jackson 1992) and the interpretive paradigm, which has been making steady progress in the field (Walsham, 1995). The interpretive paradigm takes the position that knowledge is socially constructed, and is thus essentially subjective and valueladen. This is in contrast to the positivistically-inclined view that assumes that reality is objective and can therefore be described without reference to the observer.

At the heart of the interest in the interpretive view of systems engineering is the epistemological commitment to the socially constructed nature of knowledge. In IS engineering, the types of knowledge that matter may include knowledge about the environment in which the information system may fit as a technical artifact—in other words, knowledge about the problem situation; knowledge about what features of the technical artifact are necessary for it to be successful in its intended environment; knowledge about the domain which an information system needs to model to serve its purpose; and knowledge about how the environment reacts (or may in the future react) to the information system. There has also been a reaction amongst IS scholars against the notion of the universality of the scientific method, whereby the traditional ‘scientific method’ is deemed valid for all forms of enquiry (Jackson, 1992), and towards the more interpretive stance (Walsham, 1995).

This debate may be extended to the issue of how aspects of the contexts of information systems may be brought directly to bear upon the evaluation process. This is an issue of selecting an appropriate epistemology for evaluation. If we take the position that evaluation frameworks can not be determined prior to the deployment of an information system or a part thereof; that evaluation frameworks and their meanings are in a state of flux and are continually achieved; and that the implications of any statement of requirements can not be easily grasped without reference to context, then we are implicitly adopting an interpretive perspective on the process. The same issue has been discussed in the evaluation literature in the guise of the debate between pre-ordinate and responsive evaluation models. We discuss the responsive model of evaluation next.

## Responsive evaluation

Responsive evaluation is one model that may be used to guide the practice of evaluation. It takes as its starting point the concerns and issues of the stakeholding audiences. In other words, the environment or the context of the object being evaluated is the main ‘organiser’ of this type of evaluation. It focuses on the importance of personalising and humanising the evaluation process. Responsive evaluation is attributed to Stake (1975) who emphasised taking account of the central issues that are important for each particular program that’s being evaluated, and then orienting the evaluation approach, plans and outcomes towards clarifying or resolving these issues. A responsive orientation is relevant to situations where little is known about the factors that may lead to the success, or otherwise, of an information system. Responsive evaluation makes extensive use of personal observation and other qualitative data gathering techniques. Guba and Lincoln (1989) developed a framework for conducting responsive, constructive evaluation. Guba and Lincoln ascribe the term ‘Fourth Generation Evaluation’ to their approach, to differentiate it from the three earlier generations of evaluation practices that focused on measurement, description and judgement, respectively.

Fourth generation evaluation is based on a subjective epistemology, which regards reality as a subjective or a social construction, and as such subscribes to the interpretive paradigm we explain above.

One methodological consequence for the epistemological stance of fourth generation evaluation is that a hermeneutic (or interpretive) dialectic approach is taken towards the process of evaluation. This essentially means that discussions and negotiations are the central mechanisms by which the constructions of the stakeholders of an evaluation are sought, refined and represented. This type of evaluation thus holds as its main tenet that evaluation should not be pre-ordinate or (pre-defined). This in turn means that the evaluator should aim to establish appropriate questions to ask, as well as adequate means of achieving and interpreting answers, rather than enter the situation with preconceptions about how the evaluation should be conducted. This is a more useful perspective for taking account of the contexts within which IS engineering processes take place, and within which information systems themselves need to operate and succeed. The implication of this stance is that the evaluation activity should be based upon an interpretive point of view, and that it needs to be enriched by methods competent in handling the soft, contextual and qualitative data that arise naturally in IS engineering situations. In the social sciences, there has been a number of philosophies and methods for conducting research using qualitative data as the primary input to the process of analysis and theory building (see Silverman, 1985). One such method is Grounded Theory and its associated procedures.

## Grounded theory

Grounded Theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) appeared as a counter to the then prevailing practice in social science research of focusing on the testing and verification of existing theories rather than the generation of new ones. Glaser and Strauss assert that generating new theories from data in a reliable and systematic way is just as important as verifying existing ones. They also stress that the quality and validity of a theory can not be divorced from the way it is generated. They propose a framework for generating theory from data, referring to the resultant theory as ‘grounded theory’. By the term ‘grounded’ they mean that the theory should have close links with reality as suggested by the data. A variety of sources contribute to the data used by a Grounded Theory researcher, and so human informants are not the sole source of data. The method also offers techniques for validating theoretical formulations with reference to the area under study.

Strauss and Corbin (1990) define a Grounded Theory as:

one that is inductively derived from the study of the phenomenon it represents. That is, it is discovered, developed and provisionally verified through systematic data collection and analysis of data pertaining to that phenomenon.

The above definition gives a flavour of the iterative nature of theory formulation (to explain or describe a phenomenon) using this method. The iterative verification of the developed (or discovered) theory is central and is achieved through the data collection, organisation and analysis techniques. The findings from the activities described above are analysed and related to the wider context by means of an analytical aid known as the Conditional Matrix. The Conditional Matrix aids in considering the wider range of conditions and consequences related to a phenomenon under study. It may depict the chosen levels of analysis as a number of concentric circles surrounding an inner one representing the phenomenon under study. Outer circles represent the host organisation, the business environment, or even the society at large, to enable the analyst to both distinguish and link levels of relevant conditions and consequences (Strauss & Corbin, 1990; 1998). In other words, the Conditional Matrix enables the analyst to make sense of the theoretical formulations developed earlier at a number of analysis levels. It also helps the analyst in elucidating how, when and to what extent certain conditions, such as an existing or a contemplated process, influence or interact with a phenomenon under study. The Conditional Matrix illustrates the emerging operational logic proposed by the findings, and juxtaposes such findings against the contextual levels. These levels are freely selected and may comprise individuals, groups, organisation, community etc.

The result of constructing a Grounded Theory is a dynamic description of a phenomenon that relates both causally and temporally to others, potentially at other real or conceptual levels. This dynamic description can be referred to as ‘theory’. The kind of theory we aim to develop is mostly a theory of the descriptive/integrative type, rather than the predictive type such as that one encounters in, say, physics. Depending on the scope of the data that were fed into the Grounded Theory analytic procedures, and the types of analyses possible, we may use such theories as a source for constructing evaluation frameworks for an emerging informational artifact.

## Grounded theories for contextual evaluation

Patton (1987) proposed one way in which Grounded Theory procedures might be used for theoretically-oriented evaluation. His suggestion is that one way of applying qualitative research methods in evaluation (for example, for a social intervention programme of some kind) is to use Grounded Theory procedures to produce a (inductively formulated) theory of how a particular program functions in a real situation within a naturalistic mode of inquiry. In this way, a programme’s staff can ‘reality test their own theories of programmatic action, program effects, and the relationship between action and effects’ (Patton, 1987, p 40). To this proposal we add the simple extension of formally constructing a similar theory that describes the vision of the developers, or clients, of the system as to how a particular information system should work and fit within its environment. The two theories: developer/client theory, and the actual programme theory, are compared to identify discrepancies and problem areas.

The source of the developer/client theory may be the requirements document (if such a document exists), project inception and definition meetings between clients and developers, or another suitable starting point that describes the way in which a particular information system may benefit the host organisation.

This is one way in which an evaluation of a system increment or prototype may be conducted, namely the comparison of two theoretical formulations, where both are developed using Grounded Theory procedures. Alternatively, we may employ a different strategy in which the context (as multi-layered as it may be) of a proposed information system is investigated to determine relevant risks and constraints. The established risks and constraints are used for evaluating a system increment, or to operationalise a riskdriven process model such as the Spiral. In the remainder of this paper, we explain how a framework based on Grounded Theory procedures is used to establish the risks and constraints necessary for operationalising non-sequential process models.

## Grounded theories for the determination of risks and constraints

Determining risks that need to be resolved during software or system development is what drives the Spiral model, whose proposer (Boehm, 1986) labels as a ‘riskdriven’ model. The initial phase of the spiral is that of determining alternatives and risks with reference to the development step that follows. The second phase is that of developing and applying techniques for resolving the risk(s) identified in the earlier phase. This is followed by a development phase, and finally by the evaluation and planning phase for the following iteration of the spiral. The Spiral model is very much a contingent approach to development, as far as life cycle models are concerned (Davis, 1982); as opposed to the particular modelling tools and techniques with their ‘system content’ modelling orientation. The contingency is upon the risks identified, which affect the choice of techniques for the resolution of such risks in the following phases.

In the evolutionary delivery model (Gilb, 1988), small deliveries of a system are delivered as early as possible to the users, for actual use in live situations, and evaluated. The outcome of the evaluation is used to modify an open, global system architecture. Here also, evaluation plays a fundamental role in operationalising the process model. However, the determination of aspects (or ‘concepts’) or criteria for the system to be evaluated against is left to the interviewing and investigative skills of the developer. We view the determination of constraints that the system needs to satisfy as an important part of the process of constructing needed evaluation frameworks.

It is evident that successful (although not necessarily complete) determination of risks and constraints needs to start from the context that is due to host a putative information system. Such context is primarily characterised through qualitative data, even if some of the qualitative categories are later quantified in some form as, for example, suggested by Gilb (1988). Our approach utilises a suitable variant of Grounded Theory procedures to describe a phenomenon of interest, which in our case concerns a ‘need’ for a technical intervention that has information at its centre. The description is a theoretical formulation, which we refer to as ‘need theory’ for the particular level at which the informational need has been recognised. We then move to do the same for other levels that are identified to be constitutive of the wider context of a needed intervention. This way, we obtain a succession of ‘need theories’ at a number of contextual levels, and inspect these to establish which categories may represent risks, or indeed constraints, on the informational artifact in question. ## A suggested approach

We have found the paradigmatic perspective, qualitative orientation and methodological rigour of Grounded Theory particularly suitable to the resolution of a number of issues in engineering information systems. We have devised a framework, the Grounded Systems Engineering Methodology (GSEM) to support the information systems engineer in establishing risks and constraints relevant to the evaluation and specification of the artifact; in specifying the functional and non-functional (or quality) attributes of the artifact; and in making early decisions about the architecture of the artifact. In all three uses, qualitative data were systematically analysed to render formulations that can be directly used in all three areas of decisions. In this paper, we only focus on the use of Grounded Theory to aid the evaluation of the informational artifact, or part of it, to support contingent process models. Other aspects of GSEM are the subject of other publications (eg Galal & Paul, 1999).

In the framework that we suggest, available data are analysed to render formulations from which risks and constraints operating on the artifact can be extracted. The steps that comprise the framework are outlined below:

## Step 1: Initial (open) coding of data

This step involves ‘trawling’ candidate concepts from all available data. Initially, a broadıbrush approach is adopted, whereby any label or phrase that appears to be related to or describing the need or fit of an informational artifact is recorded. The initial list may be quite long. However, such a list is successively refined and consolidated until all concepts are directly relevant to the informational need, or an artifact that might satisfy it. Much of the Grounded Theory literature (eg Glaser 1978; Strauss & Corbin, 1990; 1998) suggest several techniques for carrying out such consolidation and refinement.

## Step 2: Develop a need theory for each level

The concepts pertaining to individual levels of analysis (such as Domain, Organisation and Environment) are systematically related to each other via the Paradigm Model as suggested by Strauss and Corbin (1990), later simply termed ‘Paradigm’ (Strauss & Corbin, 1998). This model essentially provides a conceptual vehicle, through which a theory is developed, that articulates how concepts are causally or contextually related to each other. The formulation of a ‘need’ theory at a given level of analysis is achieved when a narrative (or ‘story line’) that describes the need situation is developed. Such a narrative should encompass the concepts related to each other via the paradigm model, and should preserve the relationships implied by the same model.

## Step 3: Develop evaluative scenarios

Having elaborated the concepts and conditions present at each level of analysis in the previous two steps, the systems engineer turns to describing how such conditions interact dynamically to impose constraints, or to make demands on each other. This dynamic description is achieved by tracing the conditions described earlier, through the analysis levels. Concepts are traced from the outermost level of analysis to the innermost level of analysis. This tracing sequence essentially follows the dynamic propagation of needs and constraints from the outer environment through to the domain level. This process of tracing concepts, identified through analysis, aids the discovery, of which of these concepts act as risks or constraints on the artifact, which is to be hosted by the domain. Once an initial statement of the artifact has been constructed (similar to scope statements in traditional systems analysis and design), the same process of tracing may be conducted in the opposite direction (from solution to environment) to check that the artifact satisfies the constraints identified.

## Step 4: Establish risks and constraints (based on scenarios obtained from step 3 above)

Each level of analysis is inspected to determine risks and constraints related to the informational artifact. The inspection is conducted on the inductively derived ‘need’ theories at each level, to select the concept categories that may detract from the utility of the artifact, or constrain it in some way. From the initial coding stages (open and axial), all concepts will be described beyond mere labels. Such descriptions will encompass each concept’s properties, and some postulation about how it relates to concepts occurring at the same level. The outcome of this step is central to this mode of applying Grounded Theory concepts to IS engineering. Namely: to inductively determine risks, constraints and structural conditions that are in some way relevant to the informational artifact in question.

## An application

We have applied our framework, which is one of the parts comprising the more comprehensive Grounded Systems Engineering Methodology (GSEM) that we developed, to the development and deployment of an experimental document management system for a law firm. This exercise was part of a European Community ESPRIT programme-funded project. The qualitative data analysed were collected through interviews, observations made during training and assisting users in using an early delivery of the document management system in question (a total of 18 such visits and 10 interviews), as well as conveying feedback to the system’s developers. The interviews were mainly unstructured or semi-structured to allow the interviewees freedom to comment on any factors they regarded as relevant. These interviews and visits extended over a period of approximately nine months. Six more months were taken to analyse and present data to the systems’ developers and clients, and obtain their feedback. The researcher dedicated three days a week for such activities. There were numerous delays caused by the client’s inability to dedicate time to work on the system or liaise with the researcher. We briefly describe below how each step of our framework was carried out.

## Step 1: Initial (open) coding of data

Qualitative data were collected and analysed following the sub-steps explained below:

1.1. All interview transcripts and observational field notes were read line-by-line. All candidate concepts of potential interest were highlighted. This activity was conducted in two passes to reassure the researcher that no, or as few as possible, seemingly relevant concepts were missed during this initial process.

1.2. All highlighted concepts were noted on $5 ^ { \prime \prime } \times 8 ^ { \prime \prime }$ record cards. These were labeled ‘ICn’ for ‘Incident Card’ such that n is the card’s serial number, creating a unique identification for each card. The cards were further labeled with the section identification referencing the part of the notes to which each card relates (so each card had two identifications, only one of which was unique).

On each incident card, each individual concept entry and the incident that gave rise to it, was crossreferenced to the specific location in the main body of field notes or interview transcripts, to enable tracing back to the ‘raw’ data. Thus, such crossreferences pointed to a paragraph or a sentence in the field data where some relevant incident or a quote is identified. A total of 231 candidate concepts were initially ‘trawled’ from the raw data.

1.3. To aid the process of consolidating concepts, they were transferred from incident cards onto a single consolidated list. As concepts were transferred, redundant concepts were eliminated and similar concepts were combined into one. Related but separate concepts were kept; since they were due to be further arranged into concept categories and their sub-categories later. This process resulted in the reduction of the initial incident entries (or concepts) produced in the earlier step, from 231 to 35. All cross-referenced to field data were copied onto the consolidated list. This is very important for the traceability of final formulations to the raw, primary, data.

1.4. A further refinement and consolidation step was undertaken. In this iteration, concepts were arranged into tables. Each table described one concept through listing a number of its properties, the ranges that the values of such properties can take, and where possible, a value for each property was indicated. Some properties were found to be subconcepts that were elaborated in separate tables. Table 2 is one such table. This step led to a significant re-arranging of concepts and their properties. Some properties were superfluous vis-a\`-vis the concept under which they first appeared, others were deemed trivial and removed altogether, yet others were found to belong more appropriately to categories or concepts other than the ones under which they were originally listed. In total, 18 tables resulted from the above analysis.

## Step 2: Develop a need theory for each level

The next step was to relate the obtained concepts to formulate theories about the informational need situation, one theory for each of the three levels of Domain, Organisation and Environment. Before building a theory for any one level, the concepts were fitted into a ‘Paradigm Model’, that causally relates them to each other, as a preamble to theory writing to ease initiating the process and iterating as necessary. The analyses culminated in a ‘need theory’ at each level, expressing features of each that may be related to the informational need and the nature of practice at the client organisation. We present the three theories below, but we show the concepts as we fitted them to the ‘Paradigm Model’ for the ‘Domain’ level only.

## An evaluation of GSEM and its results

We made a live presentation of the results of this work: concept category descriptions, and various types of scenarios, using the very simple representation of $\bar { 3 } ^ { \prime \prime } \times 5 ^ { \prime \prime }$ record cards to record concepts and their properties, to evaluate our findings. The Conditional Matrix was drawn on large flip-chart sheets of paper, and labeled as System, Domain, Organisation and Environment. These sheets were laid down on large tables so that all present can see and participate in modifying the models. The presentation involved explaining the major concept categories used and how they related across the levels of analysis (Figure 1) to represent scenarios.

We found that the contextual, system-bound, formulations that we obtained above were highly rated by the system’s stakeholders for the way they helped them to further understand and conceptualise the relevance of the system to their organisation. Comments made by the stakeholders also indicated that the formulations presented enabled them to understand the implications of the proposed intervention (the document management system) as regards the significance and magnitude of the organisational changes necessary for deploying the proposed system. We translate this to real benefits of our approach in helping to manage the incremental process of re-engineering the enterprise to take advantage of new information technology, that is likely (as is often the case) to have far-reaching impacts on other aspects of the organisation. Although all categories of stakeholders (clients, users and their managers) understood and supported the organisational changes proposed, it was not possible to test the efficacy for these changes after implementation. This is because another law firm took over the original client organisation, thus disrupting the earlier programme of document management technology deployment. However, the intention was not to provide a perfect or finalised set of evaluations for the (partial) intervention, but to provide the method needed for deriving them, as well as the conceptual background against which they can be juxtaposed and interpreted in a rigorous way.

A somewhat surprising observation was the way in which relatively IT-innocent stakeholders readily assimilated the analyses presented, the evidence being the ease with which they amended some of the concepts presented to them, in terms of property values and location on the model. This provided much support to the interpretation of the analysis and evaluation results. The evaluative scenarios we presented were read out as causal relationships between concept cards that were placed at the System, Domain, Organisation and Environment levels of analysis. This in turn helps stimulate stakeholders’ thinking about further risks and constraints related to the intervention in question.

The chief system’s architect also commented that the field evaluation and other analysis results correlated significantly with his perception of the system, and that the majority of facilities found to be lacking in the study were scheduled for addition to the system. The system’s architect commented that areas of risks uncovered in the analysis ‘would have been useful in setting appropriate priorities and targets during development’. As we explain above, a list of risks and constraints was a product of applying our approach. These produced valuable insights relating to issues that were of concern at some point during the engineering of the technical system.

From the wider system design point of view, the systematic analysis of data about the increment in use enabled us to design ‘environmental accommodations’ that were defensible on the basis of the data collected. These included particular procedures aimed at overcoming system utilisation problems, such as primary document coding and the quality assurance of document coding.

From the point of view of the analysis process itself, the Grounded Theory-based approach helped us in organising and analysing a very large volume of diffuse, qualitative, data. For example, the 231 concepts initially trawled were systematically and comprehensively condensed into 35 concepts and their properties. These were used to derive formulations that we presented and used above. Moreover, by virtue of the way theoretical formulations were reached, a remarkable degree of traceability was achieved. Every single ‘concept’ in the theories expressing requirements, as well as the scenarios was traceable all the way back to the original data and through all the intermediate analyses.

The limitations of the approach were that the style of analysis mandated by Grounded Theory was very time consuming indeed. Also, the quality of this type of analysis critically relied on the analyst’s skills and experience. The reader is directed to Glaser (1978) for a discussion of the techniques that can be used to develop the requisite skills for Grounded Theory analysis, and to Strauss and Corbin (1998) for a discussion on the types of skills required of a Grounded Theory researcher.

## Conclusions

We would like to emphasise here that the fundamental philosophical paradigm that underpins our methodological framework is interpretive. The interpretive paradigm takes the position that knowledge is socially constructed, and is thus essentially subjective and valueladen. (Note here that although Denzin (1994) quotes the Grounded Theory perspective as the most widely used qualitative interpretive framework in social science today, others, such as Roman (1992) have pointed out to its affinity with positivism. Denzin also notes that: ‘[as] one peels back the layers of discourse embedded in any of the numerous qualitative guides to interpretation and theory construction, the core features of [Grounded Theory] are present, even when [the method is] not directly named’. We thus choose to adhere to the interpretive interpretation of Grounded Theory and apply it in this spirit. See also the chapter by Charmaz (2000) for a discussion of this issue.) The interpretive direction, of GSEM, is commensurate with the relatively recent surge of interest in the contextual issues in software development; such as that implied by the application of ethnography to requirements engineering (Jirotka, 1993; Sommerville et al, 1993). However, our work goes beyond giving generally ‘contextual’ or ‘illuminating’ descriptions, to offer better grounded and much better integrated theoretical formulations. We have also applied our framework, GSEM, to the areas of requirements engineering and the architectural design of the information system; further publications are planned in these areas.

We must point out that the formulations that we present above, and the conclusions that we draw from them, are by no means final. These conclusions have been accepted and recognised by the actors in the domain, but their perception is inherently dynamic and is subject to change. Nevertheless, they serve as a useful starting point, and an organisational framework for later discussions and negotiations, thus enhancing the dialectic concerning the artifact and its context, by making it better grounded in the available data.

We have argued in this paper that the operationalisation of contingent process models relies fundamentally on an analysis of contextual factors to inform further engineering decisions. Such analysis typically aims at establishing risks, constraints, and to inform the construction of evaluation frameworks. We argued that since context for complex information systems is predominantly characterised in qualitative terms, methods that are competent at the systematic handling of qualitative data need to be deployed. We have shown the way in which our framework, the Grounded Systems Engineering Methodology (GSEM), may be effective in making process models more sensitive to the context, in a rigorous and grounded way. The Grounded Theory method and its procedures formed the core of our analytical framework, and thus contributed its robustness and the underlying philosophical paradigm.

GSEM was used to build ‘need theories’ at various levels of analysis, which we used to construct evaluative scenarios; inspecting these theories led us to a statement of risks and constraints concerning the informational artifact being developed. The results were recognised and agreed by the system owners and architects. They are thus capable of serving as a platform for further discussion, rather than final conclusions. As such, the GSEM framework was effective in ‘situating’ some of the salient contingent software and systems development process models.
