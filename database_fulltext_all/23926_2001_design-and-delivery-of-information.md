# Design and delivery of information

J Korn

Visiting Fellow, London School of Economics, Houghton Street, WC2A 2AE, London, UK; E-mail: janos999Kbtinternet.com

The notion of property from a linguistic point of view, the constituent parts of change of state and product as a means to execute the change, are described. Information plus medium is regarded as a product. Based on previously given definition, the structure of information is introduced together with units, meaning and precision as measures of its effectiveness in changing mental states. The design of a bundle of information as part of a problem solving scheme is described. The method of linguistic modelling is introduced for modelling delivery of information and medium from a sender to receiver. Uncertainties associated with construction and delivery of information are taken into consideration through a certainty theory. European Journal of Information Systems (2001) 10, 41–54.

## Introduction

An early reference to the notion of ‘information’ may be traced to Maxwell through his ‘demon’ which managed to separate high and low speed molecules into separate compartments, thus raising and lowering the temperature of each compartment (Jeans, 1921). This phenomenon had been a puzzle to classical scientists as change of temperature had been achieved without explicit expenditure of work. Currently we can see the activity of the demon as part of the operation of a purposive system equipped with external power supplies and with regions in which energy and information circulate. The regions are connected by an amplifier (Korn, 1995). Scientists identified information as ‘negative entropy’ in the context of thermodynamics which is mathematically related through a logarithmic function to reduction of uncertainty (Szilard, 1929; Brillouin, 1956; Skyttner, 1996). Quantitative aspects of information were developed in connection with transmission of signals (Nyquist, 1924; Hartley, 1928; Shannon, 1964). Its qualitative aspect was considered later (Bar-Hillel, 1964; Devlin, 1991). Information theory still appears to be a debated and evolving subject (Winder, 1997).

Information systems have been in existence ever since living things started to communicate. Information systems are used by humans, animals and plants at the micro and macroscopic level and by the vast variety of their organisations. Information is prepared and applied according to specifications. Thus, it is created in the context of a purposive system including hardware type control for inducing a change of state, mental or physical, directly or indirectly, in much the same way as energy, or an artefact, is utilised. However, information systems, as the term is used today, have been recognised only recently with the development and extensive use of personal computers in organisations and through their effect on the work of managers (Avison & Fitzgerald, 1995; Flynn, 1998). An information system is regarded as a means by which organisations and people utilising information technologies, gather, process, store, use and disseminate information (Anon, 1999). The nature and principles of the operation of information systems are still a subject of discussion (Anon, 1995).

## Motivation for current work

Conventional science has proved to be unable to cope with ‘modern problems’ which involve purposive activity by: (1) machines requiring a multidisciplinary approach; or (2) living/human entities and their countless organisations, both involving the notion of complexity. A cleavage first occurred perhaps with the appearance of control systems and their theoretical treatment of transfer function analysis (Brown & Campbell, 1948) which is well outside the subject matter of conventional physics. Conventional thermodynamics cannot cope with thermal transients although they are an integral part of the operation of any engineering/control system (Korn, 1981; 1987). Approaches to human activity situations are fragmented, have insignificant roots in recognised branches of knowledge, and are unable to handle dynamics. Information and design are not considered a part of a more comprehensive theory (Korn, 2000b). Current work against this background has been going on in the technical and human activity fields so as to remedy these perceived deficiencies (Korn, 1995; 1998).

## Problems in information systems

Conventional science, in particular physics, is divided into disciplines. The content of each discipline is described by a precisely defined finite number of quantifiable properties. A methodical description of engineering/control systems which are multidisciplinary, involves a classification of a finite number of quantifiable properties into ‘through and across’ (Korn, 1981; 1987; 1995). The term ‘situation’ refers to a chosen part of the world in which we perceive objects, entities or agents in dynamic interaction (Barwise & Perry, 1983). When a situation involves human activities, however, it can only be described in terms of a selection of predominantly qualitative properties out of an infinite possible (Korn, 2000b). This is so since a living, especially human, entity can vary its properties at will; they emerge in a situation or as its role changes from one situation to another. Inanimate entities by themselves are unable to act in this manner. We say that such situations in general are not repeatable. Moreover, situations are dynamic and engaged in the production of outcomes either by chance or in accordance with a purpose. Conventional science is poor in dynamical theories and can handle only specific aspects of a living/human activity situation.

Early investigators in systems science were faced with the inadequacy of conventional science to be used in purposive, dynamic situations even at the technical level and rejected it entirely (Checkland, 1981). With hindsight, this was a mistake, since aspects of scientific method such as the search for underlying generalities, the notion of experimentation, can be used (Korn, 2000b). New methods for viewing human activity situations began to appear leading to covertly problem solving methods (Checkland, 1981). Since a situation can be seen as ‘interacting objects’ an abundance of diagrams reflecting this view has appeared (Anon, 1997). Recent developments in information systems have embraced these approaches (Avison & Fitzgerald, 1995; Flynn, 1998; Atkinson, 2000).

These approaches have no sufficient basis in recognised branches of knowledge, and do not get at what may be considered the fundamentals of human activity situations. They are vague with ill-defined terms, thus, difficult to apply to specific situations. The motivation behind current work is to attempt to produce a more comprehensive theory of situations, in particular those with living/human entities, hopefully, with less of the problems just mentioned. The concept of information and modelling information systems are an integral part of this theory.

Problems associated with current approaches to information and information systems may be summarised as follows:

(1) There is no agreed description of a concept of information incorporating both meaning and quantity within the framework of a theory.

(2) There is a diversity of topics of information systems such as methodologies which are freely discussed without reference to an adequately described concept of information fundamental to these topics.

(3) Information and information systems are not related to the consideration of energy and energetic systems.

(4) Current consideration of information and information systems seems to be restricted to a narrow context of people, organisations and computer technology. Consideration of wider aspects of communication as it takes place in biology and in technical control may be desirable.

## Features of an alternative approach

A theory in physics considers physical objects which are directly identifiable. Depending on the discipline, a finite set of properties is then selected and used usually through mathematics to produce a set of integrated statements regarding specific aspects of these objects. Thus, we have mechanics, electricity and so on. When we deal with human activity situations the aspects of objects are much more numerous, they change and can be described by a selection from an infinite number of properties. Because of the diversity and variety of human activity situations, concepts considered fundamental and pervasive throughout the domain are difficult to find and difficult to relate to physical objects carrying properties contingent on a situation. For this reason there have been so many somewhat superficial approaches to human activity situations. Any theory of human activity situations is to cater for qualitative as well as quantitative properties and to retain the notion of object as a label to carry a selected set of properties. Mathematics cannot do this but processed natural language can.

Closer attention to observation of the manner and means of communication by living and technical things and their groupings may lead to empirical concepts which would enable a more comprehensive treatment of situations in which either or both energetic and information systems, play a part (Korn, 1996a; 2000a; b). This is essential since the energetic part of a situation plays the part of executing a plan embodied in the ‘informatic’ part as, for example, in a technical control system or in any everyday activity like slicing bread. The concepts of property and theoretical objects are introduced. These objects are seen to interact in terms of physical power or influence with ideally zero power carrying specific forms of energy and information respectively. To cater for treatment of situations with predominantly qualitative properties, a symbolism based on processed natural language and aspects of predicate logic called linguistic modelling has been developed. This leads to an ‘inference mechanism’ for the propagation of state in time and computation of uncertainties of occurrence of outcomes (Korn, 1999a; b). The propagation is liable to uncertainties due to frailties, mood changes, likes/dislikes and other forms of caprices associated with living, especially human, agents and to fluctuations of properties in general (Korn, 1996a; 1998). Information is defined in the context of linguistic modelling and its effectiveness is related to the number of qualifiers, their meaning and precision and to the number of information bearing sentences (Korn, 2000a; c). A bundle of information is specifically directed at specific changes of mental state expressed in terms of properties, it has to be considered from a design point of view and acts as a product (Korn, 1996b). A particular bundle of information needs to be delivered to a receiver. A system of delivery can be viewed as interacting objects and modelled by means of linguistic modelling. The objective of this paper is to introduce further developments based on and arising from the ideas just outlined towards alleviating the problems described above.

## Property, change of state, product and information

An achievement of conventional science has been the introduction of the notion of ‘property’ which can be used to create relations, preferably quantitative, for describing a phenomenon. We propose to follow a similar path in developing an approach to systems science involving predominantly qualitative properties but, unlike mathematics, also retaining the object to which the property refers. We have given a description of concepts considered to be pervasive throughout the immense variety and number of manifestation of ‘systems’ to form an empirical basis (Korn, 2000b). We intend to look again at the idea of property as used in linguistic modelling (Korn, 1996a; 2000a; b) and its consequences.

## Notion of property

At a fundamental level, human and perhaps other animate beings, appear to be able to perceive through their sense organs, chosen parts of the world, permanent or changing, not so much in detail as in their entirety. In many cases, they are capable of recognising and of reacting appropriately to such impressions. We call ’property’ the basic unit of perception which enables us to examine a part in more detail and to qualify it (Korn, 1996a; 2000a; b). This concept is used in physics for the description of state and defined as a perfect differential (Rogers & Mayhew, 1963). We admit a wider use as allowed by natural language but in much the same sense (Korn et al, 1993).

We classify properties into:

(1) Concrete: geometric (including spatial), material, numerical and energetic (Korn,1995,1996a) which are observable either through the senses or instruments as a result of small amounts of physical power created by light reflection, sound etc.

(2) Abstract: these are deduced from concrete properties (Korn, 1998; 2000a). For example, from the geometric and material properties of the skin on the face of a person we can conclude whether he/she is old, young, happy and so on. The introduction of abstract properties increases the expressive power of language by avoiding the need for repeated recital of conjunctions of concrete properties.

No concrete property can exist on its own. A material property is bounded by geometry and geometry is ‘filled’ with material. Numbers are attached to material objects which are sufficiently similar and energetic properties occur in pairs (Korn, 1995). Accordingly, properties enter into relations to form a vast variety of theoretical objects, concrete and abstract. Such an object is described by conjunctions of declarative statements, sets of which emerge as an object changes its role in a situation or changes from one situation to another (Korn, 2000b).

Properties which can be considered unchanged over a given period of time are described as ‘stative’, otherwise they are referred to as ‘dynamic’ or variable. A stative property may be described in the context of a stative verb phrase. For example, in ‘The level of liquid in the vessel is or stands at 2 m’. Change of a stative property attached to an object is described by a dynamic verb. In ‘The hoof on one of the rear legs of the horse changed its position rapidly and broke the door’, the change may be referred to as ‘kick’ with ‘position’ attached to the noun ‘hoof’. Or we can simply say ‘The horse kicked the door which broke’. All verbs are abstract.

Concrete properties are usually quantifiable; numbers can be assigned to them. Abstract properties are usually not. In linguistic terms, a frequently recurring property is labelled ‘adjective’ otherwise it is called an ‘adjectival phrase’ or ‘clause’ (Quirk & Greenbaum, 1973). Properties are used to qualify the noun constituents of a subjectpredicate form (Korn, 2000a). We extend the use of the term ‘qualifier’ to ‘adverbs’ and ‘adverbial phrases’ for qualification or modification of verbs.

The subject-predicate form as a linguistic symbol has been invented as a means to express a view or to make a comment about a chosen part of the world (Korn, 2000a). This form which without qualifiers is called ‘context-free sentence’, can be ‘stative’ or ‘dynamic’ as determined by the appropriate verb. In such a sentence each noun and the verb are seen to represent spaces of all objects, actions or states denoted by that noun or verb. Qualifiers are used to locate a noun or a verb in its space. Qualifiers themselves can be qualified so as to make the location more precise (Korn, 2000c). For example, the common noun ‘table’ represents the space of all objects which are sufficiently alike for the symbol ‘table’ to be assigned to them. When we add the qualifiers: ‘dining’, ‘with four legs’, ‘made of oak’ we are trying to locate a ‘table’ in the space of all objects called ‘table’.

## Change of state, environment and product

Any part of the world can be described as a conjunction of an infinite number of statements of the subject-predicate form carrying adjectives and adverbs as qualifiers. In practice, however, a few are usually sufficient to effect recognition of the chosen part which is seen as bounded by the relevant parts of the rest of the world called environmental objects.

When we select one property for a projected change of a changing object we have a projected change of state. The rest of the properties of the object involved in change and of the environmental objects are assumed to remain quiescent. When the property to be changed is abstract, its change can be accomplished: indirectly through changing concrete properties which are contained in the abstract property such as ‘wrinkled’, or directly in case of mental properties such as ‘happy’.

Before and after change, there is an initial (IS) and a final (FS) state. The change may take place by chance or in accordance with a purpose (Korn, 2000b). IS denotes a description/specification of a property which is in some way regarded as unsatisfactory or needing change. FS means a description/specification of a property which is desired or envisaged. The two properties are consistent in the sense that there is an operation which can convert an IS into an FS. For instance, there is an operation which can turn a ‘white’ sheet of paper into ‘blue’ but not into ‘warm’.

No change can take place by itself. Interaction in terms of physical power carrying the appropriate energy or of influence with theoretically zero power carrying the appropriate information is required. An interaction symbolised as a dynamic verb, is capable of accomplishing a change described by consistent adjectives.

Product is a set of properties delivered by an appropriate interaction to a changing object to bring about a corresponding change of state. To form a product, properties enter into relations:

(1) Stative artefacts: These are purpose-built objects consisting of concrete, stative properties. A ‘reciprocating knife’, a ‘lawn mower with a rotating blade or a ‘flat stone’ for covering a part of pavement, satisfy this criterion of product.

(2) Energy: Energy is defined in physics and chemistry in terms of energetic properties. For example, the energy in a compressed or extended spring may be described in terms of its material and stative, energetic (displacement) properties.

(3) Information plus primary medium: Information has been defined in linguistic modelling as ‘A contextfree, simple sentence with qualified constituents attached, as a subordinate clause, to a verb which designates influence interaction (Korn, 1998; 2000a; c). ‘Data’ are the numerical part of a qualifier used for achieving precision and to further qualify a qualifier without which data by themselves carry no information.

For example, in the sentence ‘The fireman shouted to the other that the corrosive liquid in the vessel stands at a level of 2 m’, ‘fireman’ is the sender, ‘other’ is the receiver and the verb ‘shouted’ designates influence interaction. The context-free sentence in the subordinate clause is ‘Liquid stands’. ‘Liquid’ is qualified by ‘corrosive’, an adjective, and the verb is qualified by adverbial phrases ‘in the vessel’ and ‘at a level’ where the latter is further qualified by data ‘2 m’.

We have introduced the notions of ‘meaning’, ‘message carrier’ and ‘medium’ as the constituents of information (Korn, 1998; 2000c). ‘Medium’ refers to the physical realisation of information in terms of concrete properties called ‘informatic’. We have two categories of medium: that which carries symbols such as natural language called ‘primary’ and the other which can be constructed of any object intended to convey a message such as works of art, a hand gesture or other forms of body language, called ‘secondary’. Any object or its change, an event, can serve as secondary medium. Information plus medium constitutes product intended to change the mental state of a receiver.

## Relation between properties

We have identified a changing object, its environmental objects and a product which operate as a part of a purposive situation depicted as a diagram in Figure 1 (Korn, 1996b). Such a situation has to be designed as it is unlikely to occur by chance.

Skilled power is defined as power carrying appropriate energy in accordance with information when driving a stative artefact as part of purposive scheme. Mechanical power required to place the rudder of a ship in a specific position in accordance with information from the helmsman, is such power. Mutually interacting objects (MIO) deliver skilled power to a product so as to execute the specified change of state.

With reference to Figure 1 at the start of a design process, only the changing and environmental objects exist, the situation is incomplete. To complete it, the product and MIO have to be designed.

Having specified an initial and final state, a change of a property invokes other properties which appear to be relevant to change. These must be discovered and then specified, omitting any or choosing an inappropriate one, can lead to inadequate design. Thus, we have:

(1) Change-related properties (CP)—these refer to the manner or form of the changing property from IS to FS, often expressed as the speed of change and symbolised as a dynamic verb.

(2) Object-related properties (OP)—these are properties of a changing object itself which emerge following a changing property. They are seen as relevant to this property. In case of change in a mental property they are a part of decision making regarding changing property.

(3) Environment-related properties (EP)—these are properties of environmental objects which are adjacent to the changing object and can affect CP as EP1 and OP as EP2 and vice versa.

CP are intended to generate requirements for dynamic performance of a product subject to EP1 usually expressed in terms of energetic properties to be delivered by MIO. OP and EP2 provide requirements for the appropriate stative properties of a product.

OP and EP2 are quiescent properties. For example, a dangerous (OP) material (changing object) when moved (changing property) quickly (CP) can damage a sensitive (EP2) surrounding. Quiescent properties of changing and environmental objects can have wide ranging effect on the design of a product. For example, the product for transporting (changing property) people (changing object) by air is affected by the sensitive nature of human body (OP) and by the low temperature and lack of oxygen at high altitudes (EP2).

## Structure of information

Usually we have no difficulty in recognising an artefact as a product, energy is defined in physics and skilled power is a part of a purposive system. The task is to define the concept of information which makes it suitable to function as a product. We have analysed information into a context-free sentence with qualified constituents. This can be represented as a diagram in Figure 2. A noun or noun phrase constituent, concrete or abstract, may be seen as a space of all objects denoted by that noun. A verb constituent represents the space of all actions or states for a dynamic or stative verb. The function of qualifiers is to locate an object, action or state within a space with increasing precision in direction of the arrows. This is done by qualifying the qualifiers towards completing the diagram ending with data which impart certainty to information within limits of tolerances.

A qualifier refers to a single feature or property of an object or another qualifier, and as such is defined as the unit of information. A qualifier has meaning in the sense that it can be used to connect a noun, a symbol of a category of objects, to experience with various degrees of precision. The number of context-free sentences attached to a verb designating influence is defined as the number of information referred to as a bundle of information.

We consider an example to show how a diagram is constructed: ‘The waiter notified the guest that the chef who was distracted at the time, 11.00 hours, irretrievably burnt the very tasty chicken soup’. The verb ‘notified’ designates influence interaction, ‘waiter’ is the sender and ‘guest’ is the receiver. The clause after the connective ‘that’ is information: ‘Chef burnt the soup’, a context-free, two-place sentence. Information as a diagram is shown in Figure 3.

Information is used for changing the mental state of a receiver. Its effectiveness with reference to this task can be related to units, meaning, precision and number of information all of which can be varied to affect effectiveness. In relation to Figure 3:

## Effectiveness of product

The effectiveness of a product is judged by its ability to bring about a change of state of a changing object from IS to FS. There is a relation between the relevant properties of a changing object, CP and OP and environmental object, EP1,2 and those of a product. The relation is expressed through the notion of requirements generated by CP, OP and EP1,2 directed at the product. The product must then be equipped with the appropriate properties which should enable it to accomplish change.

(1) Changes of properties of inanimate objects called users, although governed by chance, are also subject to the changing object/product relation. The relation is seen as causal, a matter of nature. For example, we expect the change of a volcano (changing object) from being ‘dormant’ to ‘erupting’ to be necessarily connected to molten lava through CP, OP and EP1,2.

(2) In the case of changes of properties of objects of hardware technology also called users, the relation is again seen as causal, a matter of technology. For example, we expect the ‘voltage across an electrical capacitance (changing object)’ to change necessarily from zero to another value as a result of its terminals having been switched across a suitable voltage source. The source is intended to produce electrical energy with properties which should match CP, OP and EP1,2.

(3) In case of animate objects called receivers the relation is driven by decision based on information. For example, ‘a man buying a shirt’ depends on his appreciating relevant properties of a shirt which should match his mental CP, OP and EP1,2 regarding ‘shirts’. His change of mental state from ‘being indifferent’ to ‘wanting to buy’ depends on a decision.

However, an actual ‘eruption’, ‘voltage change’ or ‘man actually buying’ depends on how close particular properties of a product approximate those required and related to CP, OP and EP1,2. This is the measure of effectiveness of a product. In the light of a gap between particular properties and requirements animate beings take a decision.

In all three cases, we can say that:

## Design and delivery of information

The physical state of a user or the mental state of a receiver is to change as a result of application of a product. With reference to Figure 1, the final state of a changing object is either given having noted an unsatisfactory initial state or postulated as a result of envisaging an as-yet-non-existent state thrown up by inspiration, a flash of imagination or insight, a matter of creativity. We have seen that the manner of change (CP), relevant properties (OP) of a changing object and EP1,2 are related to those of MIO and of product by matching through requirements. Using an example we show how linguistic modelling (LM) can be applied to model delivery of information. The stages of LM are depicted in Figure 4.

LM begins with a story or narrative describing a situation followed by linguistic analysis with two assumptions (Korn, 2000b):

(1) Any complex linguistic pattern can be converted into a combination of simple sentences with a single stative or dynamic verb respectively;

(2) In the course of linguistic analysis the meaning of the original text, story or narrative remains invariant to an acceptable degree.

The function of linguistic analysis is:

(1) To reduce linguistic complexities into combinations of simple sentences called ‘basic constituents’.

(2) To identify within each simple sentence the nouns and a verb which form a context-free sentence together with the noun initiating activity and those which are affected.

(3) To identify the qualifiers, or properties, of nouns and verb in a basic constituent. Without qualifiers a sentence of nouns, verb and functional words although meaningful, is not anchored in the space of meaning. Qualifiers make a sentence situation-dependent (Quirk & Greenbaum, 1973; Korn, 2000a).

(4) To identify those verbs which represent power/ energy and special verbs for influence/information interaction. The latter type of verbs may be divided into groups expressing: perception (see, hear, sense, %), mental processes or storage (think, believe, remember, %), transmission (describe, say, indicate, shout, announce, detect, %) and commitment (offer, request, assess, %), all capable of carrying information.

(5) To identify sentences which contribute to the structure of a situation, or scenario, ie those used for modelling, and those with information ie subordinate clauses attached to special verbs.

Linguistic analysis has made explicit the dynamic verbs which attract one or two nouns in context-free sentences, together with their qualifiers, the basic constituents. We assume that ‘a dynamic verb refers to causation’. This assumption implies that:

(1) The interaction represented by a dynamic verb is causally related to the relevant, contingent properties of an object initiating interaction: driving (dp) and enabling, facilitating/hindering (ip) properties.

(2) The change of a property of an affected object or change of state leading to an ‘acquired property’ (ap) driven by the interaction, is causally related to the interaction (in) itself and to enabling, facilitating/hindering (ep) properties of the object.

(3) A property once acquired (ap) can function as a driving property or can support the change of property of another object ie act as ‘ep’, in the course of propagation of changes of state as a scenario unfolds.

In addition we introduce the notion of calculating property (cp) with the function of comparison, decision or calculating as required in a situation with purposive action.

These implications lead to expressing a one- or twoplace basic constituent as a pair of logical conditionals. For instance, the sentence ‘As part of his duty with care about the job (dp) and with good eye sight (ip), the postman sorts (in) according to code (adverbial phrase) properly addressed (ep) letters’ can be reformulated into:

‘If (it is part of his duty and with care about the job) and (he has good eye sight) then the postman sorts letters (according to code)’.

‘If the postman sorts letters (according to code) and the letters are (properly addressed) then the letters become sorted (ap)’.

The result of the ‘postman’s’ action in the example above is a change of physical property of the ‘letters’, referred to as outcome. Exercising his skilled power, the ‘postman’ converted letters from unsorted into sorted.

## Design of information

We consider the story: ‘A mortgage company (m/c) was prepared to examine all debts by people who approached them in writing, who appeared to be unable to pay and whose circumstances made recovery of a debt difficult. However, the m/c expected an acceptable cash contribution.

John found himself in debt to the (m/c). He wanted to take steps to eliminate the debt and notified the m/c that he was a lazy kind of man not averse to a drink, had no permanent job, his income was just enough to sustain him and had an easygoing life style. He knew people who could help him financially up to £6000.

John noted that a letter with required properties was to be constructed. He was intelligent and able to write letters. So he put white paper on the table and wrote the letter inclusive of information. When he finished, he placed the paper in an envelope and posted it to the mortgage company hoping for a sympathetic consideration.

From the story the theoretical terms introduced earlier and currently are:

## Changing object—m/c

Changing property—m/c is related to John through his debt

IS—m/c is owed money by John

FS—m/c is not owed money by John (decision dependent)

CP—time needed for John’s application to be processed by a set time

OP—when asked in writing, the m/c is prepared to examine all debts by people who appear to be unable to pay and whose circumstances make recovery of debt difficult. The m/c expects an acceptable cash offer by the debtor.

## Overall application perspective

We regard information plus medium as a product defined in the context of linguistic modelling directed at changing mental states as part of a purposive scheme indicated in Figure 1. This view brings information plus product in line with artefacts and energy. The purpose of making explicit the structure of information is to enable us to develop a measure of effectiveness of communication and to see clearly the factors to improve this effectiveness. In particular, the purpose of modelling of information may be summarised as:

(1) To suggest a concept of information in the context of a theory.

(2) To unravel the structure of information.

(3) To introduce a measure of effectiveness of information in changing mental state.

(4) To show how to improve effectiveness by altering units, meaning, precision and number of information.

(5) To show how delivery of information through a medium can be modelled.

All this is carried out by the information system designer. In the application example both information and medium and the manner of delivery are described in order to demonstrate the method. The example of John versus m/c itself is based on a case that actually happened. Decision making is connected to how information is perceived.

## Conclusions

The current work is concerned with the development of a more comprehensive approach to modelling situations including their dynamical aspects, using more concrete rather than predominantly metaphysical, vague and abstract terms. This is possible by introducing the notion of property and asserting features which are regarded as pervasive and fundamental throughout the immense variety of manifestations of situations or ‘systems phenomena’. Such a feature is the combined notion of change of state and generalised product.

We have introduced a definition of information which leads to development of the notion of its effectiveness as far as changing mental state is concerned. A bundle of information is designed with the aid of a problem solving scheme in Figure 1 which is an iterative process. Delivery of information to a receiver together with primary medium is modelled by linguistic modelling. This makes explicit the outcome of a situation and computes its possibility subject to uncertainties due to living/human components.

In the introduction we stated four problems seen to exist currently in studies of information systems. To alleviate these problems respectively:

(1) We have put forward a definition of information residing in linguistics and containing meaning and quantity for a measure of effectiveness to be constructed.

(2) We suggest that perhaps the new definition can provide a common theme in the diversity of topics such as computer hard/software, human interfaces and information systems in organisations.

(3) We have treated information plus medium as a product which together with the general notion of change of state enables a unified approach to the dynamics of situations involving flows of energy and information. This is desirable since in purposive situations with skilled power there are regions in which energy and information circulate at the same time.

(4) It is likely that at some level information and information systems are used by all living things and control/computer systems in much the same way. A more ‘physics based’ approach through basic considerations and a symbolism leading to an inference mechanism, can help to produce the required more general view.

The human intellect is incessantly engaged in imposing a view or belief on parts of the world ranging from everyday gossiping to far reaching theoretical constructions. Perhaps the most widely used means of realising the imposition is natural language. Even theoretical constructions use language as a starting point. When we speak of effectiveness of a model we are interested in how a model is able to translate a view expressed possibly in natural language into theoretical constructs and how such constructs can be used.

We apply this idea to linguistic modelling which consists of general, empirical statements which define very general concepts like property, object, interaction and a symbolism of processed natural language. The latter leads to the use of formal logic with features of objects and interactions such as uncertainties in constructing an inferential mechanism. LM uses syntax and semantics in formulating rules for transforming a natural language description of a situation into its symbolism. The symbolism can then be manipulated so as to produce quantitative predictions regarding outcomes of a situation subject to limitations of restricted repeatability imposed by living/human entities. For instance, the application example shows three choices of an outcome depending on the variation of certain features of its constituent objects and information reaching the final object, the m/c.

Any part of the world can be seen as a collection of interacting objects. Current systems science has produced a variety of methodologies, and of diagrams of interconnected ‘blobs’ such as process, data flow, state transition and others. These diagrams with the exception of object-oriented modelling, represent only topology, not the characteristics/properties of objects. No diagram has a theoretical basis, can take dynamics into account; most have poorly defined concepts and none is based in existing branches of knowledge. LM appears to be able to remedy such deficiencies. However, it has not been tested on more realistic problems and lacks the appropriate software needed for applications to larger problems.

Decision making takes place in the framework of the scheme in Figure 1 when alternatives in formulating requirements and products need to be considered. The formalism in Figure 6 includes decisions through calculating properties (cp) and the feedback paths designated ‘checked by’. This ensures that a requirement has been satisfactorily implemented before proceeding to the next. The dynamics of a situation involving variations of time are taken into account by the dotted lines leading from an object to its acquired property (ap). The symbolism can accommodate other forms of decision making and mathematical calculations on which further propagation of state may depend.

Human beings use information in a vast variety of ways to achieve changes in mental states in individuals and their groups: oblique information, lies, biased information, timing and so on. These have not been considered. We need to look into the logical basis of the relations in Equation 1. The units of information in Figures 3 and 5 have been given equal weighting in their contribution to the meaning of a sentence. This is a question which needs further investigation.
