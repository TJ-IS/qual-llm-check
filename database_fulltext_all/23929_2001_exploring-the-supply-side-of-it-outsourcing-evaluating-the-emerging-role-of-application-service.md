# Exploring the supply-side of IT outsourcing: evaluating the emerging role of application service providers

WL Currie\* and P Seltsikas

Centre for Strategic Information Systems, Department of Information Systems and Computing, Brunel University, Uxbridge, Middlesex UB8 3PH, UK

The paper explores the supply-side of IT outsourcing by focusing upon the emerging role of application service providers (ASPs). It traces the transition from first wave IT outsourcing in the 1960s, to businesscentric outsourcing in the 1980s and 90s to application outsourcing in the 21st century. Drawing from empirical research on a taxonomy of ASPs, the paper explores how application outsourcing is currently changing the nature of the outsourcing contract and relationship between client and supplier. This will have significant implications for traditional outsourcing and ERP vendors in the software and computing services industry, in addition to presenting new opportunities to SMEs with little history or experience of outsourcing. European Journal of Information Systems (2001) 10, 123–134.

## Introduction

The paper explores the supply-side of IT outsourcing by focusing upon the emerging role of application service providers (ASPs). ASPs are third party service firms which deploy, manage and remotely host software applications through centrally-located services in a rental or lease agreement. During the last 2 years, the ASP phenomenon has grown considerably, with many established and start-up firms developing their ASP strategies. Forecasts for the growth of the ASP industry vary, with Ovum (Ring, 2000) predicting it will be \$25 bn and Dataquest, \$22.7 bn, by 2003 (ASP Industry Consortium, 2000). ASPs will have a significant impact on outsourcing policies and practices if the business model successfully penetrates the under-exploited SME sector.

The paper is divided into three parts. First, it explores the three waves of IT outsourcing beginning with first wave technology-centric IT outsourcing in the 1960s, to business-centric outsourcing in the 1980s and 90s, to application or industry-centric outsourcing in the 21st century. Each wave has witnessed the further growth and diffusion of IT outsourcing practice. Industry-centric IT outsourcing will result in accelerated growth, particularly if ASPs penetrate the SME sector with applications on an affordable, pay-as-you-go (rental) basis. This is because the scope and scale of ASP offerings will be large, comprising of enterprise, horizontal and vertical offerings, including web-enabled ERP systems, functionally-focused or business applications, and vertically-focused offerings to health care and financial services, among others.

Second, the supply-side of IT outsourcing is considered by exploring the changing role of ERP vendors and their strategies to develop an ASP business. ERP vendors are important since they have moved from a situation of high profitability and growth in the early 1990s to poor financial results where they have needed to develop new strategies for competing in the software industry (Bender-Samuel, 1999). One such strategy is to enter the ASP market by web-enabling their software for the SME sector. Third, the paper examines the emerging role of ASPs, in particular by focusing upon the partnering arrangements which are essential to the success of this business model. The paper presents fieldwork conducted by the authors in Silicon Valley, US and in the UK during 2000 into 28 companies offering specific application and service offerings. Drawing from empirical research which develops a taxonomy of ASPs, the paper discusses how application outsourcing may change the nature of the outsourcing contract, in particular, the relationship between client and supplier. This will have significant implications for traditional outsourcing and ERP vendors, independent software vendors (ISVs) and technology providers in the software and computing services industry, in addition to presenting new opportunities to SMEs with little history or experience of outsourcing. The paper argues that for the ASP business model to be successful, ASPs will need to develop strong partnerships and strategic alliances with companies ranging from telcos, ISVs and hardware manufacturers. This will significantly alter the outsourcing relationship from a one-to-one (vendor and customer) scenario to a oneto-many (ASP, channel partners and customers). ASPs will also need to develop effective strategies for educating the customer marketplace, particularly the SME sector. The ASP industry, however, will significantly change the shape of the supply side of IT outsourcing as competition will become intense between established companies and new start-ups.

## From first wave IT outsourcing to third wave application outsourcing

In the last 30 years, IT outsourcing has undergone many changes from the service bureau model beginning in the early 1960s to application outsourcing in the 21st century. During the 1960s, many companies signed outsourcing contracts with a service bureau firm. The fashionable term outsourcing was rarely used, as the more narrow facilities management contracts involved mainframes, datacentres and bespoke software. This first wave IT outsourcing tended to be the domain of the large company, with few affordable services available to the SME. Outsourcing tended to be technology-centric, with few additional services (consultancy, training, systems integration, etc) available to the customer. The service bureau model was moderately successful although there were many technical, communications and financial problems which precluded it from being a viable option for many companies.

The 1980s and 1990s continued to witness a growth and maturing of the IT outsourcing market (Saunders et al, 1997). The literature was largely concerned with the client-side, with the generation of case studies outlining the benefits and pitfalls of specific sourcing strategies from total outsourcing, selective sourcing to insourcing (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993). During this period senior executives sought ways to leverage outsourcing to control and shape IT costs in conjunction with changing business requirements (Strassmann, 1995). Cost savings were often at the root of many outsourcing deals (Takac, 1994).

Conversely, many Chief Information Officers (CIOs) and IT directors resisted outsourcing initiatives which they believed were against their interests. Many IT service providers won their initial outsourcing contracts by positioning themselves as the lowest bidders. Their rivals were not only other external IT service providers but also large in-house IT departments. Yet numerous inhouse IT departments found themselves outsourced. Throughout this period, the global IT outsourcing phenomenon expanded for a number of complex political, economic and technical reasons (Venkatraman, 1997). Client server technology operating in a distributed computing environment created many new opportunities for vendors (Mitchell & Fitzgerald, 1998).

This second wave of IT outsourcing was business-centric, and there were many debates about transferring responsibility for IT from technical staff to general or line managers (Adler et al, 1992). This period witnessed the growth of enterprise outsourcing where, along with mainframe and data centre outsourcing, companies were outsourcing additional IT work from systems development, testing and maintenance, to business functions (accounting, HR and procurement) and business processes (supply chain management, help desk and call centre services). This era opened up many new opportunities for independent software vendors (ISVs) to the big five management consultancy firms. During this time, companies moved away from bespoke software to purchasing shrink-wrapped software. This off-the-shelf approach was considered by management to be more cost effective than doing high-risk software development in-house. The 1990s also saw the growth in ERP systems, although implementation times could be as long as 2 years in some cases. ERP systems are configurable information packages that integrate information and information-based processes within and across functional areas in an organisation (Kumar & Van Hillegersberg, 2000).

Although there were many examples of unsuccessful, large-scale outsourcing deals during the 1980s and 1990s (Klepper, 1995), outsourcing increased as vendors offered their services world-wide. But as outsourcing continued to gain momentum, there were concerns that SMEs, with low IT budgets, were unable to reap the benefits afforded to their larger counterparts (McLellan et al, 1995). Indeed ERP systems were offered to customers with large IT budgets, who would purchase several modules on a company-wide scale (Holland & Light, 1999).

This situation has changed in recent years with the development of the Internet and all its possibilities for the SME to acquire software and support at an affordable price. This suggests that IT outsourcing has now entered a third wave, which is industry-centric. In this era, outsourcing will continue to undergo a significant shift from the centralised computing of the 1960s and 1970s, the distributed computing of the 1980s and 1990s, through to the remote computing in the 21st century. ASPs will play a central role since they will increasingly offer a utility model to customers where they will purchase applications on a pay-as-you-go or rental basis. This will be a one-to-many model, where an application will be offered to numerous customers across different sites. This is different from traditional outsourcing, which was characterized by a one-to-one approach, where applications were customised, often using the services of a systems integrator.

An important debate surrounding the ASP phenomenon is the extent to which application outsourcing is different from traditional outsourcing. The evidence suggests that subtle differences are identifiable, and this may have significant implications for negotiating contracts and service level agreements (SLAs) in the new industry-centric application outsourcing environment. Table 1 gives a breakdown of traditional and application outsourcing. Traditional outsourcing is depicted by the customer having a one-to-one relationship with the supplier. In this model, applications were owned by the customer and they were managed and maintained by the outsourcing supplier. Traditional outsourcing tended to be the domain of the large company as the set-up and maintenance costs were expensive. Outsourcing suppliers were disinterested in SME companies as they did not have the resources to enter into expensive outsourcing deals lasting 3 years or more. Neither did they have the funds to invest in staff and technology to build up their own IT departments.

Unlike traditional outsourcing, the ASP business model is targeted at the SME sector for two reasons. First, the emerging technologies coupled with economies of skills (rather than scale) make the ASP model a viable and affordable option for SMEs. SMEs using an ASP will not need to invest in hiring expensive IT staff, equipment and consultancy. The ASP will provide a point solution direct to the SME. Second, ERP vendors and others (ISVs, technology consultancies, telecoms companies, etc) see opportunities for generating new business by developing partnerships with ASP and other key players.

Initial research into the ASP industry shows that it is currently vendor-driven with few reference sites available to potential new customers to enable them to compare and contrast different ASP offerings. Notwithstanding this point, the ASP business model is justified on the grounds that it may create a situation of reverse economics as traditional outsourcing gives way to application outsourcing. Previously, ERP vendors, like the large IT services companies (EDS, CSC and Accenture) did not target SMEs, since their IT budgets and information systems requirements were too modest in scale and scope to be profitable. But the ASP business model is expected to change this by allowing SMEs to choose between a range of offerings from ERP systems, vertical (industryspecific) solutions, to horizontal (business-focused) solutions, from a variety of ASPs.

## Research study

Against a background of the changes in IT outsourcing in the last four decades, a research study was developed to compare and contrast traditional outsourcing practices with the recent phenomenon of application outsourcing via ASPs. Whilst much of the previous academic research has focused upon the client-side of IT outsourcing, with particular emphasis upon identifying success and failure scenarios through case study analysis, this research study was designed to explore the supply-side of IT outsourcing using ASPs for two reasons. First, the supply-side of IT outsourcing remains under-researched in the academic literature. Yet a deeper knowledge of client strategies and outcomes of IT outsourcing may only be gained through an understanding of how IT vendors, together with their channel partners, develop and define their IT outsourcing business. Second, the emerging ASP industry, currently vendor-driven with few customers adopting ASP solutions, is a phenomenon which may have significant implications for global IT outsourcing policy and practice, particularly as webenabled or remote outsourcing contracts may be more difficult to manage and control.

Initial survey research carried out by the authors found that out of 250 potential SME customers, 70% claimed they had not even heard of the term ASP. Only 6% claimed to use the services of an ASP, with 52% claiming they did not even outsource any of their IT work. However, 70% said they believed companies would rent rather than buy software in the future, and 57% said outsourcing had increased in their firm over the last 3 years. This small survey found that ASPs would need to target resources at educating the SME customer, as little knowledge or understanding of outsourcing and ASP offerings was apparent in this sample of SME respondents. Using the results of the pilot study into 250 UK

SMEs, a research study was developed to investigate the supply-side of IT outsourcing using a cross-national case study approach. Research funding has been obtained by the European Union and EPSRC.

## Research methodology

The research study is an empirical investigation into software and computing companies offering ASP services in the US and Europe. An inductive-descriptive approach is used with fieldwork continuing over a 3-year period, beginning in July 1999. The first phase of the fieldwork was conducted in Silicon Valley, California and in the United Kingdom. A case study approach was used to elicit responses from key personnel responsible for developing an ASP strategy, rather than to test or validate a specific theory. Thus, ‘complex problems differ in all sorts of ways. By concentrating on the kinds of problems that arise in case studies, a more specific and therefore useful approach is possible’ (Easton, 1992, p 12). Exploratory research prior to the fieldwork being carried out suggested that the emerging ASP industry was complex, comprising of many established and startup companies, all attempting to develop an ASP business. Case studies were therefore the most appropriate method of capturing rich and meaningful data from a wide range of ASP scenarios.

A semi-structured questionnaire was used to elicit data and information on the three key themes outlined in Table 2. The interviewees in most cases were founding members, CEOs and senior managers responsible for developing ASP strategies and services within their own organisations. Job titles of interviewees have included: CEO, CIO, COO, director of technology, business development director/manager, chief operating officer, VP, European and Asian operations, marketing director, product marketing manager, technology strategist. Interviews lasted between 1 and 3 hours and were tape-recorded. An ASP taxonomy was developed as it became apparent that the scale and scope of ASP offerings was wide in terms of market opportunity, company size, applications requirements, complexity of solution, integration and security issues. However, even the sub-division of the ASP industry into a taxonomy (enterprise, horizontal, vertical, pureplay and enabler) was merely an exercise in classification rather than one designed to pinpoint similarities or dissimilarities both between and within different types. Rather, the benefit of using the ASP taxonomy was to recognize the inherent complexity of the ASP model, with each type of ASP pursuing strategic alliances, channel partnering and market opportunities relevant to its own position within the ASP industry.

The evaluation of different ASP business models was divided into four broad categories of delivery, integration, management and operations and enablement which are used by the ASP Industry Consortium in their ASPire Awards program. The performance criteria may vary across different ASP types, with data security being a major priority where ERP applications are delivered by enterprise ASPs, and time-to-market the major priority where email and calendaring applications are delivered to start-ups by pure-play ASPs.

## Case study sample

Twenty-eight companies were selected for interview. The choice of these companies was based upon two important criteria. First, initial exploratory work on the ASP industry found there was a plethora of ASP offerings in the marketplace. These ranged from companies offering collaboration tools, infrastructure support to enterprise solutions, among others. Whilst some of these companies were traditional ISVs who were developing an ASP strategy to compete within this marketplace, others were new entrants seeking to establish a specific enterprise, horizontal or vertical offerings in the ASP space. For example, enterprise applications were offered by J.D. esourcing,† which was a subsidiary of J.D. Edwards. J.D. esourcing had targeted eight vertical sectors to offer web-enabled ERP software, but was also using channel partners, such as Aristasoft (a vertical ASP). Horizontal ASPs, such as Wyzdom.com in the US and E.Carisma.com in the UK, tended to offer a business solution to customers. In both cases, these ASPs offered fixed asset management solutions, among others, and were each seeking additional venture capital (VC) funds. The advantage of the horizontal ASP was breadth rather than depth since their web-enabled applications were relevant to most companies, thus giving them ample market opportunities without having to acquire vertical (industry-specific) knowledge of their customers’ business sector. Vertical ASP, Aristasoft, perceived a market opportunity in the US high-tech equipment manufacturing and healthcare sectors, respectively. Aristasoft partners with J.D. Edwards (see above), Clarify and Agile to host an integrated portfolio of enterprise applications in addition to offering industryspecific business process expertise.

Pure-play ASPs offer web-enabled solutions only. This is unlike enterprise ASPs who may offer their solutions over the internet or a virtual private network (VPN). In the UK, Netstore claims to be ‘Europe’s leading ASP’. Netstore was set up in 1996 and is a ‘special partner’ to Microsoft. Netstore offers a range of products and services to its key customers: Cisco, Avon Cosmetics Ltd and the Opus Group. These range from hosting Microsoft Exchange; on-line back-up, secure PC Refresh to weekly management reports and web hosting consultancy. Aspective is another UK-based pureplay ASP. This company was only formed in 1999 and describes itself as a ‘Wireless ASP’. This is in recognition that, with the growth and development of mobile phones, ASPs will need to offer applications remotely.

The final role that is critical to the ASP model is the enabler. These companies may provide telecommunications, hardware, or internet connectivity and colocation services, etc. ASP enablers have the advantage of partnering with a range of existing (enterprise, vertical, horizontal and pure-play) ASPs and those seeking to become ASPs (ISVs) to offer a range of web-enabled applications. Cable & Wireless plc has moved away from its traditional telecommunications background to become a major provider of ASP offerings. In a recent deal with Microsoft and Compaq, the company has set up a-Services to offer Microsoft Exchange and Office applications. It is currently exploring opportunities with

ISVs and others to offer additional applications. The taxonomy of ASPs used is more fully described in another paper by the authors (Currie & Seltsikas, 2001).

Second, companies were selected according to their ability to reach the customer market. One of the initial observations from conducting a full-scale search of ASPs was that many companies claiming to be an ASP did not have any specific or definable application or service offering. Some web-sites, for example, had list companies claiming to be ASPs, although many of them were simply fledgling companies staffed with only two or three founding members. It was therefore important to acquire some key background information about the company in terms of its commercial history, size, market strategy, application and service offerings, and existing and potential customerbase (if any). As a result, companies were selected on the basis that they had more than ten members of staff, could demonstrate they had a customer market, and a distinctive application and service offering.

## Exploring the supply-side of IT outsourcing

Reflecting on the growth in IT outsourcing over the last four decades shows that three major forces have been driving change in the software and computer services industry. They are globalisation, deregulation and consolidation (Holway, 1998). These forces have generated the growth and demand for IT outsourcing, systems consulting and integration services. The global IT outsourcing market is predicted to grow to \$303 bn by 2002 (Forrester Research, 2000). Companies like IBM, EDS and CSC, to name only a few, have extended their global reach by entering into a variety of strategic arrangements with other suppliers, largely for the purpose of serving their clients’ growing demands for products and services. There has been an increase in serving clients in vertical markets worldwide, ranging from aerospace and automotive to chemical oil and gas, financial services, utilities and, more recently, healthcare. These global suppliers now have a significant presence and scale in such markets through securing large outsourcing contracts and entering into joint ventures and by making strategic acquisitions. This trend was set in place from the late 1980s (Henderson, 1990).

Large IT service providers have been very instrumental in deciding their strategies for making inroads into vertical markets. For example, EDS’s positioning within the global defence industry, as witnessed by its recent outsourcing contract with the US government totaling \$6.9 bn to renew the IT systems of the US Navy and Marine Corps. The US Navy is estimated to spend about \$1.6 bn a year operating its 360 000 desktop computers and 200 separate computer networks. The new system is projected to cost \$1.4 bn a year to operate and maintain. Such a move enables EDS to develop core competencies relevant in defence contracts not least to serve their clients, but equally to help define and shape the future of the defence industry (Currie & Willcocks, 1998a,b). Deregulation in the financial services industry has also fuelled outsourcing, particularly where companies have turned to external IT service providers to tackle competition from potential new entrants to this market. Consolidations, mergers and acquisitions have enabled these new entrants to gain access to markets and customers in ways which were not possible prior to privatisation and deregulation.

## The changing strategies of ERP vendors

A brief analysis of the IT services market shows it has changed considerably over the last 30 years. From 1970 to 1987 the IT services market consisted of three distinct types of IT service provider. They were hardware suppliers; systems houses supplying and developing software; and IT consultancies. From the late 1980s, many new entrants entered the outsourcing marketplace from the traditional IT service providers to other sources. The reasons for this are varied. First, legislation passed in the UK and US to contract-out white collar public services led to increased outsourcing as some in-house IT departments were transferred to an external vendor. For example, in the UK, central and local government were required by law to market test their public services through a process known as compulsory competitive tendering (CCT) (Currie, 1996). This was seen as an opportunity by many IT service providers to reinvent themselves as specialists in offering strategic outsourcing services from IT consultancy through to applications management and development (Quinn & Hilmer, 1994).

Second, economic recession and increased global competition led many companies to seek cost cutting measures (Takac, 1994). This, in turn, led to increased outsourcing of IT and other business services (eg accountancy, HRM and marketing, etc). The IT service providers responded by moving from offering stand alone services, such as mainframe, data centre and applications management to IT consultancy (eg IT strategy formulation), business process outsourcing (BPO), ERP and customer relationship management (CRM), systems integration and, more recently, ASP offerings through partnerships with other companies.

The 1990s saw a change in the financial fortunes of the five largest ERP vendors, following concerns that it would not be possible for them to sustain their previously high growth rates. Given that more than 60 per cent of Fortune 1000 companies had already implemented core ERP applications, future market opportunities for these firms appeared to be receding. The ERP market saw sales rising from \$2.1 billion in 1993 to \$16.6 billion in 1998, a compound annual growth rate of 51.2%, with five companies dominating the market which accounted for nearly two thirds of total sales. Following 1998, the JBOPS group of companies (which include J.D. Edwards, Baan, Oracle, Peoplesoft and SAP), began revising their business strategies in search of new opportunities. They perceived the fertile area for business development was the under-exploited SME market. The large ERP application vendors, because of perceived low profit margins, had tended to ignore this market. Previously, expensive ERP systems were outside the financial scope for most SMEs, and smaller suppliers were unable to fill this gap because of the high development costs and ongoing maintenance and support requirements (Hirt & Swanson, 2000).

More recently, the ERP application vendor market has changed substantially, with all the JBOPS companies setting up ASP offerings: J.D. Edwards—Global ASP solutions (formally J.D.esourcing); Baan—E-Enterprise; Peoplesoft—eCenter; Oracle.com (formally Oracle)— BusinessOnline Service; and SAP—MySAP.com. The objective of this strategy is to offer tier one ERP applications to both large and medium size companies. Notwithstanding the above market changes, ERP applications remain expensive for SMEs given the large capital investment to support this type of infrastructure. Serious IT skill shortages persist and this factor drives up the cost of implementing ERP applications as well as initiating other IT-enabled change. Midsize companies (with annual sales of \$20 million to \$500 million) will need to invest heavily to benefit from an ERP system. One example is that \$300 K may be needed for infrastructure, \$300– 400 K for software, and \$1 million on implementation. Many midsize companies are reluctant to make such an investment (Bender-Samuel, 1999).

An interview conducted with the Director of J.D. Edward’s Global ASP Solutions suggested that the company was investing large sums of money to penetrate the SME sector by offering a ERP applications in key areas including health care, manufacturing, procurement, trading community support, supply chain management, knowledge management and interoperability tools. ASP solutions currently has over 20 partners specialising in a variety of industry sectors worldwide. The J.D. Edwards ASP solutions partner channel currently supports over 100 customers. Thus,

Our partner network provides J.D. Edwards software to our customers in a hosted environment with the quality and level of expertise our customers expect, delivering the power of collaborative commerce through our uniquely configurable enterprise solutions.

In order to change the mindset of the mid-size company, the ASP business model offers these firms new opportunities to procure and use software offered by the large vendors. Coupled with this, large and mid-size companies are increasingly evaluating the benefits from applications systems and software. The rapid pace of technical change, marked by frequent upgrades in systems and software, encourages companies to favour a different pricing mechanism. Responding to this change, the new business model is the hosting of these highly sophisticated, expensive and complex applications on a remote computer environment that is Web-enabled. This technology is affordable to the mid-size company via the services of an ASP, but possibly outside the reach of the small company (1–50 employees).

Over time it is predicted that software will not be sold directly to customers as a package, but be licensed through the services of an ASP. Thus, ‘In contrast to traditional outsourcing, the ASP model involves renting access to core business applications over the Internet or some other network—not simply handing over operational control of your existing data centre’ (Lauchlan, 2000, p 29).

The offerings of ASPs varied according to which vertical or horizontal markets they cultivated. Mature vertical markets were found in banking and financial services, and to a lesser extent in healthcare and government. With the deregulation of the utilities, such as telecoms and energy, ASPs were targeting all these sectors. Similarly, the types of software applications offered to customers by ASPs were web-site hosting, payroll/billing, e-mail and e-commerce applications. The market for CRM, ERP and HR applications was beginning to develop, although ASPs were aware of the important role of educating the SME marketplace. One CEO from an ASP offering a horizontal (email, calendaring, etc) application said,

At first we thought SMEs would rush to acquire our services because we can offer them applications at a very competitive price. The trouble is, most SMEs do not keep up to date about the latest technology and so we need to spend a lot of time in educating the market. This is not an easy task, but we have decided to spend less time at vendor-led conferences and more time trying to reach the SME market.

Findings from the questionnaire survey and field research suggested that the ASP market would develop slower than some of the following market forecasts predict.

## Market forecasts

According to a recent IDC survey, the enterprise ASP market will reach \$2 bn by 2003. Another survey suggests that the UK and Germany are the most promising European countries for ASPs to enter in the next 18 months, as 66% expect to be purchasing application services for websites, e-commerce and email from ASPs by 2002. The report claims that the industry sectors most attracted to the ASP model in western Europe are banking and financial services, transport and utilities, and travel and tourism (Ring, 2000).

As the global IT outsourcing market matures, companies in the software and computing services industry are repositioning themselves to develop and enter new markets. The well established IT consultancies and service providers have entered into ‘mega’ outsourcing deals worth many \$millions (Currie, 2000). Financial predictions about the growth of the ASP industry vary. Ovum forecasts a market growth to \$25 bn by 2003. This includes all ASP-related contracts, with 68% of this figure relating to North America. IDC and Forrester Research, unlike Ovum, exclude desktop applications such as email and word processing applications. The 1999 market was \$296 m according to IDC and \$438 m according to Forrester Research. IDC forecasts the market will almost double year on year, growing at a 98% CAGR to \$4507 m by 2003, whereas Forrester claims a 79% CAGR to \$4519 m for the same year. IDC claims that the management of complex, high-end enterprise applications will account for about \$2 bn or 45% of the total (\$4507 m) market. Enterprise applications are those which include ERP, e-commerce and CRM. The western European ASP market was estimated to be \$14 million at the end of 1999 and \$100 million by the end of 2000, growing to \$340 million by 2001. IDC believes that the western European enterprise ASP market is growing at a CAGR of 156% for the period 1997–2003. The US is the lead market with significant spending in 1999, though Europe is expected to grow significantly over the next 5 years (IDC, 2000).

## Evaluating the emerging role of ASPs

Whilst ASPs largely procure and implement complex systems for their customers, some ASPs provide a comprehensive alternative to building and managing internal information technology operations. Like traditional outsourcing contracts, the customer may use an ASP because they seek to control their IT costs through entering a scheduled payment scheme agreed with an ASP. But unlike traditional outsourcing, the application service is managed at a central location and not at the customer site. In essence, the core business of ASPs is to offer an applications-centric service to their customers. But for the model to work, the ASP will need to achieve economies of scale by offering a one-to-many service rather than the one-to-one relationship found in traditional outsourcing. An interview with one ASP offering a health care application offered some interesting insights into pricing strategies.

The key challenge facing our company is how to price our applications. One theory is that we should offer the software for free and bill the customer on a subscription basis. Another is to adopt a usage-based model, similar to how we pay for the telephone or electricity. Our customers are happy to rent our application, but another problem is we usually have a differential billing system—the large customers tend to pay more for the application than the smaller ones—which is based upon ability to pay rather than how much the application is used. We need to get the pricing model right if we are to make a profit and compete at the same time.

A further challenge to developing an ASP business was establishing viable partnerships. To this end, the ASP had to reconcile two competing forces. First, the ASP intended to own the customer relationship through establishing a direct relationship with the customer. The ease with which ASPs could achieve this depended on the types of applications and services they offered to their customers. For example, an ASP which offered many applications from different ISVs tended to have a direct relationship with the customer. Second, ASPs which partnered with ISVs which also offered their applications through their own ASP tended to create channel conflict. The result of this was often confusion in the marketplace as the customer needed to decide whether to use the services of a specialist ASP or go direct to the ISV.

Field research conducted in the US and UK found that the ASP ecosystem was a complex and dynamic phenomenon that incorporated many different types of company within the software and computer services industry. Many of these companies were seeking a major role within the ASP sector, with some actively developing ASP offerings from their existing businesses, whereas others were start-up ASPs, or companies seeking an enabling role within the ASP industry as IT services companies, co-locators, ASP aggregators, network service providers, and security specialists. Other key players in the ASP ecosystem were ISVs, systems integrators, data center equipment manufacturers, telecommunications companies, Internet Service Providers (ISPs) (access and connectivity) and other support firms (security, application customisation, software support, and management tools). The key roles adopted by these firms are as follows:-

Independent Software Vendors (ISVs): Traditional software vendors include the large Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) vendors such as the top five JBOPS companies: J.D.Edwards, Baan (now Invensys), Oracle, Peoplesoft and SAP. The Customer-Relationship Management (CRM) company, Siebel Systems is another leading software vendor. In addition to the enterprisewide software vendors, many other horizontal, vertical and niche players are looking towards the ASP business model with a view to offering their software over the Internet or via a virtual private network (VPN). With the growth of the ASP industry, the traditional ISVs have been presented with both an opportunity and a threat. The ASP space offers ISVs (large and small) to offer their software to new customers, notably the SME sector, via the services of an ASP. In recent months, many ISVs have considered becoming ASPs, although they recognise that delivering and integrating their software is a different business than developing software. The result has been that many ISVs have partnered with an ASP. This is witnessed in the ERP sector (see above) where many partnerships and alliances have emerged between vendors and specialist ASPs. The downside of the emerging ASP industry for ISVs is the threat of new competition from specialist or niche market ASPs, many of whom may develop software capabilities to compete with the large ISVs. Many ISVs are now concerned that they must seek ways to web-enable their software if they are to remain competitive. Field research found that whilst some ISVs are reluctant to invest in web-enabling their software, their customers are interested in the rental model of procuring software as opposed to purchasing it as a product. This may force ISVs to move to an ASP model, particularly if rival companies offer customers a more attractive value proposition.

Software Infrastructure Partners: Enabling the ASP solution is a key role, particularly as many ASPs do not own their own data centers. The range of companies in this category is wide. It includes companies which enable an ISV to become an ASP such as Digex; companies which offer co-location services like AboveNet, Digital Island and Exodus, thus enabling ASPs to colocate their servers; and companies that enable their customers to improve their computing environments to embrace the ASP business model, such as Centerbeam. ASPs will need to carefully select their channel partners as part of their delivery model will need to address important issues of data security, reliability and 999.99 service availability.

Software and Complementary Technology Partners: Hardware manufacturers and networking companies are a major element of the ASP ecosystem. Data centre equipment manufacturers (storage, servers, etc) like IBM, Compaq, Sun and HP. The deal between Cable & Wireless, Microsoft and Compaq is an example of how one of the largest suppliers of computing systems (Compaq) can benefit from the ASP market. Compaq NonStop eBusiness solutions comprise the building blocks, the network system architectures and the service professionals, to enable customers to build, deploy and support their e-business initiatives. This enables customers to benefit from a 24 × 7 × 365 service through faster deployment, reduced risk and higher availability. Compaq’s role in the a-Services deal is to provide iPAQ Internet PCs along with the internet access provided by Cable & Wireless and applications from Microsoft.

The relatively new concept of ASP is creating some confusion in the market. While ASP companies were more prolific in the US, there was still a long way to go before SMEs would fully embrace the ASP value proposition. An interview with a CIO at a leading technology provider claimed that,

Here in Silicon Valley, everybody is talking about ASPs. However, the dot.com disasters and poor value proposition of some ASPs is making some midsize firms cautious about adopting an ASP solution. In my view, ASPs will change the entire shape of the software industry, but most ASPs will not survive. The customer will need to be very careful in selecting the right ASP as a vendor. They will also need to evaluate the partnerships of the ASP.

One important area for evaluating ASP partnerships was in security and data integrity. Some ASPs had won business in spite of not having adequate security policies and procedures in place. This was a major concern to many other ASPs who felt that, as the ASP was an emerging market, disaster stories of ASPs exposing customer data, possibly to competitors on shared software and hardware platforms, would give the industry a bad name. The findings from the research study showed that, whilst the ASP industry is likely to expand as companies refine their offerings, application outsourcing poses fresh challenges to ASPs, their partners and customers.

## Conclusions

This paper has considered the supply-side of IT outsourcing in the context of the emerging role of ASPs. It argues that IT outsourcing is moving into a third wave, which is industry-centric. This is demonstrated by a shift from a traditional one-to-one outsourcing relationship between client and supplier to a one-to-many relationship where an ASP provides an outsourcing solution to several clients remotely over the Internet or a virtual private network. Findings from research presented here suggests that the jury is still out in terms of whether the ASP business model will prove to be successful by penetrating the under-exploited SME market.

As a value proposition, the ASP solution offers new opportunities for SMEs to procure vanilla-ERP systems and other vertical and horizontal applications from ASPs. The impact on IT outsourcing policy and practice will be significant, as traditional contracts and service level agreements (SLAs) may need to be revised taking into account the complex relationships between ASPs and their partners. Whilst the customer will be able to choose from a range of ASP offerings in the marketplace, they will need to understand and evaluate the various partnerships and alliances of potential ASP vendors. To this end, the ASP business model, while offering the SME an alternative in the form of renting, as opposed to buying applications, poses risks which are different from traditional outsourcing relationships. Such risks involve the customer acquiring mission critical software applications from ASPs who themselves may outsource the majority of their non core business. The customer will therefore need to evaluate the ASP in terms of the potential risks associated with multiple partnerships and outsourcing relationships. This is a different scenario from traditional outsourcing, whereby risk assessment tended to be focused upon the relationship between customer and supplier.

In conclusion, it would appear that, as the ASP market continues to develop, many ASPs will develop their market strategies by aggregating their service offerings. Simple horizontal or vertical solutions may not be enough to sustain an ASP’s competitive position, despite initial first mover advantages. Whilst it is not possible for reasons of confidentiality to convey individual business strategies of the ASPs interviewed in this research, suffice it to say that some of the larger or more established ASPs were seeking to enhance their competitive position through strategic alliances and joint ventures. The role of the ASP enabler was becoming an attractive proposition to some ASPs, as they recognized that ‘owning the customer’ was perhaps not as important as providing cost effective and reliable applications to customers, possibly through channel partners. In addition, some ASPs saw advantages in providing enabling services to other ASPs, some of whom could be perceived as competitors. Trends of this nature provide some evidence that the ASP industry, like others in the software and computing services industry, moves towards consolidation. So as this research work continues to follow the fortunes of the ASPs in question, it is likely that the next 2 years will see a further shake-out of ASPs, as some cease to operate, whilst others are absorbed by larger players.
