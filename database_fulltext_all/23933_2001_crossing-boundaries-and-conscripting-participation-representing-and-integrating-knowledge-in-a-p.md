# Crossing boundaries and conscripting participation: representing and integrating knowledge in a paper machinery project

H Karsten<sup>1</sup>\*, K Lyytinen<sup>2</sup>, M Hurskainen<sup>3</sup> and T Koskelainen<sup>4</sup>

<sup>1</sup>University of Turku, Finland; <sup>2</sup>Case Western Reserve University, OH, USA; <sup>3</sup>Done Inc, Finland; <sup>4</sup>Valmet Corporation, Printing Paper Machines, Finland

In large, complex knowledge management tasks, representing and integrating knowledge present major challenges. To understand these, we studied two processes: perspective taking across communities of knowing with boundary objects and perspective making within a community by the use of conscription devices. A mutually modifiable object, with sufficient complexity and manageability, appeared to be of crucial importance within a community. Between communities, the role of representations and negotiation over their meaning were emphasised. Implications for computer support point towards combining free form and structure, open and controlled access and modifiability, and parallel synchronised and unregulated communication. European Journal of Information Systems (2001) 10, 89–98.

## Introduction

In large projects such as paper machinery delivery projects, the many parties on customer and manufacturer sides need to represent their knowledge for the other parties, and to integrate it with the knowledge acquired from others. Due to the complexity of the knowledge to be conveyed, its criticality to the effort, and the various kinds of differences between the parties involved, knowledge representation and integration present major challenges. Because of the intricacy of the task and the potential of IT to cross boundaries by swiftly transferring design artefacts and process representations, there is great interest in developing and implementing computer support for such knowledge representation and integration tasks.

One major challenge in developing such support is to understand and capture the current ‘as-is’ practice of producing and integrating knowledge from diverse and heterogeneous domains. A part of this challenge is to come up with an adequate set of concepts and analysis tools. Here we address this challenge by discussing how some well-established concepts in sociology of knowledge can be integrated with the concepts developed in the knowledge management tradition to analyse complex knowledge sharing and production tasks.

For this purpose, we employ two related concept pairs: boundary object–conscription device, and perspective making–perspective taking. According to Boland and Tenkasi (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995; Tenkasi & Boland, 1998), boundary objects (Star & Griesemer, 1989; Star, 1993) provide a basis for perspective taking between communities of practice. At the same time communities deploy conscription devices (Henderson, 1991; 1998a; 1998b) by which they enlist and constrain participation of those who have a stake in the knowledge produced. Through this capability, we claim, they provide a basis for perspective making (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995; Tenkasi & Boland, 1998) within a community of practice.

These theoretical lenses are used to study the uses of the technical specification (TS) of a paper machine delivery project. The technical specification provides a detailed description of the paper machine to be built. We analyse how the technical specification of a paper machine, composed and used during a large paper machine delivery project, Project G, acted as a boundary object to help perspective taking. At the same time, we analyse how the TS entered and was used as a conscription device and how this enabled perspective making within communities of practice. The study seeks to highlight how well the technical specification was able to fulfil its role between and within the main communities, the manufacturer and the customer. The results of this analysis bring in-depth insight into the processes of knowledge representation and integration. They can be used to inform requirements for technologies and services that support the integration and representation of knowledge between and within communities of practice.

## Conceptual preliminaries: boundary objects and conscription devices

Boundary objects (Star & Griesemer, 1989; Star, 1993) are physical objects such as design drawings, maps, contracts, learning materials, etc that are used to facilitate cooperation while allowing diversity in interpretation. A typical boundary object is a map, which carries information, for example, from road builders to road users. Conscription devices (Henderson, 1991; 1998a; 1998b) are also physical objects, but of a different kind: they not only facilitate sharing of information within a community, but they also provide means for participating in constructing this information. Conscription devices ‘both enlist and constrain participation in creating and maintaining the object’ (Henderson, 1991). The users of a conscription device must engage in inputting its elements and in revising them, if it is to serve its intended function for the users’ purposes. For example, a map would act as a conscription device within a county surveying office.

Thus even though both boundary objects and conscription devices represent and integrate information, how they are constructed and used is different (see Table 1). Boundary objects need to have been brought to closure, to have an immutable quality (Latour, 1987), for being able to convey information from one semantic community (Robinson & Bannon, 1991) to another. They have different meanings for different communities but their structure is common enough to more than one world (Star, 1993). Thereby they provide means of translation between semantic communities. Conscription devices are, in a way, opposite to this: they need to be mutable, liable to change, to allow participation. Mutual manipulation of the object is the very means for this perspective making of the community.

Boland and Tenkasi (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995; Tenkasi & Boland, 1998) have introduced the concepts of perspective making and perspective taking for discussing how to represent and integrate knowledge within organisational communities and across their boundaries. Each person and each community of knowing (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995) has a (weaker or stronger) perspective (viewpoint, agenda, idea for action), which is based on its unique knowledge. Boland and Tenkasi refer to communication that strengthens the unique knowledge of a community as perspective making, and communication that improves its ability to take the knowledge of other communities into account as perspective taking. Without a strong perspective the community cannot tell an anomaly from noise, a challenge to their knowledge from an irrelevancy. Knowledge is produced in a community of knowing by refining and clarifying the perspective of the community. Perspective making within a community involves also mutual exchange, evaluation and integration of knowledge (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995, p 358).

Even though Boland and Tenkasi separate individual perspective making and that of a community, and call the latter mutual perspective taking, in the following we reserve the concept of perspective taking only for use between communities of knowing. Hence, perspective taking refers to estimating what the members of the other community know and taking this into account. ‘This taking of the other into account, in light of a reflexive knowledge of one’s own perspective, is the perspectivetaking process’ (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995, p 362).

The process of perspective taking involves depicting and exchanging representations of a community’s understanding of an idea. Thus the outcome of perspective taking ought to be dynamic meaning integration between differentiated communities of knowing (Tenkasi & Boland, 1998). Breakdowns in perspective taking occur due to the actors’ inability to surface and examine their differing understandings (Dougherty, 1992). Thus Boland and Tenkasi (1995, p 359) note that making explicit representations of one’s knowledge and understandings to exchange with others enables one to appreciate better the distinct ways of knowing that those others will attempt to communicate.

Crossing the boundary between semantic communities involves the use of boundary objects. Star (1993) has observed how people between communities find a way of bringing their distinctive perspectives into dialogue through the construction and discussion of boundary objects. Boland and Tenkasi emphasise that such boundary objects do not convey unambiguous meaning, but have instead a kind of symbolic adequacy that enables conversation without enforcing a commonly agreed meaning. Thus they tie perspective taking and boundary objects as follows: ‘Once a visible representation of % knowledge is made available for analysis and communication, it becomes a boundary object and provides a basis for perspective taking’ (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995, p 362).

In the same vein, we suggest a connection between perspective making and conscription devices: When participants in a community engage in creating and maintaining a conscription device, they engage in perspective making, in communication that strengthens the unique knowledge of their community. Conscription devices are receptacles for knowledge created and adjusted through group interaction, aimed toward a common goal. They represent the negotiated ideas of the group, and they also structure how work is done in it.

## Context and method

Our case company is Valmet (http://www.valmet.com), the largest paper machinery supplier in the world. In the mid-1990s, Valmet re-structured its business units to make them more independent from the specialised production units. In this way, knowledge within the company is being differentiated into several separate communities of knowing. In paper machinery delivery projects, the participating units need to coordinate their activities, however, as ‘one Valmet,’ and therefore there is a need also to integrate their knowledge. To enable this, as a company level measure, Valmet has been reengineering its key processes to increase standardisation and control throughout the production system. A comprehensive enterprise resource planning system has been implemented with Baan and related systems (in stages during 1997–2001). Free-form information sharing is encouraged, and electronic mail (in use for the past decade) and several thousand specific document databases in Lotus Notes (in use since 1992, with ‘100% coverage’ since 1997) are in use for this purpose. In addition to knowledge integration, a key aim has been to increase transparency of paper machinery projects, and as a consequence, increase efficiency within the whole Valmet.

One of the key sets of documentation for knowledge integration and work transparency is the technical specification (TS), which specifies what kind of a paper machine is to be delivered from Valmet to the customer. While the TS is a set of documents, parts of the information in it—such as product configuration, cost calculation, and pricing—are managed by the enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems. In this study we will give an account of the life cycle of a TS, and analyse it with the four concepts presented earlier, to find out how well it serves the parallel needs for differentiation and integration of knowledge. The practical purposes for our study are to contribute to redesigning the structure and use processes of technical specifications, and to inform the choices of technology use.

The empirical study was carried out in the Rautpohja units of Valmet in Jyva¨skyla¨, Finland, during 1997. Beforehand, two of us, Markku and Timo, had accumulated considerable background knowledge by working as observing participants (Taylor & Bogdan, 1998) for 9 months in a separate information systems development project in the Project Department. Due to the complexity of paper machinery projects, this time was necessary and valuable for understanding project documentation. Plausibility, credibility, and relevant representation of our interpretations (Altheide & Johnson, 1994) were further enhanced by our continued access to Valmet.

All paper machinery projects, and consequently the technical specifications of the paper machines, are different to a degree. Tracing the history and contents of more than one specific TS would have been a sizeable effort, beyond the scope of one study. Therefore the TS of a major current project, Project G, was chosen as the case for our study. The detailed progression of the TS document and its relations to other pertinent documents were traced, in Project G and in general, by interviewing 14 people, with eight representatives of the 50+ member large Project G team. Several descriptions (Yin, 1989) were made of the process, the documents, and communication practices around the TS. In the following overall description, each informant is identifiable by the number in parentheses.

## Building a paper machine

## Paper machinery projects

Building a paper mill with a new paper machine is a major effort: a new complete paper machine alone can cost 100–200 million. Due to this, the period from initial sales contacts to signing the contract can often be measured in years. But once the project has started, the start-up can take place in about 18–20 months. This is very compact, considering the size of the effort, allowing very little room for mistakes or delays, on either side. Even though there are parallel and recursive chains of activities, three points of closure—signing the contract, reaching the freezing point for the design and, ultimately, the start-up of the machine—bring the processes again together. Three sets of documents represent the integrated knowledge at these points: the contract (of which the technical specification is a part), the design drawings, and the operating and service manuals.

In Valmet, at each stage there are several departments and subcontractors involved. Due to the large size of the project, even though the project manager (PM) is the key mediator between the customer and Valmet, this boundary is kept rather leaky (Brown & Duguid, 1998), with information going back and forth at all levels, that is, information that is ‘within the contract’. This is also true within Valmet and toward the subcontractors. At the same time, the boundaries are impermeable, each Valmet unit guarding its technology and innovations with fervour. Even with this restriction, the amount of detailed information moving constantly between Valmet units and between customer and Valmet is well beyond the scope of any one person. Therefore the project is supposed to proceed along predefined paths. Only exceptions and major changes are brought to the attention of the project manager.

## The technical specification documentation

The technical specification (TS) documentation in large projects can be 500–600 pages long, including texts, spreadsheets, and design drawings. It is relatively complex both in terms of structure and uses. During the sales phase, information or sub-bids are collected from the units and subcontractors. During the sales negotiations, there are often five to six cycles of revision before the customer is ready to start comparing the bids. The more knowledgeable the customer is, the more changes are likely to be required. In the actual contract negotiations, both parties initial those pages of the TS to be included in the contract. Therefore the valid pages and information in them can only be confirmed from the paper document. By the time the project begins, the customer has approved of the main points of the technical specification. This does not mean that the technical specification is now approved and final: details in it will most likely be changed several times during the early stages of the project.

The project is started in a kick-off meeting, where the sales engineer tells how the sales process went and in what kind of spirit the deal was struck. The focal issues are what was sold, what was not sold, and what was sold with an option to add or remove. Dimensioning information is crucial, because they tell what size of machine is sold, how fast, and for what kind of paper with what kind of production. The PM goes through the specifics with the teams, ensuring that everybody has understood the idea of the new machine. The head designers and their teams then pick out from the technical specification all pertinent information, and compare it to the current design guidelines that spell out the latest machine concept, ie the ‘model’ of the type of paper machine to be built. This is also referred to as the ‘standards’. Thus there are (at least) three different ‘paper machines’ discussed during the project: the machine concept, the machine specified in the technical specification, and the actual machine that is being built. The insides of the parts of the actual machine are built according to the latest machine concept(s), but its ‘interface’ is built according to the (revised) technical specification.

During the project, the monthly 2-day Project Meetings are the central events between Valmet and the customer, to clarify open issues, to make necessary changes, and to decide on additional investments. These are large meetings, with all the relevant people from both sides attending. Both parties may want to keep the TS as it is, because it is a part of the contract, and any major changes would need to be re-negotiated also in terms of price and delivery time. Therefore it is likely that changes to the TS may no longer be included into it as revision pages, but that the changes are recorded in the meeting minutes, in acknowledgements of change orders, and as changes in internal orders (job number lists), and conveyed by these to everybody concerned. Therefore, by the start-up of the machine, the original technical specification is no longer valid but information in several other documents, taken together, describe in detail the paper machine that was built. On the boundary between Valmet and the customer, the operating and service manuals will form the key boundary object after the start-up of the paper machine.

## On the boundary between communities

If the technical specification is looked at as a collection of sub-documents, these gain their near-immutable quality already when they leave the part of Valmet where they were drafted. This happens quite early on when the sales engineer puts together the first draft of the technical specification for the bid. In the sales department, the sales assistants might possibly format the sub-documents, if received in an electronic form, but they or the sales engineers do not change their content. These subdocuments now hold stable the materials from which they were composed, and which can now be conveyed, collated, and compared.

However, the tailored quality of the paper machines dictates the need to modify the technical specification, and opens it up again for changes during the contract negotiations. The sales team makes the changes after consulting the source of the sub-document. From the viewpoint of the customer, the pages of the technical specification gain their immutable quality when they are initialled, when both sides have approved the changes. The chain of changes can be followed by tracing the versions of the technical specification. In Project G, this was achieved by retaining the whole history within the TS document, marked with asterisks. Thus the TS document contained the process that had been required to reach the current status. In this way, it also provided its readers a means for legitimate peripheral participation (Lave, 1991) in this particular community of practice, the negotiators.

Between Valmet and the customer, the technical specification document has different meanings and uses for both sides. It translates the wishes of the customer to Valmet, and what Valmet is promising to build, to the customer. Each side needs to ‘discipline’ their information so that it can become a part of the translation, that is, they need to explicate their knowledge into a visible format in some way, available to the others for their perspective taking. A series of boundary objects are generated, to maximise both the autonomy and communication between the camps. These boundary objects have been refined over decades of negotiations between machinery suppliers and buyers in general and between Valmet and its customers in particular. During the sales phase, the subsequent versions of the technical specification and the meeting agendas and minutes, confirming the process, will become a cascade of boundary objects (Henderson, 1991), culminating in a signed contract, with all pages of the technical specification approved by both sides.

As an agreement on what kind of a machine will be built, the technical specification contains the commitments from the customer to build the rest of the plant so that the machine fits in, and the commitments from the Valmet units that their machine fills its place. However, these commitments are never complete or totally stable. The dilemma in the use of the technical specification is to work on something that is likely to be according to the commitment, but, at the same time, acknowledging the continuous need for negotiations, for repairs of breakdowns, with incomplete, possibly dated, information. The negotiations repeatedly revisit issues until they are sufficiently well defined for both parties; yet leave enough room for specific interpretations within a community. Thus the role of situated communication (Karsten, 2000) needs to be acknowledged in the perspective taking process over a boundary object.

To sum up, the technical specification is one of the key boundary objects between the customer and Valmet. Established tradition (or strong perspective) in the negotiator’s community of practice means that the use of the technical specification at this boundary is relatively well defined, and the inherent problems are understood, even though they are not simple or transparent. The degree of immutability is appropriate for the situation. The TS is modified in a punctuated way during the negotiations and the modifications are highly controlled. When it finally reaches closure during the design stage of the delivery project, it also becomes secondary to design drawings and other documents that depict in more detail what is actually being built.

## Conscripting participants within a community of practice

Within Valmet, the technical specification is a boundary object between the different units. It tells the areas of design and manufacture and their relationships. Within the project team, however, the technical specification could have a special role as one of the conscription devices, it could act to enlist and constrain participation in creating and maintaining it. In this, the technical specification could be similar to a set of drawings for a turbine engine (Henderson, 1991) where its users must engage in inputting its elements and in revising them, if the drawing is to serve its intended function for their particular purposes.

We assumed that as a conscription device, the TS would not only support the coordination of the design and manufacture of this particular paper machine between various individuals and groups within Valmet and its sub-contractors, but that it would also contribute to developing and strengthening their knowledge domain and practices, their perspective making. According to Boland and Tenkasi (1995, p 356), as a perspective strengthens, it ‘complexifies’, that is, the detailed views of the machine implementation would start to emerge and take shape.

With the TS, this perspective making did not take place. Even though the information in the technical specification was collected from the units that would use it again during the project, and even though the sales team consulted the units on changes during the negotiation process with the customer, the designers in the units still could feel that the connection between the information in the TS and the unit was lost or that involving the designers in the TS came too late in the project. Also, the machine concepts were likely to have changed while the sales negotiations took place.

In Project G, the PM engaged in careful re-feeding of the information back to the units when the project started, so that the design teams could relate their detailed design guidelines to the TS. According to the PM, the TS alone was not sufficient to enlist the project team members to the project, but the input of the PM was vital.

The technical specification is the backbone for all we do. Feeding information to the project members is one of the most essential issues in the beginning. % I usually hold these reading meetings, which ensure that the team members have received the documents, that they have read them, and that they have even understood them. Very often the machine is different from the machine concept, and therefore I need to ensure that they have noticed the differences. % In the beginning of the project, studying the technical specification takes up an enormous amount of time, because one needs to learn it and read through it all. But then, during the project, less and less. During the first weeks it is very important, you almost study it by heart, so that you yourself have internalised it and then you go through it with the guys. It is one of the main means for us to get the message through to them. (PM of Project G)

When the project team members returned to their units, to the unit project teams, they engaged in perspective making within that community of practice. In this process they related the specifications of the new machine to the machine concept and to the part standards in their design guidelines, listing out the differences. The head designer then discussed these differences with the PM, after which the unit project teams started making the designs. They may still have used the TS but finding relevant information in it required practice, due to its size and structure.

It comes to us only when the project is starting. There are usually shortcomings. An item can perhaps be found there, but it is not included in the price calculations. Then it will go free or we need to invent a bit to get the money. It is like this also in the Project G. There was a whole mechanism in one part missing in the price calculations, even though that is included in the standard. % We have to ask the PM whether this and this are included and he then asks the sales department. % Reading the technical specification is pretty much about picking out information of what has been sold. It should be relatively accurate because it is the starting point for design phase. Usually it is according to the standards, but there can be a lot of extra sold and we need to dig it out from there. (Informant No. 12)

The structure of the technical specification drives me crazy, it is so comprehensive. Information from different units is just piled up in there under separate headings, the information you are looking for can be found scattered in several places, and you need to leaf through the thing. No page numbers in the table of contents, it is difficult to find anything. (Informant No. 6)

Project meetings with the customer can result in changes to the TS, and the usual procedure for the PM was to inform those concerned quickly and directly. The information was then confirmed, for example, in the meeting minutes, or in the internal orders (job number list changes), due to the size of the project. The project team members could then trace changes by reading these and the related correspondence, but this was experienced by some as not coherent enough, and requiring too much attention to find the relevant changes among all the information. Also, as soon as the customer requirements were met and the interfaces of the part to other parts were specified, they became translated into set conditions for the part and the specification lost its importance for this part.

What the machine is, is then written into the operating and service manuals. They are quite important for us. When they are done, nobody looks at the technical specification any more; it might have old information and cause errors. The information in the manuals is very detailed, but the technical specification is not. If the technical specification were to be updated all the time, it would end up being equally detailed. The actual machine % is different on the detail level; it has a different tailoring, even though on the surface it looks quite the same. (Informant No. 12)

To sum up, the technical specification did not act as one of the conscription devices for the project members. While the design teams clearly engaged in mutual perspective taking, they worked, for example, on their sketches and drawings (Henderson, 1991) and on the operating and service manuals. They used the TS as a boundary object, which set certain limits to their actions. Also, within the company-level Valmet project team, the role of the TS appeared to be problematic, due to the independence of the units (of whom the team members were just representatives), the planned nature of the project with boundaries spelled out, and the goal of adhering to the machine concept and part standards. Only a minimum of information was supposed to be exchanged, and that was usually related to exceptions.

The TS did not support perspective making, as it was a part of the contract and therefore its constant update and refinement were restricted. Therefore the TS was supported and gradually replaced by other documents that could act as partial conscription devices: internal orders, design drawings, operating and service manuals, and the like. Having a complete, up-to-date technical specification on paper or in file, in one place, available to all, would thus have been practical only in the beginning of the project. Also, as a set of documents, the TS was unwieldy and complex, and updating it would probably have resulted in inconsistencies between the different parts. Therefore, it was gradually left to the role of telling what was agreed in the beginning, with other documents taking over. When seen in this way, the uselessness of this particular document, the technical specification, as a conscription device lost its potential dangers within Valmet, and a ‘graceful departure’ from it became a more focal issue.

Thus the ideal situation for perspective making in a community, where ‘distinctive individual knowledge is exchanged, evaluated, and integrated with that of others in the organisation’ (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995, p 358) apparently did not take place with the TS during the project G on Valmet level. It may have taken place earlier, during R&D for the machine concept, and in separate design teams around the drawings. Thus on Valmet level, the ‘complexification’ through perspective making was hampered. This might partly explain why the management felt that instead of ‘one Valmet’, they had to deal with a group of independent units. This might also give clue as to why a need for company-wide ERP had emerged and acquired such primacy.

However, the issue of perspective making on Valmet level within a project team remains unsolved. Earlier, when the whole process took place within one small physical area, the Rautpohja plant in Jyva¨skyla¨, making the paper machine was something very tangible. The machine itself was the conscription device. With distributed planning and production, clearly something is needed to hold the project together during its course. The machine concept and the design drawings cannot function as the conscription device for the whole Valmet delivery project team.

## Discussion

The main focus of our study was on establishing the value of the two concept pairs: boundary object / perspective taking and conscription device / perspective making, in knowledge representation and integration. We started with two statements to focus our attention:

When knowledge is made available to others in a boundary object, it provides a basis for perspective taking.

When participants in a community of practice engage in creating and maintaining a conscription device, they engage in perspective making.

## Perspective taking and boundary objects

With our case study, the first pairing informed us of several issues. To begin with, when we looked at the technical specification as a boundary object between Valmet and its customer, it provided enough flexibility to allow different interpretations and sufficient common structure for carrying out work in both communities. It was used for perspective taking by both parties. Thus the concept pairing appeared to hold in our case.

In Table 2, we have summarised the main issues related to knowledge representation and integration that the study brought to the foreground. The first issue is negotiation over a boundary object. Perspective taking is not simple sending and receiving information, but the meaning of the information, the knowledge, rests on past experiences, previous knowledge, and communication during the process, as well.

In our case this was emphasised as the boundary object did not only convey knowledge from one community to another, but it was also the object of business negotiations. Thus the pairing of perspective taking and boundary objects required the addition of a third dimension, situated communication (Karsten, 2000), as the parties were engaged in a negotiation over the boundary object. Negotiating over a boundary object has not been emphasised either by Star or by Henderson in their original discussions (Star & Griesemer, 1989; Star, 1993; Henderson, 1991; 1998b).

This negotiating is different from the articulation work that Star has discussed, as it is not used to ensure due process (Gerson & Star, 1986). What this negotiating resembles more is participating in constructing a conscription device, but with the important difference in the nature of the perspective to be made. With conscription devices, the goal is perspective making of a community, whereas here we have a situation where the perspectives of the opposing communities remain clearly different.

The second issue is the punctuated and regulated process of participation. The modifications of the boundary object and the subsequent perspective taking happened in our case during a well regulated process of preparations and meetings. The participants in these negotiations needed to have full control over the TS. If this full control were to change, it would be likely to have significant consequences in the power relationships between the communities.

The third issue is that the cascade of boundary objects provides perspective. When the history of the negotiations was explicitly spelled out in the TS, the perspective taking extended also to past negotiations, and allowed the cascade of boundary objects (Henderson,

In terms of learning, the negotiations and the TS with its included history provided an important means of learning not only for the negotiators, but also for the related parties who followed their actions, in the form of legitimate peripheral participation (Lave & Wenger, 1991). This kind of ‘organisational memory’, grounds the current interaction in a very solid way, providing continuity (Karsten, 2000).

The fourth issue is timing. The negotiation process appears to be very time-dependent, and it could be disturbed by availability of information not in alignment with the schedule. Also during the delivery project, the timing of information giving becomes a major issue. Examples of the negative consequences could be loss of slack time that has been used to give room for recovery of (unavoidable) errors, and loss of ability to smoothen the wrinkles in ones own performance prior to making it public. These kinds of losses could be detrimental for both parties, limiting flexibility and responsiveness.

The fifth issue is the system of boundary objects. The TS was combined from many subdocuments and it was itself part of the contract. These documents could be studied as a system of boundary objects (Star & Griesemer, 1989), to outline their connections. This would bring out further the many channels for perspective taking. Interpretation of one boundary object is likely to be influenced in many ways by the other boundary objects in the system.

When IT support is planned and designed for knowledge representation and integration, these focal areas can be addressed with a number of more concrete requirements. Some of these are summarised in Table 2. Taken together these would point to an integrated document management system, where structure is built in and exploited to a great degree. The structure would not only describe the documents and their fields, but it would also have meta-information about authoring, access, timing, versions, and relationships to other documents, also in other document databases.

## Perspective making and conscription devices

The second pairing, conscription device and perspective making, also informed us of several issues. This second pairing did not find much support in our case, for several reasons.

First, perspective making took place with the TS only early in the delivery project, with the active intervention of the project manager, due to the nature of the Valmet project team. This met, as a whole, only in the beginning of the project in the kick-off meeting, and after that, the communication was mainly between the PM and each individual project team member. The Valmet project team was only a short-term virtual construct, not a longliving community of knowing (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995). The permanent communities of knowing for the team members were their respective design and manufacture groups.

Second, because the creation of the TS was unidirectional, and it could only be changed via negotiations with the customer, other documents, kept fully within Valmet, gradually replaced it as the description of the paper machine to be. These other documents therefore become conscription devices, more focal to perspective making. Thus there was no unifying conscription device for the whole Valmet project team, no means to negotiate and integrate the knowing involved in the paper machine process. Due to the increased differentiation of knowledge between the independent Valmet units, this carried a risk of disintegration of perspective, and thereby difficulties in maintaining the ‘one Valmet’ front.

Third, due to the relatively general level of description, the TS was not very useful for the design teams, after the layout of the machine was fixed. After that, the design guidelines based on the current machine concept became the guidelines to follow, with the possible risk that the current machine concept would be more influential in design than the machine ordered. However, this risk was actually not seen as a risk, since Valmet openly professed to supply the latest technology, whether the customer knew to ask for it or not. The preference to design guidelines was further enhanced by the cumbersome format of the TS: finding relevant information in it was difficult and required considerable practice.

Fourth, information about the changes to the TS were distributed by several means. Keeping track of all relevant changes required active reading, and even then some may have passed unnoticed. This can be taken to indicate that, after all, a comprehensive, up-to-date, detailed description of the machine, accessible to project team members, could be the kind of conscription device that would aid perspective making within Valmet, in the circumstances of distributed production. However, this comes with risks attached, as the object could be too comprehensive and accessible and it could raise issues hitherto unrecognised (Henderson, 1991).

What is focal in knowledge representation and integration within a community of knowing thus remains to be studied with a more appropriate candidate for conscription device in a community of knowing. However, even from our problematic case, we can learn some tentative lessons (Table 3).

If IT support were designed for knowledge representation and integration, the key issue appears to be support for the perhaps virtual community. Access to the current information is the minimum required for participation, but perspective making entails active involvement. Therefore the conscription device must be mutually maintainable: everybody in the community should be able to modify the entries and see others use one’s own work. This has appeared as focal also in earlier studies on constructing interdependencies (Karsten, 2000), that is, on making long-term communities.

## Conclusions

The approach taken in this study was to look at the technical specification in crossing the boundary between Valmet and customer, and in conscripting participation in the Valmet project team. As a boundary object, the technical specification provided enough flexibility to allow different interpretations and sufficient common structure for carrying out work in both communities of practice, the customer and the manufacturer. However, we needed to add a third dimension, situated communication, to this pair, to study negotiation around the object.

As a conscription device within the manufacturer project team, however, the technical specification fell short in perspective making, due to its limited modifiability. It also had a diffuse relationship to other conscription devices and there were practical difficulties in using it. Consequences of this were its diminishing importance in the web of conscripting objects, and its inconsequential use as a means for learning.

All in all, connecting conscription devices and perspective making emphasised that a shared, modifiable object is of crucial importance in knowledge production and integration. Likewise, the connection between boundary objects and perspective taking emphasised the role of representations in conveying knowledge between communities of practice. While these pairings necessarily simplify the relationships and the processes, they appeared to focus our attention to essential issues in a sufficiently accurate way. Implications for computer support point towards combining free form and structure, open and controlled access and modifiability, and parallel synchronised and unregulated communication.

This study enlivened the theoretical constructs used— brought meat to the bones—and gave indications for extending the theory to paying more attention to the actual processes of perspective taking and making with boundary objects and conscription devices. In perspective taking, the emergent issue of situated communication would need to be further studied to distinguish the particulars of an explicitly opposing situation with business negotiations from, say, a collaborative or helping situation. In perspective making, the possibility of having one conscription device to enlist participation appears idealistic. Our case would indicate that perspective making could be studied with a focus on a system of conscription devices. Also, situated communication is likely to have an important role here also. Moreover, the previous knowledge of the participants in both perspective making and taking, that is expertise in paper making in our case, should receive more attention.

We are dealing here with knowledge processes that are far from mechanistic, isolated, or unidirectional. Knowledge representation and integration in a complex process, between professionals, is intricate and dynamic. Much communication is needed, over and on the ‘containers’ of knowledge, the boundary objects and conscription devices. Modifiability and the control of this modifiability with the ‘containers’ is the main issue in knowledge integration. Negotiation of meaning and negotiation over changes appeared to be what perspective taking and making was about. Significance of negotiation directs future research towards addressing issues such as power and legitimacy in addition to those of interpretation, in knowledge representation and integration.
