# What is knowledge and can it be managed?

DC Sutton\*

System Six Ltd, PO Box 107, Macclesfield, Cheshire, SK11 9SX, UK

Philosophical and linguistic debates about the nature of knowledge, information and data should be of more than academic interest to the practice of knowledge management. Grounds are proposed for showing that not all enterprise-critical knowledge can be captured and transferred by the use of data warehouses or knowledge repositories. A key difference highlighted is that between the natural execution of a task and the ability to talk about a task which is discussed as a distinction between ‘thrown’ and ‘breakdown behaviour. It is shown that this contrast renders even that which can be codified an incomplete representation of the knowledge concerned. Knowledge management must therefore concern itself with both tan gible issues and artefacts (such as codified representations that can be secured) and intangible features such as the skills which people require to interpret and exploit those representations. Furthermore, as organisational structures and procedures themselves represent codified knowledge, they must be designed to be inherently changeable in order to permit the enterprise continuously to adapt as new knowledge is acquired. European Journal of Information Systems (2001) 10, 80–88.

## Introduction

The management of knowledge as a corporate function would seem to have two core objectives:

 to improve the exploitation of the knowledge resources of an enterprise

to protect the knowledge resources of an enterprise These objectives require more than the purely technical and engineering attitude that typifies many knowledge management approaches. Whilst the vendors of data mining systems would have us believe that knowledge can be extracted like nuggets from the ore of a data resource (Van Epps, 1996; Verity, 1997), for others the issue is not so neat. Before knowledge can be exploited or protected, it must be located and elicited. It is well established in the computing literature that identifying and codifying relevant knowledge is hard (Welbank, 1983; Kidd & Welbank, 1984; Kidd & Cooper, 1985; Paton, 1992; Gaines, 1995; Stowell, 1995; Brooke & Maguire, 1998).

For the human race, both knowledge and language clearly have ‘survival value’ (Scarrott, 1979b; Miller, 1991, p 3). Knowledge codification makes it possible to accumulate and make available the social experiences of the past in order to guide the present and it is upon language that we depend to codify and store this knowledge (Gaines, 1995). In essence, without written language we would not be able to:

‘%accumulate the experience of society over much longer periods than the lifetime of an individual’ (Scarrott, 1979a).

This clearly has a bearing on the claim that librarianship is the ‘second oldest profession’ (Drake, 1996)!

This article will discuss views upon the nature of knowledge and human communication which suggest both why difficulties arise and some directions in which current knowledge management practices may look to enhance their effectiveness. It will be suggested that all knowledge codification and transfer incorporates subjective elements and depends to a considerable extent upon individual interpretation. The areas of codification and interpretation will be shown to have problematic features which make the management of knowledge more than just a technical issue. Some implications for knowledge management practice will be illuminated.

## Thinking about knowledge

A plan for an aircraft, building, chemical compound etc can enable someone to construct that artefact. This is possible even when the reader is distanced by some considerable time from the authoring of the plan, witness the recent success in building the Babbage machine after 150 years (Woolley, 1999, p 131). Procedure manuals for many operations contain clear directions on what to do and what to produce. Cookery recipes are an accepted means of conveying the ability to create a certain kind of meal from one person to another. However, whilst there is a generally held assumption that books can contain ‘knowledge’, some question the notion that any codified material can, by itself, be knowledge.

Some point out that the possession of knowledge, whether of a ‘fact’, a ‘skill’ or ‘know how’, cannot be recognised other than by inference from certain ‘dispositions’ to behave in certain ways (Ayer, 1956, p 12, p 153; Tsouvalis & Checkland, 1996). Knowledge is suggested to be revealed only by the ability to ‘respond correctly to the contingencies or problems presented’ (Pask, 1984). In essence, knowledge has been characterised as:

‘%a capacity to behave adaptively within an environment; it cannot be reduced to representations, of behaviour or the environment’ (Gaines, 1995).

Thus knowledge may reside in a capacity to behave and only be detectable in the exhibition of behaviour (Ayer, 1956, p 15). On the other hand, there is no doubt that books have influenced behaviour over the ages. If knowledge is embodied in a ‘disposition to behave’ rather than any codified representation, we need to understand the relation between what is transferable in books, plans, etc and this more precise definition of knowledge.

## Thinking about knowledge transfer

It has been observed that knowledge transfer is really achieved by information flows and whether that information is moving or static does not alter the fact that it is information and not knowledge:

‘information is a flow of messages, whilst knowledge is created by that very flow of information, anchored in the beliefs and commitments of its holder’ (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995, p 58).

In essence:

‘Knowledge flows through technology, but resides in people’ (Cannon, 1998).

There is no doubt that something can be codified, stored and reused to enable effective action at a later date, but:

‘a representation is not equivalent to knowledge (Clancey, 1989).

Whilst that which can be codified and transferred may not actually be knowledge, there is clearly an intimate relationship between them. At the very least, it is clear that any ‘knowledge management’ technology does not in itself store and retrieve knowledge, but merely manipulates codified representations. The next section will examine points of view discussing the extent to which it can be possible to codify and hence store and transfer knowledge successfully, whether with a book or as output from a data-mining search.

## Can (all/any) knowledge be codified?

Certain important types of capability that are agreed to be indicative of ‘knowledge’ cannot be conveyed by means of texts. For example, how to ride a bicycle, drive a car, hit a golf ball to a desired location, how to make a perfect cake or how to ensure a good working relationship with another person. It seems that ‘know-how’ is harder to codify than ‘know-what’ and, to successfully create meaning that can be effectively acted upon, may require a ‘reader’ competent to provide the skill at execution. However, we need not assume that such skillbased knowledge cannot be transferred at all.

‘from the fact that one cannot literally share the experiences of another, it does not follow that one cannot understand what he says about them’ (Ayer, 1956, p 209).

For example, wine tasters and art critics employ elaborately metaphorical language as they try to convey and discuss the essentially subjective experiences of the taste of wine or impression given by looking at a painting or sculpture.

There is another view of human behaviour which contributes an understanding of why there is such difficulty in codifying and transferring certain types of knowledge and expertise. In the normal course of affairs most people ‘just do’ a task. This is equivalent to the Heideggerian notion of ‘thrownness’ referred to by Winograd & Flores (1986, p 34). When using a tool, it may be said to be ‘ready-to-hand’ (Rathswohl, 1990), that is a person’s focus is upon the task not the tool (as when a computer interface is ‘intuitive’). Asking people to reflect upon what they are doing requires that they enter a different state of mind from thrownness or ready-to-hand, known as ‘breakdown’ (Winograd & Flores, 1986, p 36) or ‘present-at-hand’ (Rathswohl, 1990). Often a person is shifted from the thrown state into the breakdown state when the tool fails to work as expected and the person is forced to ‘see’ the tool rather than the task (as when an interface is non-intuitive or, say, the mouse slips and the cursor does not move as expected). This was the origin of the term ‘breakdown’ as it denoted that the thrown state had been broken down.

This reflection upon and discussion about a task is an entirely different mode and content of thought from when people are engaged in their skilled activity. In normal working, the ideal is to perform actions automatically and without any thinking or critical reflection upon one’s practice and certainly without articulating what it is one is doing or thinking. In the ideal working situation, a person’s awareness or degree of ‘internal information transfer’ is minimised (Pask, 1984), relevant knowledge is part of their Background (Searle, 1992, p 195) and their behaviour is effectively ‘reflex’.

Thus, when people are absorbed in doing a task in real-time, their behaviour is ‘thrown’, when they converse about their behaviour they leave the thrown state and enter a reflective, articulate, ‘breakdown’ state. Once in the breakdown state, ability to transfer knowledge is limited to that which is articulatable or ‘interpreted’. It might also be considered that reflective thought itself is a type of ‘thrown’ behaviour and uttering speech entails a further level of ‘breakdown’. All these transitions and enforced reflections upon its features and requirements ‘distance’ people from the task or domain knowledge itself (Boland, 1990).

The distinction between the natural unconscious execution of a task and the ability to talk about a task equates to what knowledge engineers have termed ‘compiled’ knowledge as opposed to ‘interpreted’ (Minsky, 1968). Others have seen the distinction as between ‘facts’ (the full ‘text-book’ treatment of a topic) and ‘heuristics’ (the requirements for good practice and judgement), ie actual competence in a field (George, 1985). Thus there are strong indications that even successfully getting people to articulate their knowledge will result in explanations that cannot relate in a simple direct manner to what they think about, actually do, or wish to experience, in their thrown state.

These points suggest that there are issues to be addressed with regard to the notion that critical knowledge can be fully elicited, codified, stored and transferred. The following section will suggest that there are also problematic issues associated with the recovery of knowledge from any products of codification.

## Considering interpretation

Any book, plan, work instruction of output from a database search is, linguistically, a ‘text’, and a text must first be processed to recover its meaning. The hermeneutic discipline explicitly recognises the non-triviality of the role of the reader in discerning the meaning intended by the author of a text (Klein & Hirschheim, 1987). Some deny that any publicly sharable meaning can reside in texts and that all meaning is derived from the creative capacities and inclinations of the ‘reader’ (Jones, 1991; McNamara, 1994). Documents, indeed any ‘sign’, created in one time and culture can require considerable effort to discern the ‘original’ meaning (Rathswohl, 1990), if indeed it can ever be comprehended except in the culture in which it originated (Winograd & Flores, 1986, p 28). It must be recognised that extracting meaning from institutional ‘texts’ is at base a subjective process and this will have implications for the transferability of ‘knowledge’ however it is codified.

A point seldom given its due relevance when matters of interpretation are discussed is the fact that there is no intrinsic property of spoken or written words or of graphic signs or symbols that makes it necessary that they convey meaning to anyone. Meaning and its raw material, syntax, are not ‘intrinsic to physics’ (Searle,

1992, p 207). This has been noted by others, for example Ashby (1964, p 124) states that ‘the information conveyed is not an intrinsic property of the individual message’. Searle argues that what makes speech or marks meaningful is simply that they were intended to be, as the performance of a speech-act (Searle, 1983, p 163). This assertion implies that anything can be treated ‘asif’ it had meaning. The core determinant for meaning attribution is ‘intentionality’ on the part of the observer as observer, receiver or sender.

An intrinsic feature of intentionality is that it is ‘aspectual’, ie always from a perspective, ‘point of view’ or focus of interest (Searle, 1992, p 131). In effect any ‘appreciated world’ is ‘selected by our interests’ (Vickers, 1970, p 97). This means that all reference to ‘meaning’ implies ‘meaning to someone in some situation’, all reference to a ‘signal’ implies a ‘signal in the eyes of some observer’ and all reference to ‘sign’ means ‘sign in the eyes of some community’ (Stamper, 1997). This ‘observer relativity of meanings’ is a very significant feature and relates to the question of how does anyone understand anyone else?

Searle points out that there are two fundamentally different classes of empirical reality (1992, p 94). A first person, ontologically subjective, phenomenon (such as an opinion, a desire or a pain) has an existence totally dependent upon a specific observer. A third person, ontologically objective, phenomenon (such as the Earth, an electric current or the boiling point of water) has an existence independent of any single observer.

Epistemological objectivity can be achieved when things or events (whether first or third person ontologically) can be determined in terms of universally accessible facts or otherwise verifiable by third person means. This is the essence of the scientific method, where access is equally open to and testable by all competent observers. It can be applied to ontologically subjective things, for example, it can be agreed by observers that someone is in pain without those observers being able to experience that pain for themselves. Epistemological subjectivity relates to phenomena that can be determined only in terms of socially established agreements (eg those two people are married, this sign means ‘Stop’).

Others are concerned that the ‘Western’ view of epistemology and third person science in particular, places too much emphasis upon codifiable knowledge anyway. They argue that this inheritance lies far in the past, in Plato’s assertion that ‘true knowledge’ can be stated precisely. Anything which cannot be so formulated is not knowledge but merely belief (Gill, 1991). This leads to the modern Platonist view that knowledge is an organised set of statements of facts or ideas presenting a reasoned judgement or an experimental result, which is transmitted to others through some form of communication medium in some systematic form. It is this that underpins the view of knowledge as a commodity and thence to the notion of ‘intellectual property rights’ (ibid).

The Platonic position is challenged by Nonaka and Takeuchi (1995) who acknowledge Polanyi (1966) as their source for making a distinction between ‘explicit knowledge’ and ‘tacit knowledge’. Explicit knowledge is: objective, rational, mental, sequential, displaced (there and then) and digital (relating to theory). Tacit knowledge is: subjective, experiential, bodily, simultaneous (here and now), analogue (relating to practice). This distinction has many overlaps with the thrown/breakdown contrast discussed earlier.

Another commentator on Polanyi’s definitions elaborates the characterisation of tacit knowledge as ‘that which we cannot express, but know’ (Gill, 1991) and that it derives from:

personal knowledge from family culture, personal life experiences, school, friends—social values, beliefs

experiential knowledge, based on specific work contexts, colleagues, group culture, etc.

It is crucial to recognise from this that explicit knowledge by itself is insufficient to enable effective performance. Before it can be effectively deployed, explicit knowledge requires the development of relevant tacit knowledge by ‘internalisation’ through practice, a point echoed by others (Brocklesby, 1997; Skyrme, 1997). Another critical point is that tacit knowledge can only be transferred amongst groups that share an operational context, perhaps through participation, apprenticeship or professional training. Thus, social context is important in deriving meaning from data:

Fundamentally, tacit  explicit conversion can only be done by individuals (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995, p 225). For people to agree a meaning they must share a viewpoint and its associated array of implicit understandings and skills. This brings us back to the question of why any two readers should ever construe even approximately the same meaning from a text.

## How are interpretations achieved and aligned?

Humans communicate, by and large successfully, on the basis of a vast amount of shared understandings that are never explicitly discussed. Anthropologists term this ‘meta-messaging’ (Bateson, 1972), ethnomethodologists term it ‘common-sense’ (Geertz, 1983) and Wittgenstein termed it the ‘form of life’ (Kenny, 1973, p 163). Searle argues that, in making sense of any utterance, people draw upon a complex system of pre-existing understandings which he terms the ‘Background’ (Searle, 1983). Searle distinguishes ‘intentional’ and ‘non-intentional Background knowledge, differentiating ‘how to do things’ and ‘how things are’ which can again be seen as a form of tacit/explicit distinction.

A problem this raises for knowledge management is that the Background structure need not be, almost certainly is not, consciously or unconsciously, stored as ‘data’, ‘facts’ or ‘rules’, even if a particular instance of a behaviour is ‘explainable’ in terms of the ‘use’ of a rule. For the same reason, it can be misleading to suggest that meaning is derived by ‘interpreting’ messages.

‘% the mistaken supposition that all understanding must involve some act of interpretation. %. from the fact that alternative interpretations are always possible, it simply does not follow that in all discourse one is engaged in constant ‘acts of interpretation’’ (Searle, 1992, p 192).

The key is to note that all observation is ‘aspectual’ and so, therefore, are all explanations. If all explanations, and hence all definitions of meaning, are inescapably from points of view, all ‘knowledge’ is fundamentally dependent upon properties of the unique actor concerned, including their personal, professional and cultural Background. Some suggest that people routinely communicate by behaving as-if they were working to two hypotheses:

(i) ‘‘idealisation of the interchangeability of standpoints’—if we swap places, we see what each other sees;

(ii) ‘idealisation of the systems of relevances’—that our differences in background, training, functional objective etc are irrelevant for the purpose in hand, %’ (Heritage, 1984, p 55).

It is appropriate to consider how this issue bears upon knowledge elicitation or the seeking of statements of requirements to enable knowledge management systems to be designed. It is very doubtful that, in the field of formal knowledge management and computer systems development at least, the above are reliable assumptions. Given that:

 intention is in the eye of the beholder,

 the interpretation of meaning is grounded in the observer’s aspectuality,

 no two observers will have totally congruent points of view,

users and developers are viewing requirements from very different aspectivities and it seems clear that, at best, formal representations of knowledge can only relate to the explicit part of a domain’s expertise or system requirement.

A cybernetic view of conversation (Pask & Gregory, 1986) adds the suggestion that what is being communicated, namely ideas and concepts, are more usefully thought of as dynamic processes which reside inside individuals but which interact both within and between individuals. Communication is achieved as individuals develop shared understandings and agreements about meanings which are functions of, but never identical to, their private understandings. As all understandings and ideas are processes, they are liable to change over time.

A Russian psychologist studying the learning of children in 1934 (Vygotsky, 1962) also concluded that ideas and concepts are privately ‘held’ and, moreover, are held in a form that does not correspond to their form when articulated. A distinction between a mental concept and its textual expression is drawn by others (Ayer, 1956, p 165; Searle, 1992, p 185). Thus, it is suggested that it is physically and psychologically impossible to express in ‘external’ language internal thought processes, as a final quote on this point says:

‘most of our mental processes may be not so much prelinguistic as utterly independent of language’ (Gaines, 1995).

Formal methodologies do not cater for these dynamic, non/pre-verbal tacit elements, nor recognise that most elicitation of requirements takes place in contexts well displaced from the thrown experience of actors performing their tasks. If there is to be any basis for ‘objective’ or at least consensual ‘inter-subjective’ (Mingers, 1993) descriptions and definitions, there has to be some means to reduce the consequent ambiguity.

The classical approach to resolving ambiguity is to seek clarification through ever more detailed explanations and analysis. Unfortunately, asking anyone what they want or are doing can, in seeking to eliminate all ambiguity, generate an extending series of ‘explanations’ of successively greater mechanical detail or successively broader scope. This is because the explanations are couched in further words or signs whose interpretation is again observer relative. This phenomenon is termed by Searle the ‘accordion effect’ (1992, pp 88–89) and it is remarked upon by many other authors (Ayer, 1956, p 110; Heritage, 1984, p 158; Winograd & Flores, 1986, p 5; Maturana & Varela, 1987, p 135; Andersen, 1990; Magee, 1997, p 534). It is also described by Vickers (1970) as resulting from the fact that the world people see is ‘selected by our interests’ (ibid, p 97), ‘structured by expectation’ (ibid, p 98) and that ‘the hierarchic nature of reality calls for a series of descriptions, each valid for the appropriate level’ (ibid, p 113).

This section has discussed factors which relate to what is needed to be a reader competent enough to recover the knowledge from its codified form. Given the above features and viewpoints, the issue of ‘what is knowledge and how can it be managed?’, would appear to require more consideration and effort than is provided by conventional approaches.

## Discussion

Even if ‘knowledge’ is only ultimately manifestable as a potential to behave in a characterising manner on the part of an actual person, the reading of a text or the examination of a working drawing can profoundly influence the behaviour of people. Whilst libraries are conventionally considered to be repositories of ‘knowledge’, there is a strong case to be made that knowledge only resides in people. This is not a point of purely philosophical interest. Failing to recognise a distinction between data as an objective, external, codified, static ‘resource’, information as a flow of data, knowledge as an internal, subjective ‘capacity to behave’, and the visible use of knowledge ‘in anger’ as actual behaviours can compromise efforts to manage knowledge effectively.

In order to exploit enterprise ‘knowledge’ there will clearly be a need to create sharable and transferable representations in some form. If knowledge is a ‘disposition to behave’ it might also be influenced by, say, company procedures and structures which impose an institutionalised ‘disposition’ to behave upon multiple individuals in organisational contexts. Knowledge is defined by Wikstrom and Norman (1994) as embracing ‘know how, what, who, why, where, when’, and it seems reasonable to consider that different elements of an organisation are capable of embodying different categories of knowledge. In examining where knowledge resides, the authors distinguish the following locations:

 people

 machines

 technical and administrative systems

 documents, blueprints, plans, recipes, programs, to which author would add the actual departmental structures and relationships. All these categories of entity may be considered to place constraints upon the humans who are required to interact with them and so can be argued to be bringing about ‘dispositions’ to behave in certain ways. Thus company procedures and structures clearly are examples of codified knowledge and provide a ‘Background’ against which other types of texts need to be interpreted and utilised.

As discussed above, to employ a plan or a recipe successfully depends upon pre-existing knowledge and skill on the part of the reader. Having ‘knowledge’ seems to relate to the capacity to act ‘in appropriate ways’ and this includes the pre-existing capacity or knowledge to make sense of relevant data. It is one thing to ‘understand’ instructions, quite another to perform the operations described and create a quality product. So, in any field, a personal Background of both ‘know how’ and ‘know what’ is needed to ‘read between the lines’ and ensure the ability to act on that reading. Searle considers that so much of an interpretation of a text or image originates from the observers that:

‘sentence meaning not only underdetermines the content of what is said, it radically underdetermines it’ (Searle, 1992, p 181).

It has been suggested that knowledge may be created by an information flow, but that it will be anchored (and made sense of) in the beliefs and commitments of the reader (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995, p 58). This factor is recognised in the natural sciences where universality is based upon the premise of equally ‘competent’ observers who are presumed to have adequately similar backgrounds. Unless one is a ‘competent observer’ with both skill and resources, does one really have the capacity to behave appropriately? Does possessing the instructions for how to make an atom bomb mean one has the ‘knowledge’ or the ‘information’? The recovery of knowledge from any representation requires a competent observer and so any codification of knowledge would appear to require some additional information as to the capacities any reader will require to be sufficiently ‘competent’ to recover it.

All this is really alerting us to the non-triviality of the apparently trivial observation: that to understand something requires prior understanding. It is true that the decoder of a text requires a similar enough frame of reference to the original codifier and knowledge elicitation brings in the added complication that the codifier may not have the same frame of reference as either the possessor or the intended user of the ‘knowledge’. The aforegoing viewpoints show that there will always be omissions and differences that may cause problems unless an explicit concern for calibration is in place. There are other issues which need to be recognised.

## Linguistic sources of error and incompleteness

Just because people express their memories of, or reasons for, actions in the thrown state in terms of ‘facts’, ‘experience’, ‘rules’ or ‘requirements’ does not mean that such entities were consciously referenced during their actions or are stored as such in their memory (Ayer, 1956, p 135; Pask, 1984; Searle, 1992, p 187). In addition, language and thought have been found to spring from different roots and develop along different lines (Vygotsky, 1962, p 79). From the foregoing, some types of knowledge can be presumed to be available to conscious inspection and reporting whilst other types, such as those which relate to expertise or totality of experience, are not accessible and articulatable in the same way (Stevenson et al, 1988). These other aspects may be primarily transferred through social interactions in appropriate operational contexts.

## Social and background issues

It is being suggested that knowledge is better considered as a ‘disposition to behave’ which is revealed by the ability to ‘respond appropriately’ in a given situation.

Whilst these may seem to be unremarkable if pedantic definitions, it must be admitted that the question of ‘appropriateness’ is itself problematic. Issues of who is determining the criterion of appropriateness and the adequacy of any such criteria are also vigorously debatable, involving as they do issues of participation and power and what to do where multiple views may legitimately be considered necessary. Checkland (1988) observes:

‘it is rare that there is an adequate, single, unitary view of an organisation so delineated and accepted that (a computer based) information system provision can be based upon it without further thought’.

Davies (1993) advises that ‘looking at the ‘whole’ system is better than optimising each element in isolation and many others argue that pluralism in respect of what is desirable or required from any technology-based solution must be accommodated (Klein, 1984; Achterberg, 1990; Gable, 1994; Jackson, 1997a; Mingers & Gill, 1997). Others consider that how multiple views are to be accommodated and combined brings in issues of power and coercion (Jackson & Keys, 1984; Midgley, 1990; Visala, 1990; Romm, 1995; Spaul, 1997). Many argue for critical perspectives in this area (Klein, 1984; Klein & Lyytinen, 1985; Midgley, 1990; Walsham, 1991; Prior, 1992; White & Taket, 1997; Jackson, 1997a, b).

However, there is not the space in this article to engage with that debate. The main point in this paper is that, even where adequate and acceptable consensus has been reached by an adequate community of actors, the limitations of language will still be a source of limitation and misunderstanding.

Uncertainties have been identified attached to the codifying of knowledge and to the recovery of knowledge from any codified forms. Further uncertainties need to be discussed relating to the even more fundamental act of interpretation of ‘texts’. There is also debate on the issue of whether any ‘Background’ understanding can be considered to be any more valid than any other, a theme that may be related to what some term ‘post-modernism’.

## On post-modernism and relativism

Knowledge as a ‘capacity to behave’ has been argued above to relate to individuals, and so must be a subjective entity. However, it has been proposed that organisational artefacts, such a rules, can ‘predispose’ behaviour. It also seems reasonable to recognise that a professional community may be characterised by a common ability, perhaps even certified, to deploy a standardised body of knowledge. So the attribution of the possession of knowledge and the characterisation of that knowledge seem to be consensual, third person, matters, whereas the actual possession of knowledge is entirely personal, subjective and first person.

The act of reading a ‘public’ text may well entail a process in which a reader generates ‘private’ information, but whether that information is exactly that envisaged by the author, or only fairly close, is a matter of variable significance. To argue, from the self evident premise that the meaning created by the reader cannot be exactly the same as that envisaged by the author, to the conclusion that it is totally different and therefore the author’s intentions are totally irrelevant, is dramatic rhetoric, but hardly valid in logic or pragmatic utility.

It seems likely that, when arguing that no-one can lay down definitively what is the mind of the writer but can only create their own readings, what is being considered are primarily literary texts (McNamara, 1994). The postmodern position seems to be built out of consideration of ‘literature’, and a reaction to the, principally French, repressive academic and intellectual system which ‘rigidly administered a unique and definitive interpretation of literary texts’ (ibid).

The matters with which ‘literature’ deal are classed as ‘narratives’. Narratives concern feelings and the vicissitudes of human life, this is in contrast to ‘logico-scientific’ reporting and discussion of facts and mechanical causation (Bruner, 1986). Narratives, in focusing on first person experiences and phenomena, will be harder to ‘calibrate’ than representations of publicly accessible phenomena. However, there must be some success in calibrating even those, otherwise the language lexicon could not contain terms referring to such phenomena as purpose, hopes, fears, etc.

Language and texts have conferred survival value to societies. There seems little doubt that some ‘meanings’ are codifiable in a way that information is not. Whilst the meaning of a sign, text or data is always specific to each individual ‘reader’ at some moment in time, place etc, something generally meaningful can be codified in such a way as to transcend time and space—without that possibility, there could be no such thing as language and writing.

## Review

It is clear that knowledge has some elements that can be codified and transferred, albeit with some risk of error, to readers with sufficient shared Background understandings. If ‘knowledge’ is considered to reside in people as a ‘capacity’ to ‘respond’ correctly to situations, then that which is stored in codified forms such as books, and popularly considered to be knowledge, is more appropriately considered to be data which have the potential to become information which may change the response capacity, or knowledge, of an appropriately competent individual.

Information changes knowledge by adding to it, restructuring it or recontextualising it. Information and knowledge are context-specific in that they depend on the situation and are created dynamically in the social interaction between people. As has already been established, the meaning of a text, or an utterance, is not in the physics, it inheres in the intentionality of the participants.

In order for a person to exploit some codified information and turn it into actionable knowledge, they not only need the appropriate Background, including cultural and domain knowledge and expertise, they also require the capacity to change their behaviour. It has been argued that organisations also have elements (procedures, directives, structures etc) which influence the behaviour of people and which may therefore be considered to embody the ‘knowledge’ of the enterprise. Thus, if an enterprise is fully to exploit any new or transferred knowledge, it too must have the capacity to change the institutional arrangements which embody that knowledge at any given time. Thus, the ability to change manuals, procedures and structures will be a critical element in the ability of an enterprise to fully husband and exploit its ‘knowledge’.

There are powerful lessons for knowledge managers in the above observations. For example, in relation to ‘data warehousing’, knowledge inventories, knowledge repositories etc, the fact that only the explicit part of knowledge can be codified must be appreciated and efforts made to understand and indicate what the tacit component may be and how it can be regained. The knowledge audit approaches which treat not only written materials but also specific individuals as repositories of knowledge clearly begin to address this area, but attention must also be paid to the extent to which other organisational artefacts, such as departmental structures and procedures also embody knowledge. For example, the protection of something that can be codified is a different proposition from that which resides in some less tangible form such as a skilled ‘capacity to behave’. Programmes of ‘downsizing’ which encourage, for example, all those with more than 30 years service to leave have been found to devastate the organisation’s store of tacit expertise.

In addition, the hermeneutic and epistemological aspects of knowledge recovery must ensure account is taken of the multiplicity of viewpoints and agendas of potential ‘readers’ of the stored material. Attempts to remove all ambiguity are here shown to be philosphically flawed due to heavy influence of context and reader aspectivity upon the conversion from information to knowledge. The critical perspective also highlights the coercive and potentially destructive aspects of attempting to force a consensus where diversity is essential to both goodwill and adaptability. These points highlight the danger of seeking to homogenise the data in repositories in the interests of so-called consistency.

## Conclusions

Viewpoints have been discussed which suggest good theoretical reasons why:

(i) Knowledge may be codified into texts and artefacts but only functions in people.

There are dangers in presuming that knowledge management can simply concentrate upon what can be codified and stored. Whilst this may accord with what some may consider ‘common knowledge’, making clear why this is relevant is intended to help those wishing to do something positive about it.

(ii) People are critical elements in any knowledge management system. This is because:

– only competent human readers with the right Background of general and domain skills can recover knowledge from its codified forms,

– only humans can contribute or develop the tacit knowledge necessary to operationalise the explicit knowledge regained from codified knowledge forms.

– only humans can cope with the unavoidable ambiguity of language which is the major medium for codification of knowledge.

Each reason may suggest refinements to knowledge management practices, but, these are likely to be strongly situation specific in nature. At the most general level, the presumption that all users will be the same as one another and as the developers of the systems must be rooted out and abandoned.

(iii) Enterprise cultures must shift from viewing knowledge purely as an object independent of human subjects to accepting that some critical knowledge is a human capacity only partly codifiable and transferable by mechanical means.

Care must be taken to recognise the importance of individuals, not just as domain experts but as repositories of more generally required Background and tacit knowledge. Intellectual property rights can only be established for material which is codifiable, protecting tacit knowledge will require other means to encourage its holders to remain accessible.

(iv) Enterprise cultures must see capacity to change internal structures and systems as a critical success factor in the effective exploitation of their knowledge resources.

This is highlighting that all institutionalised aspects of an enterprise are also forms of codified knowledge, and as such must be both recognised as such and recognised to be just as liable to change as other forms. Failure to recognise this and permit organisational structures and procedures to change is a particularly insidious and deep-seated block to organisational exploitation of advancing knowledge.
