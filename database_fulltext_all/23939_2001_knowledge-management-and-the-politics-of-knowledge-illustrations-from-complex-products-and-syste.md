# Knowledge management and the politics of knowledge: illustrations from complex products and systems

N Marshall\* and T Brady

Complex Product Systems Innovation Centre, University of Brighton, E Sussex, UK

While there is an increasing recognition of the social character of knowledge in segments of the literature on knowledge management, this paper argues that there has been a limited engagement with issues surrounding the politics of knowledge. There is now a well-established critique of technically-led knowledge management practices in which knowledge is treated in passive, objectivised, and static terms. A key element of this critique concerns the limits of codification strategies directed at making tacit knowledge explicit. This is founded upon an interpretative conceptualisation of inter-subjective understanding as always provisional, incomplete, and embedded in contexts of social action. While sympathetic to this position, it is argued that there is more to considering knowledge practices than the problem of understanding. Shared understanding, where participants to an interaction achieve a satisfactory interpretation of their respective positions, in no way implies mutual agreement about the validity of these positions. Drawing on insights from Habermas about models of social action and knowledge-guiding interests, we offer a series of concepts encouraging sensitivity to the relationship between knowledge, power, and interests. Aspects of the argument are then illustrated with case examples drawn from research into organisational knowledge in companies producing and using complex products and systems. European Journal of Infor mation Systems (2001) 10, 99–112.

## Introduction

Central to the literature on knowledge management is the claim that within organisations there ‘lies, unknown and untapped, a vast treasure house of knowledge, knowhow and best practices’ which can contribute a significant source of added value and competitive advantage if only they could be ‘leveraged’ (O’Dell & Grayson, 1998, p. 154). The key questions of knowledge management have thus become how far and through what means it is possible to tap into this fund of under-exploited organisational knowledge. There are those who assign a privileged position to new information technologies which are portrayed as providing the foundation for capturing, storing, and disseminating this knowledge (eg Haerem & Sgholt, 1995; Dutta, 1997; Clark, 1998; Moad, 1998; Lotus Development Corporation, 1998; Gore & Gore, 1999; Gupta et al, 2000). These accounts, while sometimes acknowledging that there are important, tacit, implicit, and routinised dimensions to knowledge and action within organisations, believe that it is possible and desirable to capture such knowledge by converting it into an explicit, codified form which can then be shared across the pathways of digital networks. This assumption has been challenged by a number of authors who have argued that the preoccupation with codification distracts attention away from the multifaceted, dynamic, provisional, and socially-situated character of knowledge under particular activity settings (eg Blackler, 1995; Cook & Brown, 1999; Gherardi, 1999; Pan & Scarbrough, 1999; Scarbrough et al, 1999; Swan et al, 1999).

Much of what might be called the codification debate (cf Ancori et al, 2000) revolves around the problem of inter-subjective understanding. For those critical of an unqualified drive towards codification, it is important to appreciate the social context of understanding on the grounds that joint participation in specific activity settings helps in the development of shared interpretative resources out of which mutual understanding can emerge. While we broadly agree with this position, this paper attempts to move beyond the problem of understanding to consider the political character of knowledge. This is based on a recognition that even when the problem of understanding has been solved and participants in interaction reach a satisfactory interpretation of each other’s position, in no way does this imply mutual agreement about the validity of such positions. Consequently, it is essential to consider the conditions promoting or inhibiting such agreement between participants. In attempting to do this the paper engages critically with the work of Habermas (1972, 1974, 1984, 1989, 1991)

concerning alternative models of action and knowledgeguiding interests to provide a series of concepts encouraging sensitivity to the interest positions and power relations associated with knowledge practices. We then attempt to illustrate some of the key features of this discussion with reference to examples drawn from research into organisational knowledge and learning in companies involved in the production and use of complex products and systems. In so doing, we hope to contribute to the somewhat limited volume of existing research which does not attempt to downplay the role of power and organisational politics in knowledge practices (eg Orlikowski, 1992; Coopey, 1996; Myers & Young, 1997; Ciborra & Patriotta, 1998; Olesen & Myers, 1999; Schwartz, 1999).

## Knowledge management and contexts of understanding

There has been a shift in the literature on knowledge management since its emergence as a popular area of investigation from the mid-1990s onwards. From an initially enthusiastic and largely uncritical focus on the potential for new information and communication technologies (ICTs) to unlock and optimise the knowledge assets of organisations, accounts of knowledge management have become more diverse and less credulous. While admitting that ICTs have opened up new possibilities for the storage, transfer, retrieval, and processing of data and information, a growing number of commentators are unconvinced by the enthusiastic claims made by ICT-led knowledge management approaches. Their critique of technologically biased accounts has done much to address the hitherto neglected social dimension in knowledge management, drawing in part on existing work in areas such as organisational learning, memory, and cognition (Argyris & Scho¨n, 1978; Levitt & March, 1988; Cohen & Levinthal, 1990; Easterby-Smith, 1997; Bain, 1998; Gherardi et al, 1998), the sociology of knowledge (Berger & Luckman, 1966; Latour, 1999), resource-based theories of the firm (Prahalad & Hamel, 1990; Amit & Schoemaker, 1993; Connor & Prahalad, 1996), evolutionary economics (Nelson & Winter, 1982; Winter, 1987; Teece, 1998, 2000), and studies of professional knowledge and knowledge-intensive work (Blackler, 1995; Fleck, 1996, 1997).

Cook and Brown (1999) have provided a particularly useful contribution to the comprehensive critique directed at the epistemological underpinnings of ICTdominated accounts of knowledge management. They argue that ICT-led approaches prioritise an epistemology of possession whereby knowledge is treated in static, objectified, abstract, and explicit terms. Following such an epistemology, knowledge management appears as a largely technical issue of how to extract appropriate information and deliver it to where it is needed. This leads to an impoverished understanding of the crucial social dimensions of knowledge as situated, contingent, and context-dependent, constituted through negotiated processes of inter-subjective meaning formation and shaped through mutually intelligible communicative practices (Lave & Wenger, 1991; Orlikowski & Yates, 1994; Blackler, 1995; Weick, 1995; Gherardi, 1999).

An epistemology of possession diverts attention away from knowing as a dynamic activity involving the continuous creation, reproduction, modification, and destruction of streams of meaning under specific contexts of action. For this reason, Cook and Brown (1999) emphasise the importance of an epistemology of practice. They argue that knowing does ‘not mean something that is used in action or something necessary to action, but rather something that is a part of action . . . Knowing is dynamic, concrete, and relational’ (Cook & Brown, 1999, p 387, emphasis original). Neither an epistemology of possession nor one of practice is itself sufficient. Instead, they argue for an interplay between the two, a generative dance between knowledge and knowing.

Many accounts of knowledge management tend to prioritise an epistemology of possession over one of practice. Thus, while much of the knowledge management literature, following Nonaka (1991, 1994) and Nonaka and Takeuchi (1995), recognises a distinction between tacit and explicit knowledge, the emphasis is typically on finding ways of surfacing, externalising, or converting the former into the latter so that it can be captured, transferred, and re-used (eg APQC, 1996, 1998; Clark, 1998; Davenport & Prusak, 1998; Greengard, 1998; Gore & Gore, 1999; Lim et al, 1999). Codification or the production of mutually interpretable symbolic structures is, of course, an important precondition and outcome of any communicative practice. However, it is doubtful whether a simplistic preoccupation with converting tacit into explicit knowledge can get us terribly far in understanding the multifaceted and situated character of organisational knowledge practices.

There is a real sense in which such arguments about tacit and explicit knowledge are effectively miscast. There is a tendency to depict these forms of knowledge as substitutable equivalents, rather than as co-existent and inter-penetrating dimensions in the process of knowing. For some, following Polanyi’s (1958, 1966) characterisation of tacit knowing in the maxim ‘We can know more than we can tell’ (Polanyi, 1966, p 4), it is meaningless to speak of articulating what by its nature is inarticulable (cf Winter, 1987; Teece, 1998). Gherardi (1999) has criticised the tacit-explicit knowledge conversion thesis for abstracting from the ever-present practical dimension of any knowing act where tacit knowledge is a precondition for all knowledge (see also Cowan & Foray, 1997). This is closer to Polanyi’s original intention when he speaks of tacit knowing as an act of indwelling (Polanyi & Prosch, 1975).

To some extent, the critique of codification has highlighted the individual, cognitive limits on articulating and assimilating knowledge (cf Shariq, 1999; Steinmueller, 2000). However, any cognitive approach needs to be supplemented by a conceptualisation of the social dimension of understanding which recognises that the inter-subjective interpretation of communicative acts is a necessary condition for building collective knowledge. This is close to the concerns of the hermeneutic tradition, which has provided useful insights into the conditions that make possible the formation of shared understanding. Writers such as Schu¨tz (1962, 1972) and Gadamer (1976, 1989) have argued that in order to participate with adequate competence in inter-subjective acts of reaching understanding, actors must have access to shared linguistic and interpretative resources, including relevant aspects of what Schu¨tz and Luckmann (1973) called the social stock of knowledge (cf Kogut & Zander, 1992). This taken-for-granted knowledge is socially distributed and forms the background assumptions, typificatory schemes, and symbolic resources which individuals in interaction draw upon. Through a ‘fusion of horizons’ (Gadamer, 1989) or synthesis of background knowledges (Schu¨tz, 1972), the mutual understanding of ego and alter can emerge. As Lash (1999, pp 150–151) explains, ‘the more closely contexts of experience match, the more possible it is to achieve simultaneous flow of experience’.

Others broadly within the hermeneutic tradition have regarded the formation of shared understanding as more problematic. Heidegger (1962), for example, argued that since we inevitably bring presuppositions to acts of interpretation, this suggests an arbitrary or at least continuously revisable aspect to interpretation. Ricoeur (1967, 1970) highlighted the interpretative ambiguity surrounding symbolic expressions, creating the need for a hermeneutics both of suspicion and belief, ‘willingness to suspect [whether we are imposing our own meanings], willingness to listen’ (Ricoeur, 1970, p 27). Furthermore, he spoke of the ‘autonomy of the text’, as distinct from speech acts, as involving a distancing of texts from their original context of creation, making it difficult to recapture the author’s original intention (Ricouer, 1988). Derrida goes even further by problematising the whole notion of authorial intention. He is constantly at pains to expose ambiguities and ambivalences in writing that derive, not from lapses in argumentation, but rather from conditions of usage of which the author is unaware and which are beyond his or her control (eg Derrida, 1976, 1978, 1981). Even where an author takes great care to specify the sense with which a particular word is being used, alternative senses inescapably reappear, wanted or not, in the form of a trace. Linguistic meaning is never complete and final, based on an unproblematic presence in words. It is unstable and open to potentially infinite interpretation and reinterpretation in an unending play of substitution.

These debates are relevant for knowledge management because they emphasise the limits to understanding associated with any attempt at communication, either through face-to-face interaction or the more distanced medium of texts. This provides a necessary corrective to the assumption of the transparency of meaning associated with static, object-like depictions of knowledge. However, pace Derrida, the theoretical possibility of an infinite play of substitution has to be tempered by the recognition that there are always countervailing influences which effect closure on the field of possible meaning. Certainly it is unrealistic to expect the total elimination of interpretative ambiguity, but it is equally misplaced to deny the existence of some level of intersubjective understanding between competent actors as an everyday practical accomplishment. It is the participation, either directly or virtually, in a shared context which provides the conditions of possibility for this accomplishment.

Within the literature on organisational knowledge, this has been alluded to by Cowan et al (2000, p 234) with their notion of ‘epistemic communities’ which ‘at the very least accept some commonly understood procedural authority as essential to the success of their collective knowledge-building activities’. Contextualist accounts of organisational knowledge have also supported the broadening out of conceptions of knowledge from the narrow concern with converting tacit into explicit knowledge. Blackler (1995), for example, has characterised knowledge as multifaceted, involving a complex interplay between a number of different dimensions (embrained, embodied, encultured, embedded, and encoded).

## From understanding to the politics of knowledge

. . . it would be better to think of the whole process of understanding as a violent conflict . . . Understanding is not a fusion of horizons, or a reconciliation between the interpreter and her ‘object’. It is a process of mutual questioning.

(Sonderegger, 1997, p 205, emphasis original)

Thus far the argument has focused upon the critique of ICT-dominated accounts of knowledge management, highlighting the importance of a conception of knowledge as embedded in specific social contexts and action settings. Central to this has been an examination of varying attempts to address the issue of inter-subjective understanding. However, the problem of understanding is not the whole story. As Rorty (2000) has pointed out, just because individuals have reached a satisfactory shared understanding on an issue, this does not mean that they necessarily agree with what each other has to say. It is important to acknowledge the potential for interests to diverge even when understandings converge. Despite important limitations (see below), the work of Habermas provides a fertile source of insights about the relationship between knowledge and interests, the most developed statement of which can be found in his theory of communicative action.

Habermas supplies a series of foundational concepts which are helpful in theorising the complex and incomplete connections between knowledge, beliefs, and action. Two key notions are models of action, reflecting different types of rationality, and knowledge-guiding interests, which partly find their expression through differing models of action. Drawing inspiration from Weber’s distinction between alternative rationalities underpinning different types of conduct (purposively rational, value rational, affective, and traditional) (Weber, 1978), Habermas works up a multiple conception of rationality which subsumes, but extends well beyond, the conventional equation of rationality with instrumental reason (cf Macy & Flache, 1995) and its preoccupation with the ‘performance principle’ (Marcuse, 1956).

While some commentators focus solely on Habermas’ distinction between action orientated to success (purposively rational action) and that orientated to reaching understanding (communicative action) (cf Lodh & Gaffikin, 1987; Myers & Young, 1997; Stephens & Cobb, 1999), Habermas in fact outlines five main models of action: teleological, strategic, normatively-regulated, dramaturgical, and communicative. He also introduces the associated notion of knowledge-guiding interests, comprising technical, practical, and emancipatory variants (Habermas, 1972).

Teleological and strategic action are alternative manifestations of instrumental or purposively rational action involving means-ends calculations aimed at maximising expectations of utility. Teleological action refers to attempts by actors to achieve ends through the choice and application of appropriate means. Strategic action is where the anticipation of decisions on the part of at least one additional goal-directed actor enters an agent’s calculation of success (Habermas, 1984, p 84). Underlying these forms of action are predominantly technical knowledge-guiding interests, concerned with explanation, prediction, and control. In normatively-regulated action, by contrast, members of social groups orientate their actions towards common values. These find their expression in norms denoting particular expectations of behaviour which are actively reproduced and sometimes modified through the participation of actors in interaction (cf Giddens, 1984). Drawing on Goffman (1959), Habermas (1984, p 90) refers to dramaturgical action ‘as an encounter in which participants form a visible public for each other and perform for one another’, presenting themselves in particular ways. Crucially, as Goffman argued, not all (or even most) performances are driven by full intentionality but instead occur without conscious attention as actors adjust to different social settings and role expectations. The normatively-regulated and dramaturgical models of action are more associated with practical rather than technical knowledge-guiding interests. They are about the everyday participation of actors in social settings where the key issue is one of inter-subjective understanding occurring largely unreflexively, or in what Schu¨tz (1972) called the natural attitude.

Recognising the limitations of accounts which refer solely to instrumental and/or practical rationality, Habermas introduces a third set of emancipatory knowledgeguiding interests. This is the most provocative and contentious aspect of Habermas’ theory of communicative action. In contrast to earlier generations of critical theorists, who were generally pessimistic about the possibility of rekindling the emancipatory potential of modernity (eg Marcuse, 1956, Adorno, 1973; Adorno & Horkheimer, 1979), Habermas argues that a potential for emancipation remains based on a mode of reason which has not been atrophied and is not subject to an ‘abridgement of meaning’ (Marcuse, 1964, p 87), closely parallelling accounts of reflexive modernisation (eg Giddens, 1991; Beck, 1992; Beck et al, 1994; Lash, 1999). The resilient emancipatory potential of reason can be found in actions genuinely orientated towards reaching shared understanding free of the distortions which normally accompany communicative acts. Such communicative action involves what Habermas terms an ideal speech situation founded on the exacting condition of argumentation whereby communicative participants ‘have to presuppose in general that the structure of their communication . . . excludes all force—except the force of the better argument’ (Habermas, 1984, p 25).

It is difficult not to agree with critics of Habermas that situating the basis for emancipation in a communicative reason is problematic (eg Lyotard, 1984, 1986; Greisch, 1999; Rorty, 2000). This potential is supposed to derive from the allegedly symmetrical character of all communicative acts. ‘The supposition of a common objective world is built into the pragmatics of every single linguistic usage. And the dialogue roles of every speech situation enforce a symmetry in participant perspectives’ (Habermas, 1992, p 138). This ostensible symmetry leads Habermas to propose the possibility of a universal validity capable of transcending purely local standards. He is deeply suspicious of any unqualified contextualism which he sees as inevitably leading to a relativism unable to distinguish between competing validity claims and incapable of supplying what he regards as a necessary moral ground.

However, as Rorty (2000, p 16) has persuasively argued, Habermas is in danger of running ‘together the need for coherence and for justification which is required if one is to use language at all, and a commitment to . . .

‘universal validity’, a commitment which can only be consistently acted upon by aiming at the sort of domination-free communication which is impossible as long as there are human communities which remain exclusivist’. While there is much to sympathise with in the critique of Habermas’ attempt to ‘clarify the universal core of our moral intuitions’ (Habermas, 1990, p 211), this does not prevent a more modest reading of his work as a useful way of thinking about the interest-laden nature of knowledge. One does not have to accept the argument for a universal validity built into the structure of communicative action to agree with Habermas’ portrayal of the interplay between knowledge and interests where the demanding conditions of truly communicative reason are rarely if ever met (cf Benhabib, 1992).

As well as scaling down the universalistic ambitions of Habermas’s programme, we would also suggest that his ideas about social action need to be augmented to yield intermediate conceptions which are more amenable to the study of actual knowledge practices. There are instances where it appears that competing models of social action take the form of monolithic blocks rubbing up against each other, as exemplified by the tension he introduces between system and lifeworld. The potential for the coexistence of multiple rationalities and bases of action, often within a single individual, remains generally unconsidered. Associated with this is the danger of a prioritisation of rationality, however expanded and modified, which might be taken to imply a portrayal of the subject as constituting a ‘homogeneous, perfectly lucid identity’ (Lechte, 1994, p 190). Indeed, the question of identity hardly receives any attention in Habermas. This is unfortunate because knowledge practices and identity-forming processes are frequently interwoven.

The ideas of Maffesoli are informative here as he questions the transparent unity of self, indicating instead the construction of multiple identities through engagement in diverse social aggregations: ‘Participating in a multitude of tribes, which are themselves interrelated, allows each person to live his or her intrinsic plurality. These various ‘masks’ are ordered in a more or less conflictual way and fit together with other surrounding ‘masks’’ (Maffesoli, 1995, p 147). This opens the way for a conceptualisation of knowledge practices which does not regard them as being driven in any straightforward fashion by coherent or unitary knowledge-guiding interests, but instead permits an appreciation of the identity-defining struggles which individuals and groups are often engaged in attempting to resolve (cf Levinas, 1981; Lamont & Fournier, 1992).

Within the organisational knowledge literature, Fleck (1996) has gone some way in considering the relationship between claims to expert knowledge and group identity. His concept of the credibility cycle, for example, depicts how the validation of expert knowledge is conditioned by the emergence of identity-affirming group processes. Power relations have a central part to play in this cycle. In the most developed cases it becomes virtually self-generating and expertise may become effectively sedimented if expert groups are themselves able to define the criteria of value and efficacy through which expertise is recognised. This provides an important reminder that questions of power are never far away where competing knowledge claims are at stake. Combined with an understanding of the interestladen nature of knowledge practices, the notions of identity construction and power relations provide the point of departure to consider three illustrative cases.

## Illustrations from complex products and systems

The following case examples illustrate the importance of proceeding from a conceptualisation of knowledge practices which not only takes into account the role of social context and participation in activity settings for inter-subjective understanding, and the attendant limits to codification, but also considers the frequent organisational reality of divergent interests, political struggles, and power relations. In each case it should become clear that accounts of knowledge management which promote an individuated view of information processes, treating knowledge in objectivised and passive terms, are poorly placed to interpret the crucially social character of knowledge practices. These illustrations also provide further fuel for the now well-established critique of approaches which encourage a view of technologies, including IT, as neutral tools whose implementation and use is driven solely by instrumental or efficiency considerations.

The three cases are drawn from an investigation into inter-project learning and knowledge management in companies providing and using what have been termed complex products and systems (CoPS) (Hobday, 1998). The term CoPS is used to refer to capital-, engineering-, and IT-intensive, business-to-business products, networks, constructs, and systems, examples of which include telecommunications systems, aircraft, air traffic control systems, baggage handling systems, and ‘intelligent’ buildings.

The typically project-based nature of CoPS production generates a number of important implications for knowledge practices in these industries. The one-off or small batch nature of these products and systems, involving high levels of customisation to specific customer and end-user needs, makes it difficult to re-use and incrementally refine technological solutions from one project to the next (cf March et al, 1991; Bonaccorsi et al, 1996). Certainly there is an evolving base of technological and organisational knowledge and competences which provides the necessary foundation upon which to build customised product solutions. However, these foundational knowledge resources typically have to come together in novel assemblages in order to meet the particular requirements of each project. This lends an open-ended and emergent character to the project process, further heightened by uncertainties and contingencies associated with the problem of predicting the interactions and knock-on effects of attempting to integrate diverse components and sub-systems into a complex whole. Consequently, the basic, albeit product-specific, knowledge of the project organisation typically needs to be supplemented by an ability to draw upon these resources in a creative and responsive manner. This raises the relevance of meta-knowledge, or knowledge about how to apply knowledge within given settings, taking the form of heuristics, recipe knowledge, and intuitions developed through practical experience of participating in CoPS projects.

The multi-functional and multi-disciplinary nature of projects needed to cope with the scope and technological sophistication of product offerings also has important implications for knowledge practices. Projects tend to be composed of a diversity of functions (eg planning, design, implementation, commissioning, contracts, finance, human resource management, etc) and technical disciplines (eg electrical and electronic engineering, mechanical engineering, structural engineering, software engineering, systems integration etc). The identity of these groupings is often constructed, regulated, and maintained through institutional mechanisms, such as membership of professional associations. Moreover, they tend to develop their own communication communities with elements of shared language and overlapping value and belief systems (cf Wittgenstein, 1953). Consequently, a central problematic for projects concerns the integration and co-ordination of these diverse identity groups and knowledge domains with the intention of negotiating, agreeing, and realising project requirements. This integration is by no means assured. Not only is there the frequent possibility of difficulties in communication and understanding between different groups, such as engineers explaining detailed design proposals to nonspecialist managers, but also the real threat of incompatible interest positions which are not easy to resolve. This is often exacerbated by the involvement of multiple organisations where disputes over the performance of respective actions and the distribution of commercial gains and losses often come to the fore.

The following cases are taken from a 3-year study, conducted between 1998 and 2001, involving two complementary styles of investigation performed in parallel. The first was an international interview-based survey of inter-project learning practices involving companies in Europe, North America, and Japan. While this part of the research was designed to explore the breadth of alternative practices and approaches to project learning, involving a total of 45 companies representing a range of CoPS activities, the intention was not to do this entirely at the expense of the depth of investigation. For this reason, the survey was based on face-to-face, indepth, and semi-structured interviews with at least one informant drawn from each of three organisational levels: a senior manager, a project manager, and a project practitioner; although, where possible, opportunities to conduct more interviews were taken. The aim here was to get some insight into the perception of project learning practices at different points of the organisation. Each interview lasted approximately 90 minutes. Relevant company documentation was also collected and those interviewed were subsequently asked to complete a short written questionnaire. Depending on the practicality of making a return visit, the results of the interviews and questionnaires were reported back to the companies, providing an opportunity for the findings to be validated. These data provided the basis for the development of a series of mini case examples, and it is from these that the following illustrations were selected.

The second aspect of the research, not reported here, involved much more intensive engagements with six collaborating companies representing a range of activities spanning aircraft engines, mobile and fixed telecommunications, simulation equipment, point-to-point communication equipment, and airport operations. Here more in-depth and longitudinal studies were possible, involving repeat visits to conduct semi-structured interviews with managers and engineers at a variety of levels, observe work activities, and collect documentation. This dimension of the research also involved joint participation with the companies to try to develop approaches and tools to assist with inter-project learning. Evidently, the more extensive survey component of the research could not be expected to supply the same detail of analysis as these more intensive engagements, involving as it did a compromise between breadth and depth. However, despite their limitations, we nevertheless believe that the mini case examples give a reasonable flavour of the main contours of knowledge and learning within the participating organisations. These three particular examples were selected because they bring differing aspects of the conceptual issues outlined above into sharp relief, representing as they do contrasting images of the nature of knowledge and learning, the role of codification, and the politics of organisation. In the interests of confidentiality, the companies referred to have been given assumed names.

## DesignConsult

DesignConsult is a medium-sized engineering design consultancy engaged in a broad range of civil and structural engineering activities, providing design and management services for projects as diverse as maritime structures, roads, railway stations, bridges, and industrial plant. It was in the process of rolling out a knowledge management initiative centred around an Intranet-based real-time project reporting system where problem-solving and lessons learned are captured, codified, and archived for future reference. For senior management, who were strongly supportive of the initiative, this ITintensive knowledge management system was introduced to reduce what was perceived as an excessive dependence on the knowledge and experience of a limited number of senior engineers and project managers. The intention was to capture, depersonalise, and distribute these expert knowledge bases. Hitherto, much of the work surrounding projects involved intensive face-toface communication for exchanging information and joint problem-solving. Social networks had evolved in which these key engineers and managers occupied nodal positions. A junior engineer recounted how, since joining the company, she had undergone a gradual process of acceptance into different networks until she developed the social knowledge necessary for knowing who to approach about particular issues.

Two important aspects of these networks are relevant to the current discussion. Firstly, the construction of expertise is such that the knowledge contributed by those recognised as experts is collectively valued, which in turn is a source of status (and power) for those individuals. This means that they are able to exert considerable influence in decision-making activities. For these experts it appears that the issue of hoarding knowledge does not arise because the very act of sharing experience is affirmative of their expert status. It is an act with performative content in which particular types of technical and experience-based knowledge form the currency of value. However, this is not to say that competing interests were irrelevant in this case. Indeed, the second aspect concerns the boundedness of these networks. What often disappears from view in less sophisticated accounts of communities of practice is the fact that socia processes regulating inclusion necessarily imply converse processes of exclusion (cf Gherardi, 1999). While exhibiting a certain amount of boundary spanning, the social interactions within DesignConsult were largely concentrated within product areas, around which the organisation was primarily structured. Those interviewed identified the existence of strong ‘internal walls’ and compartmentalisation on the basis of product area specialisation. In part, these divisions are maintained through a defensive process of ascribing the basis of legitimate knowledge. This depends on discursive strategies emphasising the distinctive character of working in particular product areas, simultaneously downplaying any commonality between them. Thus, a senior manager explained that, despite having previous experience in designing maritime structures, his views were not listened to by those in the maritime division because he was identified as working in another product area and his expertise was not validated. This is a good example of exclusionary processes surrounding a communication community which operate and are reproduced through definitions of what does and does not constitute a competent audience. In addition, divisions between these groups are reinforced by competing interests in terms of competition for resources and relative status within the company.

Given the emergence and reproduction of compartmentalisation around specialised knowledge bases and interest groupings, it is useful to consider the likely impacts of DesignConsult’s IT-led knowledge management approach. It is possible to identify at least two competing rationalities at work here. On the one hand, the emphasis placed on extracting, codifying, storing, and distributing expert knowledge reveals an instrumentally rational, systemic bias. This is in sharp contrast to the intense, if fragmented, sociality exhibited by the dense and largely informal social networks through which work activities have traditionally been carried out in the company. Just as Habermas has proposed at the societal level, it is likely that there will be the same paradox of the irrationality of narrowly rational action if the systemic bias of the current knowledge management programme is taken to its logical conclusion. By setting itself up as a replacement to, rather than a supplement of, inter-subjective interactions and emergent problemsolving activities, it runs the risk of degrading those very social networks which are central to the company’s way of working.

However, these competing technical and social rationalities are by no means mirrored in any straightforward way in the interests and beliefs of individuals and groups. The coexistence and overlap of divergent belief systems is typified by the contradictory impulses that a senior manager was struggling to reconcile. While strongly in favour of the knowledge management initiative in its IT-dominated form, he also expressed a high degree of sensitivity to the importance of more sociallyembedded knowledge activities, highlighting in particular the role of storytelling as a way of communicating experience in a memorable and context-rich manner: ‘it all comes out in a mishmash of stories and anecdotes and jokes because that’s the way people relate’. It is too early to say what the outcome of the knowledge management initiative in its current form will be. While the degradation of social networks would be the consequence of the programme as it is formally espoused, there are likely to be countervailing influences in practice. There were, for example, indications that the takeup of the new system by people in their day-to-day work activities was limited, effectively by-passing it altogether. There is also little reason to believe that the introduction of the new IT system, hoped by senior management to promote integration, will do anything to reduce the barriers between different product groups. Indeed, the present situation is one of parallelism in which the different groups are developing their own largely independent Intranet-based resources.

## WaterCo

WaterCo is a large privatised water utility company. It was entering the second 5-year period of a major investment programme aimed at ensuring compliance with the European Urban Waste Water Treatment Directive, supervised by the UK water industry regulator, Ofwat. This company was also in the early stages of implementing a new knowledge management initiative. Again a central role was accorded to the IT infrastructure supporting this initiative, taking the form of an Intranetbased feedback system and document archive, intended as a central repository for collecting information from a wide variety of existing sources identified in a ‘knowledge mapping’ exercise (eg project reviews, incident reports, internal audits, value management workshops, etc). In addition, there was a strong emphasis on soliciting input from employees at all levels in the organisation through an individual feedback system where people were encouraged to submit any suggestion, insight, or advice that they wished. Importantly, a feedback co-ordinator was appointed, responsible for acknowledging incoming contributions, categorising them, and distributing them to a number of designated subject matter experts for evaluation. Decisions would then be made about any action that might need to be taken.

Intriguingly, senior management accorded the knowledge management system an interim role in an ongoing process of setting and modifying standard procedures. It was designed to bridge the time-lag between identifying deficiencies in existing procedures and being able to change those procedures, which typically involves a lengthy process of decision-making by committee. In the intervening period, the Intranet is expected to provide a source of information about problems which have been identified but for which there are as yet no standardised solutions. Consequently, what was envisaged was a continuously moving throughput of information, with contributions being evaluated, categorised and stored, and then removed once they had been absorbed into standard procedures. This involves a process of validating knowledge, whose relevance is determined by the subject matter experts charged with the task of sifting through the contributions received. Here again the influence of instrumental rationality is clearly in evidence, driving decisions based on calculations of expected utility and displaying a strong faith in codification, formalisation, and standardisation.

However, as with the previous illustration, the grip of instrumental reason was by no means all-embracing. A project manager described the importance of close and regular social encounters for being ‘able to shape the decisions as you go’, depicting a relatively informal and emergent approach to dealing with issues. We have already seen the relevance of such open-ended processes of problem resolution in project environments characterised by novelty, uncertainty, and contingency. This project manager went on to explain that ‘most of the business gets done in the corridor, you know, as you’re passing, cup of coffee and you’ve solved the problem’. However, some managers were uncomfortable with what they saw as the accidental and unreliable character of such serendipitous encounters. Rather than encouraging a greater density of social network relations to maximise the potential pool of knowledge and experience out of which ideas could emerge in interaction, this discomfort was driving a move towards increased documentation and proceduralisation in the hope of reducing the element of chance. As we saw earlier, it is unlikely that such codification strategies will be able to provide the context-richness and iterability of direct communication between participants sharing in a common lifeworld. This may not be so problematic in relatively stable, timeunconstrained situations, but it is less suited to the rapidly changing, time-constrained, and emergent environment of complex projects. In addition, a resident engineer highlighted the role that social contact plays in affecting the perceived value of different ideas. Face-toface communication, particularly with those whom one encounters regularly, was viewed as a rich source of information not only about the ostensible subject of the exchange, but also providing clues about the status of the meanings communicated. Partly for this reason, and partly because of the temporal constraints on finding and assimilating information, this employee described a hierarchy of knowledge sources spreading outwards from the immediate environment of everyday encounters to what were seen as more distant sources.

As with the previous example, there were indications of potential contradictions and paradoxes between the differing technical and social rationalities. Again, senior management was convinced of the benefits of codification and standardisation, and yet at the same time recognised the importance of localised, idiosyncratic knowledge about the performance of plant and machinery not captured in formal specifications and standard procedures. The IT-dominated approach to knowledge management was also seen as unlikely to be effective without wide-ranging cultural change, for which a specific programme had been initiated to encourage the sharing of knowledge. The existing culture of the organisation was described by a senior manager in terms of a ‘fortress mentality’, with defensive relations between different departmental fiefdoms. Regulating access to information was one strategy through which departmental barriers could be maintained: ‘If you get some knowledge that someone doesn’t have in this business, it puts you in a privileged position that they have to come to you for it’.

As well as evidence for competing interests within the organisation, it was arguably the case that relations with external organisations, such as contractors, design consultants, and suppliers, brought potential divergences more sharply into relief. In particular, there was a perception that the interests of WaterCo, as client, and those of contractors were at times mutually incompatible. According to a project employee, the pressures of cost and time created a temptation for contractors and suppliers to cut corners, potentially threatening the quality of the project: ‘we’ve always got this problem with a contractor wanting to do something as cheaply as possible to achieve his target cost’. It was observed that this tended to be accompanied by an unwillingness to be forthcoming with information, particularly when serious defects resulted. One example was recounted in which a supplier was having difficulty meeting a delivery deadline, but was unwilling to admit that anything was wrong until it could no longer be avoided. In this case, the ‘noblame’ culture which WaterCo endorses was no guarantee that the connection between knowledge and sectional interests could be broken.

## TelCo

TelCo is a wholly-owned subsidiary of a large supplier of telecommunications equipment for both the fixed and mobile markets. Compared with the other two examples, it demonstrated greater sophistication in its programme for knowledge management, adopting what was arguably a more balanced social and technical approach. Unfortunately, having developed and partially implemented this programme, senior management support was withdrawn and it ground to a halt only just over a year after being introduced. Consequently, this provides a particularly interesting case because it involves the failure of a knowledge management approach which by all accounts appeared to offer the potential for achieving a balance between codification and personalisation, formalisation and informal interaction, proceduralisation and improvisation. While narratives of efficiency surrounded the initial introduction of the knowledge management programme, the reasons for its withdrawal were motivated by less than neutral technical-instrumental considerations and more by political manoeuvrings among senior management. After looking at the main characteristics of the discontinued knowledge management initiative at TelCo, we will consider the political conditions and power plays which led to its demise.

While a number of the individual components of the knowledge management programme existed in some form prior to its introduction, the main difference was that it attempted to promote a more systematic, holistic, and integrated approach to organisational learning and knowledge practices within the company. Key existing elements which it built upon included a process-based system for project management and software development, as well as procedures for identifying learning points from projects. The idea was to integrate these activities into quality management and process improvement practices in a more proceduralised, co-ordinated, and less ad hoc fashion. This was partly to address an existing difficulty whereby, as a project manager explained, there was a less than consistent relationship between identifying improvement opportunities from projects and actually changing the way that work was carried out. It was regularly the case that lessons learned from one project would fail to be incorporated in other projects and that learning would take a largely individualised form in terms of the experience-based knowledge of project members. Learning processes were effectively truncated because there was a failure to complete the cycle between knowledge and action. This was despite enhancements to the project reviewing process through the addition of interim appraisals at key points throughout the project instead of just a single appraisal on completion. This change in approach was intended to address the fact that there is typically an overlap in the timing of projects such that lessons learned from one project would be generated too late to have any impact on immediately subsequent projects. The potential advantages of mid-project reviews were, however, largely diluted by the truncated relationship between learning and working practices. This truncation also made the practice of documenting and archiving project appraisals less than worthwhile, particularly if one considers Bateson’s (1972) characterisation of knowledge representations as ‘a difference that makes a difference’.

The new knowledge management initiative was aimed not only at codifying, storing, and distributing learning points from projects, but crucially at attempting to create the social and organisational conditions for promoting a more productive relationship between knowledge and practice. Central to this was the introduction of a business improvement system comprising different groups responsible for steering, co-ordinating, implementing, and evaluating possible improvements. At a more strategic level, there was a senior management steering group responsible for guiding how improvements aligned with overall company goals and for making decisions on which initiatives to provide sponsorship, resources, and support for. Reporting to the steering group was an improvement management group responsible for driving the identification and analysis of possible improvements, for presenting the business case for different initiatives to the steering group, and for managing the interfaces between initiatives. These more senior groups were put in place to ensure that resources to underpin the implementation of improvements were forthcoming. According to a project manager, changes to project practices identified as desirable often did not happen because of a lack of resources and support from senior management. He argued that projects ‘lacked the political clout’ to secure access to such resources.

At the heart of the business improvement system were best practice teams and action teams located at the working level, judged by the manager responsible for the initiative to be in the best position to identify and implement improvements because of their detailed localised knowledge of processes generated through practice. Best practice teams were responsible for taking ownership of improvement initiatives, conducting detailed studies, and generating implementation plans. Action teams were then charged with actually introducing the planned improvements. To avoid the problem of failure to incorporate lessons learned into new projects, the improvement process was effectively integrated into the project process by making the adoption of specific improvement initiatives an expected part of project planning to be treated in the same way as any other project requirement, such as cost, programme, customer needs, and so on. As such, planned improvements would be subject to the same performance evaluation as other requirements.

While IT was accorded a role in supporting this change-orientated knowledge management programme, there was a clear recognition that putting an appropriate IT infrastructure in place was arguably one of the less challenging aspects of what was envisaged as a much wider overall programme. Certainly IT was regarded as a useful tool for capturing, storing, and distributing information about processes and their improvement. There was, for example, an electronic baseline library of improvements, as well as the regularly updated project management system available on the Intranet, which provided a record of project procedures and sets of project management tools and templates. However, the mere existence of a supportive IT system was seen as no guarantee that it would be used. It was in any case regarded as secondary to the structures and procedures put in place to promote organisational learning. Here as well managers demonstrated a clear understanding that the introduction of formal procedures on its own was unlikely to be sufficient for ensuring the intended changes. In part this was because, as a project manager observed, there was a difference between official processes and the way that work was actually performed. Indeed, in an analysis of learning opportunities from projects in the previous few years it was found that 80% of improvements identified in project reviews related not to the refinement or replacement of existing processes but instead to so-called process exceptions or failures to follow established procedures.

Consequently, there was a strong emphasis on the motivational conditions underpinning the new knowledge management approach. This comprised both financial rewards in the form of individual performancerelated pay linked to satisfactory participation in the improvement programme, as well as other incentives such as improved career progression, status-enhancing perks (eg attendance at external seminars, membership of professional bodies), and explicit recognition of contributions made. There was also an appreciation that formal processes should not be expected to supply a total and invariant description of work activities in all contexts. The dangers of over-prescriptive procedures were clearly understood and efforts were made to permit the flexible implementation of processes as appropriate. For this purpose, transformation guidelines were introduced to provide guidance on how generic procedures could be adapted to specific work situations. Moreover, the manager responsible for process improvements suggested that institutionalisation and routinisation were important for ensuring that new procedures were embedded into everyday working practices. It was also apparent that managers did not think it possible or desirable to crystallise idiosyncratic and highly contextualised knowledge into generic processes. This was evident in the role accorded to mentoring whereby junior engineers were paired with experienced staff and encouraged to learn through observation and joint participation in actual work situations.

Given the promising features of TelCo’s knowledge management approach, with its balanced concern for technical and social considerations, it is unfortunate and perhaps surprising that the initiative has been discontinued without any apparent successor. Certain practices, such as the project management system, remain, but the integrated and holistic character of the initiative as a whole has dissolved, along with the commitment of resources and support from senior management. The reasons for this failure can be traced to an emerging dissonance between the goals of senior management and people working at the project level, fuelled by divergent interests and inconsistent interpretative schemes. For senior management at TelCo, the interpretation of organisational reality occurs through the narrowly instrumentalist prism of short-term financial performance. This financial bias was strongly in evidence in the bargaining process whereby projects attempted to get their business plans approved by senior management. As a project manager explained, senior managers would, as a matter of course, adjust project business plans downwards to yield what they perceived as a more attractive cost proposition. There would then ensue a game of negotiation as projects attempted to claw back some resource.

There is a clear incompatibility in the interest positions adopted by both parties. Senior management was primarily concerned with reducing the headline cost of projects as far as possible. Projects, on the other hand, were keen to maximise their resources to enable them to cope with contingencies. If, in Habermas’s terms, the force of the better argument were sufficient to resolve this dispute, then by all accounts the project groups should have been in an advantageous position. With their practical participation in carrying out projects, their direct experience suggested that a narrow focus on lowest cost resulted in unrealistic project requirements, frequently leading to unfavourable budget and programme outcomes. However, this experience counted for little among senior management. Nevertheless, there was a temporary reversal of this situation following the serious failure of a project for a major client. The problems were so severe that the client itself launched an audit of TelCo and senior management came under close scrutiny. It was partly as a result of this incident that senior managers decided to support the improvement-focused knowledge management initiative outlined above. Playing no small part in this decision was the political motivation of defending their position against threats emanating from the parent company. However, the realignment of attitudes has proved to be short-lived as the memory of this project failure has faded. Financial short-termism has re-established itself as the dominant way of thinking among senior management. This created the conditions out of which the decision to discontinue the knowledge management programme emerged. Faced with a perceived intensification of competitive pressures and an associated desire to cut costs, resources were withdrawn from the initiative, which was presented as an organisational ‘luxury’. Ironically, it was on not dissimilar grounds of efficiency that it had been justified in the first place.

## Discussion and conclusions

Through these three examples we have endeavoured to illustrate different aspects of organisational knowledge practices for which the earlier theoretical discussion provided a series of sensitising concepts. In particular, they should be read in the light of the two main threads of argument developed in this paper, namely:

(1) That it is important to consider the active, provisional, and situated character of knowing whereby participation in shared contexts helps forge the necessary interpretative resources for inter-subjective understanding.

(2) That there is more to questions of organisational knowledge than the problem of inter-subjective understanding since mutual understanding between participants in a communicative practice in no way implies the necessity of mutual agreement. This highlights the need to consider the potentially divergent interests, identity projects, and power relations which influence knowledge practices.

The first dimension of the argument has crucial implications for assessing the appropriateness of knowledge management initiatives which focus too narrowly on the technical capacities of IT for capturing, storing, and distributing information while paying scant attention to how this information is interpreted and used. The contextdependent nature of interpretation comprises one aspect of a critique of codification which has emphasised the limits of a static, object-like, and decontextualised portrayal of knowledge. It was precisely such a treatment of knowledge which predominated in the first two illustrations. The knowledge management initiatives at DesignConsult and WaterCo were primarily focused on implementing IT systems as vehicles for capturing, retaining, and disseminating information. While in itself this is not something with which to take exception, it becomes more problematic when it is set up as a replacement to, rather than an enabler or supplement for, ongoing social interaction. This was especially evident in the first case where the successful implementation of the knowledge management initiative would have run the risk of degrading the informal social networks upon which project activities and knowledge practices were based. Frequent and typically face-to-face communication is especially suited to complex project environments because it permits sufficient density, iterability, and context-richness to evolve emergent solutions to ambiguous or changing problems. The third case, with its more balanced socio-technical approach, thus appeared to offer a more adequate basis upon which to support knowledge practices under conditions of complexity.

The second strand of the argument concerning interests, power, and identity is arguably something which has received less attention in the knowledge management literature. However, the examples have demonstrated that it is limiting to consider knowledge practices in isolation from the interest positions, power relations, and identity projects through which they are conditioned, and which in turn they help to construct. Drawing on the work of Habermas, this paper has offered one way of thinking about the relationship between knowledge and interests. Through the case examples we have tried to illustrate different aspects of this relationship. A particularly important distinction was drawn between actions guided by narrowly technical-instrumental rationalities and those influenced by socio-practical considerations.

This distinction was relevant to all three cases. In the first two illustrations, it took the form of divergent interpretations about what form a knowledge management initiative should take. Broadly speaking, there was a dissonance between the faith in technical solutions emphasised by those responsible for introducing ITdominated initiatives and the strongly social accounts of action emphasised by project members. The third example was different in that the short-lived knowledge management initiative appeared to be seeking an accommodation between social and technical rationalities. However, the conditions surrounding the discontinuation of this initiative can be read as involving the reassertion of an instrumentalist bias in the form of the preoccupation among senior management of short-term financial performance. Having said this, it is crucial not to interpret the apparent dominance of technical or instrumental rationality according to its own terms of reference. By this we mean that instrumentalist narratives are rarely all pervading and self-contained, able to provide their own grounds for justification.

This is reflected in three features of the cases. Firstly, competing technical and social knowledge claims tended not to be resolved, largely in favour of the former, purely by recourse to instrumentalist arguments, but were also influenced by political manoeuvrings and relations of power. This came through particularly clearly in the third case in the form of the asymmetrical relationship between senior and project management. Secondly, the pursuit of technical-instrumental action by no means implies the total elimination of social knowledge-guiding interests and forms of action. Despite the technicallybiased approaches outlined in the first two cases, it was evident that the intense sociality of organisational relations proved irrepressible, making itself felt in the processes through which different groups constructed their identity and attempted to secure their interests and knowledge-claims from external threat. Finally, the incomplete and contested nature of instrumental reason also emerged at the individual level in the form of an internalised struggle by managers to reconcile their promotion of technically-dominated knowledge management initiatives with their practical experience of embeddedness in dynamic social relations.

We would emphasise the following key implications. On the one hand, we strongly support the continuation of attempts to provide a more contextualised theorisation of inter-subjective understanding as a necessary corrective to the obsession with codifying tacit knowledge. On the other hand, it is crucial to move beyond a concern with the interpretative dimension of knowledge practices to embrace an understanding of the relationship between knowledge, interests, power, and identity. In short, we believe in the necessity of a greater engagement with and sensitivity to the politics of knowledge.

He is a Principal Research Fellow in the Complex Product Systems Innovation Centre at CENTRIM, University of Brighton and his current interests are about how learning and capability building take place in CoPS firms. Prior to joining CENTRIM in 1994, Tim was a research fellow at the School of Management at Bath University, and SPRU, University of Sussex.
