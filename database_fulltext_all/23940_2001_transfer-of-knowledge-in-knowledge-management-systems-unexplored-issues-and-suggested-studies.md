# Transfer of knowledge in knowledge management systems: unexplored issues and suggested studies

GP Huber\*

The University of Texas at Austin, McCombs School of Business, B6300, Austin, TX 78712, USA

The management practice literature is replete with reports of practices being used to motivate a firm’s knowledge workers to transfer knowledge into and out of the firm’s computer resident knowledge repositories, ie, to participate with commitment in the firm’s Knowledge Management System. Unfortunately, little is known with any certainty about which of these practices have what effects under which conditions. It appears that in many cases the practices are ill suited for the particular situations where they are employed, with unknown but perhaps sizeable losses in opportunities foregone because valuable knowledge is not as fully or completely transferred as is possible. In addition, it seems that some of these practices are likely to be interfering with the effectiveness of other practices, just as some drugs interfere with the potentially positive effects of other drugs. About these matters, our knowledge is exceeded by our ignorance. The paper identifies some major problems associated with knowledge transfer, and articulates some of the most important issues associated with these problems. Eight research questions are raised that, if answered with sound studies, would enable organisations to be more effective in their transfer of knowledge. European Journal of Information Systems (2001) 10, 72–79.

## Introduction

Since the time of the Luddites, motivation has played a role in the adoption of man-machine technologies. In particular, in the 1970s and 1980s the motivations of various participants in the man-machine technology labelled Information Systems (IS) was a subject of considerable concern and interest, and many IS conferences and a significant portion of the IS research literature focused on these motivations. Many articles addressed the reluctance to use Information Technology (IT). Others, in the context of arguing or studying the benefits of desktop computing and Decision Support Systems, addressed the reluctance of Electronic Data Processing departments to share ‘their’ information. Aided greatly by improvements in the user-friendliness of IT, organisations won their battles with the reluctant individuals and departments. By the 1990s, these particular motivational ‘problems’ were seldom observed in either practice or in the research literature.

The roles of IT continue to expand, both in society at large and in the functioning of organisations. This article addresses motivational issues in the relatively new manmachine technology known as Knowledge Management Systems (KMSs). Unlike the two motivational problems noted above, which have largely disappeared, the motivational problems associated with effective functioning of KMSs are not only associated with the initial adoption of the technology, but are also associated with its ongoing use. Further, the problems tend not to be susceptible to amelioration through ‘fixing’ the technology to make it more user friendly, nor are they satisfactorily attenuated with financial incentives or organisational directives (Pfeffer, 1998; Snowden, 2000).

Let us assume what is commonly believed—that the survival of many firms depends on their ability to acquire or develop, and then exploit, knowledge more effectively than their competitors. Thus, while the hype associated with organisational learning, knowledge leveraging, and the like may pass, the need to engage effectively in these practices will not. Knowledge is more and more recognised as a key organisational resource, and many firms have taken steps to manage it. Currently, the use of computer-aided knowledge management systems is one of the two major thrusts for managing this critical resource (the other being the aggressive acquisition and retention of knowledge workers).

Judging from the content of recent Knowledge Management conferences, IS managers and researchers— having rather forgotten the ‘people problems’ of the 1970s and 1980s—seem a bit taken aback at the motivational problems associated with KMSs. Yet, as professionals, IS managers and researchers cannot ignore the effects of these problems on the performance of KMSs as whole systems, as contrasted with limiting their attention to only the hardware and software components. In this spirit, one purpose of this article is to articulate and clarify some of the important issues associated with these motivational problems. In particular, the article focuses on problems and issues associated with the transfer of knowledge in the context of KMSs. It also suggests the nature of studies that can lead to the design of more effective knowledge management systems.

Firms have in place various practices for motivating their knowledge workers to transfer knowledge into and out of the firm’s computer resident knowledge repositories, ie, to participate with commitment in the firm’s knowledge management system. Although success stories appear and gain attention, no comparative research has appeared, and little is known with any certainty about which of these practices have what effects under which conditions. Dispassionate reflection suggests that in many cases the practices are ill suited for the particular situations where they are employed, with unknown but perhaps sizeable losses in opportunities foregone because valuable knowledge is not as fully or completely transferred as it might be. Equally interesting is the fact that some of these practices are likely to be interfering with the effectiveness of other practices, just as some drugs interfere with the potentially positive effects of other drugs.

It seems important to examine these matters at this time in the evolution of KMSs because once practices become ingrained or institutionalised in an organisation, or in a field (such as knowledge management), they gain a life of their own and become extremely difficult to supplant—even with qualitatively superior practices. I am concerned that—contrary to the impression one might get from the current management practice literature—what we know about the transfer of knowledge is greatly exceeded by what we do not know. I believe our knowledge is exceeded by our ignorance. Thus, the second purpose of this paper is to suggest—by raising questions—what we yet need to learn.

Of the obstacles to getting knowledge transferred, motivational problems stand out in terms of the attention they have received. A great deal of anecdotal evidence (see, for example, Berry, 2000; Koudsi, 2000; Rappleye, 2000) indicates that motivations of the sender and receiver greatly affect the success of organisational efforts to get knowledge transferred. Hear some of the knowledge workers interviewed by Standing and Benson (2000, p 343) in their study of an emplaced KMS:

‘If I share my knowledge others may take advantage of that. Will they do the same for me?’

‘People are afraid to share their knowledge and experiences as they feel their positions might be taken away from them.

Motivational problems are not limited to the reluctance of knowledge workers to share their knowledge. Anyone who has worked in an organisation has encountered the bias against using knowledge from other sources (sometimes referred to as the ‘not-invented-here’ syndrome). Thus, we have motivational problems of the knowledge holder and motivational problems of the potential user.

Among the remaining obstacles to getting knowledge transferred (see Szulanski, 1996, for an enumeration), cognitive problems deserve brief but explicit mention here because they interact with motivational problems. As examples, cognitive difficulties diminish the motivation to transfer knowledge, and high levels of motivation can often overcome cognitive problems. One type of cognitive problem follows from the tacitness of some knowledge, which makes the knowledge difficult for the possessor to explicate and communicate. Another type is associated with the inability of the receiver to absorb the knowledge due to the inadequacy of his or her background. Although researchers often view this inability of the receiver to absorb knowledge as having its basis in the lack of the recipient’s background knowledge (see the classic article on this subject of ‘absorptive capacity,’ Cohen & Levinthal, 1990), certainly ‘packaging’ counts—some communications are more interpretable than others even when they contain exactly the same objective information. Thus, we have cognitive problems of the knowledge holder and cognitive problems of the potential user, just as we have motivational problems of the knowledge holder and motivational problems of the potential user. It is easy to see, of course, that combinations and interactions among the four types of problems contribute to ineffective knowledge transfer.

This fact noted, let us move from this introduction about the problems of transferring knowledge to the practical matters of unexplored issues and needed studies. I first use some anecdotes and examples from the management practice literature to stimulate thought, and then compare the resulting ideas with some findings from the research literature to identify issues and questions in need of investigation. The anecdotes and examples are organised around three knowledge contexts—archived codified knowledge, archived non-codified information, and person-to-person communications. The first two contexts are associated with those KMSs where knowledge originally possessed by one or few people is deposited into a computer-resident knowledge archive from which it can be subsequently accessed by many potential users.

The third context tends to involve knowledge that is especially ‘sticky’—knowledge that is difficult ‘to acquire, transfer, and use in a new location’ (von Hippel, 1994, p 429). Rather than ever being stored in a public repository or archive, the knowledge involved in personto-person communications is transferred directly from the minds of those who possess it to the minds of those who seek it. In these KMSs, IT is used in two ways. One way is in the computer-based ‘people finder’ systems that enable those who need particular knowledge and those who possess particular knowledge to identify and find one another. The second way IT is used in these systems, exemplified by Lotus Notes, is to enable the parties to communicate easily with respect to the transfer of the knowledge or—more important in the long run— is to facilitate the development of the social networks that lead to the trust so often necessary for the sharing of expertise. Facilitating development of social networks is of considerable importance—hear again knowledge workers interviewed by Standing and Benson (2000, p 343):

‘With the increased workloads over the years there just isn’t the time and space to network informally and develop the trust to share knowledge and ideas.’

‘Trust is vital. People can’t share their knowledge with each other if there is no trust. People are scared to share because they do not trust others.

## Knowledge forms and associated issues and questions

## Archived codified knowledge

Codified knowledge is systematised explicit knowledge that can be transferred easily if the sender and receiver have the same or very similar vocabularies and cognitive models in the domain of the knowledge being transferred. ‘How-to’ procedures are a typical example of codified knowledge. In most contexts, receivers are capable of absorbing codified knowledge, but even highly explicit codified knowledge is not likely to be transferred if the balance of motivating factors does not favour its transfer. For example, the knowledge possessor may not wish to share because retaining the knowledge provides him or her with a competitive advantage. Or the potential user of the knowledge may have a ‘not-invented-here’ bias that works against adopting best practices.

When using KMSs built around archived codified knowledge, organisations employ ‘an electronic document system that codifies, stores, disseminates, and allows reuse of knowledge’ (Hansen et al, 1999, p 107). Other authors have labelled this approach as integrative (Zack, 1999, p 50) or as an ‘access to documents’ approach (Rappleye, 2000, p 63). Xerox’s Eureka is a good example of an archive and channel of codified knowledge. Eureka is an enormous knowledge base that helps Xerox’s service representatives solve particularly difficult problems. Xerox’s Director of Corporate Strategy, Knowledge Initiative describes the motivation for Eureka’s development—‘Once in about a thousand service calls, there is a problem nobody has ever seen before. The service rep is out there trying to fix it at the customer site. That takes a long time and costs a lot. We don’t want to have anybody have to do that again. So, the question becomes: How do we capture that solution, from the service rep who is the only one who knows, into a knowledge base they all can use?’ (Rappleye, 2000, p 60).

The answer to this question was obtained from a series of studies by a team of behavioural scientists: ‘In these communities whose world is their work team—five to 10 people in a local geographical area—the opportunity to become known as a thought leader, to have solved a difficult problem that nobody else had ever seen, and to be personally identified with the solution, before all Xerox reps, all over the world, was enough to incent them to take the time to write up their solution, and put their name to it, and put it into the knowledge base’ (Rappleye, 2000, p 60).

What can we draw from this example that we might be able to use later when developing guidelines for future use, assuming that the findings are representative of the situation in which they were obtained?

(a) Extrinsic rewards were not used as motivators. ‘It is personal pride, and recognition by peers, that makes Eureka work—motivation far beyond money, or promotion, or any other conventional rewards’ (Rappleye, 2000, p 60).

(b) Stickiness was not a problem. Indeed, the context seems a perfect example of the fact that ‘when communicators share similar knowledge, background, and experience, they can more effectively communicate knowledge via electronically mediated channels. For example, by means of a central electronic repository, an organisation can disseminate explicit, factual knowledge within a stable community having a high degree of shared contextual knowledge’ (Zack, 1999, p 59).

Note that, due to a high level of shared contextual knowledge, communication effectiveness was high. But knowledge transfer could still have been a problem—if the service reps had not been motivated to share their hard-earned knowledge. Did they share their knowledge because they sought recognition as experts? Or did they share it out of more altruistic, ‘we’re a team’ motivations? Or did they share it out of a felt need for equity and reciprocity—they were benefiting from the knowledge contributions of others and felt a need to ‘give back’? Or did they share it because doing so might prompt others to share—in order to maintain social equity—and thereby provide themselves and other service reps with solutions that would make their jobs easier? Or, finally, did they share because it was ‘the right thing to do,’ ie, because it was the organisational norm? Let us call the need for recognition, the need to serve, the need for equity, the need to adhere to organisational norms, and the potential intrinsic satisfaction associated with fulfilling certain of these needs through knowledge sharing, ‘social-psychological forces.’ (See Constant et al, 1994; 1996, for reviews and studies of such forces.)

Xerox’s Eureka is a widely discussed knowledge management success story. But recognise the inconsistency between the reported behaviour of these knowledge workers and the likely behaviour implied in the above quotes from the knowledge workers interviewed by Standing and Benson (2000). Or contrast the Eureka story with the concluding observation of Moore and Birkinshaw (1998, p 90) in their study of knowledge workers who were involved in equipment repair in a service organisation (just as were the Xerox workers): ‘Most of the individuals we talked to had an existing responsibility that was measured, and a responsibility (to contribute to the firm’s KMS) on top that was not formally measured. These individuals obviously knew that (this second, more general, collective responsibility) was important, but if the compensation and reward system favoured their existing responsibilities, it was very clear which part of their work would get neglected when time was short’ (Moore & Birkenshaw, 1998, p 90). So we have two situations involving workers doing essentially the same type of work, but in one situation the transfer of knowledge is highly reliable and in the other it is highly problematic.

What important questions are raised by this example that, if answered with sound studies, would enable organisations to be more effective in their transfer of knowledge? It seems there are at least four:

(1) Which social-psychological forces cause knowledge holders to contribute their knowledge to a public archive?

(2) What factors determine the likelihood that these various social-psychological forces will motivate knowledge holders to contribute their knowledge?

(3) How can organisations cause specific social-psychological forces to come into play and counter or overwhelm opposing forces (forces such as the knowledge holder’s need to fulfill other responsibilities)?

(4) What are the effects on knowledge sharing of interactions between extrinsic rewards and these various social-psychological forces?

This last question is especially critical. Careful thought makes clear that, in a great many situations, only the possessors of knowledge know what they know. Thus, it is at their discretion whether they identify what they know or share what they know. And certainly the amount of effort they put forth to make complete and clear what they share is under their control. Thus, even when knowledge sharing is a formal responsibility, full sharing is an extra-role behaviour. Given this reality, what makes question 4 critical is that a large body of research shows that extrinsic motivators can adversely impact the positive effects of intrinsic motivations or social-psychological forces on extra-role behaviour. For example, their extensive review of the literature, including the most recent and sophisticated meta-analyses, caused Osterloh and Frey to conclude that ‘tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation for interesting tasks . . . in a highly significant and very reliable way and that the effect is moderately large’ (2000, p 542) and, importantly, that ‘the relevance of (this) effect is also supported by numerous field studies’ (2000, p 543).

Altogether, these four questions suggest an agenda for research that could help managers avoid pouring energy into fruitless or counterproductive efforts to transfer even highly codifiable knowledge. In this way, research studies could lead to improvements in the transfer of knowledge, and thus could improve organisational performance. Let us move on to the situation of non-codified knowledge.

## Archived non-codified information

The consulting industry is a leader in knowledge management. Most large consulting firms include within their knowledge management system an archive of consultant-deposited information about specific engagements. A goodly portion of such information is impossible, or at least tortuous, to codify, but can still be very useful if it is interpretable. The Director of Arthur Andersen’s knowledge management system, KnowledgeSpace, describes how such a system can be used: ‘When a client calls for help with a proposal, we can browse over other engagements where we have helped clients get educated, and by looking at each individual’s story and gleaning a sense of how he went about the process, we can gain the confidence to recommend an approach. We can piggyback off prior stories, and put the project in context’ (Rappleye, 2000, pp 63–64). In other words, the general knowledge and background of Arthur Andersen’s consultants enables them to transform the information contained in the ‘stories’ into knowledge they can use.

It is easy to imagine particular instances in any industry where stories and other loosely structured non-codified information would not be informative or interpretable. Two important research questions raised by this example—beyond the earlier questions concerning motivation to participate—are:

(5) How can archived, non-codified information be made more likely to satisfy the needs of potential users, ie, be made less sticky? Structured databases? Key words? Templates?

(6) What is the relative effectiveness of these approaches in contexts where variation in users’ backgrounds causes the approaches to vary in ease of use and general satisfactoriness from user to user?

One approach that comes easily to mind, and that is likely to be effective in many circumstances, is to implement communication systems and procedures that enable potential users to query those who authored the non-codified materials. This brings us to the third KMS context—person-to-person communications.

## Person-to-person communications

Transfer of expert-respondent knowledge involves interaction. A person who needs some particular knowledge not available in archives (at least not available in a readily usable form), queries a directory of experts to find one or more who are likely to possess the needed knowledge, and asks the more likely expert(s) for help. Sometimes the query is a request for an elaboration of the content of a non-codified narrative that the ‘expert’ (about that narrative) deposited in the archive. Sometimes the query is a request for over-the-phone or email technical assistance about a situation that the potential user does not understand, or is having trouble articulating, or is finding not to be treated in any known archive. Sometimes it is a request for knowledge that is hard to make explicit and that can be transferred only with a good deal of interaction, perhaps with the participation of intermediaries.

An example of this last situation is that of a partner in an international consulting firm who was confronted with a client’s difficult strategy problem. Drawing on her own ‘mental directory,’ she identified several of the firm’s partners who possessed relevant expertise. But she also used her organisation’s ‘people finder’ database to identify even more contacts, and eventually put together an internationally based team of experts in the problem from her firm. During the course of the engagement, she and her team ‘consulted with expert partners regularly in meetings and through phone calls and e-mail. In the process of developing a unique growth strategy, the team tapped into a worldwide network of colleagues’ experience’ (Hansen et al, 1999, p 108).

I have no doubt that this rather inspiring report is factual. However, it is true that—for the most part— authors of the management practice literature have a message to sell. And it is also true that these authors tend to rely heavily on the testimony of ‘champions’ of the management practice about which the article is written. So it is not surprising that relatively few articles describe instances where attempts to transfer knowledge ended in failure. However, off the record, managers often report instances where those who had valuable knowledge were reluctant, or at least hesitant, to share it, and sometimes successfully avoided sharing it. This fact prompts some questions about how to motivate experts to respond positively to requests for their knowledge:

(7) What is the relative effectiveness of different types of extrinsic rewards, and of the various socialpsychological forces, as motivators for causing experts to provide in-depth responses to those who ask for access to their expertise, and how does the answer to this question vary across different circumstances? (Circumstances such as high—or low— trust, or common—vs different—cognitive models.)

(8) What are the effects of interactions between the different types of extrinsic rewards and penalties and the various social-psychological forces, and how do these vary across different circumstances? For example, if trust is low, this might reduce the positive effects of social-psychological forces on sharing. Does this mean that extrinsic rewards must be increased? Or would offering extrinsic rewards initiate the suspicion that ‘if they need to bribe me to do this, shouldn’t I be worried about why I’d better not?’

The possibility of negative interactions between use of extrinsic motivators and social-psychological forces in the context of knowledge sharing is suggested by the occasionally observed negative effects of extrinsic rewards on desired extra-role behaviours (Kohn, 1993; Eisenberger & Cameron, 1996; Kerr, 1997; Pfeffer, 1998).

## Organisational culture: an achievable solution to the problem of motivation?

Organisational culture is the set of values, beliefs, norms, and expectations that are widely held in an organisation. Both social control and members’ need for a sense of self-worth influence member behaviour in a direction congruent with the organisation’s culture. Thus, if management wants the sharing and transfer of knowledge to be a common practice in the organisation, then those responsible for knowledge management need to: (a) ensure that no organisational policies and no features of the organisation’s reward systems discourage this; (b) articulate management’s expectations that people will engage in the practice because it is the right thing to do—thus communicating management’s values; (c) set the example by doing so and by getting other influential organisational members to do so; (d) publicise instances where sharing knowledge and using transferred knowledge benefited the organisation and its members; (e) celebrate instances where the practice occurred; and (f) privately encourage laggards to share. (Constant et al, 1996 and Orlikowski, 1993, report studies demonstrating the effects of organisational culture on knowledge sharing.)

These management actions, if carried out sensibly and sensitively, will move the organisation’s knowledge workers in the direction of forming and adhering to an organisational culture where seeking, sharing, and using knowledge in the context of a KMS is the norm. However, management needs to be sensitive to three issues.

One is that, not only organisational norms but also societal norms, have a strong influence on behaviour. It is the norm in most societies to admire and reward those who perform well in fulfilling their primary responsibility—which often depends on exploiting one’s personal knowledge. This societal norm competes with organisational attempts to create or maintain a norm of knowledge sharing.

A second issue is that ongoing globalisation is intensifying the need for organisations to perform more effectively. This very often translates into pay-for-performance compensation schemes for the organisation’s knowledge workers, from chief executive officers to computer programmers. That is, simplistic thinking leads to a trickle-down effect—inter-organisational competition serves as a model to implement intra-organisational competition. Because the ‘performance’ in payfor-performance systems tends to apply to a person’s primary responsibility, the systems discourage knowledge sharing if such sharing reduces a person’s competitive advantage.

Finally, a third issue is that the social-psychological forces noted earlier are very closely associated with an organisation’s values, beliefs, norms, and expectations, ie, with an organisation’s culture. Because the use of extrinsic motivators can interfere with intrinsic needs or social-psychological forces as motivators to create or perform extra-role behaviours (such as some forms of knowledge transfer), the use of extrinsic rewards to create an organisational culture of knowledge sharing should be undertaken with great care, if at all.

## Discussion and suggestions for study

This analysis of particular problems and issues has generated questions that seem important to those involved in the management or study of knowledge transfer.

The foremost issue that crosses several of these questions is the problematic nature of the relationships among extrinsic motivators and the various socialpsychological forces that may operate in the context of knowledge transfer. It has long been known that the presence of extrinsic rewards can have, and often does have, adverse impact on the positive effects of intrinsic motivators or the social-psychological forces described in the Xerox example. Extrinsic rewards can deflect attention from the intrinsic satisfaction or social-psychological motivators that may, in the long run, be more effective at prompting knowledge transfer. Especially for personnel steeped in the culture of knowledge workers, the use of extrinsic rewards for sharing expertise can be seen as demeaning (O’Dell & Grayson, 1998a) or, conversely, can create a competition among people who should be—for the sake of the organisation—in a highly cooperative mode. Occasionally the management practice literature recognises these facts, but generally it reports the currently fashionable emphasis (especially in the US) on ‘pay for performance.’ For example, Koudsi (2000) reports the use of a bonus system to get knowledge workers to participate in a knowledge-archiving system, and Huber (1999) describes the practice in some consulting firms of ‘disqualifying’ consultants who do not participate actively in the organisation’s knowledge management system.

Pay-for-performance compensation systems have a place in knowledge management and knowledge transfer in some situations. But exactly what that place is, and what those situations are, require further investigation (see, for example, Deckop et al, 1999). Less-than-successful compensation systems, once implemented, may be hard to shut down and, even if successfully shut down, may leave a lasting negative legacy. As noted earlier, many factors are at play in the complex environment of knowledge-intensive organisations. For example, some industries have cultures (and the consulting industry seems to be one of them) that attract people who are especially motivated by extrinsic rewards. Given this cultural feature of the industry, it might be very unwise for a consulting firm either to be a ‘first mover’ by discontinuing extrinsic rewards for an activity that is commonly extrinsically rewarded in the industry, or to expect that its personnel would put forth much effort in any activity that was not explicitly extrinsically rewarded. Other industries have cultures where social-psychological forces or the culture of withinwork-group-cooperation are very strong, as it apparently is in the case of Xerox’s service reps. Higher education may be one of these industries.

There is an exciting and important need for research to examine the additive, conflicting, and interactive effects of extrinsic motivators, intrinsic motivations, and social-psychological forces in the context of knowledge transfer. Individual articles in the management practice literature often suggest that managers should simultaneously employ practices drawn from all of these approaches to motivating knowledge transfer. This can be compared to a physician prescribing several drugs at the same time, hoping that one or another will conquer the patient’s illness. But physicians are cautious about prescribing multiple drugs simultaneously—because doing so would often be wasteful and, due to possible interactions among the drugs, could result in none of the drugs having the expected desirable effect or a combination of the drugs having an adverse effect. Workers in the field of knowledge management are more ignorant about the effects of their knowledge transfer practices than are physicians about the effects of their pharmaceutical remedies. Managers should be cautious, or at least thoughtful, as they consider their options. And researchers should be sceptical, or at least curious, about the additive, conflicting, and interactive effects of extrinsic motivators, intrinsic motivations, and social-psychological forces. Given the importance of effective knowledge transfer in today’s world, and even more so in tomorrow’s, it seems likely and worthwhile that these matters will receive attention and investigation.

The second issue that crosses some of the questions raised here has to do with the capabilities of the sender and receiver to communicate effectively. With regard to this issue and also the problematic nature of the relationships among extrinsic motivators and the various socialpsychological forces, I think we need to view the world of organisations as a generator of many ‘natural experiments,’ ad hoc developments of different practices in different organisational contexts that can be compared by both researchers and managers for their effectiveness. (I am a firm believer in the practice of organisations improving their performance by purposively learning from their own experiments, whether these are natural or designed. See, for contrasting examples, Huber et al, 1979; March et al, 1991.)

For knowledge managers, the potential usefulness of the above eight questions—one at a time or in small sets—may be to provoke thinking and debate about what their organisation ought to be doing to facilitate transfer of knowledge, as contrasted with what it is doing at the present time. Organisations get into ruts.

Organisations adopt practices, become adept at the practices, and so do not see the merits of alternative, even superior, practices.

For researchers, the potential usefulness of the questions may be to set forth a research agenda. In general, the questions raised are fresh questions, but they are questions that can readily be tied to both the research literature and to real problems. This is a happy combination of features. I would add to this the observation that only a small fraction of the massive empirical literature on motivation is comprised of field studies, or fieldlike simulation studies, of knowledge workers. And, with the exceptions of Constant et al (1994), Ruggles (1998) and Szulanski (1996) and—I’m sure—a few others (see also O’Dell & Grayson, 1998a, b), there are very few systematic studies of the social-psychological forces affecting the transfer of knowledge. Our ignorance in these matters is considerable. Great opportunities exist for making important contributions.

## About the author

George Huber holds an endowed chair at the University of Texas at Austin. His recent writings focus on organisational change and organisational cognition. Earlier writings dealt with information technology and individual and group decision making. His articles, ‘The Nature and Design of Post-Industrial Organizations’ and ‘Fit, Equifinality, and Organizational Effec tiveness,’ won international competitions. Two others, ‘A Theory of the Effects of Advanced Information Technologies’ and ‘Organizational Learning: The Contributing Processes and the Literatures,’ were among the most highly cited articles in their journal of publication during the 1990s. Dr Huber is currently writing a book, The Necessary Nature of Future Firms.
