# The process of global knowledge integration: a case study of a multinational investment bank’s Y2K program

JC Huang<sup>1</sup>\*, S Newell<sup>2</sup> and S-L Pan<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Nottingham University Business School, Jubilee Campus, Nottingham, NG8 1BB, UK; <sup>2</sup>School of Management, Royal Holloway, University of London, Egham, TW20 0EX, UK; <sup>3</sup>Department of Information Systems, School of Computing, National University of Singapore, Singapore 117543

In this paper we examine the phenomenon of Y2K as an example of cross-functional knowledge integration. We start with the observation that although an enormous amount of investment was made for year 2000 (Y2K) readiness, a smooth millennium rollover has ended with widespread debates about the worthiness of the investment. These debates not only reflect a common perception that the Y2K investment has been a waste of money, but also indicate that organisations often fail to learn from what they have done in the past in order to prepare for future actions. A case study of a multinational investment bank’s Y2K program was conducted to analyse its main features and conceptualise the underlying cross-functional knowledge integration process. Based on our findings, we identified the following as the main processes involved in knowledge integration: (1) the penetration of different boundaries to obtain required knowledge and support; (2) the expansion of different paradigms to achieve shared understanding; (3) the reconfiguration of organisational memory to create new organisational routines and knowledge. European Journal of Information Systems (2001) 10, 161–174.

## Introduction

Following a smooth millennium rollover, the once dominating hot issue—Year 2000 (Y2K) readiness—has gradually lost its media popularity. While some companies are still trying to make sense of their investments in preventing a so-called ‘false-alarm’ event, some have already abandoned the Y2K issue and shifted their foci back to their day-to-day business. Such neglect not only reflects a widespread belief that Y2K readiness was merely a one-time event with no added value to an organisation’s competitiveness, but also indicates that organisations often fall short in learning from and capitalising on their past actions, in particular, when the learning opportunities and outcomes are unexpected. The aim of this case analysis is to conceptualise the process of global cross-functional knowledge integration and illustrate what can be learnt from managing a Y2K program implementation that can be strategically useful for subsequent Information Technology (IT) implementation programs. In particular it is argued that for multinational organisations the Y2K problem initiated the process of global cross-functional knowledge integration that is relevant to any project or program that involves the implementation of a large, geographically distributed IT system. Thus, although the Y2K program at the case company was the largest IT program undertaken by this company to date, the need for global IT programs is increasingly common, as indicated by the growing popularity of Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems. This suggests that, where IT programs need to integrate knowledge and expertise from multiple, even globally dispersed sources, the experiences and lessons gained from the Y2K program can be potentially beneficial. Therefore, this case study analyses the experiences in organising a Y2K program with a view to providing valuable learning for future IT implementation programs where cross-functional knowledge integration on a global scale is the key to success. Building upon the definition of knowledge as ‘justified true belief’ (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995), knowledge integration is defined by this study as ‘an ongoing collective process of constructing, articulating and redefining shared beliefs through the social interaction of organizational members’ (Huang, 2000; p 15). If the lessons learnt from a successful Y2K program can be articulated and applied, such lessons can be used to contribute to post-millennium business growth (Portanger, 1999). In other words, this study sees the experience of the Y2K preparation process as ‘an investment in future performance, and in long-term business performance and financial goals (Robertson & Powell 1999; p 43).

The Y2K program to be described occurred within a multinational investment bank and involved virtually every member of staff in the organisation, including worldwide employees. For instance, the program was led by a London-based team but involved every branch of the bank across the globe. The number of computer systems used, the number of people involved, the length of time required, as well as the dispersion of participants— all pointed to a program on a much larger scale than any previously undertaken in the bank. The study concludes that the Y2K program illustrates a number of processes that are important for integrating knowledge on a global scale. In particular, this study articulates three interconnected processes that appeared to contribute to effective knowledge integration in the case company: (1) the penetration of different boundaries to obtain required knowledge; (2) the expansion of different paradigms to achieve shared understanding; (3) the reconfiguration of organisational memory to create new organisational routines and knowledge.

The paper is divided into four parts. Following the introduction, Section 2 highlights current debates and discussions related to the issues of Y2K and cross-functional knowledge integration. Section 3 presents the methodological issues and concerns, while Section 4 outlines the case study of the multinational investment bank, with an emphasis on the background information of the case company and the Y2K program. Section 5 provides the analysis and considers the lessons that were learnt from this event. Section 6 summarises the main theoretical and managerial implications of the study.

## Current debates and discussions

The Y2K problem arose because in the 1960s and 1970s computer programs were designed which used only two digits to represent the year (in both software and hardware) in order to lower computer storage costs (Wardley, 1997; Zetlin, 1998). Before the year 2000, some of the earlier reports on Y2K were practitioneroriented, focusing on different phases of the Y2K program implementation by different organisations (see for example, Barker and Marshal, 1997). They included reports on impact analysis, planning and scheduling, conversion, and testing and implementation of Y2K programs (see for example, Kappleman & Cappel, 1996; Barker & Marshal, 1997; Mathieson, 1998).

Despite the enormous amount of practitioner media coverage during the late 1990s that reported on the issue of Y2K readiness, the focus of that coverage was restricted almost exclusively to the technological aspects (eg Estes et al, 1997; Chaabouni, 1998; Lewis, 1999; Li et al, 1999). Non-technical issues, such as the management of Y2K programs, have tended to be either ignored completely or given very little attention. Since no major Y2K-related IT disaster occurred during and after the millennium rollover, discussions relating to Y2K in the media have centred primarily on the question of the worthiness of the investment of time and money in Y2K readiness. There are relatively few studies that report what lessons firms learnt or could learn from the processes of organising, preparing and testing their Y2K compliance.

The main reason for this neglect appears to be the belief that Y2K readiness was a unique, one-time event and that no long-term added value can be derived from such experience. This outlook supports the organisational learning literature, which argues that organisations often fail to learn from their past actions and hence do not apply the lessons of the past to future actions (eg Fiol & Lyles, 1985; Senge, 1990; March et al, 1991). This lack of continuous learning is also reflected in the current knowledge management literature, which indicates that organisations often fall short in systematically articulating knowledge to leverage their competitiveness (eg Pisano, 1994; Boisot et al, 1997; Sanchez, 1997). In this paper it is argued that far from being a unique and isolated event, the Y2K readiness program can be used to illustrate and conceptualise complex processes of cross-functional knowledge integration which are likely to be central to the success of any global IT program.

Pisano (1994) argues that firms can leverage their competitiveness by effectively utilising the multiple sources of internal knowledge. This suggests the strategic significance of cross-functional knowledge integration. This strategic significance is also central to those who advocate a knowledge-based view of the firm. The knowledge-based view of the firm thus emphasises the strategic and managerial importance of knowledge embedded within firm-specific capabilities (Grant, 1996; Grant & Baden-Fuller, 1996; Spender, 1996). The concept of cross-functional knowledge integration is also examined by some within the area of innovation studies. For example, knowledge integration is often viewed as a mechanism that enables information-sharing to take place across organisational functions, resulting in the generation of new knowledge through the reduction of internal and external uncertainties (Cooper, 1988; Moenaert & Sounder, 1990). Others address the importance of integrating customers’ and suppliers’ knowledge for the success of new product development and innovation effectiveness (eg Bozdogan et al, 1998), as well as for the commercialisation of R&D outcomes (Souder et al, 1998). Furthermore, cross-functional knowledge integration is seen as one of the key mechanisms that enable process innovation (Davenport, 1992), through which organisations are able to ‘transfigure’ themselves (Jones & Hendry, 1992) and renew their memories (Ackerman & Halverson, 2000). Summarising the above discussion, it is clear that the importance of cross-functional knowledge integration is threefold. First, crossfunctional knowledge integration underpins the process through which divisionalised expertise and activities are coordinated and synthesised. Second, cross-functional knowledge integration serves as an essential mechanism to refine and create knowledge. Third, knowledge refined and created cross-functionally through this integration process promotes the revitalisation of an organisation.

However, although the concept of knowledge integration has been discussed in various research disciplines over the last five decades, the emphasis has been on understanding its implications and outcomes rather than its processes, in particular on a global scale (Hauptman & Hirji, 1999). This paper reports the findings of an empirical study, which set out to bridge this major theoretical gap. It describes and analyses the processes by which a multinational investment bank integrated its knowledge by organising, managing and implementing its Y2K program. Before discussing the case, the next section describes the various methodological steps by which empirical data were collected and analysed.

## Research methodology

The research is based on a one-year case study conducted between March 1999 and February 2000. Lack of theoretical development in the conceptualisation of cross-functional knowledge integration processes led the authors to adopt a grounded theory approach (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) as the foundation of the research design. The following discussion is structured into three sections dealing with research design, data collection methods and data analysis.

## Research design

The advantages and disadvantages of case study The rationale behind the case study research design is threefold. First, case study design affords flexibility with respect to the adoption of multiple data collection methods to enhance the richness of research findings (Yin, 1984; Robson, 1993). Secondly, a case study generates insightful stories rather than statistical information, and this permits a better understanding of organisational complexity (Van Maanen, 1979; Stake, 1995) from an ‘insider’s view’ (Everd & Louis, 1981). Thirdly, a case study enables the researchers to gain a more holistic picture of the studied phenomenon (Lee, 1989), and in particular to explore a ‘previously little-studied area (Benbasat et al, 1987) that demands extensive theoretical development (Eisenhardt, 1989; Orlikowski & Baroudi, 1991).

In addition to the advantages listed above, this study also recognises the limitations of case study research, in particular in terms of the generalisibility of social phenomena, and the validity of the findings (Yin, 1984; Gummesson, 1991; Scott, 1997). As far as the issue of generalisibility is concerned, a case study aims to develop rich contextual data, not to generalise the chosen phenomena into a concrete set of laws for measurement and prediction (Platt, 1988; Bryman & Burgess, 1999). In relation to the aim of the present study, which is to investigate the processes of integrating individual and group knowledge, a nomothetic approach, which is group-centred, standardised and takes place within a controlled environment, is clearly inappropriate (Luthans & Davis, 1982). In terms of the validity of the findings, construct validity is ensured by using multiple sources of evidence as well as having a case study report (Yin, 1984). Secondly, internal validity is achieved through three stages of coding: open, axial and selective coding (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). Finally, reliability is established by using a case study protocol and developing a case study database (Yin, 1984).

## Data collection methods

The case conducted in the chosen site can be characterized as a multi-method, in-depth field research study (Miles & Huberman, 1994). The paucity of prior theoretical and empirical research on this topic necessitates ‘going into the field’ to observe organisational efforts as a means of advancing our understanding of global crossfunctional knowledge integration. Consequently, the general approach of this study is interpretative in character, with qualitative research methods employed to explore and explain the central research question. In terms of research methods, several approaches were employed for data collection. The primary source of data was 16 semi-structured interviews conducted during March and April 1999, and six follow-up interviews conducted during January and February 2000. The 16 interviewees were selected from various management levels as well as different business functions. In terms of business functions, interviewees were drawn from Technology, Architecture, Risk and Finance Technology, Interest Rate Derivatives, Global Money Markets, Human Resources, Strategy, Program Management and Operations. The interviews were conducted mainly on a face-to-face basis, with the exception of overseas interviewees in the USA and Singapore who were interviewed through conference calls. Additional information was collected through informal interviewing and intensive discussions with team members via telephone and e-mail between March and September 1999 and between January and February 2000.

In addition to these formal and informal interviews, on-site observation was also used. Fourteen formal meetings were observed. These meetings involved various members of staff with the observations taking place at various stages of the data collection, in particular during September 1999 and February 2000. In addition, informal observation took place during the various site visits, in particular during coffee- and lunch-breaks. Additionally, physical artifacts such as the office layout were also included as part of the observation. On-site observation serves the purpose of gaining first-hand knowledge of how the researched act in their social settings (Altheide & Johnson, 1997). In this study, the primary objective of on-site observation was to understand how the Y2K program was organised and managed through the social interaction of the team members and other organisational members. In addition to interviews and on-site observation, data from documents were also collected to form the third source of evidence. The documents included letters, written reports, administrative documents, newspapers, as well as information from the database and the Intranet.

## Data preparation and analysis

Prior to the coding, the preparation stage consisted of activities, such as transcribing interview tapes, typing and filing research notes (taken during and after interviews as well as on-site observation) into a database, summarising documents and categorising them based on the sources. There were, therefore, four main categories of data grouped during this stage—interview transcripts, research notes, photocopied documents, and information downloaded from the case company’s Intranet. A brief note that described the content of each file and potential linkages with different files was inserted in virtually every file stored in the four categories of data.

The initial stage of analysis—open coding—aimed to identify concepts and categories based on the collected data. This stage of analysis was open-ended and generative (Orlikowski, 1993). Concepts and categories, such as subcultures, communication and program implementation, emerging from the open coding were used as a basis to proceed to the axial coding stage. Here the primary aim was to generate interconnections between these themes by building a paradigm model which was further refined to form the basis of the theoretical framework outlined in Figure 1. Finally in the last stage of selective coding, similarities and differences between categories and concepts were further explored and refined. For instance, categories, such as boundary penetrating, paradigm expanding and organisational memory reconfiguring, were generated during this stage of coding to depict the process of global cross-functional knowledge integration. This coding technique enabled the researchers to identify richer categories and concepts, as well as more dynamic relations between these categories and concepts. Such dynamic interrelationships between concepts and categories shaped the story line of the study and served as the basis to ground the theory of crossfunctional knowledge integration processes. Theoretical sampling was also employed to ensure the density of the emerging theory (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). Specifically, open coding was paired with open sampling, relational sampling with axial coding and discriminate sampling with selective. Such an iterative process between coding and sampling was ended when theoretical saturation was reached (Glaser & Strauss, 1967). The next two sections outline details related to the case and findings derived from the coding processes.

## Case study: a multinational investment bank

## Company background

The case company was restructured and renamed in 1997 and provides financial products ranging from foreign exchange and currency options to interest rate derivatives. It has operations in financial capitals such as London, New York, Singapore and Tokyo. Globally, 1100 employees produced £393 million profits before tax in 1998, an increase of 61% compared with 1997. Structurally, the company is divided into various departments such as Technology, Business, Infrastructure, Human Resource, Research and Finance. However, an alternative way to understand the organisational structure is through its financial product ranges. Each product range has its own staff, including traders and technologists, with hierarchical relationships through which reporting lines are laid and responsibilities are delegated.

## The Y2K program: background, coverage and process

Under regulations established by the Bank of England and IMRO, every bank in the UK was required to ensure that all its systems were millennium-compliant and that there would be continuity of business operation should any unforeseen millennium crisis occur. The case company initiated its millennium program in 1997 and ended it in March 2000. During 1998, the Y2K program focused primarily on areas including IT applications, IT infrastructure, end user computing, premises, and managing client and regulator inquiries. More specifically, the major tasks of millennium compliance were threefold:

 to identify all individual systems used in the case company;

to map interrelationships amongst various systems including those networked with other parts of the parent group and other financial institutions;

 to test and prove that these systems were millennium compliant.

The second stage of the Y2K program commenced in 1999 and aimed to address non-compliant situations and shift the effort from becoming compliant to maintaining compliance, as well as monitoring clients’ millennium preparations and individual credit policies. Other initiatives that were part of the Y2K program included millennium business continuity planning, millennium rollover planning, contract reviews and external testing. Specifically, business continuity was required to ensure that the business could still function continuously even if a disaster occurred. The three major tasks of business continuity were:

## The diversity and complexity of IT systems

IT systems employed in day-to-day business vary not only from the front, middle to back office, but also from product to product. In addition to office- and productspecific systems, there are systems which network these three offices for various products. There is a large variety of IT systems used by the bank, some of which had been developed in-house while others had been acquired externally. As explained above, one of the primary objectives of the Y2K program was to identify existing systems, their interrelationships and to test their millennium compliance. In order to achieve these objectives, a Y2K program team was established in 1998. Most of the individuals in this team were London-based but a couple of the core team members were located in overseas offices and communicated via video-conferencing, telephone and email. This core team was responsible for the Y2K program but relied on others within the bank to supply the relevant information and knowledge needed to identify the systems, their users, and the uses to which they were put in terms of business functions. To gather such information, these core team members needed to have background knowledge not only of technology but also of the business. The resulting information and lessons were codified into a centralised database. Lessons and the information codified in the centralised database were then distributed to various branches across the globe both through the case company’s Intranet and through hardcopy.

While the task of identifying systems, users and uses may appear to be straightforward, the core team quickly found that it was not. Problems arose in particular because standard IT systems that had been implemented, had been developed and modified gradually over time by the joint efforts of the end users and the technologists. According to interviewees, handbooks provided by the software house, could no longer provide valid information about such systems, because of the gradual modification of the standard systems. For custom-built systems, developers and end users were the only people with the necessary knowledge of such systems in terms of their application and development processes. The difficulty was found to be intensified because there was no system and practice in place to systematically codify the modification of each system. Therefore, the Y2K program members were required to integrate and articulate this knowledge by interacting with developers and end users. Moreover, the modification of systems is an ongoing process. Therefore, even though a system was designated millennium-compliant, a constant effort was still required to maintain the compliant status. Thus, constant interaction with the developers and the users was vital if the Y2K program team members were to keep track of system modifications and ensure their millennium compliance.

## Analysis and lessons learned

The purpose of this case study is to examine the mechanisms by which functional-specific knowledge was integrated during the process of implementing a Y2K program. Findings derived from the analysis are conceptualised in Figure 1 and the cross-functional knowledge integration process is outlined as follows: (1) the penetration of three interrelated types of boundary; (2) the expansion of different paradigms to achieve shared understanding; (3) the reconfiguration of organisational memory to create new organisational routines and knowledge.

## The process of boundary penetrating

The analysis of the case material suggests that one of the key processes involved in knowledge integration is the penetration of boundaries. Program team members needed to penetrate various boundaries in order to acquire the necessary knowledge for the program implementation. As noted by one of the interviewees: ‘individual managers who are three layers down have gone around the organisation and tried to implement the program because they were told to do so by their boss. The manager himself, who was going around to make the changes, has been beaten, abused and demoralised by not getting the support of his peer group and his boss’s peer group.’ Various types of boundaries that inhibit cross-functional knowledge integration were identified. These are summarised based on individual, group and organisational levels in Table 1, and each type of boundary is also broken down in terms of its taskrelated and socio-emotional aspects. In addition, various mechanisms that encouraged the penetration of each type of boundary are outlined.

## Types of boundary

Formal organisational structures, which separated divisions or departments, were found to create boundaries that inhibited cross-functional knowledge integration. The analysis further indicated that such a type of boundary was further intensified by the geographic dispersion of organisational units due to the case organisation’s global operation. Even though information communication technology (ICT), such as groupware and email, was intensively used to network these dispersed units, the constraints of physical distance and time differences were still evident. This has been found by previous research (eg Ciborra & Suetens, 1996; Jarvenpaa et al, 1998). In particular, the global operation was structured in such a way as to minimise each branch’s dependence on others. As a result, working relations were formed primarily between the head office and each branch, rather than across various branches world-wide; and face-to-face social interactions were limited mainly to organisational members who worked in the same geographic location.

However, the case material demonstrates that boundaries were not merely created by formal organisational structures intensified by the dispersion of organisational units. They also originated from differences in organisational subcultures in which unique beliefs and distinctive practices were constructed, understood and applied (Schein, 1996). As is evident in the case, subcultural differences were reflected in different sets of tasks performed by different groups of individuals (Schein, 1996; Sheptycki, 1998), such as the front office was in charge of trading and the back office was responsible for payment settlement. Also, subcultural differences were observed in the distinctive ‘social identity’ that was shared by each specific group of individuals intellectually and emotionally (Tajfel & Turner, 1985). The implication for cross-functional knowledge integration is that organisational subculture creates a barrier that restrains communication (Schein, 1996) and the creation of coherence (Tajfel & Turner, 1985) across different subcultural groups.

In addition to the boundary of subcultures, the penetration of the knowledge boundary is equally critical. Knowledge required by the Y2K program team was dispersed within the organisation (Tsoukas, 1994) and existed in various forms (Collins, 1993; Blackler, 1995; Spender, 1996). It was enclosed and often concealed by departmental or functional boundaries, reflecting the stickiness and embeddedness of knowledge (Nonaka, 1994; von Hippel, 1994). In the case organisation, the team members penetrated into each function not only to gain collaboration and support, but also to identify what knowledge was needed, who possessed it, and how such knowledge could be externalised and codified.

## Interrelationships between various types of boundary

Interrelationships between these types of boundary indicate that the formal organisational structure, in particular the dispersion of organisational units around the globe, inspires the development of distinctive subcultures. This is reflected in the statement of one interviewee: ‘you can easily sense a significant difference in terms of attitudes, norms and working procedures from branch to branch. Here, we are not only talking about the differences between London and New York, but also between various offices across the London Headquarters.’ Subcultural differences are further reflected in differences in knowledge possessed by different groups. The case material endorses the finding that knowledge is socially embedded (Nonaka & Konno, 1998) and subculturally specific (Sackmann, 1991; Collins, 1993). Hence, the boundary penetrating process involves penetrating not only departmental barriers, but also subcultural and knowledge barriers.

Unlike previous research (eg Cooper, 1988; Moenaert & Sounder, 1990), findings from this study suggest that processes of cross-functional knowledge integration are not simply triggered when information is shared or made available across departments. Instead, organisational members are required to penetrate various types of boundary created by the dispersion of organisational units, the existence of subcultural differences (Schein, 1996), as well as the socially constructed nature of knowledge (Berger & Luckmann, 1967). Only when these boundaries have been penetrated could the knowledge be integrated cross-functionally. The following section outlines some of the approaches taken by the team to penetrate the various types of boundary.

## Approaches taken to penetrating departmental and subcultural boundaries

In terms of penetrating departmental and subcultural boundaries, evidence from the case suggests that legitimate power did not always appear to be the most appropriate approach. This echoes the notion of ‘social defenses’ (Bain, 1998) as one of the syndromes associated with the exertion of power in forcing collective learning to take place. As one of the interviewees stated: ‘you can’t just tell them that ‘because the CEO wants you to do it, so you have to do it’ . . . It is like asking them to do you a favor.’ Instead, the team members argued that trust was one of the most important requirements for penetrating such boundaries—in particular the ‘companion trust’ identified by Newell and Swan (2000).

The need for trust has been identified in relation to processes of knowledge creation. For example, Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998) identified the relational dimension of social capital. The Y2K case suggests that trust is also crucial in relation to the process of cross-functional knowledge integration. This has been ignored in previous accounts of knowledge integration. For example, Grant (1996) fails to take account of the influence of trust on the dynamics of knowledge integration. In the Y2K case trust was found to be crucially related to crossfunctional knowledge integration since it facilitated the penetration of the various types of boundary, as outlined in Table 1. This need for trust highlights the inadequacy of considering knowledge sharing as a merely psychological issue related to the individual’s willingness to share what they know. This is because boundaries that inhibit cross-functional knowledge integration and sharing are not merely created by individuals’ mental barriers, but are also created by other contextual issues, such as the existence of organisational subcultures and the reality of functionally specific knowledge. In other words, lack of common knowledge (Demsetz, 1991) and subcultural distinctiveness across organisational members can also impede knowledge integration and sharing. Approaches taken to penetrating the knowledge boundary are considered next.

Approaches taken to penetrating knowledge boundary The transferability and communicability of knowledge was found to directly influence the process as well as the outcome of the boundary penetrating process, in particular, in the case of tacit knowledge (Polanyi, 1958; Spender, 1996). One interviewee recalled: ‘we are constantly having meetings with people from the technology as well as the business side. One of the problems we face is to obtain knowledge which they know well but struggle to describe.

Evidence from the case suggests that team members need to be equipped not only with the skill to absorb this knowledge, but also with the competence to help participants to articulate and externalise it. The knowledge boundary was penetrated by having and developing common knowledge (Demsetz, 1991) or ‘redundancy’ (Nonaka, 1994) between organisational members. It was found in the case that when the team was formed initially, a degree of knowledge redundancy was ensured through the selection and recruitment of core team members. The Team Leader and the Global Head of Human Resources explained that, in addition to communication skills and project management expertise, individual’s background knowledge in business and technology was one of the main criteria for recruitment and selection.

Another approach to penetrating the knowledge boundary found in the case echoes the concept of mutual learning (March, 1991; Boland & Tenkasi, 1995) by which individuals acquire knowledge outside their own expertise area to create redundancy with others. Mutual learning between the technologists’ and traders’ communities was facilitated by using various specific training courses. This allowed technologists to acquire knowledge related to trading, and the traders to obtain knowledge needed for understanding technology development. Demsetz (1991) and Grant (1996) argue that this approach is an ineffective and inefficient way to integrate knowledge cross-functionally. However, the Managing Director of the case organisation saw this as ‘a time-consuming but strategically critical step for the long-term technology development’. According to him, this radical approach towards training aimed not only to break down the conventional separation between technology and business as found in many investment banks, but also to speed up technology development through improving communication between the two communities. The importance of mutual learning will be further addressed during the discussion of the other knowledge integration processes.

The penetration of various types of boundary, in particular the boundary created by knowledge, is related to the creation of shared understanding which is more specifically addressed in the following knowledge integration process—paradigm expanding.

## The process of paradigm expanding

This mechanism refers to the process by which program participants (both core team members and other organisational members involved in the Y2K program) communicate, confront and anticipate different concerns, attitudes and perceptions as a means of building a shared understanding and collectively making sense of knowledge created during the implementation of the Y2K program. The rationale behind using the term ‘paradigm expanding’ rather than ‘paradigm shifting’, a term which has often been used in the current literature (see for example, Chia, 1996), is threefold. First, it is because of the ingrained characteristic of paradigm as reflected in the concept of incommensurability suggested by Kuhn (1970). According to Kuhn, incommensurability indicates the rarity of an individual’s completely changing or shifting his/her paradigm. This surfaces the second point which is that individuals do not necessarily need to fundamentally shift their paradigms to solve their paradigmatic differences with others, they can simply expand their existing paradigm. Third, it reflects the difficulties involved in actually encouraging paradigmatic change (Argyris & Scho¨n, 1978).

Findings suggest that the process of paradigm expanding can be characterized based on two dimensions: intellectual and emotional. The former is termed ‘intellectual buy-in’ and refers to the acquisition of knowledge needed to meet the program’s requirements and the achievement of consensus between different stakeholders’ interests and concerns. The latter is labeled ‘emotional buy’ and reflects the way in which social networks are developed and reshaped within a community and between different communities (Scott, 1992), in particular through ‘emotional attachment’ (Lembke & Wilson, 1998) between the team, end users and technologists. Despite the fact that intellectual and emotional buy-in are analytically separated, we are aware of their interconnected relationship. In other words, paradigm expanding is not merely an intellectual or emotional activity. Instead, it is a collective process characterised by an interplay between the intellectual and emotional dimensions.

Referring to the case, it is clear that even though most of the core team members had some background knowledge in technology and business, the challenge to obtain required knowledge from the technologists and users involved more than just the acquisition of knowledge. Rather, it is a process by which paradigmatic differences are resolved through the expansion of organisational members’ paradigms to create a degree of overlap that further enables the development of shared understanding.

From the intellectual aspect, the evidence indicates that the way in which IT systems were developed and modified in the case site contributed to the difficulty of building paradigmatic overlap between the program team, technologists and end users. Firstly, systems had often been modified through the collective efforts of technologists and users. It was often difficult for either party to explain why these modifications had been undertaken, and yet this understanding was necessary so that the program team could understand the rationale for the change. The dynamics of system development and modification can be seen in the metaphor of a ‘black box’ (Latour, 1987) in which specialised knowledge is embedded and the complexity of social interaction is concealed (Scarbrough, 1995). In this study, opening up the black box was necessary, so the Y2K team faced the problem of externalising the context-specific knowledge and of making sense of the social context in which knowledge was constructed. The case findings suggest that it was difficult to externalise knowledge that had been collectively constructed by the technologists and the end users because of the limited overlaps of background knowledge between these two parties. On the one hand, such limited overlaps can be explained by lack of sufficient common knowledge (Demsetz, 1991) or redundancy (Nonaka, 1994) between the end users and the technologists. This made knowledge integration difficult. On the other hand, the limited overlap can be explained by paradigmatic differences (Kuhn, 1970). The distinctiveness of paradigms not only differentiated one community from the other, but also meant that there were differences in for example, core values, norms, stories and mindsets.

Secondly, most of the modifications had occurred through day-to-day operations and had often not been codified. This made the retrieval of related information and the management of IT changes remarkably difficult and problematic. Additionally, program team members found it extremely difficult to educate users about the technological importance of millennium compliance and to get technologists to understand the business significance of the Y2K problem. In other words, people in the business function and technology function did not have sufficient knowledge to understand each other’s needs. This is a more generic problem in the investment banking industry (and probably other industries). As explained by the Global Head of Technology: ‘technology in its own right is a very interesting business. But to be a technologist who understands investment banking and understands where you need to build is incredibly difficult. The real issue installing technology is that users don’t really know what they want. So the traditional model of business requirement, design, develop, test, and implement doesn’t work in investment banking.’

Hence, the team members needed not only interpersonal skills to gain sufficient time from the technologists and users, but also the ability to facilitate dialogue among and between these groups in order constantly to clarify the knowledge that was articulated. More importantly, the process of ongoing socialisation helped to create a shared understanding amongst all members of staff about the technological and business imperatives of the Y2K problem. Referring to the emotional aspect, the way in which this continuous socialisation process helped to achieve a shared understanding echoes the importance of ‘perspective taking’ in facilitating crossfunctional knowledge integration (Boland & Tenkasi, 1995). According to Boland and Tenkasi, understanding different perspectives that are distinctive to each community serves as the first step to enable communication and knowledge sharing between different communities. This reflects the empirical account of Sessa (1996) who argues that perspective taking is vital to minimise intragroup conflicts, because group members are more likely to recognise, understand and take into account individual differences, rather than perceiving conflicts as personal problems. Similarly, in the study of social capital, intellectual capital and organisational competitiveness, Nahapiet and Ghoshal (1998) address the need for exchanging knowledge, as a way of communicating paradigmatic differences, through the development of social capital.

In the present case study, it is evident that through initial contact with the end users and the technologists, team members not only gradually exploited end users’ perspectives but also continuously demonstrated the team’s perspective to the end users and the technologists. By expressing their own particular concerns and interests, the parties were able to negotiate an acceptable way forward. In this way, end users were able to understand the needs of the team, as well as the potential benefits they could gain from their participation and collaboration. It was believed that through the mutual understanding of different interests and concerns between parties, intellectual buy-in, as the representation of group consensus, was achieved through the process of paradigm expanding. However, the findings also suggested that simply achieving intellectual buy-in was not sufficient to explain the on-going character of cross-functional knowledge integration. As one of the interviewees put it, ‘during the past two years, we have spent 20% of our efforts to ensure they [the end users and technologists] understand the importance of Y2K readiness to our business and reputation, and 80% of our efforts to build up a robust relationship with them. So we all feel like a team, think like a team and act like a team.’ This study sees expanding a paradigm to create a degree of overlap with others through nurturing social networks and emotional attachment as the process of achieving emotional buy-in. The need to achieve intellectual and emotional buy-in not only depicts the dynamics underlying the process of paradigm expanding, but is also related to how organisational memory is reconfigured over time.

The process of organisational memory reconfiguring The notion of ‘organisational memory reconfiguring’ refers to the process by which knowledge embedded in the existing business processes and routines, and distinctive ways of ‘knowing’ (Blackler, 1995) pursued by individuals and groups are challenged, cultivated, added to and renewed gradually through the progress of the program. Most conceptualisations of organisational memory treat it as a static collection of stored information (eg Walsh & Ungson, 1991; Moorman & Miner, 1997). However, in this study organisational memory was seen to be reconfigured over time, with this reconfiguration influencing and changing the activities of individuals and communities. In particular, the notion of organisational memory reconfiguring is used here to explain how organisational routines and processes are not diminished, but enhanced and stimulated, by the replacement of new organisational routines and practices through collective learning and unlearning (Hedberg, 1982; Nystrom & Starbuck, 1984; Huber, 1991).

For a program with an organisation-wide coverage, the reconfiguration of organisational memory is a process to which all participants contribute collectively. While the Y2K program team played the leading role in the implementation of the program, it was not just the team that led the organisational memory reconfiguring process. This is supported by studies of organisational learning (eg Fiol & Lyles, 1985; March, 1991) and the creation of organisational knowledge (eg Matusik & Hill, 1998), which show that the synergy of collective efforts cannot simply be considered as an aggregation of individual contributions (Senge, 1990; Hutchins, 1991). In this study, the reconfiguration of organisational memory was found to take place in various ways, eg through the refining of procedures by which IT changes were managed and the approach by which knowledge generated from system modifications was codified. This was illustrated by the Team Leader who explained why the Y2K program team adopted certain approaches and discarded others. According to him, a network of systems was normally tested through two methods; either the entire network was tested or two or three connected individual systems are tested. The former was called endto-end testing and the latter as near-neighbor testing. Despite the fact that the result of end-to-end testing was known to be more reliable, the team adopted nearneighbor testing, because of the time required to conduct end-to-end testing. Also, the team decided to have a centralised approach led by the core team rather than allowing deviation between branches. In addition, during and after the implementation of Y2K, to codify each step of system modification in the database built and maintained by the Y2K team became a vital practice, which was compulsory for all technologists in the case company to follow. Implications derived from the process of organisational memory reconfiguring suggest not only the importance of continuous learning and unlearning to facilitate organisational renewal (Jones & Hendry, 1992), but also the need for having a system in place to ensure that knowledge generated from business activities can be systematically codified and managed to facilitate the process of cross-functional knowledge integration.

Moreover, throughout the Y2K program organisational memory was reconfigured through a process of social interaction between communities so that common practices were defined and redefined (Brown & Duguid, 1991; Lave & Wenger, 1991). On the one hand, organisational memory represents collectively shared ‘frames of reference’ (Shrivastava & Schneiderit, 1984) that guide individuals’ decision making and actions. On the other hand, it is also reflected in a diversity of functionally specific knowledge and practices (Lawrence & Lorsch, 1967; Demsetz, 1991) and a variety of social identities (Tajfel & Turner, 1985; Schneider & Northcraft, 1999). Those two contrasting aspects indicate that the dynamics of organisational memory reconfiguring are conceptually similar to the continuous evolution of ‘loosely coupled systems’ (Orton & Weick, 1990) in which distinctiveness between systems co-exists with similarities.

Furthermore, organisational memory reconfiguring was found to influence not only the implementation of the Y2K program but also other organisation-wide initiatives. As the Head of Technology observed, ‘given an increasing need for implementing global IT programs, we have to make sure that we constantly and consciously learn from each program we implement, in particular the Y2K program, and use the experience gained for other new programs.’ This viewpoint was also supported by the IT Security Manager who suggested that the Y2K program had been valuable in relation to the implementation of global IT security. This was not simply because of its technological relevance, but also because it had generated lessons related to managing IT programs on a global scale.

The case analysis suggests that the direction in which organisational memory was reconfigured sometimes deviated remarkably from what had originally been intended. For instance, subcultural boundaries faced during the program implementation prompted the organisation to examine the influence of subcultural differences in its global operation and to initiate an organisationwide cultural change program. However, it was not the Y2K program itself that led to the initiation of the cultural change program. Instead, it was various problems faced during implementation that energised the ongoing reconfiguration of organisational memory. This echoes the concept of ‘incidental learning’ (Marsick & Watkins, 1990), which highlights the deviation between what organisations intend to learn and what they actually learn.

Additionally, the reconfiguration of organisational memory was associated with a change in the landscape of social networks which evolved along with the Y2K implementation process. The formation of new social networks enabled the achievement of emotional buy-in and further eased the implementation by reducing interpersonal conflicts. This has been found by other studies (eg Sessa, 1996; Lembke & Wilson, 1998). As recalled by one of the interviewees, ‘often, you assume you know someone, simply because you go past his or her desk 3 or 4 times a day, but in fact you don’t. For example, [consider] one of the chaps here. I used to complain he was rather unhelpful and uncommitted when we started. . . . I didn’t know his wife used to work in my department many years ago—until he mentioned it a couple weeks ago. Now, we tend to chat a lot about football, family, holiday, work, indeed everything. . . . I would now say he is definitely not the type of guy I knew before.’ The importance of emotional buy-in not only surfaces the socially constructed nature of organisationa memory reconfiguring, but also pinpoints that emotional buy-in is equally critical for the process of paradigm expanding as for the process of organisational memory reconfiguring.

Amongst various issues, which influenced the reconfiguration of organisational memory, this study found that the enrolment of new members was especially critical. By bringing in expertise and experience from other organisations, new members directly and indirectly contributed alternative approaches through which existing working procedures could be carried out. This mirrors the concept of ‘grafting’ as a means of enabling organisational learning (Huber, 1991). Moreover, it highlights the advantages of recruiting ‘contingent workers’, ie people employed on a temporary basis, as advocated by Matusik and Hill (1998), to augment knowledge creation.

## Discussion and conclusion

In this paper we have examined the phenomenon of Y2K as an example of knowledge integration. Based on an empirical case study of Y2K, we have focused on the process of cross-functional knowledge integration and its significance for the implementation of future global IT programs. Unlike most earlier studies, we have argued that Y2K is not only a technological challenge but also involves cross-functional knowledge integration processes, notably: (1) the penetration of different boundaries to obtain required knowledge; (2) the expansion of different paradigms to achieve shared understanding; (3) the reconfiguration of organisational memory to create new organisational routines and knowledge. As elaborated in Table 1 and Figure 1, the complexity of boundaries suggests that the process of boundary penetrating is important not simply in order to create common knowledge between different parties. It is also a trust building process intertwined with understanding different perspectives possessed by individuals as a means of triggering the paradigm expanding process. The analysis has identified two distinctive dimensions of paradigm expanding that help to resolve paradigmatic differences at the intellectual level and, equally importantly, at the emotional level. As shown in Figure 1, the expansion of paradigm leads to the creation of new knowledge, new processes and routines, as well as new landscapes of social networks, as illustrated in the process of organisational memory reconfiguring. These continuous reconfigurations lead however to the reinforcement of new knowledge boundaries, the reformation of new subcultural boundaries, as well as the recombination of formal and informal structures that lead to the development of new structural boundaries. Taking this knowledge integration stance on the issue of Y2K leads to a number of interesting areas that demand careful future investigation.

The analysis highlights a number of managerial lessons that can usefully be applied to other global IT programs. In particular, we have derived three vital guidelines for managers to ensure the future success of knowledge integration within a cross-functional, globally oriented IT program. First of all, the findings suggest that one of the most crucial functions of boundary penetrating is that this process enables team members to sustain program awareness by continuously interacting and communicating with various business functions. In the case company, this interaction enabled the program team not only to promote the strategic importance of Y2K readiness, but also to gain collaboration and support from the end users and technologists. As a result, various boundaries were penetrated and paradigmatic differences were resolved through the achievement of intellectual and emotional buy-in. Hence, it is critical for managers to be aware that programme implementation does not simply involve identifying required expertise and pooling experts together. Equally important, is the issue of achieving emotional buy-in between participants through developing trust and encouraging social interactions. Therefore, we suggest that a large amount of effort should be devoted prior to and during the planning stage of any global IT program to ensure that the various types of boundary are clearly identified and possible mechanisms to enable their penetration are considered.

Secondly, evidence from the analysis suggests that the existence of common knowledge (Demsetz, 1991) between the team, technologists and end users was vital for Y2K implementation, because it facilitated effective communication and enabled the process of paradigm expanding. As elaborated in the case, common knowledge was created during the recruitment and selection of program participants, as well as through training programs that encouraged mutual learning (March, 1991; Boland & Tenkasi, 1995) between different communities. In addition to the corporate training programs, it was evident that mutual learning could also be facilitated by various forms of social interactions between the team, technologists and end users, in particular during informal occasions such as lunch- and coffee-breaks. The significance of mutual learning is crucial not only for the purpose of acquiring knowledge, but also to ensure that there is an appreciation and toleration of emotional differences between individuals. The achievement of intellectual and emotional buy-in is reflected not only in the process of paradigm expanding between team members and end users, but also in the process by which organisational memory is reconfigured. Moreover, the importance of organisational memory reconfiguring also indicates the need for having a system in place to facilitate the management of codified knowledge and the integration of knowledge cross-functionally.

The third implication is that organisations can constantly learn from what they practise and apply what they learn to their future actions. This is not just a matter of the accumulation of program management skills and knowledge but can equally well be considered as a foundation for reconfiguring existing organisational practices. For instance, it is clear from the case study that a crossfunctional program such as Y2K can generate numerous valuable lessons which can then be further applied by the company in its future actions. In this case study, we found that the implementation of the Y2K program provided the case company with valuable experience of managing cross-functional programs on a global scale with a long program life span. Moreover, organisations can learn from and capitalise on their past actions, even when such actions involve a one-off situation (March, 1991), as in the implementation of the Y2K program. Hence, we conclude that the reconfiguration of organisational memory does not merely involve the accumulation of new knowledge, regardless of whether such new knowledge is expected or unexpected by the organisation. It also serves as a vital mechanism to inspire the generative learning (Senge, 1990) that enables organisations to trigger continuous transformation (Bush & Dooley, 1992).

In conclusion, while the Y2K computer problem presented a unique opportunity to reveal and test global computer-related dependencies (McHenry, 1999), the problem also had some vital organisational implications, especially in relation to the issue of global cross-functional knowledge integration. Instead of contributing yet another account of the technical processes of the Y2K problem, this analysis has concentrated on the processes of cross-functional knowledge integration as evidenced by the case company’s experience in the hope of articulating and preserving the valuable lessons which have emerged from this unique computer challenge. More specifically, the analysis of the case has illustrated why the implementation of the Y2K program in the case company was not simply a technological issue but also posed social and psychological-emotional problems.
