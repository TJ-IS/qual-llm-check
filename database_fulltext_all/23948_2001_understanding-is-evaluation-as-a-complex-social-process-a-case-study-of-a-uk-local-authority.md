# Understanding IS evaluation as a complex social process: a case study of a UK local authority

S Jones<sup>1,2</sup>\* and J Hughes<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Conwy County Borough Council, Bodlondeb, Conwy, Wales, UK; <sup>2</sup>Information Systems Institute, University of Salford, Salford, UK

The increasing concern from both IS practitioners and academics about the lack of confidence in the returns on IS investment is evidenced in the IS literature and is further illustrated by the growing number of methods and initiatives in this area. This is particularly evident in the UK public sector where national and local government have a duty to ensure that public money is spent appropriately. In this paper the authors explore the area of IS evaluation by categorising the methods available for use and considering the underlying assumptions of these categories. Building on recent research work with interpretative IS evaluation in the private sector, the authors specifically consider UK local government initiatives and use an in-depth case study of one UK Local Authority to elicit lessons for IS practice. The authors contend that there is currently a prevailing paradigm for understanding IS evaluation based on mechanistic and functional principles that are flawed in practice. On the basis of the case material and from the literature, the authors argue that a complementary approach to this paradigm based on situated practice provides a richer view and a more practically oriented approach to IS evaluation. European Journal of Information Systems (2001) 10, 189–203.

## Introduction

According to Willcocks and Lester (1999), the significant global expenditure on Information Systems (IS) is increasing and organisations will continue to invest heavily in IS. Despite this investment however, Walsham (1999) maintains that there is widespread concern that IS investment does not always deliver value and that many IS projects do not meet business and organisational objectives. This phenomenon has been labelled the ‘IS productivity paradox’ and is most accurately linked to research studies in the early nineties (Brynjolfsson, 1993; Wilson, 1993; Loveman, 1994). Willcocks and Lester (1999) contend that rigorous, or indeed any form of, management evaluation of IS projects is rarely undertaken. A good deal of research supports this view (Ballantine et al, 1999; Bannister, 1999; Hirschheim & Smithson, 1999; Irani & Love, 2001) in which several reasons for the lack of use of methods are cited, including it being too costly and too resource intensive. This research points to a conclusion that evaluation of IS projects, demonstrating the benefits and effect of IS, is rarely achieved.

This prevailing scenario is further supported in a recent Price Waterhouse (2000) review of UK organisational practice. The review highlighted that a top concern of many Chief Executives was how to demonstrate value for money from IS investments. This issue has emerged primarily due to the increasing level of IS expenditure experienced by many organisations and, subsequent difficulty in demonstrating that IS investment delivers value and benefits through an IS evaluation process (Ballantine & Stray, 1998).

With regard to the UK public sector, Central Government has recently further raised the profile of general evaluation issues by introducing the Best Value (BV) legislation in April 2000 (National Assembly for Wales, 2000). The rationale underpinning BV is that all local authorities and other public sector organisations, such as the police and fire services, must ensure that their functions are performing well and that they are delivered in a cost effective and efficient way to ensure that best value is being obtained. Performance is often a subjective and judgmental issue and to assist with the BV process, benchmarking, evaluation and comparison between organisational and service delivery performance form key components of BV. IS benchmarking and evaluation therefore, must now be considered by public sector IS stakeholders. Consequently, organisations such as the Society of Information Technology Managers (SOCITM), the Society of Local Authority Chief Executives (SOLACE) and the Audit Commission (AC) are becoming increasingly concerned with IS evaluation.

The issue of IS evaluation was, unusually, a major topic for discussion at a national public sector Chief Information Officer seminar held in March 2000. A number of telling conclusions were drawn from the plenary discussion. The first being that many organisations invest in IS without value and benefit appraisal being undertaken prior to implementation, and that no evaluation occurs after its introduction. Secondly, that IS managers are concerned that their professional domain is often unfavourably perceived by senior management and IS stakeholders due to IS not achieving expectations. Thirdly, that IS managers are aware that there is an increasing focus on the difficulty in demonstrating the value of IS to an organisation, via current evaluation practice. Fourthly, that IS managers are disappointed that poor IS implementations are often cited as the reasons why organisations fail to reach their objectives. Finally, that IS managers are frustrated that they have difficulty persuading IS stakeholders that IS evaluation is an important aspect which must not be ignored (CIO Seminar, 2000). However, against this background they are, paradoxically, under constant pressure to implement IS solutions to organisational and business problems and to be seen as champions of the information society age by promoting IS capability and potential within organisations.

Looking to the future, UK Central Government has published ambitious targets in its public sector modernisation agenda for delivering all government services electronically to the general public by 2005 (Central Office of Information, 1999). This will be achieved, at least in theory, by implementing online services via the Internet, which will be available for 24 hours a day and 7 days a week. This will require significant additional public sector IS investment. However, many e-commerce initiatives in the private sector have not delivered increased profits or added value or benefit to service delivery (Bannister, 1999). This will further place the IS practitioner community under scrutiny with regard to IS evaluation issues in the public sector.

Against this backdrop the paper is structured as follows. A brief summary of the current thought pertaining to IS evaluation is presented that outlines the state of current IS evaluation practice. It contends that formal, traditional, mechanistic approaches to IS evaluation, when employed, have failed to satisfy IS practitioners and the concerns of senior executives. The paper goes on to maintain that it is time to review the prevailing paradigm and re-assess the IS evaluation process. A complementary, interpretive approach to IS evaluation based upon situated action (Suchman, 1987) and hermeneutics (Heidegger, 1976) is suggested, which the authors present in the paper as ‘situated hermeneutic evaluation’. The ideas are explored and analysed through a case study in which the appropriateness of this approach is considered. The case study uses Grounded Theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967) in an interpretivist paradigm to explore the issues from a rich, contextual and experiential perspective. As such the research provides a view of IS evaluation issues from a position rarely adopted by other researchers in the IS evaluation field, despite the established tradition of interpretivism more generally in IS research. The paper concludes with an analysis of the salient issues, lessons for academics and practitioners and practical guidelines for undertaking situated hermeneutic IS evaluation.

## IS evaluation

The deployment of IS has been heralded as the solution to many organisational and business problems (Hammer, 1991; Introna, 1997; Storey et al, 2000). There are many well-documented examples to support this view, such as the analysis of the American Airlines reservation system in the late eighties (Copeland & McKenny, 1988) and the recent analysis of e-commerce systems (Galliers & Newell, 2000). However, for every claim of IS delivering value there are seemingly an equal number of claims of poor returns on IS investment. There is much research to support this view at both the macro level (Wisemann, 1996; Ballantine et al, 1999) and the organisational case study level, such as the London Ambulance System (Page et al, 1993) and the UK Benefits System (National Audit Office, 2000). Willcocks and Lester (1999, p 1) contend that ‘despite the massive accumulated and rising investment in information technology, on the whole these have not contributed to significant rises in productivity’. Strassmann (1997, p 9) puts the scenario more succinctly, ‘IS investment is everywhere, except in the productivity or profit statistics’. Reasons for the mixed findings include variations in an organisation’s conversion effectiveness (Weill, 1992) and variations in the efficacy of the implementation process (Soh & Markus, 1995). With specific regard to public sector organisations, Willcocks (1994) in the mid-nineties predicted that IS would continue to cost more, but still deliver no more tangible benefits. This prediction has proved to be accurate according to Heeks (1999) and Bannister (2001). IS investment therefore, has not delivered the anticipated benefits.

IS investment is both costly and risky (Ward et al, 1996) and should be appraised for its contribution, value and benefit to an organisation (Ginzberg & Zmud, 1988; Angell & Smithson, 1991; Ballantine & Stray, 1999). Willcocks and Lester (1999) contend that it is difficult to effectively evaluate IS in organisations unless there is a clearly documented, systematic and formal approach to both IS investment justification and post implementation audits. Irani (1998) has classified over 50 IS evaluation methods that aim to assist with this process. At first sight this approach appears to be both appropriate and necessary and difficult to argue against conceptually. However, Walsham (1999) maintains that IS evaluation is extremely complex and difficult in practice. This is evidenced by the continuing disagreement as to the factors and metrics to include in any formal, quantitative approach to IS evaluation, together with inherent difficulties associated with quantifying estimates and the subsequent analysis (Walsham, 1999). Strassman (1997) further maintains that there is widespread disagreement over the usefulness of formal methods and over which model to adopt. Research demonstrates that many organisations have no management processes to govern and measure the achievement of the desired outcome, neither do they have processes to establish what benefits were actually achieved (Ballantine et al, 1999; Willcocks & Lester, 1999). In practice therefore, IS evaluation has not been given a high level of importance in organisations, and indeed is often overlooked.

Remenyi et al (2000) maintain that the sheer size of IS expenditure, its pervasiveness in everyday organisational life, and the uncertainty of its value, has led to growing concern, especially amongst senior executives, about the casual approach to extremely high levels of investment. Willcocks and Lester (1999) also contend that many Chief Executives are uncomfortable about IS expenditure and increasingly frustrated by the inability to find appropriate means to evaluate its effect. This growing concern has led Farbey et al (1999) to conclude that IS evaluation practice is now a key area. This is particularly true of the UK public sector, where IS evaluation has traditionally been neglected (Farbey et al, 1998; Khalifa et al, 2000; Bannister, 2001) and where the Best Value initiative is rapidly raising the profile and importance of IS evaluation.

## Prevailing IS evaluation paradigm

Serafeimidis and Smithson (2000) contend that in common with much of IS research, IS evaluation has been dominated by the scientific, positivist, mechanistic paradigm. Walsham (1999) supports this view and maintains that where organisations have evaluated IS, attention has been given to formal, overt, quantitative methods that attempt to define and measure IS investment costs and benefits. There are many formal IS appraisal models from the modernist, positivist, philosophical perspective, including cost benefit analysis (CBA), return on investment (ROI), discounted cash flow (DCF) and payback, which are based upon economics. These methods are often employed by IS professionals, and other non-user stakeholders such as functional management, company accountants, management consultants and IS suppliers. Hirschheim and Smithson (1999) have further argued that most IS evaluation undertaken by IS professionals concentrates on the technical aspects, such as ‘does it work’, rather than the social aspects, such as ‘is it used successfully’, or business aspects, such as ‘does it deliver value’. Indeed there are many examples of good technical solutions being introduced, only to find that anticipated benefits were not realised because the social elements were not fully considered (Sauer, 1993; Walsham, 1993; Introna, 1997; Irani & Love, 2001). Technical evaluation therefore, is unable to demonstrate that IS implementations deliver benefits.

Typically therefore, formal IS evaluation, when employed, is conducted via formally documented mechanistic processes. In addition, the implementation and operation of IS is monitored and measured in terms of broad costs, technical aspects and perceived accrued benefits. It would appear that formal evaluation approaches based on quantification and technical criteria have considerable legitimacy (Hirschheim & Smithson, 1999). Indeed, there appears to be a continuous striving from academics and practitioners to develop and adopt better positivist, mechanistic methods to improve the prevailing paradigm. A recent addition includes the IT Balanced Scorecard (Willcocks et al, 1998), which is based upon the Balanced Scorecard model (Kaplan & Norton, 1992).

Introna (1997) contends that IS evaluation is more likely to be a symbolic expression of objective and accountable management, to perpetuate an image of the rational manager, rather than an accurate method to aid decision makers. Willcocks and Lester (1999) further maintain that even when formal evaluation processes are in place, these procedures are often not undertaken rigorously, and may even be ignored. Various reasons are cited by stakeholders including ‘it is not necessary’, ‘it is too difficult’, ‘it is too time-consuming’ and ‘it is too costly’ (Jones & Hughes, 1999). Formal evaluation therefore would appear to be ritualistic rather than substantive and whilst formal approaches have met with limited success, the degree to which these methods are useful continues to be cause of much current debate (Strassman, 1997; Farbey et al, 1998; Walsham, 1999). Serafeimidis and Smithson (2000) argue that an explanation for this can be found in terms of Giddens’ structuration theory (Giddens, 1976) whereby existing practices constantly reinforce existing structures, which serve to reinforce the practice. The result is that there is no change in action and no questioning of the underlying values and beliefs.

Even when formal methods are applied rigorously, their relevance in the public sector domain is open to question (Bannister, 2001). This is due to the fact that the standard economic and business measures of added value, productivity, financial payback, return of capital employed are relatively easy to define in a manufacturing environment but have little meaning in public administration (Bannister, 2001). Indeed, the concept of Value for Money (VFM), which has been postulated as the most appropriate model for use in the public sector, has met with limited success. This is especially true with regard to IS projects (Bannister, 2001) due to the complexity of VFM and the difficulty in defining IS value. Margetts and Willcocks (1993) conclude that IS is not a proven cost cutter in the public sector as the costs of IS implementations often outweigh costs and efficiency savings. Heeks (1999, p 44) provides an insight into the difficulties associated with formal methods, when he contends that to understand IS evaluation in public sector organisations ‘one would be better advised to understand the ‘wetware’ between people’s ears rather than evaluating hardware and software costs’, thereby highlighting the importance of ‘opinion’ rather than metrics in the IS evaluation process.

Progressive methods such as the Benefits Management Approach (Ward, 1990) and the Active Benefits Realisation Approach (ABR) (Remenyi et al, 1998), which outline comprehensive benefits management frameworks for IS developments and include both quantitative and qualitative evaluation factors have also had limited adoption and success in practice (Walsham, 1999). This has mainly been due to their large scope, as there are many evaluation factors and stakeholders to include in the process, together with their complexity due to the continuous and iterative approach, which is applied to each stage of the investment lifecycle. The growing number of well-developed, predominately mechanistic, methods for assessing returns, even those that have emerged from practice such as the IT Balanced Scorecard, are viewed with much scepticism by IS practitioners and key IS stakeholders (Hirschheim & Smithson, 1999).

Many IS observers (Mumford & Weir, 1979; Checkland, 1981; Walsham, 1993; Introna, 1997; Smithson & Hirschheim, 1998) have maintained that IS are predominantly social systems and therefore the social aspects are significant. Many authors (Walsham, 1995a; Hirschheim & Smithson, 1999; Serafeimidis & Smithson, 2000; Irani & Love, 2001) have further argued that IS evaluation would be improved by using an interpretive epistemology. The emerging view from this paradigm is that social actors are important and that IS evaluation is a socially embedded process in which formal procedures entwine with the informal assessments by which actors make sense of their situation. This leads to the view that these actors are in the best position to assess IS, offer opinion, and persuade senior executives of the value of the IS. The authors concur with this social view and, in the next section, develop the arguments and consider an approach to IS evaluation that they term ‘situated hermeneutic evaluation’, which draws from this theoretical base to suggest a practical way forward.

## Situated hermeneutic evaluation—a new approach

There is an increasing trend towards broadly interpretive methods of IS research (Walsham, 1995b; Butler, 1998;

Klein & Myers, 1999), based upon hermeneutics (Heidegger, 1976) aimed at understanding the subtleties of the social, contextual, situated and dynamic world in which IS is implemented. Hermeneutics can be treated as both an underlying philosophy and a specific mode of analysis. It is a philosophical approach to human understanding, which provides a philosophical grounding for interpretivism. Hermeneutics is primarily concerned with interpreting meaning, in an attempt to make clear an area or object of study, which would be in some way confused, incomplete, cloudy or contradictory. The interpretation aims to bring an underlying coherence or sense and is therefore the work of thought to decipher meanings (Introna, 1997).

The most fundamental principle of hermeneutics is that of the hermeneutic circle (Heidegger, 1976). This asserts that individuals come to understand their lifeworld, which is a complex whole, from preconceptions about meanings of its parts and their interrelationships. The hermeneutic perspective suggests that realities are constructed from multiple, intangible mental models. These are socially and experientially based, local and specific in nature, and dependent on their form and content on the individual person or groups holding the constructions. Context is therefore, of key importance to an interpretive perspective.

Heidegger’s (1976) notion of throwness argues that social actors are thrown into their life-world, and that their existence is therefore ‘situated’, in that it is specific, individual existence with others within a specific tradition and history. Gadamer (1975) broadens this concept by proposing that tradition shapes an individual’s understanding and prejudices. The concept of situated and lived experience describes the relationship between actors and the scenario in which they are embedded. This in turn provides the context for their understanding and contributes to the formation of prejudices. The social world is therefore composed of a network of interrelations, which are generated by actors’ goals and objectives in the course of their existence. In social situations, actors often find themselves in scenarios where their knowledge and understanding is incomplete. They have to act, but they often have difficulty acting, reflecting on their actions and predicting the outcome of their actions. Context therefore, plays an important idiographic role. However, traditional IS evaluation methods tend to overlook context (Walsham, 1999) and the fact that people are active makers of their real-world reality. Heidegger (1976) refers to this as Dasein— being-in-the-world. Individuals have opinions and consequently, they evaluate, based on their knowledge, experience, background, understanding and intuition, as part of everyday Dasein. These are not formal, overt, evaluation processes but informal, covert processes. Nonetheless, they have significant importance to the individuals and peer groups concerned. With regard to

IS evaluation, these opinions should also be of major importance to an organisation. Yet they are rarely, if at all, requested or valued. Introna (1997) maintains that this is due to the hierarchical and political nature of organisations, which creates a barrier to change and reinforces current practice, in accordance with Giddens’ (1976) structuration theory.

Suchman (1993), an anthropologist, whilst not explicitly concerned with IS evaluation makes an important contribution to this area, because in trying to understand fields such as cognitive science and humancomputer interfacing, she is concerned with ‘situated action’. Suchman (1993, p 71) is ‘dedicated to constructing situated accounts of relations between people and the historically and culturally constituted worlds that they inhabit together, to understand what sense we can make of everyday activities between the acting person and those social and material circumstances in relation to which she or he acts’. In using the word ‘situated’, Suchman is concerned with social interaction in context, which can only be fully understood in relation to the particular situation in which it actually occurred. With regard to IS evaluation, social interaction and actor perception play an important interpretive role, which can provide deep insights into the IS evaluation phenomenon. This situated actor perception must therefore, be obtained and valued. Applying this interpretive perspective to IS evaluation must include the situated social actors, in context, utilising the IS and not detached accountants, economists, managers, IS professionals and IS suppliers which is the case with mechanistic evaluation approaches.

The authors argue that developing and applying an IS evaluation approach based upon the philosophical perspectives of Heidegger’s hermeneutics and Suchman’s situated action would offer an interpretive paradigm for IS evaluation which would be useful in practice. The authors aim to develop this interpretive approach, situated hermeneutic evaluation, which would actively analyse the experience of organisational reality, focusing on multiple stakeholder interests and perspectives. Hirschheim and Smithson (1999, p 403) state ‘it seems certain that IS evaluation will remain a key issue . . . . it seems likely that interpretivist approaches face an uphill struggle for acceptance. It can only be hoped that pioneering researchers continue to experiment with interpretive approaches and are able to demonstrate their validity.’ Wilson and Howcroft (2000) maintain that there is a general absence of interpretive approaches. Recent work by Serafeimidis and Smithson (2000) has however, explored interpretive IS evaluation via case study research in the private sector. The authors wish to build upon this work by undertaking research in the public sector. The research investigates the prevailing scenarios in several UK local authorities and explores the feasibility, desirability, usefulness, acceptance and credibility of a situated hermeneutic IS evaluation approach. One case study is presented here and the research methodology is described in the next section.

## Research methodology

The case study method that was adopted for the research is in the interpretivist tradition, whereby the researcher enters a social setting without any a priori constructs but with a good understanding of the literature. The aim of such a case study is to learn about, and to try to understand, the phenomena (Cavaye, 1996). In this instance the aim was to understand underlying IS evaluation practice, the nature of IS evaluation, the approaches that were used, and the problems that arose. It also explored whether a situated hermeneutic approach to IS evaluation would be appropriate and whether it would be acceptable.

This type of interpretive case study is characterised by Walsham (1995a) and is one in which the subjectivity of the interpretation refers to how the meaning and understanding of the actors is reflected in the final report.

This type of case study emphasises reliability—such as the quality of the recorded data, the process of selection of data—rather than emphasising rigour, which is typified in positivist case research (Weick, 1984; Yin, 1994). For positivist case studies, Cavaye (1996) identifies four evaluation criteria, namely replication, generalisability, control and formality. However, the interpretivist seeks to demonstrate that they have been able to grasp and explain the rationale behind described actions, processes and opinions. The case study draws upon Grounded Theory as the method for data collection and analysis of qualitative data.

The use of Grounded Theory (GT) developed by Glaser and Strauss (1967) fits well to the interpretivist case study. Although the method dates back to 1967, its use in IS research is very recent. It is growing in popularity particularly in the interpretivist IS research paradigm for enabling rich and context-sensitive analysis of social situations (Baskerville & Pries-Heje, 1999; Urquhart, 1999; Hughes & Wood-Harper, 1999; Trauth, 2000). Useful summaries of the use of GT in IS research can be found in Hughes and Howcroft (2000) and Urquhart (2001). GT provides a set of procedures for coding and analysing data and is well suited to the interpretivist style since it keeps the analysis close to the data and provides for inductive ‘discoveries’ about the phenomena under study. However, critics of GT point to its positivist tendencies—not least the canons for evaluation (Corbin & Strauss, 1990)—and also to the contingent ways that GT is used in interpretivist studies. However the authors would argue that from its roots in the social sciences, its philosophy is post-modernist, and that it relies less on dogma and positivist validity models and more on the practical application of methods to suit real situations. In a given methodological context therefore, one would expect that the intended method could differ from the method in use because of the dynamics of the situation and this is entirely consistent with the need to extract richness from social situations. Hughes and Howcroft (2000) consider the use of GT in interpretive research and point to a number of examples in the IS literature where the method has been used to aid the interpretive researcher in guiding the interpretive research process. The procedures associated with GT are given in Figure 1.

A key part of GT is the discovery or generation of theory. The term ‘theory’ is used in the sense that it refers to local empirical models devised on the basis of data. As noted by Strauss and Corbin (1998, p 168), ‘Theory consists of plausible relationships proposed among concepts and sets of concepts’. They maintain that plausibility is strengthened through continued research and that the concepts induced from the study become the basis for future research. The presentation of the theory may be through a mixture of hierarchical concept diagrams and a rich text account, and it is this account, which must always be present. The account attempts to discover process rather than to describe stages or phases. In the case study below the account of theory is presented through the elucidation of categories and then, as part of the interpretive process, lessons are drawn from the generated theory.

## Case study background

The case study concerns one local authority in the UK. It is not the local authority to which one of the authors has affiliation. The organisation provides a full range of public services, including Education, Social Services, Highways, Housing, Planning and Public Protection. It serves a population of 147 000 and has a staffing establishment of 6000. It has an overall annual revenue budget of £150m, and an annual IT revenue budget of £2.5m.

The case study was undertaken using qualitative methods of data collection and analysis as discussed above. Specific techniques included informal, in-depth semi-structured interviews, a review of documentation and participant observation. Six senior IS stakeholders were interviewed. These were the Chief Executive, Chief Information Officer, IS Operations Manager, IS Systems Manager, Assistant Director of Finance and a Social Services Management Officer. The data were subsequently transcribed, validated with interviewees and analysed using GT. In the method, conceptual properties and categories are ‘discovered’ or generated from the qualitative data. This is achieved by following a number of guidelines and procedures. The focus is the actors in the social setting as it is they who provide important transcript data from interviews.

In the case study presented here, the aim was to understand underlying IS evaluation practice, how widespread IS evaluation is, what approaches are used and what problems arise. It is also explored whether an interpretive approach to IS evaluation would be appropriate and whether it would be acceptable. Further, the case study helped to shape what this methodological approach would look like in theory, how would it work in practice, and whether an interpretive approach would offer advantages over conventional evaluation methods.

## Data collection

Coding began as soon as the first interview transcript was prepared. ‘Coding’ is the process of annotating and labelling interview transcripts with a piece of text. At this early stage, the coding was ‘open’ and fairly unrestricted. Each line and each word was scrutinised to produce a code that initially appeared as a simple repetition or summary of the text. This ‘opened’ the inquiry into the data. At this stage, any element of the data was deemed relevant. As more data were collected, the researchers continued the open coding and also began to identify ‘categories’ rather than labels. The categories were formed from groups of words from the initial coding which pertained to the same phenomenon. These categories are at a higher level and more abstract than the concepts they represent. Concepts had to be repeatedly present in the data to warrant the abstraction to the level of category. Both in vivo (words used by an interviewee) categories and categories developed from the data emerged. The categories were generated through the analytical process of making comparisons in order to highlight similarities and differences. To ensure the process was rigorous, the currently available data were revisited and re-coded in relation to the new categories. Knowledge of the domain and previous experience was also drawn upon in order to expand and clarify categories and to increase sensitivity to further ways of exploring the data. As categories were formed, each was allocated an associated memo, which elucidated it and related it back to the data.

As the number of categories increased they were related to each other and to sub-categories. This process is known as ‘axial’ coding. The relationship between categories and sub-categories was explored. This necessitated the gathering of further data to explicate quite specific points in order that a category became saturated. This saturation of a category occurred when the collection of data and its analysis added nothing further to the properties of the category. Categories were then related to each other to provide an integrative framework. During axial coding the analytical memos enabled the development of the properties relating to categories and developed the relationship with sub-categories.

As the number of saturated categories grew and the relationship between them was established they were unified around a ‘core’ category. This core category represented the central phenomenon of the study. As is usual with the GT method, this evolved towards the end of the investigation. At this stage several schemes that linked the data together were identified. The central category was derived using abstraction. That is to say the abstracted central category was widely applicable to the sub-categories to which it related. The ‘filling in’ of poorly developed categories was also undertaken at this stage, in order that all categories in the final integrative framework were saturated.

Two analytical techniques—theoretical sampling and constant comparison—were the means by which the analysis proceeded. These techniques enabled the coding activities to relate to the pool of data and memos that emerged. Theoretical sampling is an inductive technique that was used when the data did not exhaust the theoretical category that was being developed. This checked both the scope and the depth of categories. Constant comparison of new data to existing categories served three purposes. Firstly, it helped to ensure the researchers’ neutrality by constantly being challenged with new data. Secondly, it helped achieve greater precision in grouping together like phenomena and thirdly, it helped further subdivide categories to ensure consistency.

## Case study findings

The end point of Grounded Theory analysis is the generation of theory as noted above and this section elucidates the key categories to produce the rich contextual account. Three key IS evaluation themes emerged from the study (Figure 2). Firstly, that IS investment decisions are usually undertaken in isolation by Information Systems Department Management and that they are largely intuitive and political in nature. Secondly, that evaluation methods, traditional or otherwise, are not utilised. Thirdly, that an interpretive approach to IS evaluation would be worthy of consideration. Each of these is considered further below.

## IS investment decisions

The organisation has three main vehicles for addressing IS investment and evaluation issues. Firstly, a Corporate Information Technology Client Group (CITCG) has been established. This is a steering committee of senior representatives from each Directorate, which is chaired by the Chief Information Officer (CIO). This group is charged with IS investment decisions. Secondly, monthly IS Account Management meetings occur between the IS Departmental Account Manager and staff in individual Directorates to elicit IS requirements and to review IS performance and service delivery. Minutes of these meetings are presented to the CIO and CITCG where subsequent review, discussion and decision making is undertaken. Thirdly, monthly IS Departmental Management Team meetings are held to discuss IS matters. The CIO, in accordance with perceived good practice, has established these three groups.

As a consequence of issues arising from Account Management meetings and dialogue at the CITCG, the IT Departmental Management Team, consisting of three senior IT managers and the CIO, meet to discuss key issues and to make recommendations to the CITCG. IS investment recommendations are therefore usually undertaken in isolation by Information Systems Department Management. In practice, this means that it is almost exclusively the CIO who is the final decision maker at IS Departmental Management Meetings. This is not best practice, as it is likely to produce less effective decisions than if the business staff were integral to the decision making process. However, the CIO maintains that he understands the priorities of the organisation and what IS should be contributing and therefore is best placed to make IS investment decisions.

The decision to invest in IS therefore appears at first sight to be strategic, since the CITCG endorses the choice suggested by the CIO. There is also a seemingly well-planned and participative approach to IS investment decisions. However, in practice, IS implementations are not introduced through a positive commitment by the Authority in its entirety, as the Director’s Management Team and many key stakeholders are not consulted. This is a major weakness of the decision making process and the effectiveness of this approach is open to question. The CIO simply reviews the deployment of IS and makes recommendations for enhancements. The CITCG legitimises the choice of the CIO, but it is seen as an academic exercise. The CIO stated ‘It’s a talking shop. I talk, they listen and that’s it. I’ve never really been challenged’. This was borne out by all interviewees, some of whom were frustrated by the process. ‘I don’t feel I have any influence over the decisions’, commented one interviewee. ‘IS is only introduced when something is failing’, commented another. ## Evaluation methods

The organisation is coming under increasing pressure to adopt IS evaluation metrics, in an attempt to benchmark and better quantify IS value and benefits due to the BV initiative. Traditional mechanistic evaluation processes, however, are not utilised in the case study organisation, although there is a high level of awareness of them. There is no significant pre or post implementation evaluation of IS value and benefit via any formal review processes, ‘except for an estimate of costs’, as these are not undertaken. One interviewee commented, ‘In theory, benefits should be defined and measured, but in practice this is extremely difficult. The problem is the effort required to quantify benefits that you know are going to accrue’. Interviewees have an assumed high expectation of IS implementations and believe that IS will always bring benefits. Interestingly, no risk assessment is undertaken, neither are potential dis-benefits identified. However, the IS Account Manager described how IS implementation and operation is ‘monitored and evaluated in terms of broad costs and accrued perceived benefits’. This is undertaken via regular Account Management meetings between the IT Department and user departments. However, indirect costs, such as staffing costs, are not included. Discussion and discourse during Account Management meetings is widely used to determine level of satisfaction with IS within the organisation. Consequently, difficulties are raised and, where necessary, problems are formally documented. Refinements to IS are then undertaken where possible. Questionnaires are also used to ascertain user satisfaction. This is a form of qualitative, interpretive evaluation of IS implementation. However, questionnaire responses have not been used to any great degree to steer IS issues. Rather, they have been used to obtain a simple measurement of satisfaction. This is a wasted opportunity.

Various reasons for not undertaking formal evaluation were offered by interviewees, including ‘it is not necessary’, ‘it is too difficult’, ‘it is too resource intensive’ and ‘it is too costly’. There is clearly a lack of will to undertake evaluation. Several candid interviewees confided that the main reason was the potential consequences if the results of the evaluation were poor, in that a failure would have to be admitted. Furthermore, ‘the failure would be difficult to rectify in any event, so what was the point? It would be impossible to simply discard the investment and fund and implement a replacement’. One interviewee stated ‘I’m cynical about people being open—decision makers have difficulty owning up to a poor investment decision’. It is evident that it was unusual for a mistake to be admitted—‘no one wants to admit failure, a system goes in and is made to work, and I’m not at all comfortable with that’, stated one interviewee.

IS practitioners and stakeholders regard formal evaluation methods as being of little value. This is the main reason cited why they have been disregarded in the case study organisation. Furthermore, the number of methods available confused matters and made it difficult to select an appropriate, meaningful approach. However, there is now growing concern in this area, especially with the onset of BV. This was of particular interest to one IS manager who stated that ‘we really do need to evaluate, and BV will force this upon us’. There is an increasingly important need to further understand IS implementations in organisations in terms of value, benefit and effect, and it was acknowledged that the organisation currently has a weakness in this area. The lack of management will to undertake evaluation studies is therefore beginning to change.

Despite the lack of evaluation methods, it was interesting to note that the £2.5m annual revenue budget was regarded as being spent appropriately by almost all interviewees. It was perceived that no wastage had occurred, even though it was impossible to demonstrate that this was the case. Indeed, some interviewees suggested that additional IS expenditure was required. All interviewees recognised that IS evaluation is not a simple process and that it is complex and difficult to achieve.

## Interpretive approach to IS evaluation

All interviewees acknowledged that IS monitoring, review and evaluation processes are important to enable problems and issues to be raised, documented and resolved. Despite this acknowledgement, IS evaluation has not been given a high level of importance in the organisation. Any approach to IS evaluation was perceived by non-IS managers as the responsibility of IS management. However, the authors are uncertain as to whether IS management should be solely responsible for, or capable of, IS justification or IS benefit evaluation.

All interviewees considered that user opinion is an extremely important aspect, which is overlooked. ‘User opinion is an important factor, but we haven’t really obtained and reviewed it’ commented one interviewee. This is unfortunate, as it was recognised that user opinion has the potential to produce deep insights into IS evaluation and could greatly assist in this area. One interviewee commented that ‘user opinion is everything really’. For example, when IS performance tools indicate that a particular IS is performing well and user opinion suggests a contradictory view, it is invariably the subjective user perception that prevails. ‘It is the user view that determines the course of action’ stated the IS Operations Manager. This leads to the conclusion that the basis on which individuals form opinions is an important interpretive act. However, what is problematic, as described by the interviewees in the case study, is the articulation and documentation of the process. One interviewee commented that ‘it may be difficult to obtain opinion and properly analyse it’. This is the main barrier to undertaking interpretive studies in the case study organisation.

Interviewees acknowledge that improving IS evaluation may lie, not with the development of better quantitative methods, but rather in the experiences of multiple IS stakeholders. A comment from one interviewee was typical, ‘we really need to understand what IS users think of information systems’. Furthermore, they are well aware that practical elaboration of the evaluation is predominantly grounded in tacit knowledge. To better understand IS value and benefit, it may be necessary for the organisation to change its attitude towards IS evaluation. Informal, covert, situated, hermeneutic evaluation occurs within the IS user community. Although it is acknowledged by senior management that this happens, it is not obtained or valued, with the exception of informal discussions and questionnaires. However, the ethos of interpretivism is one of subjective assessment and opinion, and whether interpretive evaluation is valued or disregarded by senior management may, as one interviewee commented, ‘depend on whether the evaluation findings support the management decision’.

It was suggested that a role could be created for an independent, neutral, IS business and evaluation professional to facilitate discourse and undertake interpretive IS evaluation studies. However, it was acknowledged that evaluation cannot consist of one prevailing view from one stakeholder perspective, which is usually the position obtained via mechanistic evaluation methods. IS evaluation therefore, must embrace multiple, subjective, stakeholder views, which cannot be obtained via mechanistic evaluation methods seeking an objective view. However, one interviewee acknowledged that ‘user opinion is a major factor to consider and the bigger the project, the bigger the likelihood of it spanning multiple interest groups’. Interviewees stated that there are inherent intuitive and political dimensions to all IS investment and evaluation issues. Within the case study organisation, there exists different stakeholder groups with differing interests, opinions, motivations and objectives. These require identifying and utilising in the IS evaluation process.

## Discussion

The empirical work indicates that IS practitioners are coming under increasing pressure to adopt IS evaluation metrics, in an attempt to benchmark and better quantify IS value and benefits. The prevailing situation with respect to IS evaluation is of major concern to the case study organisation, as prescriptive mechanistic methods are consistent with the BV benchmarking approach to IS evaluation (National Assembly for Wales, 2000). However, IS practitioners and IS stakeholders in the case study are not concerned with the specifics of IS assessment metrics, detailed benefit measurement formulae, evaluation concepts or cost benefit justification techniques as asserted by the traditional positivist approach. They are concerned with the success, operation and effectiveness of IS in practice, which concurs with the view of Walsham (1999) and Bannister (2001). In addition, they are concerned with issues that must be considered and addressed to ensure the organisation obtains maximum value from the implementation of new technology as articulated by Hirschheim and Smithson (1999). This cannot be assessed unless interpretive IS evaluation is both undertaken and articulated (Hirschheim & Smithson, 1999). Analysis of this articulated user opinion leads to an understanding of IS evaluation via ‘situated’ practice, where evaluation is a local, embodied, emergent and contingent process, and where evaluation remains ‘open’ in the light of future events. The conjunction of hermeneutics and situated action would involve specialist interpretive evaluators with the necessary skills to engage with social actors in order to evaluate IS and assist with the articulation process, as argued by Jones and Hughes (1999). The aim would be to offer feedback to both IS practitioners and senior executives, and to improve the understanding of the value, benefit, suitability and success of the particular IS from the subtleties of the users’ contextual perspective. The situated hermeneutic evaluation would include discourse at the feasibility stage, development and procurement stage and post implementation stage. An organisation would therefore have an interpretive IS evaluation approach to complement the IS systems development, project management and implementation functions. Given the levels of IS expenditure currently being experienced and the potential impact of IS, the authors argue that this is not an extravagant use of resources.

One of the greatest IS challenges facing UK public sector organisations is to ensure that IS implementations deliver value and furthermore, to demonstrate this to senior executives. This is certainly true for the UK local authority in the case study, which along with other UK local authorities, have been charged with the task of demonstrating BV. In organisations such as the case study, where IS evaluation procedures do not exist, and stakeholder opinion is not significantly canvassed, it makes it extremely difficult to judge whether IS delivers value. In an era when organisations depend on the successful use of IS (Introna, 1997), where large costs are involved (Bannister, 2001), and where chief executives are dissatisfied with the level of return on IS expenditure (Remenyi et al, 2000), this can no longer be acceptable. The authors argue that IS practitioners will need to articulate this view to senior executives, rather than attempting to defend their current practice.

A further interesting finding which emerged when undertaking the empirical research was that the interview process, and particularly the procedures associated with the Grounded Theory method itself, stimulated IS practitioners and key stakeholders to question their own underlying assumptions. This in turn provoked a significant amount of internal self-questioning and review. The research process became a process of self-evaluation of each individual’s decision making process.

At the outset, the authors maintained that in spite of the numbers of methods and techniques available for IS evaluation the prevailing situation is one of under-use or non-use of these methods and techniques. The authors argue that evaluation is not a static event, based upon grand mechanistic design, rather it is a continual process, based upon experiential and subjective judgement. The authors contend that this situated hermeneutic evaluation process occurs in the IS user community, as part of Dasein—being-in-the-world, and that it is this judgement that is of paramount importance to senior IS practitioners, and ultimately, top executives. The case study adds empirical weight to that view because this important issue has been recognised by the interviewees. However this is not an entirely new finding (Walsham, 1999; Hirschheim & Smithson, 1999; Serafeimidis & Smithson, 2000). What is significant is that the case study leads to a view that amongst the IS practitioner community and amongst the IS stakeholders that IS evaluation is taking place but it is neither being gauged nor is it being articulated. This raises a number of questions about the dominant prevailing IS evaluation approach that largely depends upon mechanistic and functionalist principles. Not least amongst these questions is the possibility that where methods are used they may be being used simply to legitimise the evaluation process (Introna, 1997). As a result the grounded concerns about the IS evaluation process are left unexposed.

The critical aspect of the case study research is that via the GT methodology it explicitly sought to discover the underlying assumptions, the contexts and the experiences of those involved in the IS evaluation process. No claim for generalisation is made for interpretive studies of this type (Walsham, 1999). However, the richness of the case study does lead to lessons for IS practitioners.

Lesson 1 Organisations must question the underlying assumptions of existing IS evaluation methods and seek alternative perspectives where these methods fail to achieve desired objectives and outcomes.

Lesson 2 IS evaluation must have an explicit concern for the social context and interpretive methods must be adopted which explicitly situate stakeholders at the centre of the evaluation process.

Lesson 3 The views, beliefs and assumptions of stakeholders must be exposed and considered within the IS evaluation process and not be ephemeral to it.

Lesson 4 Organisations must have an interpretive IS evaluation approach to complement the IS development, project management and implementation functions.

Lesson 5 A new role must be created for an IS evaluation professional to facilitate and conduct interpretive situated hermeneutic IS evaluation studies.

Lesson 6 The hierarchical and political nature of public sector organisations creates a barrier to change and this must be overcome to ensure interpretive approaches are successful in practice.

The research at this point has been exploratory, not confirmatory, and it has helped to guide the researchers to indicate practical guidelines for organisations. However it should be stressed that these guidelines do not arise directly from the research. They simply help to focus on future work where the guidelines will be applied in a practical domain to ascertain their usefulness. In Table 2 the authors indicate how these practical guidelines map to the characteristics of situated practice.

The important feature of any practical guidance is the method by which a facilitator can elicit the views and concerns of stakeholders in the evaluation process. Interestingly, a positive side effect of the research above suggests that methods in practice similar to the GT method for research may provide this set of procedures for both the articulation and the dissemination of a grounded view from the stakeholders in IS evaluation. Furthermore, the GT procedures would enable the situated processes to be documented. This clearly is important both for the IS evaluation domain in which it is applied but also as a resource for future evaluations. Indeed, it may provide an organisational situated record from which all stakeholders could learn. Finally, as highlighted in the early part of this paper, the engagement of stakeholders to explicitly address the political situation and power relations is also key. ## Conclusions

The ever-increasing expenditure on IS and the introduction of BV in the public sector has resulted in growing concern with regard to the returns delivered from IS investments. This is compounded by the fact that many organisations do not evaluate IS and it is, therefore, difficult to demonstrate that IS delivers benefits. Where IS evaluation is undertaken, formal methods prevail. However, there continues to be disagreement over their usefulness and over which model to adopt. In practice, these formal methods are often not used rigorously or are used as a means of political justification. It is difficult, therefore, to demonstrate that formal evaluation methods work well and that IS investments deliver the anticipated effect and benefits using these methods. There is currently dissatisfaction with traditional IS evaluation processes, because they have failed to satisfy the concerns of senior managers in organisations. The future outlook cannot improve unless organisations give sufficient attention to IS evaluation issues and seek alternative approaches.

The authors’ initial empirical case study research reinforces this prevailing view and also gives insight into a new interpretive IS evaluation approach, situated hermeneutic IS evaluation. In the case study, IS investment decisions are undertaken in isolation by IS departmental management. They are largely intuitive and political in nature. IS evaluation methods, traditional or otherwise, are not utilised and are viewed with scepticism. It is difficult therefore, for the organisation to demonstrate that IS investments deliver the anticipated benefits. Informal, interpretive IS evaluation is taking place in the user community, but it is not being articulated or gauged. User opinion is largely ignored and the grounded concerns with regard to IS evaluation are not exposed. Furthermore, the current hierarchical and political structure of the organisation creates a barrier to interpretive approaches. These obstacles must be overcome and opinion gathered if situated hermeneutic evaluation is to be successful in practice. The case study organisation acknowledges that a situated hermeneutic approach to IS evaluation is worthy of consideration and would be useful. However, prior to this research, the organisation had not previously considered this approach.

The literature and the case study demonstrate that IS evaluation is an extremely complex issue, which has far more than just an economic dimension. Undertaking IS evaluation is a multi-faceted, difficult, continual and essentially social process. Emerging interpretive approaches to IS evaluation, such as situated hermeneutic evaluation, can assist in this area. To be more effective in understanding and gauging IS value and benefit it is necessary for organisations to change their attitude towards IS investment. Traditional, formal methods have failed to satisfy concerns in practice. However, the informal, situated hermeneutic evaluation that occurs within the IS user community is of key importance and organisations should request and value this perspective. This interpretive approach could overcome weaknesses within the prevailing mechanistic paradigm and help address practitioner concerns.

IS evaluation is a subject that traditionally has not been given significant attention, particularly in IS practice. This is evidenced in both the literature and the case study. However, with the growing disillusionment of top executives with IS and the imperatives of BV in the UK

public sector, it is an area which is now attracting a growing level of interest and where the authors contend that situated hermeneutic evaluation of IS must play a key role.

Jim Hughes works at the University of Salford and also holds an adjunct senior lecturer position at the University of South Australia. He has a PhD in Information Systems from the University of Salford. He has worked as a software engineer and systems analyst for the Ford Motor Company and has consulted to a number of small and medium sized enterprises in the field of information technology. Jim is a lecturer and researcher in the fields of knowledge management, and IS evaluation and has published over 30 articles. Jim is particularly interested in the situated—contextual—nature of information and in the course of his PhD he explored a methodology for eliciting situated requirements. Jim’s work is interpretive and he has made innovative use of the procedures associated with Grounded Theory in an interpretivist mode.
