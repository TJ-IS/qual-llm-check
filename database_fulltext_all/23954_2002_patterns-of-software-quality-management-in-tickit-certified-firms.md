# Patterns of software quality management in TickIT certified firms

B Work

Department of Computer Science, Cardiff University, Cardiff, UK

This study is a type of practice research known as repertoire building. Its aim is to identify patterns in the way software companies approach quality management in pursuit of ISO certification. This study exam ines eight UK software companies, all of which were early users of TickIT guidance for gaining ISO certification. It uses a research method which combines multiple, embedded case studies with grounded theory in order to identify patterns in the ways in which these companies perform software quality management. The study finds that each company has adopted either a policing, an advisory, or a hybrid approach towards software quality management. A company’s attitudes towards both internal and external auditing characterise these approaches. The approach selected by a company affects the structure and the tasks of its quality department. These approaches reflect senior management’s reasons for seeking TickIT certifi cation. Once a company adopts an approach, it is unlikely to change unless there is new management. The study concludes that the TickIT guidance provides a suitable baseline for software quality control. This requires a company to attain Level 2 in the Capability Maturity Model. However, once a company obtains certification, TickIT does not encourage further improvement in its processes. As a result, most companies focus on product quality and largely ignore process quality. In fact it appears that none of these approaches include an adequate mechanism for software quality improvement. Hence, the failure to address process innovation in the TickIT guidelines may explain why it was difficult to distinguish among the effectiveness of these three approaches.

## Introduction

The problem of software quality dates from the birth of the industry. Experts at the first Conference on Software Engineering in 1968 coined the term ‘software crisis’ to describe the inadequacy of the software written then. Dijkstra’s opinion was typical of those who attended when he observed that ‘. . . the massive dissemination of error-laden software is frightening’ (Naur & Randell, 1968, p 9). Thirty years later the failures of high-profile projects, such as London Ambulance (South West Thames Regional Health Authority, 1993), Taurus (Griffiths & Willcocks, 1995), and the British Passport Application System (Islam, 2001) suggest that the problem of software quality remains.

Software quality is ‘the totality of features and characteristics of . . . [the software] that bear on its ability to satisfy stated or implied needs’. (Ould, 1990, p 98). Thus, the quality of software may depend on a variety of characteristics, such as reliability, cost, maintainability and portability, as well as an absence of faults (Sommerville, 1996, p 590). Moreover, the importance of different features varies from one program to the next.

The testing and debugging of software has always been a significant task. Computer scientists have argued for more formal techniques of verifying software (Hayes, 1987). The evidence from practice strongly suggests that neither testing nor formal verification is very effective. One reason for this is that both activities focus on the detection of errors. They do not seek to confirm other important aspects of the software (Sommerville, 1996, p 408). Another reason is that both are inspection based. They only rectify faults which arise out of the process of software development. They are reactive, not anticipatory. They emphasise correction rather than prevention. They are techniques for quality control (Ould, 1990, p 71).

Ideally, firms should never create faulty products as finding and correcting errors is a wasteful activity. Rather, they should expend their energies on assuring that their software development processes reduce the possibility of error. Oakland, for example, defines quality assurance as:

the prevention of quality problems through planned and systematic activities. These will include the establishment of a good management system and the assessment of its adequacy, the audit of the operation of the system and the review of the system itself (1993, p 16).

The distinction between planning and reviewing is explicit in this definition. If software developers depend on standardised tasks and best practice, they will find it more difficult to make mistakes. Quality planning is the activity of selecting appropriate procedures and standards for preventing software faults from occurring (Ould, 1990, p 71). However, the effectiveness of current procedures and standards needs to be audited continuously. Thus, quality assurance should be on-going.

Inherent within the philosophy of quality assurance is the idea that organisations must not just make their processes more effective, they must work relentlessly to better them. For example, Deming (1986), one of the founders of the quality movement, lists constant process improvement as one of the central points of his programme. Herbsleb et al reinforce this view within the context of software when they write ‘A central value of the quality paradigm is that processes must not only be improved, but they must be continuously improved’ (1997, p 26).

A number of authors, for example, Deming (1986) and Juran (1988), argue that ‘[if] quality isn’t ingrained in the organisation, it will never happen’ (Crosby, 1980, p 19). This entails the nurturing of a quality conscious culture within the firm by senior management. This approach is central to the concept know as Total Quality Management (TQM) (Oakland, 1993).

Initially, consultants assisted software houses to develop their own approaches to quality management. However, as the global economy became increasingly dependent on software, governments and international organisations have become interested in software quality. This has led them to promulgate standards for quality management and to encourage the growth of external validators of these standards. There are four major standards: The Capability Maturity Model (CMM), Software Process Improvement and Capability determination (SPICE), the European Model of Business Excellence (EMBE), and TickIT (not an acronym).

The best known standard for managing software quality is CMM (Humphrey, 1989). The US Department of Defence developed this model during the 1980s in order to evaluate the quality of its software vendors. It incorporates the idea of process improvement by using a fivelevel model reflecting the increasing sophistication of a firm’s software assurance activities. The CMM method begins with an assessment to determine a company’s level of maturity. This leads to the creation of an improvement plan, so that it may move to the next level.

SPICE is a European standard. It focuses on individual processes rather than the organisation as a whole. However, it provides a five-level model for characterising the maturity of software development processes in much the same manner as CMM (Dorling, 1993). A SPICE assessment begins by placing the specific process in the context of organisational goals. The assessment takes place. The firm derives and implements a plan for improvements. Finally, an evaluation attempts to confirm that the improvements have been realised.

The European Foundation for Quality Management (EFQM) oversees EMBE (European Foundation for Quality Management, 1997). It is a general quality standard and is not particular to software. Some UK software companies have recently become interested in this standard because of its emphasis on customer satisfaction and because it applies to all of their business processes, not just software development. The method requires a company to prepare a written assessment of its quality system which includes a discussion of its quality facilitators: leadership, policies and strategies, business processes management, people management, and resource management and of its results in terms of customer satisfaction, staff satisfaction, impact on society, and business results. EFQM then assembles a panel of experts who score the assessment on a 1000-point scale.

The TickIT scheme arises from the International Standards Organisation’s (ISO) 9000 which offers a definition of quality assurance systems (British Standards Institution, 1994). This standard is a general reference model, rather than an inflexible requirement, to assure quality. In practice, ISO 9000 requires that a company formally documents its processes and acts accordingly. ISO 9000 certification requires an audit by an accredited external assessor. In fact, certification under this standard does not guarantee quality software because it depends only on conformance between documented and realised processes. Hence, there are only two ways in which an organisation may fail assessment. The documentation may be incomplete or it may not be followed.

ISO 9001 is an improvement to the earlier standard in that it provides a means of judging quality assurance. It institutes a programme for the certification of external assessors of quality and lays down guidelines for evaluating organisations’ quality systems. However, ISO 9001 describes a mass production environment and so, it is not easily translated into a software manufacturing context.

The TickIT Guide, which was sponsored by the UK Department of Trade and Industry, provides an interpretation of ISO 9001 for the software industry (British Standards Institution, 1995). Under this scheme a software company contracts with an approved certification board to assess its software quality system every 3 years. Passing this external audit entitles the software company to TickIT certification. By August 2000 there were 963 TickIT registered firms in the UK. There were also 426 others outside of that country, over half of these in the US, India and Sweden (TickIT Home Page, 2001).

Certification of quality is of increasing concern for

UK software firms. The British government has adopted a ‘lead user’ approach to disseminating quality assurance concepts. In most cases it now requires companies to be certified under TickIT before it will buy software from them. This has meant effectively that major software vendors must have certification.

The TickIT Guide states ‘The main objectives [of TickIT] are to stimulate developers to think about what quality really is and how it may be achieved. Unless certification is purely a by-product of these more fundamental aims much of the effort will be wasted’ (British Standards Institution, 1995). The TickIT Guide does discuss the need for process improvement, but it gives no guidance on how this is to be achieved. In order to receive TickIT certification a firm must define and document its software processes in detail and convince an external auditor that it follows these processes. Some researchers have identified the failure to enforce process improvement explicitly as a serious weakness in this standard (Braa & Øgrim, 1995).

This paper reports on a research project conducted for the Computer Services and Software Association (CSSA), an industrial trade association which includes approximately 100 UK software companies. The CSSA Quality Group, which consists of quality managers of CSSA companies, asked the author to identify the best approach for software firms to adopt in order to achieve TickIT certification. The intention of the study was to determine best practice with relation to TickIT certification, based on an examination of software companies which had much experience of TickIT audits, and to codify this as guidelines for software companies which were not yet certified. For this reason the study sought to answer three question. First, what are the range of approaches used by UK software companies to gain certification of their quality management processes under the ISO standard? Secondly, are senior management attitudes to quality management important in selecting an approach? Thirdly, which of these approaches is most effective?

The following section discusses the conduct of the research. The next three sections consider the findings. The first describes the range of strategies used. The second assesses the role of senior management in selecting an approach. The third attempts to evaluate the effectiveness of these approaches. The final section of the paper offers conclusions, recommendations for practitioners and suggestions for improving the TickIT guidance.

## The conduct of the study

This research deals with a little studied area of practice, the certification of software quality systems. Scho¨n refers to this form of practice research as repertoirebuilding. He explains repertoire-building in the following terms:

when practice situations do not fit available theories of action, models of phenomena, or techniques of control, they may nevertheless be seen as familiar situations, cases, or precedents. Repertoire-building research serves the function of accumulating and describing such exemplars. (Scho¨n, 1991, p 315)

In order to support his claim he points to the importance of court cases and judicial precedence in the practice of law, the role of case histories in medical diagnosis, and the dependence on the case study method in the Harvard philosophy of business education.

In legal, medical, administrative and other practical disciplines, theories of action, models of phenomena, and techniques of control are relatively rare. Even where theories, models, or techniques do exist, their effectiveness may be constrained by unique features of the problem. In these circumstance Scho¨n argues that ‘. . . cases may usefully display linkages between features of action, outcome, and context’ (Scho¨n, 1991, p 317).

A review of the literature of software quality management and particularly of the IS 9000 series standards and the TickIT guidance, uncovered no theories, models or techniques which were relevant to the questions addressed in the study. Moreover, a single detailed case study would make an insignificant contribution to the repertoire and would be difficult to generalise.

It was decided to use a method which is known as multiple, embedded case studies (Eisenhardt, 1989). This technique depends on a replication logic. A series of short cases act as a set of progressive ‘experiments’. The researcher draws inferences from the first case study. Each subsequent case serves either to affirm or to falsify the inferences made in the previous ones. This procedure continues until the resulting inferences become stable. The outcome is a set of propositions which have been affirmed by the case studies. This procedure promotes more reliable induction than a single case study.

A series of interviews was conducted within each company between June 1998 and August 2000. Table 2 indicates the number and titles of those who were interviewed in each firm.

Company A began in the early 1980s with a strong commitment to software quality. Its founders believed that they could be successful by providing significantly better software than the firm for which they were currently working. Therefore, there was prima facie evidence that

Company A represented best practice in software quality management.

In June 1998 interviews were conducted with the head of quality for the entire corporation, the head of quality for the management consultancy division, and the head of quality for the bespoke software division. During the study, it became obvious that embedding (ie analysis at several organisational levels, namely quality management and project management) was necessary because multiple perspectives would allow for better triangulation of the data. Therefore, two senior project managers within the bespoke software division were also interviewed. These interviews elicited information on the company, its software development processes, its quality management systems, and its major problems. The questions were largely unstructured and open-ended. The interviews were recorded and subsequently transcribed. In order to confirm this information a transcript of each interview was sent to each interviewee for confirmation. In some cases this procedure led to further discussions between the participants and the author.

Because there were no theories, models or techniques available in the existing literature, it was not possible to use Company A as an experiment in hypothesis testing. Instead, Company A provided an opportunity for an exploratory experiment in which the object was hypothesis generation (Scho¨n, 1991, pp 141–156).

Grounded theory was selected as the approach to formulating the necessary inferences. Grounded theory is a method of qualitative research in which theory is inductively derived by means of the systematic collection and analysis of data (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). Grounded theory uses three analysis techniques. The first technique is open coding which identifies preliminary categories, their properties, and the possible values of these properties. The concepts are identified in the transcripts of interviews and then placed into categories. This prompts further questions regarding the properties of these categories. The second technique is axial coding which develops the categories further. The specific features of each category, such as the conditions which cause the category to occur, are validated against the data. The third technique is selective coding which relates the categories together in order to create a grounded theory. This is achieved by creating a narrative that links the subsidiary categories to the core categories and validates the story line against the data.

An illustration of such a hypothesis is ‘the quality department audits most projects’. These hypotheses will be referred to as Hypothesis Set (A).

Company B was then used as a ‘laboratory’ for testing Hypothesis Set (A). The Quality Manager for Systems Integration was the first person interviewed in Company B. This was followed by interviews with a Project Manager and a Manager of the Development Support Group. These interviews could be more structured than those

The procedure which was now established was applied in Companies E, F and G, in order to test Hypothesis Sets (A), (B) and (D). Companies E and F affirmed Hypothesis Set (D) and Company G affirmed Hypothesis Set (B). At this point the duplication logic had determined two distinct approaches.

However, the third approach represented by Hypothesis Set (A) had failed to be affirmed. This situation posed a dilemma. First, the only company which was characterised by Hypothesis Set (A) was regarded by several of those who participated in the study as representing best practice. Secondly, Hypothesis Set (A) seemed to be an amalgam of Hypothesis Sets (B) and (D). For these reasons Hypothesis Set (A) was deemed to be important even though it had not received affirmation. It appeared though that software companies which had these properties were relatively rare. Therefore, two hypotheses, which were unique to Hypothesis Set (A), were selected. They were:

(A1) Most projects have, at least, one internal audit

(A2) The Company has only ever had one external auditor

Software companies which had these characteristics were actively sought by consulting the Quality Group of the CSSA. Company H was chosen and examined in detail. The ‘experiment’ in Company H was found to affirm most of Hypothesis Set (A). It was felt that this ability to predict features of a company’s software quality management from two structural characteristics suggested that further ‘experiments’ would offer little additional assurance.

## What approaches do companies use to gain certification under TickIT?

The study reveals three distinct approaches among the eight companies. The first group incorporates companies B, C and G. Their approach towards quality management will be referred to as advisory. The second group incorporates companies D, E and F who have an approach towards quality management which will be referred to as policing. The third group consists of companies A and H which have an approach towards quality management which will be referred to as hybrid. The companies in each group display common structures, tasks, and attitudes towards software quality management.

## Advisory approach: Companies B. C and G

In these companies the quality departments perceive their role as primarily advisory. They have three functions. The first is to define standards. The second is to promulgate those standards. The third is to perform internal audits on a selective basis.

The approach for performing internal audits is to select a small number of projects randomly or to attend only to projects which are risky. Because the quality department has little involvement with projects, assurance of products depends on the individual project manager. The quality manager is likely to have little status or responsibility.

Software companies which adopt the advisory approach tend to select a single certification board for the external audit and then further develop their relationship with that body over time.

In general, these companies focus on improving employees’ understanding of the company’s quality assurance system. The prevalent attitude towards quality management in these companies is described by a Project Manager in Company B who says:

. . . [m]ost projects would not warrant external [to the project] involvement, financially speaking. The quality manager is there in a consultancy role so the project manager can draw on that experience if need be. The company does not perform proactive quality management. (Interview, Project Manager, Company B, 1998)

## Policing approach: Companies D, E and F

In these companies the quality departments define standards and attempt to enforce them for most projects. The quality department achieves this by conducting internal audits on a majority of projects. They make little attempt to promulgate the standards.

This structure has the potential to make the quality manager very influential and to bring him into conflict with project managers. As a result members of the project team may feel that quality management is being forced upon them.

Companies which adopt a policing approach are also more aggressive towards certification. They always tender for certification bodies. Although they may give some preference to the incumbent or limit the tendering process to a small list of favourites, they nevertheless view the undertaking as competitive. They may insist on inspecting proposed auditors. Because of the extent of internal auditing, these companies appear to take the external TickIT audit in their stride.

In general, these companies focus on ensuring that employees abide by the firm’s software assurance procedures. The prevalent attitude towards quality management in these companies is described by the Quality Manager in Company F who says ‘I feel that my presence is needed as a police force because I am fully accountable for the risk involved . . .’. (Interview, Quality Manager, Company F, 1999)

## Hybrid approach: Companies A and H

In these companies the quality department is also responsible for creating standards. The quality department performs internal audits on a majority of the company’s projects. In this sense these companies are similar to those which adopt a policing approach.

On the other hand, these companies also have a mechanism for promulgating quality standards which is informal and not directly responsible to the quality department. As a result, there is little apparent tension between quality managers and project managers. In these companies project managers see the internal audits as helpful and quality management is seen as a fast track to promotion.

Companies which employ this approach have a longstanding relationship with a single certification body. They seek to negotiate with this organisation rather than use competitive tendering. In this way their approach is similar to the advisory approach adopted by companies B, C and G.

These companies have two different foci for their efforts in software quality assurance. One is to ensure that employees abide by the firm’s quality management system. The other is to improve employees’ understanding of the firm’s quality management system. The prevalent attitude towards quality management in the companies which adopted a hybrid approach is described by the Corporate Head of Quality in Company A who says:

. . . [W]e do the audits so that we do highlight if the processes are falling down . . ., but our main concern is we want to make sure that the people understand what the processes are. So the assurance people really are there to help the project team in understanding what the processes are and how they should be run. (Interview, Corporate Head of Quality, Company A, 1999)

Inherent in these approaches are three activities performed by the quality department. The first activity is definition—the specification of the standard software processes for a company. All three approaches incorporate definition. The second activity is promulgation—promoting the standards and providing support to project teams. In companies which adopt an advisory approach the quality department’s main task is promulgation. The third activity is auditing—the task of inspecting whether the standard software processes are being observed. In companies that adopt a policing approach the quality department must perform auditing.

Companies that adopt a policing approach may undertake some form of promulgation, but these activities are minimal. For example, Company F provided an educational programme in preparation for its first external audit, but found that it was not very effective. In companies that adopt an advisory approach project teams usually review important project deliverables. However, the quality department performs a limited amount of internal auditing. This is governed by a sampling policy. This means that the auditing activity depends largely on the external certification process.

In contrast, the companies that have adopted a hybrid approach conduct all three of these activities with equal emphasis. The most interesting feature of this approach is that while the promulgation activity is the responsibility of the quality department, it is implemented through mechanisms which are not under the direct control of that department. For example, Company A has a number of quality advisors who do not work for the quality department. Each project has a quality advisor who is not part of the project team and who is available upon request.

The need for this dual structure may arise from the inherent tension between the promotional and the auditing activities. The evidence gathered in this study indicates that the views of project and quality managers are most divergent in companies which adopt a policing approach. This possibly reflects a politically charged relationship. For example, the Head of Quality in Company D said ‘. . . project managers are not at all sold with the more bureaucratic side of quality procedures’ (Interview, Head of Enterprise Performance and Quality, Company D, 1998). Both project managers interviewed in Company F referred to the quality management system as ‘a necessary evil’ (Interview, Project Managers, Company F, 1998).

It may be for this reason that project managers are cautious about accepting assistance from the quality department. Such requests might suggest inadequacies in their own abilities. For instance, the Head of Quality at Company D said ‘project managers can contact the [quality] department for assistance although there is a great difference between the project managers in their willingness to do so’ (Interview, Head of Enterprise Performance and Quality, Company D, 1998). Creating an organisational mechanism for providing assistance to project teams which is independent of the quality department may improve the effectiveness of promulgation.

In companies that adopt a policing approach the aggressive attitude of the quality department carries over into the arena of contracting. Every 3 years these companies have a new tender in order to select their external certification body. The incumbent must rebid. Companies that adopt an advisory or a hybrid approach tend to see their relationship with their chosen certification board as long term and attempt to nurture it over time.

## Are senior management’s attitudes important?

A number of authors have proposed that the commitment of senior managers is a critical factor in determining the success of quality management initiatives (eg Crosby, 1980, pp 41–64). This suggests that differences in senior management attitudes may account for these three approaches. However, the study does not produce emphatic evidence supporting this inference.

In Companies A and H, which have both adopted hybrid approaches, the senior management is very supportive of quality management. Senior management believes that software quality is an essential function and shows their commitment by having the corporate quality manager report directly to them. The attitudes of senior management at Company A are particularly significant. This firm began in the early 1980s with a strong commitment to software quality. A quality manager was one of the first employees hired. Company A had quality assurance procedures in place long before it received TickIT certification in 1991. Likewise, the Corporate Quality Manager in Company H claims that the board strongly supports quality management. He says that they view his remit as being responsible for the development and improvement of the business itself, not just the quality practices.

In companies which employ a policing approach senior management believes that control of the quality of their products is essential, but see quality assurance as an operational function. The quality manager does not report directly to the senior management. In two of the three companies (D and E) the quality manager is two levels removed from the managing director. In the third company (F), the quality manager has reported to the managing director since the company received its first TickIT certification in 1994. The current quality manager of Company F confides that the promotion was a ‘reward’ for getting the company certified initially.

In fact, there is evidence that Company F is evolving from a policing towards a hybrid approach. The quality managers in this firm observed that the internal audits were becoming less contentious. The firm has also recently reintroduced a quality assurance training programme after the failure of the earlier attempt. The quality department now sees the project teams as more able to benefit from these courses. The quality manager of Company F feels that this change has resulted from new ownership. He explained:

The worst offenders [of ignoring quality procedures] were the founders of the company themselves who believed that rules were necessary for everything and every one, but that they themselves were above the law. There was no demonstrable management commitment, and the directors’ behaviour set an example for those below them who saw that corner-cutting by ‘good chaps’ would be condoned. The quality system operated effectively at a mechanical level but functioned in spite of rather than because of senior management attitudes and behaviour.

When the company was taken over . . ., the founders of the company were made redundant, and the new team rose to take their place. We’re by no means perfect but the new philosophy is not only ‘do as I say’, but also ‘do as I do’. (Interview, Quality Manager, Company F, 1999)

If Company F does successfully change to a hybrid approach, then the upgrading of the organisational level of the quality manager may have presaged that move.

In companies that adopt an advisory approach, software quality management is viewed as being imposed by customers. The effect which that attitude has on the company is not consistent. In two of the companies (B and G) the quality manager reports directly to the managing director. This appears to be an anomaly because the case studies suggest that the quality managers in these companies have little influence. The most obvious explanation for this is the nature of their duties.

Firms that adopt a policing approach employ about three and a half times more quality personnel (a weighted average of 0.28% to 0.08% of employees) than firms that have adopted an advisory approach. This is because providing advice requires fewer employees than auditing projects. Promulgation is more likely to be seen as a staff function than a line function. Therefore, a quality department in a company which employs an advisory approach might be expected to be a small, staff group reporting directly to a senior manager.

The situation in Company C, the third firm which has adopted an advisory approach, is different. Here, a board member once spoke for quality issues, but that individual resigned from the company. Since that time the quality department has been reorganised and its manager is now three levels removed from the board. This is the lowest position of any quality department in the study. Company C is the only organisation in which quality management is delegated from the project manager to a member of the project team. In addition, it seems that the quality department conducts virtually no internal audits, but acts entirely in an advisory capacity. Hence, Company C may be regarded as an extreme case. Here, the lack of importance of the quality management department is actually reflected in the organisational structure. This has resulted from changes to the senior management team of Company C.

While the evidence which relates senior management attitudes towards software quality management and organisational level appears a little ambiguous, it seems clear that companies adopt an approach of software quality management at the outset. They are unlikely to change this approach unless there is a change in senior management.

## Which approach is the most effective?

Because all three approaches have led companies to gain certification under TickIT guidelines for their software quality processes, all may be said to be effectual. However, it is unclear whether one approach results in a more effective software quality system than the other two.

TickIT does not require a company to collect standard metrics regarding either its products or processes. Most of the companies studied measure some aspects of software quality. The prevailing attitude in many of the companies is that it is costly to measure and the benefits are few. A project manager in Company B expressed this view when he said ‘Metrics take a lot of time to collect and cost a lot of money to maintain and once a project is signed off on, no one cares anymore’ (Interview, Project Manager, Company B, 1998). Hence, these eight companies do not have comparable quantitative data which would allow for a straightforward comparison of the relative effectiveness of their approaches.

It was, therefore, decided that any evaluation of the effectiveness of the eight companies’ approaches to software quality management would have to be qualitative.

## CMM maturity level

TickIT provides a baseline which a software company must meet or exceed in order to receive certification. It has no mechanism for grading the effectiveness of the software quality management systems of certified companies. It regards all as having met the minimum standard. Therefore, in order to gauge the relative sophistication of each company’s quality management system something more was needed. At the heart of both CMM and SPICE are maturity models of quality management. These models provide a better means of differentiating companies’ capabilities than any TickIT mechanisms. The CMM model is the more simple to apply, so it was chosen as an evaluative tool. CMM distinguishes five different levels of maturity within software companies. These are:

## (1) Initial

Software processes are ad hoc and occasionally chaotic. This is because they are ill-defined. Success depends on individual heroics. In order to succeed a firm needs competent software developers.

## (2) Repeatable

Software processes have definition. This arises from the use of basic project management techniques in order to track cost, duration and functionality. This regime allows for the repetition of earlier successes.

## (3) Defined

Software processes and their management are standard and fully documented. These standards are consistently observed. If a project adopts an approach which differs from the standards, this variation must be approved. This necessitates the creation of support units, such as training and assistance.

## (4) Measured

Software process and product quality is measured.

## (5) Optimised

The software process and product quality metrics gathered in step (4) form the basis for continuous reevaluation of a firm’s software processes. This allows experimentation with new ideas to be planned and managed. The focus thus becomes defect prevention and change management (Herbsleb et al, 1997).

The prime purpose of a CMM assessment is to place a firm within one of these five levels.

It was not possible to undertake a full CMM appraisal of any of the eight companies. This activity would have required too much time and too great an access to companies’ records. Also, the author is not a certified CMM auditor. Hence, an approximate evaluation of each of the participating firms was all that was possible. The first thing that became apparent was that the level of maturity was not consistent throughout any company, so it was necessary to estimate each firm’s mean CMM level. This proved to be uncontroversial since the quality manager in each of the eight companies agreed the author’s assessment for his firm. Table 3 presents the results.

In this assessment seven of the eight companies fell within Level 2 (Repeatable). Only company A was at Level 3 (Defined). In several areas Company A was at Level 4 (Measured). When asked about quality management within his firm a project manager with Company A said:

I’ve worked in four companies. I used to work for . . . (a pan-European software house). They went through the accreditation process. I also worked for . . . (a UK software house) at the point which they were accredited as well. . . . (Company A) has a much more serious approach towards quality. . . . [I]t is a very core part of our culture, not something that was bolted on. (Interview, Project Manager, Company A, 1999)

Quality managers in two of the firms in the study also identified Company A as being one of the UK leaders in software quality management.

The companies that have adopted a policing approach are all firmly placed within Level 2. They use a standard project management method most of the time, although it is not mandatory. Nevertheless, the success of project management is not uniform. There is little training or advice available. As a result, projects are still occasionally late or over budget. When this happens, it may not be realised until the project is in its final phases in spite of reporting procedures.

The companies that have adopted an advisory approach are also all within Level 2. ## Mechanisms for software process improvement

As a company’s experience of software quality management increases, it should manifest a more coherent approach to software process improvement. Therefore, there should be a correlation between the sophistication of a company’s mechanisms for software process improvement and its maturity. Table 4 characterises the predominant means of software process improvement in each of the eight companies.

In Companies B, C and G, which have an advisory approach, the identification of process innovation usually comes from members of the project team. However, internal audits are not the key avenue for their suggestions. The formal mechanisms are seen as too bureaucratic. In Company B project staff informally make recommendations to the quality department. In Company C project staff exchange ideas informally among themselves. Company C is frank in admitting that most suggestions come from new or contract staff. More alarmingly, in Company G most suggestions have arisen from customer complaints, although internal audits are becoming a more frequent means of identifying process improvements. In general, the systematic methods for identifying these improvements do not work because they are seen as being too onerous and good ideas tend to get lost.

In Companies D, E and F, which have a policing approach, internal audits serve as one forum for identifying potential improvements. The number of ideas which are implemented is, however, disappointingly small. However, in these companies there are other means of identifying improvements. For example, Company F indicates many improvements are recommended by senior management. Company E has had ideas from customer complaints. In these two firms there also seems to be few mechanisms for disseminating process improvements. In Company D most suggestions come from the quality department as a result of their experience of what they hear in internal audits. Because the quality management department in Company D is the source of improvements, their distribution is more systematic.

Company A has procedures which allow anyone in the organisation to make suggestions for process improvements. This procedure is not tied to the internal audits. However, most ideas for process improvements come from senior managers. This is because suggestions have to go through a rigorous process to be adopted. Most proposals get turned down because they are seen as being too narrow. As a project manager in Company A explains:

Most of our changes seem to come from people who are in management positions within the company. Anything up to the guys who own the company themselves and all the way down to team leaders. But typically it is around the level of project managers, business group managers, or directors.

They are the people who have an overview of more than one project. If you’re working on a project as a developer, you’re inclined to say this is the way to do it and you may make comments. The project manager may pick up those comments and look at how they affect other projects. At some point they’ll say we can improve our process. A change request will be fired off at that stage. (Interview, Project Manager, Company A, 1999)

The quality department incorporates newly approved ideas into the firm’s documentation and depends on holders of this information to distribute it further. Company A also has a very systematic approach to building and maintaining costing models of different types of projects.

Even though Company H employs a hybrid approach, its procedures for software process improvement do not resemble those of Company A. In fact, this closely resembles Company D, the most systematic of those in the policing group.

All firms have a formal procedure for putting forward improvements in software processes. Most revolve around the internal audit and project review. The consensus of opinion is that these procedures are too bureaucratic. In many companies project team members feel that few good ideas for improvements survive the procedure. Quality managers in several companies justified the low success of their procedures as being because the experience of project teams is too narrow to be generalisable.

In the firms that adopt an advisory approach the most effective way of process improvement seems to be by means of informal interaction between team members. This is largely unobserved by the quality department. In a company that adopts a policing approach internal audits are hardly a more productive means of identifying process improvements. In all companies customer complaints and senior management appear to be the only mechanisms for ensuring that suggestions will be treated with urgency.

In fact, there seems to be some confusion regarding process improvement in most of the eight companies. Project managers still think largely in terms of quality control, that is ensuring that products have passed inspection. Hence, most consider improvement only in the sense of specific software products. Few understand clearly the need for process control and improvement.

## Patterns of behaviour

Another means of assessing these companies’ abilities to learn are the rules of thumb proposed by Herbsleb et al (1997). These authors conducted a study of a number of companies that undertook CMM certification. They identified several patterns in the behaviour of both successful and unsuccessful companies. They found that successful companies have the following attitudes:

 Senior management actively monitors quality management progress

 The goals of quality management are clearly stated

 The staff is dedicated to quality management

There is a clear assignment of and recognition for responsibility

 The quality management department is highly respected

 The technical staff is involved in improvement

On the other hand, they suggested that unsuccessful companies have the following characteristics:

 There are high levels of organisational politics

 There is turf guarding

 There is cynicism

There is a belief that quality assurance gets in the way of ‘real’ work

There is a need for guidance on how to improve, not just what to improve

Using these statements as an evaluative framework, companies that adopted an advisory approach seem to have a poor capability for process improvement. In two of the three companies in this category (B and C) the quality department was not highly respected. In the third (D) the attitudes towards this department were mixed. In two of these three companies (B and D) technical staff played some role in improvements. In the third (C) they had no role at all. In two of the three companies (B and D) some staff were seen as being dedicated to quality management. In the third (C) there was no commitment at all. Finally, in two of the three companies (B and C) some employees felt that quality assurance got in the way of ‘real’ work.

The situation differed little with the companies that have adopted a policing approach. The absence of respect for the quality department was characteristic of the tension between project and quality managers in all three of these companies. Also in all three some staff doubted the necessity for quality management. In two of the three companies (E and F) staff saw quality assurance as getting in the way of ‘real’ work. In short, there is little reason to believe that these companies have any greater predilection for software process improvement than those which adopt an advisory approach.

The companies using a hybrid approach offered a distinct contrast. They stated the goals of quality management clearly. Most staff understood the necessity for quality management. Senior management actively monitored quality management progress. On the other hand, these firms prided themselves on having a minimum of politics. In Company A the project managers attributed this to its policy of job rotation which reduced ‘turf guarding’ and ‘empire building’ (Interview, Project Manager, June 30, 1999).

It is difficult to distinguish between firms that adopt an advisory and a policing approach in terms of software process maturity. Those companies that employ a policing approach are able to repeat their successes somewhat more consistently, but they are no more prone to incremental improvement of processes.

While it is tempting to conclude that the hybrid approach is more effective, the evidence for this is mixed. Company A has greater maturity, as defined by CMM, than any of the other companies in the study. It also has a culture which Herbsleb et al claim should support software process improvement. However, in practice this seems not to be the case. Although anyone may suggest improvements, few are accepted. These largely come from senior managers. This is because they feel that they are the only ones with a sufficiently wide experience to evaluate the merits of the suggestion. This explanation suggests that the company is still quite hierarchical and does not have the degree of participation in decision making necessary to encourage the systematic organisational learning required for effective process improvement. Moreover, Company H, which also adopted an hybrid approach, is even more problematic. Its culture seems suited to software process improvement. However, its organisational maturity and its procedure for process improvement more closely resemble a policing than a hybrid approach.

The most obvious interpretation of this evidence is that since TickIT does not enforce a notion of software process improvement, companies which seek certification usually ignore software process improvement. In seeking TickIT certification a software company does not need to take a stance on process improvement. In order to gain certification a company must achieve roughly Level 2 of the CMM standard, but to move to a higher level they must have a means for process improvement. Since this is not required by TickIT many companies never adopt one and so they do not progress. An earlier study by Braa & Øgrim (1995) reached a similar conclusion.

## Conclusions and recommendations

The study examines eight UK software companies by interviewing quality and project managers in each. It concludes that three of these companies (B, C and G) have adopted an advisory approach, three of these companies (D, E and F) have adopted a policing approach and two of these companies (A and H) have adopted a hybrid approach towards software quality management in their pursuit of ISO certification. Each approach represents structures, tasks and attitudes towards software quality management. Once a company adopts one of these approaches, it is not likely to change unless it has new management. In Scho¨n’s terms these approaches provide three models of quality management in the software industry.

In companies that have adopted an advisory approach, the quality department’s chief activities are the definition and the promulgation of standards. This includes promoting standards and helping project managers to pass the external audits. Few internal audits occur in these companies. These companies tend to select a certification board and then further develop their relationship with that body. Senior management seeks certification because customers demand it and they usually regard software quality management as a staff function requiring a small number of personnel. In these companies ideas for process improvement usually arise from programmers and are passed by word-of-mouth.

In companies that have adopted a policing approach, the chief activity of the quality department is to conduct internal audits on a majority of projects. This may lead to the quality department being seen as adversarial and creating tension with project managers. These companies also take a more aggressive approach towards external auditing by tendering for their certification bodies every 3 years. Senior management seeks certification because they feel it essential to insure themselves against the risk of poor products. They view this as more of a line task which requires a larger number of personnel to carry out the internal audits. In these companies ideas for process improvement usually arise from the internal audits, from senior management, and from customers. There is no systematic method.

In companies that have adopted a hybrid approach, the quality department performs internal audits in much the same way as in firms using a policing approach. However, its relationship with its certification body resembles that of companies using an advisory approach. Moreover, the companies that employ a hybrid approach depend on organisational mechanisms outside the quality department to perform the promulgation activities. Senior management see their ability to deliver highquality software as a distinctive capability. TickIT certification publicises this capability. The quality department reports directly to the managing director and has significant status. Internal audits are also a systematic mechanism for identifying ideas for process improvement in these companies. They rarely work and are viewed by project members as too bureaucratic.

The study was unable to distinguish among the effectiveness of these three approaches. The TickIT guidance does not require companies to use common metrics. Hence, it was not possible to collect any quantitative data which would differentiate these approaches. The study attempted to evaluate qualitatively the effectiveness of these approaches in three different ways. However, the results were inconclusive. In fact, it seems that in most of the companies the emphasis is still on the control of product quality, while the concept of process quality is somewhat misunderstood.

This study affirms that the TickIT guideline does provide a suitable baseline for software quality assurance, but it does not offer specific guidance on how to ensure the improvement of software processes. Once a company reaches the level of TickIT certification (approximately Level 2 of the CMM standard), there seems to be little need for it to improve. For this reason there was limited evidence of systematic process innovation in any of the eight companies. This may explain why it is difficult to distinguish which of the three strategies is the most effective. Therefore, the study points to the need to improve the TickIT guidelines and the ISO standard in three ways. Firstly, the notion of process improvement should become a central feature of both. Secondly, these documents should recommend software metrics in order to encourage companies to measure their achievement. Thirdly, the guidelines should encourage companies to reduce the bureaucracy associated with software quality management. This is particularly true of the documentation, acceptance, and distribution of process innovation.

In light of these findings companies wishing to gain TickIT certification are probably best advised to select an approach which is aligned with senior management attitudes towards quality management. If senior managers seek TickIT certification because their customers demand it as a condition for further business, then the advisory approach would seem preferable. If senior managers seek TickIT certification because they feel that they cannot risk creating low-quality software, then the policing approach is the most likely to succeed. However, they should be aware that this approach is likely to create new tensions between project and quality managers. The best way to ease this tension is to create quality consultants and/or trainers within the organisation who do not report to the quality department. This is characteristic of a hybrid approach which is usually adopted when senior managers wish to promote the image that software quality is a distinctive capability of their company. However, since none of these approaches adequately addresses software process improvement, senior managers should explicitly consider how to implement a systematic means of organisational learning and how they can integrate it into the organisational culture.
