# The role of boundaries in knowledge processes

Y Merali

Information Systems Research Unit, Warwick Business School, The University of Warwick, Coventry, UK

Advances in communications and information technology deliver an increasingly dynamic and interconnected competitive context. To survive and prosper in this environment, organisations must be innovative and adaptive. Intellectual and relational capital comprise key resources for competitive success. This paper concerns the knowledge processes that enable organisations to undergo transformational change in an interconnected world whilst retaining the integrity of their organisational identity. It views organisations as complex adaptive systems, and uses a case study example to demonstrate the importance of boundary phenomenology in defining organisational knowledge processes. The underlying conceptual framework draws on Maturana and Varela’s concept of autopoietic unity as a metaphor to explore issues of organisational identity and integrity, and uses Merali’s Cognitive Congruence Framework to examine the cognitive infrastructures underpinning adaptive behaviour. The discussion reflects on the implications of this work for knowledge management practice in emergent competitive contexts, and in concluding, advocates active boundary management.

## Introduction

The speed with which information is exchanged across the world and the ability to integrate business processes across national and organisational boundaries has resulted in an increasingly interconnected world (Castells, 1996). The impact of changes involving multiple players in the competitive context is felt more rapidly, more widely and more acutely than was formerly the case (Shapiro & Varian 1999; Evans & Wurster, 2000). Information, knowledge and relational assets now play a critical role in shaping the ‘rules of the game (Kale et al, 2000).

To survive and prosper in this environment, organisations must be innovative and adaptive, positioning themselves advantageously in relation to other players in the emergent competitive context (Arthur, 1996; Shapiro & Varian 1999; Evans & Wurster, 2000). Such transformational capability is predicated on organisational intelligence: perceiving how the context is chang ing, knowing what constitutes appropriate behaviour and what role to play in that context, and knowing how to carry out appropriate actions to enact the perceived appropriate role (Teece, 1998; Merali, 2001).

Attainment of advantageous positioning may involve developing partnerships and alliances with other entities resulting in the emergence of networks of collaborating organisations (Ohmae, 1989; Murray & Mahon, 1993; Ahuja, 2000; Hite & Hesterly, 2001; Amit & Zott, 2001). The inter-organisational relationship is important in two ways:

an organisation can realise the value of its information and knowledge resources through its relationships with external players (Arthur, 1996; Teece, 1998; Shapiro & Varian, 1999), and

the existence of the relationship itself can have value in the competitive context (Cronin, 1994; Kale et al, 2000; Kaufman et al, 2000).

Where processes, expertise and people are shared, organisational boundaries become indistinct, giving rise to new issues of organisational integrity, identity and ownership of intellectual capital.

This paper deploys an information perspective to look at inter-organisational relationships. It looks at acquisition (from outside), development (inside and across boundaries) and leveraging of intellectual capital by organisations operating in dynamic contexts. It proposes that the organisational boundary is a complex and important concept, and that it is intimately coupled with the idea of organisational identity. At any given time, the state of the boundary affects the content, valuation and usage of intellectual capital by the organisation and vice versa.

A clear understanding of the boundary phenomenology and its role in intra- and inter-organisational knowledge processes is important in developing effective strategies for managing organisational transformation in relation to the dynamic, interconnected, competitive economic context. This paper provides a conceptual framework, using a case study example to demonstrate the importance of boundary phenomenology in defining organisational knowledge processes. The conceptual framework draws on Maturana and Varela’s concept of autopoietic unity (Maturana & Varela, 1973) as a metaphor to explore issues of organisational identity and integrity, and uses Merali’s Cognitive Congruence Framework (Merali, 2001) to examine the cognitive infrastructures underpinning adaptive behaviour.

The next sections introduce the autopoietic metaphor and the Cognitive Congruence Framework. Together, these form the conceptual framework for identifying aspects of cognition and boundary phenomenology salient to later discussions. This is followed by a case study example that will be used to illustrate the role of the boundary in knowledge processes. The final section discusses the implications of this work for knowledge management practice in emergent competitive contexts, and, in conclusion, advocates active boundary management.

## The autopoietic metaphor

The use of metaphor to explore new domains is wellestablished (Lackoff & Johnson, 1980; Morgan, 1986). Biological systems display many characteristics that also appear desirable for organisational survival in a dynamic context, for example coordination, robustness, requisite variety and adaptability in the face of significant changes in the environment. Consequently, the organic metaphor is popular amongst writers in strategy, organisational development and knowledge management.

Recent literature has been concerned with new ways of organising and the emergence of new organisational forms that are well-suited to survival and competition in uncertain dynamic contexts. In particular this writing has engaged with the concept of self-organisation (Axelrod & Cohen, 2000; Merali, 2000b; Stacey, 2001). Self-organisation represents considerable design elegance, accommodating responsiveness to local changes whilst maintaining the organisation and integrity of the global form through relational contiguity and intelligence networks. The globally positioned, locally generated action capability supports speed, high granularity and fineness of control in interactions with the environment and generates a robustness in the face of changing conditions.

The autpoietic metaphor provides ways of thinking about the mechanisms underpinning self-organising networks that retain their integrity in the face of external perturbations. These concepts serve as the referential basis for characterising boundary phenomenology and its relationship to the persistence of the organisational entity in the transformational context. The following description of the metaphor derives from earlier work on its application to social systems (Merali, 1998a). Autopoietic unity (see below) is used as a metaphor to gain insights into organisational entities and to explore ways of organising boundaries in dynamic contexts.

In Maturana and Varela’s conception, autopoiesis (self-production) is a universal property of living entities, and autopoietic systems (Maturana and Varela speak of these as autopoietic machines) are self-producing systems:

An autopoietic machine is a machine organized (defined as a unity) in a network of processes of production (transformation and destruction) of components that (i) produces the components which through their interactions and transformations continuously regenerate and realize the network of processes (relations) that produced them; and (ii) constitute it (the machine) as a concrete unity in the space in which they (the components) exist by specifying the topological domain of its realization as such a network. % an autopoietic machine continuously generates and specifies its own organization through its operation as a system of production of its own components and does this in an endless turnover of components under conditions of continuous perturbations and compensation of perturbations % therefore an autopoietic machine is an homeostatic (or rather a relations-static) system which has its own organization (defining network of relations) as the fundamental variable which it maintains constant. (Maturana & Varela, 1973 pp 78–79)

It is important to distinguish between ‘organization’ and ‘structure’ in the sense that Maturana and Varela use them to define autopoietic systems. Throughout this paper these terms are italicized when used in the specific sense defined by Mingers (1997):

The organization of the autopoietic unity is

% the subset of the relations between components that determine the properties of the unity as a whole, and thereby its identity, type or class. All unities of a particular type have the same organization. A change of organization implies a change of identity and vice versa.

## The structure of the autopoietic unity is

% the total set of actual components and relations belonging to a particular concrete example or instance % the structure of a particular entity may change without its organization changing. Organization is abstract, structure is concrete.

The organization is the abstract characterisation of the unity that persists over time. The components may be replaced over time. Individual components relate to each other in specific ways and participate in production processes that constitute the integrity of the unity over time.

According to Maturana and Varela, the relationship construct is fundamental in explaining the mechanism of persistent self-organisation. They identify three types of relations (relations of constitution, relations of specification and relations of order) that must obtain between components in order to maintain the substance, form and integrity of the unity over time. Relations of constitution determine that the components produced in the autopoietic system constitute the topology of the organization. Topology determines the features of the occupation of space by the organization and is manifest in the form or shape of the autopoietic organization. Relations of specification ensure that the components produced in the autopoietic organization are specifically defined by their participation in the ‘being’ of the autopoietic organization. Relations of order ensure the dynamic co-ordination of the relations of constitution and specification in the organizational existence of the autopoietic unity, so that the unity persists throughout its existence in time and space. These definitions will serve as the referential basis for our discussions on the role of relationships in maintaining organisational integrity in the transformational context.

An external observer perceives the autopoietic unity because there is a visible cleavage from the environment of the components that constitute the unity. Maturana and Varela use the notion of structural coupling and neighbourhood relations to speak of the boundary relationship and the internal/external partitioning. Components of the autopoietic unity have strong neighbourhood relationships with other components belonging to the same unity. Boundary components at the interface of the unity with the external environment have recurrent interactions with environmental elements, but these interactions do not display the strength of sustained linkages that characterise internal neighbourhood relations.

The autopoietic unity is a self-referential, self-regulating, self-producing, self-organising entity capable of maintaining a stable state under non-equilibrium conditions. The autopoietic ‘network of processes of production’ is realised by components interacting with each other through structural coupling and neighbourhood relations of variable strength. The relative positioning of the components gives the unity its persistent form. Perturbations in the environment are sensed by boundary components and appropriate adjustments are propagated though the network. Individual components make adjustments relative to their local neighbourhood relations to maintain a stable global organization.

## Applying the autopoietic metaphor to organisations

Maturana and Varela’s unity is not ‘about’ achieving anything beyond existence in the environment in which it is situated. In social systems there is the issue of free will and intent: businesses have a purpose (often enshrined in mission statements and performance targets) and seek to change their environments to suit their strategic intent. Individuals seek to satisfy their personal values, needs and agendas. For these reasons, many are dissuaded from applying the autopoietic concept to social organisations.

This paper does not assert that organisations are autopoietic systems. Autopoietic unity is phenomenological – the observer attributes the boundary to the unity, and discerns networks of production and relational arrangements that perpetuate the existence of the unity. Here autopoietic unity is used as a metaphor (see Merali 2000b for a discussion on the use of organic metaphors), not as an analogue of social organisations: it is used to explore referential correspondence between features of the intelligent organisation in cyberspace and the autopoietic organism in the physical medium.

Issues of organisational identity, integrity and ownership emerge as organisations exploit technology to develop new types of relationships, sharing resources and processes across boundaries in cyberspace. To deal with these issues, we need a way of recognising the persistent organisation (unity) in cyberspace – ie a way of defining a self-organising ‘architecture’ that is the abstract characterisation of the unity that persists over time.

Applying the autopoietic metaphor of self-producing systems to organisations, we illuminate their self-referential, self-regulating, self-producing, self-organising nature. These attributes arise out of social relationships (see Merali, 1998a, and the discussion below) between individual members of the unity. The emergent properties of the relationship network determine the content and structure of intra- and inter-organisational knowledge networks and are significant in determining ideas of ‘self’ and ‘non-self’. This in turn gives us a basis for elucidating the mechanisms for maintaining integrity of the organisational ‘self’ in the complex network of interconnected ‘others’ that is evolving in cyberspace.

## Boundaries and identity

The boundary delineates the individual firm (the unity) and its intellectual property. Boundary agents (boundary spanning individuals) have a role in regulating information fluxes in and out of the firm. The boundary marks where ‘self’ (the organisation) ends and ‘the other’ (external environment and other entities in that environment) begins. Two important questions concerned respectively with internal and external issues of identity present themselves:

What constitutes ‘the organisation’ from the viewpoint of the organisational actors?

What does an observer recognise as the intact ‘organisational entity’ from the outside?

Weick (1995) highlights the importance of identity as a focus for sensemaking and as a basis for congruent action:

Each % organisation chooses who it will be by first choosing what actions, if any it needs to explain, and second by choosing which explanations for these actions it will defend. An inability or unwillingness to choose, act and justify these leaves people with too many alternatives and too few certainties. Binding decisions affect the tasks we are attracted to, the reasons that move us, the values we try to realise, the plans we admire, and the people we seek out. Avoidance of such decisions slows down the development of attractions, reasons, values, plans and associates.

The perceived identity of the organisation persists over time for those within it and for observers outside it. If we consider a community, the community is the unity; its members are the components (Merali, 1998a). Although there will be a turnover in the membership (individuals come and go as time passes) the identity of the community persists (for as long as the community is recognisably a social entity), reinforced by rituals, practices, beliefs and rights of passage acted out through relationships and according to custom.

From the internal perspective, identity underpins individual ‘knowing’ of what is appropriate to do as a member of the social entity. From the external perspective, projected identity (being recognised for what you stand for) is the basis of trust, reputation and branding.

For an organisation operating in a dynamic environment in which knowledge acquisition and evaluation are significant activities, establishing and understanding its own identity is vital. It is the embodiment of this identity through action that impacts on the world. Just as a living organism interacts with the world and persists as an externally recognisable physical unity over time, ‘identity’ is the abstract characterisation of the unity that persists over time. The next section characterises the features of the intelligent organisation that correspond to the autopoietic referents of networks of processes of components that regenerate and realise the relations that produced them and the unity (which is characterised by the organisational identity).

## The Cognitive Congruence Framework

In this section, we introduce the Cognitive Congruence Framework (Merali, 1998b; 2001) which will be used in subsequent sections to examine the role of identity and cognitive processes underpinning the production and distinction of ‘self’ in the enactment of boundary relationships.

The identity of the social unit in terms of distinguishing the ‘self’ from the ‘other’ is embedded in cognitive structures, shared models and recipes and routines within the organisation (Nelson & Winter, 1982). The Cognitive Congruence Framework establishes the relations that underpin the organisation’s perception of what exists, of what its own place in the world is, and what the embodiment of the organisational ‘self’ is in the world, for the persistence of the organisational identity.

The framework is depicted in Figure 1, and its components (schema, self-concept, relationship script and relationship enactment) and their relationships are described in the subsequent text (for a more extensive description see Merali, 2001).

The schema is the total cognitive construct containing the organised collection of interconnected beliefs and perceptions about self and the universe together with their spatial, temporal and semantic relationships. Schemata embrace self-concept, scripts and environmental sensemaking, together with mechanisms for their linguistic and semiotic articulation. Organisational selfconcept is a perception of organisational identity (for the observer, identity is manifest in the observable actions of members of the unity).

Relationships between individuals determine the content and structure of intra- and inter-organisational knowledge networks. The relationship network is the mechanism for sharing and diffusing ideas and the intersubjective realisation of synergistic relationships. Every distinctive relationship is influenced by the perception of each participant about him or herself in relation to other participants. The relationship script refers to the internal conceptual structure containing a set of rules, norms and perceptions that each party in the relationship has about self and the world, and interactions between the two. The collective enactment of relationship scripts is the mechanism by which the selfconcept is realised, capabilities are leveraged, experiences are assimilated and learning takes place (ie the collective enactment of scripts is the manifestation of the organisational being).

The Cognitive Congruence Framework provides a mechanism for defining and reinforcing that network which constitutes the organisational entity, as distinct from the extra-organisational networks that individuals may participate in. With reference to the autopoietic metaphor, self-concept characterises the persistent organisational unity (Merali, 1998a) as the organisation transforms itself over time, delivering new products and services, exploiting new channels, experiencing staff turnover and developing new capabilities. Relationship scripts (being grounded in the self-concept) constitute relations of order, enabling dynamic co-ordination of individual perceptions (ie they provide the relationship mechanism for aligning individual perceptions). Congruence between the self-concept and relationship scripts is necessary to maintain relations of specification, ensuring that components produced in the system are congruent with the organisational identity. The socially situated enactment of relationship scripts constitutes the relations of constitution determining the features of the occupation of space by the organisation.

The framework has a strong resonance with Luhmann’s (1990) view:

Social systems use communication as their particular mode of autopoietic reproduction. Their elements are communications that are % produced and reproduced by a network of communications and that cannot exist outside of such a network %

It is also congruent with Maturana’s (Maturana, 1980; Maturana & Varela, 1973) view that communication is a coordination of behaviour between living organisms through mutual structural coupling. The model also highlights the importance of languaging in developing congruent cognitive infrastructures. Earlier work (Merali, 2001) on knowledge creation and leveraging intellectual capital suggests that the language of codification of existing and new knowledge affects its incorporation into the organisational schema. The language of codification evolves as concepts diffuse through the relationship network, shaped by the linguistic coupling between individuals in the network. Thus there is a recursive relationship between the evolution of the language and its use, and language often demarcates the boundary of the social unity, reinforcing the communal identity of individual members constituting that unity. The evolution of professional jargon is one example of this type of phenomenon.

## Organisational identity

Organisational identity is instrumental in characterisation of ‘self’ and ‘non-self’ recognition mechanisms that determine boundary interactions, such as:

 The variable directional permeability of the boundary for information exchange

The inclusion/exclusion of information agents through coupling

The selection of sensory and positional information from the environment.

The organisational identity, operating through the definition of ‘self’ (the bounded organisation) and the ‘other’ (agents and entities outside the boundary) plays a powerful role in developing intellectual capital. Organisational identity:

is instrumental in legitimising knowledge claims within the organisation (ie what is and is not worth knowing)

determines the filters for recognising and accepting information and agents from outside (relevance filters) and

shapes the actions necessary for maintaining (or establishing) the desired position of ‘self’ to multiple ‘others’ (through coupling).

In relatively steady competitive contexts, we would expect organisations to find stability by implementing a good fit between the established organisational schema and external environment characteristics with regard to competitor activity and market characteristics. The enactment of relationships with external entities, the selectivity exercised by boundary agents and the channeling of informational resources would be consistent with the established organisational schema.

In dynamic contexts (where there are often discontinuities in the competitive environment), when perturbations in the external environment challenge the internal status quo, boundary agents can dissipate the impact of that perturbation. Boundary agents may reorientate relative to ‘self’ and ‘non-self’ components, renew the internal schema or revise relationship scripts. The organisational history of structural coupling determines the new pathways that become available for enactment of organisational identity.

## The role of boundary agents

The strong internal neighbourhood relations of individuals (or components) that share the organisational identity define the boundary of the unity. Biological analogies align with observations of organisations. The integrity of the organisational boundary and its role in selectively channeling resources into the organisation’s network of production is analogous to the importance of the outer integument of a living organism.

Boundary agents (ie in terms of the Cognitive Congruence Framework, individuals of a given organisation engaged in the enactment of the boundary relationship) are potentially key actors in modifying the organisational schema. Their significance depends on the extent to which they are meaningfully networked (both inside and outside the organisational boundary) through relationship scripts.

## Case vignette

The following case vignette is introduced for the purpose of illustrating features of the boundary phenomenology and its role in knowledge processes. The vignette is derived from an extensive interview-based case study. The scope of the original study was to investigate a major transformation initiative in a medium-sized building society (referred to as ‘Finsoc’), and the researchers interviewed a cross-section of the society’s 2000 employees ranging from the CEO through to the receptionists. A detailed account of the study can be found in Merali and Meadows (2002).

Finsoc underwent a program of rapid growth through acquisition in the early 1980s. Many of the acquired businesses were outside Finsoc’s traditional portfolio for building societies and were managed as satellite operations complementing the traditional core business. The recession of the 1980s reversed the expansionist trend: to survive, Finsoc divested itself of its satellite businesses. After the rationalisation, Finsoc retained a branch network for face-to-face transactions, but the majority of its workforce was based at headquarters. Financial consultants attached to branches worked on a commission basis to recruit new members. A significant proportion of the society’s membership base came through the acquisition process and was not expected to have institutional loyalty to the society. They were dealt with via postal channels from headquarters.

Throughout this period the organisation was hierarchical. The strategic imperative for rationalisation and the related urgency reinforced a strong command-andcontrol culture. Staff who survived the cuts were compliant and management directives were not challenged. Work organisation was task-based and the skills base was narrowly focused on specific product lines. IT systems were accounts-based (rather than customer-based) implementations on outdated technologies and provided little in the way of support for customer-oriented crossselling of products. The downsizing exercise (a bloody period in organisational memory) coincided with the appointment of a new CEO who launched a major business transformation programme.

Finsoc emerged from the downsizing exercise at a time when the whole of the financial services sector was in a state of flux, with traditionally separate sectors (banking, insurance, housing finance and brokerage) becoming increasingly integrated in the deregulated UK market. IT-driven innovations were reshaping delivery mechanisms and product offerings across the sectors (Channon, 1998). To survive in this dynamic context and to take advantage of emergent opportunities, Finsoc had to reduce its cost–income ratio and be innovative. Costeffective mechanisms were required to retain existing customers by satisfying their changing needs with innovative products and delivery channels, and to attract/acquire new customers. The transformation drive came from this new strategic imperative.

The transformation programme was designed to turn Finsoc into a customer-focused organisation in every aspect. Issues of strategy, structure, technology, people and roles were all defined and planned for. Several consultancy firms were employed to work with Finsoc personnel throughout the transformation process. Issues of change management were addressed, and resources were allocated for change management experts to run workshops with ‘Finsoc’ staff to enable them to adjust to their new roles and modes of working.

The organisation was restructured: business-focused teams were created to develop multi-skilled personnel to cross-sell products. A new IT and information systems infrastructure (providing facilities such as consolidated customer profiles, product databases, workflow management) was installed to enable teams to work effectively. The software was designed to support relationship-oriented personalised transactions, allowing staff to update customer histories with details of individual circumstances as they became apparent. The system provided cross-referenced up-to-date product and service information. For contingencies where front-line staff lacked sufficient product expertise to deal with customer requirements, calls could be forwarded to dedicated product experts. This was called ‘one-hop shopping’ and minimised the number of customer hand-offs required for a successful customer encounter. The technology infrastructure was designed to support future introduction of internet-based transaction channels.

Traditionally the staff had a ‘back room’ role dealing with postal queries and transactions. The new mode of operation required them to be proactive and to act on their own initiative during customer encounters to create a personalised experience for the customer. The conversational mode was intended to capture richer customer information and provide a more responsive and tailored service to the needs articulated by customers. Software design included support for personalised transactions and customer relationship management. Scenario-based training was used to enable customer-facing staff to articulate and record changing customer circumstances and requirements, and, where appropriate, to respond with offers of additional or alternative services. Where such offers were welcomed by clients, the lead would be passed on to a financial consultant to follow up.

A pilot implementation of the new system was carried out in ‘ACE’ (a part of Finsoc dedicated to servicing customers acquired with a new mortgage lending business). In the initiation phase of the pilot, the ACE team developed an intimate relationship with an independent consultant who unilaterally advocated the implementation of self-managing teams. The consultant left Finsoc before the beginning of the pilot implementation, leaving the ACE team members to define their own mode of operation, based on their personal understanding of self-management. The team developed a two-layered structure with a core of ‘workers’ who coped with customer interactions as they arose, overseen by two operational managers who monitored progress but did not engage in day-to-day resourcing or processual issues. Leaders emerged within the worker group operating in a fire-fighting fashion with emergent issues, and no time was spent on planning internal resource utilisation or on reviewing performance. The leaders were perceived by their comrades as local heroes, and the two operational managers became alienated from the socially cohesive core.

As the pilot project progressed, the ACE team became increasing isolated from the rest of Finsoc. The business leader who ‘owned’ the pilot was moved elsewhere. The financial consultants (supposed to consolidate the new sales leads generated by ACE staff) rejected the leads claiming that they were of an inferior quality, but did not work with the ACE staff to improve the situation. Performance figures indicated that the pilot had not been successful in leveraging the capabilities that Finsoc had invested in.

A study to review this early experience revealed that despite the successful implementation of the technological and information systems infrastructures, the ACE team was not effective. Despite the workshops they attended, staff felt unable to work in the new mode. Operational managers and team members reported feeling isolated. Whilst teams were given responsibility for organising their activities to deliver to targets, they had no input into the processes for resource allocation or target setting. As new contingencies arose, the two operational managers had no power base from which to negotiate targets or marshal supplementary resources to overcome emergent problems. Team members interpreted this as abandonment and abdication of responsibility by management in the name of empowerment.

## Case analysis

This section reviews the boundary phenomenology illustrated by the Finsoc case, with reference to both the autopoietic metaphor and the cognitive congruence framework. Rationalisation of Finsoc and divestiture of its satellite businesses resulted in a consolidation of related business activities. The relatedness of the residual business activities conferred a clearly recognisable boundary and reinforced Finsoc’s identity as a traditional building society with strong customer-focused values and a team-based structure. In terms of the autopoietic metaphor, this clearly defined entity constitutes the autopoietic unity, and the processes that perpetuate its identity and values constitute the networks of production.

## Design characteristics and the role of the boundary

The design of processes and systems to support customer-focused operations introduced the following changes to the boundary phenomenology, increasing the potential permeability of the organisation to customer intelligence:

Increased number of channels: Traditionally customer interactions with the society were confined to the postal channel or face-to-face encounters with branch staff and financial consultants. The implementation of the telephone channel (and the provision for a future internet channel) increased the available modes of interaction.

Increased capacity to exchange complex material: The move from account-based transaction interfaces to customer-based interfaces increased the richness of information exchange. Staff could converse with customers who held multiple accounts on the basis of a holistic customer profile, instead of limiting exchanges to one account at a time.

Increased variety and volume of material exchanged: The telephone transactional interfaces would enable staff to capture unstructured customer information during personalised telephone service encounters, and the resulting customer record would reflect customer views more faithfully.

Increased surface area and number of receptors: Back office staff effectively became active boundary agents (a role previously confined to branch staff and consultants), increasing the surface area exposed to the external environment and the number of receptors (boundary agents) available for collecting customer intelligence (Figure 2).

Increased sensitivity: The open interface and support software enabled staff to pick up conversational cues for generating leads for the financial consultants to follow up and consolidate.

The flatter organisational form with its increased surface-area-to-volume ratio also had implications for the organisation’s internal responsiveness to external information:

Direct responses: The boundary agents could access the information they needed to deal with new encounters (the ‘one-hop shopping’ concept), and consequently deal more directly with contingencies as they arose.

Figure 2 The new organisational form results in an increased surface area/volume.

Simplified co-ordination of boundary agents with internal components: Fewer internal components were without direct contact with the external environment: the distance between the deepest component and the boundary was reduced, simplifying the coordination of individual perceptions about the nature of external changes and suitable responses.

The new organisational form had greater numbers of individuals interacting directly with customers. This held significant implications for the Cognitive Congruence Framework. Engagement of larger numbers of boundary agents with the external environment afforded an improved opportunity to refresh the schema and maintain a more accurate representation of the environment. Reducing the number of individuals isolated from the boundary and increasing their proximity to the boundary afforded better alignment of the self-concept with the schema. There were fewer layers for information to penetrate between boundary agents and those in the interior; there were fewer hand-offs, resulting in more faithful transmission of information.

The new design offered significant benefits in acquiring and acting on external intelligence captured by the boundary agents. Realisation of the benefits implicitly stipulates congruence between the four elements of the cognitive congruent cycle. Whilst the new design of Finsoc had the potential to improve the quality of the schema and its congruence with the self-concept, it did not directly affect the alignment of the relationship scripts and relationship enactment with the schema and the self-concept. The following discussion shows how incongruence between relationship scripts and their enactment may have contributed to the performance shortfall of the ACE pilot.

## Issues of internal congruence

A detailed analysis of the Finsoc transformation using the Cognitive Congruence Framework revealed the pattern shown in Figure 3. There was a gap between the organisational self-concept and the relationship scripts:

The study revealed that despite the team-based restructuring and the resounding organisation-wide team-based rhetoric the relationship scripts were not transformed. The power base remained with a small number of individuals who had been responsible for the directive rationalisation in the recession. Work with the consultants and the change management workshops established a strong rhetoric about the self-concept along the lines of ‘We used to be a command and control organisation % now we are a team-based organisation’.

Superficially, the rhetoric was an affirmation – the self-concept was never challenged. The scripts for relationship enactment did not fundamentally change: managers and team participants continued to behave as if the command and control culture survived. However, in the new structure, people were not sure where the authority lay: they were reluctant to make decisions and were afraid to experiment or act on their own initiative. There was a feeling that the organisation was less effective than it had been under the old regime (when people knew what was expected of them and understood how the reward and punishment mechanisms worked).

In terms of the model, ‘Finsoc’ had a well-developed schema for the external environment. The internal schema was consistent with the self-concept of a ‘teambased organisation’. However, the self-concept was incongruent with the actual relationship scripts – they still conformed to the command-and-control behaviours. The enactment of the relationship therefore failed to achieve the capabilities that could have been leveraged in a more empowering context. The incongruence between the rhetoric and reality of the relationship scripts was not confronted. It remained unchallenged until data relating to the business performance indicators signalled the problem.

The retention of old scripts was manifest in the relationships between boundary agents concerned with customer relationship management. The new credo of customer-focused service delivery was designed to replace the individual ownership of customer welfare with collective ownership. The difficulties between the ACE team and financial consultants demonstrated the retention of old internal relationship scripts privileging financial consultants as the relationship holders for customer sales. Whilst the organisation underwent restructuring in line with the team-based rhetoric, financial consultants continued to function as semi-autonomous agents with commission-based individual reward systems. While they were expert at spotting and realising opportunities for cross-selling products and services, their privileged position meant they had no incentive to invest in coaching the ACE team to improve its performance. The inappropriate incentive mechanism disrupted development of congruent relationship scripts across a diversity of boundary agents.

With reference to our conceptual framework, the elements of the Cognitive Congruence Framework responsible for maintenance of those elements corresponding to autopoietic referents of relations of constitution and relations of order failed, causing the autopoietic network of self-production to break down: Finsoc was not able to realise its self-concept through enactment in the real world.

Following this analysis, ‘Finsoc’ addressed the issue of incongruence between the self-concept and the scripts. Significant changes include a new competence-based appraisal scheme and a comprehensive program of continuous personal development to promote the desired behaviour and attitude changes. ‘Finsoc’ has won a number of awards for the quality of its service and for its financial performance over the time that these changes proceeded.

## Enactment of cross-boundary relationships with external agents

The strategic importance of synergistic relationships (ie relationships that are of mutual benefit to the participants) is highlighted in the literature on the internet-enabled economy (Shapiro & Varian, 1999; Tapscott, 1999; Evans & Wurster, 2000). Typically such relationships entail exchanging or sharing processes, people, physical resources, information or know-how in a manner that is defined by the particular understanding between the parties involved. Theories of competitive advantage based on the resource-based view of the firm (Wernerfelt, 1984) assert that sustainable rents are earned by imperfectly mobile, imperfectly imitable resource bundles (Peteraf, 1993).

In synergistic relationships, inimitability is related to the degree of specificity, strength of structural coupling and relative complexity of the relationship between the participating firms.

The role of the boundary in determining the relationship characteristics is a fundamental one. In terms of our conceptual framework, the relationship is realised through the interactions of the boundary agents at the operational level. Finsoc’s relationship with external consultants illustrates a number of important characteristics of cross-boundary relationships.

Figure 4 depicts the enactment of cross-boundary relationships with varying degrees of organisational coupling. The Finsoc case had relationships with different consultants that spanned the range.

Firms engaged in simple transactional relationships retain their individual identities through arms-length transactions. Synergistic leveraging of capabilities is as described by Williamson’s (1975) transactional analysis, and is of the type seen in simple outsourcing arrangements. Finsoc was successful in managing this type of relationship with the consultants it engaged to develop and implement communications and IT infrastructures.

As organisational boundaries become more plastic, with processes, people and information being shared across boundaries, activities of individuals interacting at the boundary become more intimately coupled. Such relationships may acquire some intrinsic value (Kale et al, 2000) and are difficult for competitors to replicate. They can be amorphous, only lasting for as long as the collaboration is mutually beneficial (Hamel, 1991).

Finsoc and a systems house established a strategic partnership for process design, information systems development and implementation. The intention was to selectively combine the capabilities of the two organisations for mutual benefits that would accrue from the collaborative learning process. Finsoc staff, working alongside consultants, acquired systems expertise from the consultants. The consultants acquired expert knowledge about financial services process implementation from the Finsoc staff. The evolution of this relationship demonstrated the following emergent behaviour.

When individuals interacting at the boundary level were engaged in tightly coupled activities in inter-organisational teams working on joint projects, they acted as boundary components (ie points of interaction between the two firms). The team’s success depended on successful alignment of these components and was observable in the internal congruence of team members’ behaviours. While the project was active, the strength of structural coupling between the team members exceeded that of the individual team members to their non-team Finsoc ‘compatriots’ who viewed them as elitist.

Consequently, as the team attempted to realise a new script derived from the enactment of ‘selforganised team’ identity, it isolated itself from the rest of the organisation.

## Discussion

The Finsoc example illustrates features of boundary phenomena important in sustaining organisational integrity in an interconnected world. We now consider the more generalised implications of boundary phenomena at the macroeconomic, interfirm and organisational levels.

## The macroeconomic level

By enabling networked connectivity on a global scale, the internet has catalysed the emergence of new competitive spaces, the development of inter-organisational networking (Amit & Zott, 2001) and the development of new organisational forms. The new competitive arena has yet to be defined.

At the macroeconomic level, boundaries are important in the emergent economy because they are instrumental in charting and bounding the hitherto undefined competitive arena. Boundaries define the rules for incumbents and new entrants and are the only references in otherwise uncharted space. We suggest that the overestimation of the dot.com potential and subsequent investor panic in the face of unsustainable burn-rates (Economist, 2001) illustrates the difficulty of evaluating the emergent competitive opportunities in the internet-enabled economy.

Networking in cyberspace allows for unprecedented variety and complexity. It enables entities to participate in multiple networks simultaneously, and allows connections to be made and broken with relative ease (compared with EDI systems). Networks transcend traditional industry boundaries. Because organisations have finite resources, they must be selective about their relationships. The non-deterministic character of the competitive space erodes the efficacy of traditional goaloriented strategy processes. To be viable, choices must equip organisations to survive in incompletely charted territory. These same choices affect the nature of the context the organisations operate in. As networks of variable dominance evolve, boundaries will emerge. Boundaries will give form to the competitive landscape in cyberspace. Boundaries demarcate ownership of (and legitimate access to) resources, whilst inter-boundary transactions and relationships give rise to emergent rules of interaction and provide a basis for scrutiny of legitimate behaviours and articulation of ‘the rules of the game’. Membership of viable networks provides a measure of stability in an uncertain world analogous to the stability that organisms find in ecosystems in the natural world.

## The inter-firm level

Recent strategy literature on the resource-based view of the firm and core competence management (Peteraf, 1993; Hamel & Prahalad, 1994; Rumelt, 1994; Teece et al, 1997; Merali & McGee, 1998) highlights the dynamic nature of competitive contexts and suggests that:

For individual firms, competitive rents derive from leveraging co-specialised firm-specific capabilities

 Future competitive contexts will be defined by firms competing over competences

Future successful organisations will continually ‘reinvent’ themselves to be competitive, and discontinuity is a key feature of the reinvention concept.

Firms need to access a diversity of capabilities from which they can dynamically generate complex ‘valuable resource bundles’ and deploy them to compete in a changing context. To access the requisite variety (Ashby, 1956) of capabilities necessary for viability, a firm needs to adopt one or more of three strategies:

Develop the requisite variety of capabilities internally

 Acquire a company that has a complementary set of capabilities, or

Gain access to capabilities owned by another firm through a special relationship.

Internal development of capabilities carries a consequent time implication that may render it ineffective in a fastmoving competitive context. Similarly, acquisition may be attractive in the short-term, but the need for reinvention and the problems associated with post-acquisition integration and divestiture rates make it less attractive as a long-term proposition.

Within the synergistic relationship model a firm accesses part of another firm’s capability pool. This offers a greater degree of flexibility and choice in terms of the diversity of options available. By participating in the relationship, both firms can leverage their selectively combined capabilities and resources to accrue greater rents than would be possible on the intra-firm resourcebased competitive model. The emerging literature on inter-organisational networks and alliances illustrates this concept (Nohria & Eccles, 1992).

As illustrated by the Finsoc example, sustainability of the synergistic relationship is a function of congruence between the individual and collective perceptions of the organisational self-concept. If enactment of the synergistic relationship destabilises individual scripts to such an extent that the authenticity of either of the parent organisational identities is threatened, the coalition is unlikely to survive.

This is an important issue for the emergent internetenabled economy for two reasons. As the technological ease of implementing cross-boundary processes and integrating cross-boundary information resources becomes easier, the strategy literature suggests:

Firms engage in strategic partnerships and alliances to access capabilities they do not possess, ie they realise synergistic advantages by selective interorganisational combination of resources (Ring & Van de Ven, 1992; Subramanium & Venkatraman, 2001) and

Firms tend to outsource non-core activities to specialist firms (Evans & Wurster, 2000).

Issues of boundary management are critical in evolving, managing and dissolving synergistic relationships. The management (including the protection) of a firm’s own intellectual capital and the leveraging of inter-organisational resource pools depends on effective management of boundary characteristics (eg permeability and selectivity) and distinctions of ‘self’ and ‘non-self’, with boundary agents collecting, filtering and channeling relevant intelligence between the two organisations.

## The organisational level

At the level of the individual organisation, boundaries are paradoxical. Boundary agents isolate the ‘inside’ from the ‘outside’, reinforcing ownership of organisational resources, preventing internal resources from leaking out and keeping harmful external influences at bay. However, they also connect the organisation to external players and resources through relationships. Boundary agents filter information. The coupling between agents (through the enactment of the relationship scripts and the subsequent schema refreshment) creates boundary phenomenology. The Cognitive Congruence Framework describes the mechanism for inter-subjective legitimisation of what should and should not be allowed to enter or leave through the boundary.

Specialisation of boundary agents is one aspect in which organisational boundaries in cyberspace do not mirror the biological metaphor exactly. In biological systems, boundary agents are specialised, having particular characteristics that make them effective in carrying out the specific tasks of maintaining boundary functionality (eg selective permeability for materials entering and leaving the organism; sensing the external context, etc). In the internet-enabled economy, the provision of intranets, extranets and internet access enables increasing numbers of workers to connect with the external environment. A move from hierarchical to networked organisational forms (Rockhart & Short, 1991) increases the potential for every member of the organisation to be a boundary agent. Formerly, organisations depended on library research services to access, retrieve and assess information from various sources. In the internet-enabled economy, ‘knowledge work’ is no longer confined to ‘knowledge specialists’, and all workers are, to some extent, knowledge workers.

The problem of collecting, filtering and channeling relevant intelligence is central to knowledge management. Organisations look to social mechanisms (eg development of communities of practice (Brown & Duguid, 1991)) and technological developments (information portals and knowledge management environments) to deal with these. The following discussion argues that understanding the way boundary agents operate and connect internal and external sources and users of intelligence is central to knowledge management in dynamic environments.

For organisational viability, boundary agents need to detect significant perturbations in the environment and initiate appropriate responses through the internal relationship network. For learning and knowledge acquisition they must select information that needs to be internalised. Intelligent agent and data mining technologies can select information that fits individual profiles, but there are two critical aspects that human boundary agents cannot relinquish to technology. Judgments about the quality of information cannot be automated, and tacit knowledge cannot be transmitted without human connectivity. Boundary agents use external social networks to carry out this qualitative filtering and knowledge assimilation activity (eg professional bodies and transorganisational communities of practice).

The strength of internal coupling (via relationship scripts) and the congruence of components of the Cognitive Congruence Framework determine the extent to which the learning of individual boundary agents is exploited by the organisation as a whole.

The boundary exhibits both selectivity and plasticity, whilst maintaining organisational integrity. As the competitive terrain changes, the value of existing interorganisational relationships may erode, atrophy or evolve. New relationships may be formed. At different times and in different relationships, different types of information will be appropriate. Boundary agents may be members of different external networks concurrently and information they exchange will vary with their activities in these networks.

Boundary agents must be managed to stay aligned with dynamic internal and external contexts, redefining relevance filters for selecting cross-boundary information transfer and engaging in appropriate internal and external relationship networks. At the same time, they must maintain the integrity of the organisational identity. The Cognitive Congruence Framework outlines a mechanism for addressing this: new insights of individual agents are transmitted through relationship networks, and the organisational relevance filters are updated through the enactment of relationship scripts and schema refreshment. In practice, this necessitates managing:

The congruence between the four components of the framework (see Merali, 2000a, for more detailed treatment), and

The balance between the strength of coupling between members of the organisation and their freedom to make useful relationships with the outside (particularly important from the perspective of organisational learning and knowledge creation).

Schemas and scripts evolve over time and are pathdependent (shaped by history and individual organisational memories). Taking the Schumpeterian (Schumpeter, 1934) view that new knowledge is created from existing knowledge, the innovative capacity of an organisation is dependent on its existing knowledge. Organisational stock must have the requisite variety for dealing with an evolving external environment. Boundary permeability and plasticity control the rate of assimilation of external knowledge; internal coupling of boundary agents determines the rate of organisational innovation and transformation. These observations lead us to conclude that effective knowledge management in dynamic environments requires active boundary management.

## Conclusions

This paper began by asserting that technology is instrumental in realising a dynamically inter-connected world, enabling organisations to share information, expertise and processes across boundaries. Organisational being is entwined with that of other organisations. Using the case study illustration, we addressed the question of how organisations persist as distinct recognisable entities over a period of time in which they continually transform themselves, delivering new products and services, exploiting new channels, experiencing staff turnover and developing new capabilities.

The paper focused on the boundary as the phenomenological device for demarcating the cleavage of the organisation from its environment and introduced the autopoietic metaphor to characterise organisational being. The information perspective was used to examine and define the organisational entity and its boundary phenomenology, to describe the relationship between organisational intelligence (as the set of perceptions held in the collective schema) and actions in the real world (as realised through relationship enactment). This device enables us to deal in a holistic way with non-orthogonal relationships between individual and collective perceptions and actions. It captures ‘the generic dance of knowledge and organisational knowing’ (Cook & Brown, 1999) encapsulating both a view of knowledge as object and that of knowing as action or enactment – central to knowledge management (Polanyi, 1967; Nahapiet & Ghoshal, 1998). Because it deals with the cognitive infrastructure underpinning transformation and is defined in informational terms, the Cognitive Congruence Framework retains its utility regardless of whether it describes transformation of bricks-and-mortar entities or virtual ones in cyberspace.

Breakdown of congruence between elements of the cognitive congruence cycle corresponds referentially to a defect in relationships (of constitution, specification or order) in the autopoietic metaphor, signaling a breakdown in the self-organising, self-producing capability of the autopoietic organization. In the Finsoc case, this translated as an inability to realise the organisational self-concept (ie it failed to live up to the espoused values). In knowledge management terms, the conceptual framework presented in this paper enables us to examine and diagnose the effectiveness of maintaining and leveraging what is known through interactions in an interconnected world

The autopoietic mechanism of relations for maintaining a persistent identity has referential correspondence to the mechanism described in the Cognitive Congruence Framework. This was used as a device for

exploring boundary characteristics and management in cyberspace. The relationship between organisational identity and boundary phenomenology is recursive: boundaries are emergent properties of organisational identity, and boundary agents sustain organisational integrity by their active participation in relationship networks. The discussion highlighted the paradox of boundaries and identified characteristics critical for effective knowledge management in dynamic interconnected cyberspace.

The importance of individual boundary agents in utilising external networks to identify and assimilate new knowledge for organisational transformation, and their role in maintaining the integrity of the evolving organisation suggests that effective knowledge management in cyberspace necessitates active boundary management.
