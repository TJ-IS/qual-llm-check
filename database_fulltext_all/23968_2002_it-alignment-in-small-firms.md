# IT alignment in small firms

H Hussin<sup>1</sup>, M King<sup>2\*</sup> and P Cragg<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>International Islamic University, Malaysia; <sup>2</sup>Loughborough University Business School, UK; <sup>3</sup>University of Canterbury, NZ

This study focused on the alignment of business strategy and IT strategy among 256 small UK manufacturing firms. An instrument was developed and used to measure IT alignment in small firms. Evidence was gained of high IT alignment in some firms. The study also indicated that IT alignment was related to the firm’s level of IT maturity and the level of the CEO’s software knowledge, but did not seem to be linked to the CEO’s involvement or the firm’s sources of external IT expertise.

European Journal of Information Systems (2002) 11, 108–127. DOI: 10.1057/palgrave/ejis/3000422

## Introduction

This study focuses on issues surrounding IT alignment in small firms. In particular, it is concerned with the alignment or ‘fit’ between the business strategy and the IT strategy adopted by individual small firms. Other authors have used the term IT alignment with a variety of meanings relating to different aspects of fit, but, in this study, IT alignment refers exclusively to the fit of a small firm’s IT strategy with its business strategy. Many authors suggest that the alignment of business and IT strategy is important for firms but that many firms struggle to achieve alignment. Hence, this study sought to examine IT alignment in small firms and investigate some factors that might influence a small firm’s level of IT alignment.

Previous studies have emphasised the need to develop a fit between IT strategy and business strategy and argue that IT alignment has advantages for firms (Chan et al, 1997; Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993). For example, Chan et al (1997) found a positive relationship between IT alignment and firm performance. However, as yet, there has been little study of IT alignment in small firms. This is despite Storey (1994) identifying business strategy as one of the three main components that contribute towards growth among small firms. Thus, there is a need for further studies of IT alignment in small firms and to understand how it might be achieved. So, the objective of this study was to identify factors that might lead to greater IT alignment in small firms. The method chosen to achieve this objective was a cross-sectional study using a mail questionnaire, followed by statistical analysis.

The existing literature on IT alignment and IT in small firms is reviewed in the first section, with particular attention to previous attempts to measure IT alignment and to identify the factors that might encourage or enable high alignment, and how these might relate to small firms. This leads to the development of the research model, involving three hypotheses about the three variables which are identified as likely to influence alignment. The research model section also explains the chosen approach to measuring IT alignment. Details of the sample used are provided in the research methods section and then the key questionnaire data is carefully analysed in the section on identifying IT alignment. Factor analysis followed by cluster analysis were used to identify an aligned group and a not aligned group of small firms. These two groups were then used to test the research hypotheses and the results are explored in the discussion section.

## IT alignment

Henderson & Venkatraman’s (1993) strategic alignment model identifies four domains of strategic choice: business strategy, IT strategy, organizational infrastructure and IT infrastructure. They argue that this requires organizations to manage the fit between strategy and structure, as well as the fit between the business and IT. Typically, different researchers have focused on parts of the Henderson & Venkatraman (1993) model. For example, Chan et al (1997) focused on the link between business strategy and IT strategy, while Raymond et al (1995) focused on the link between organizational structure and IT structure. More recently, Bergeron et al (2001) have included environmental uncertainty in exploring the links between all four aspects and business performance, using six different perspectives for alignment.

Studies have indicated that practitioners struggle to achieve alignment (Baets, 1992; Chan et al, 1997;

Luftman, 1996). As a result, some researchers have examined linking mechanisms in an attempt to understand how firms can best achieve alignment. For example, Pyburn (1983) examined the role of the MIS plan. Reich & Benbasat (1996) recognised that such linking mechanisms have both an intellectual and a social dimension. The intellectual dimension refers to methods and techniques, while the social dimension refers to factors like who is involved, and in what ways. Furthermore, both the content of strategy and the process of strategy can be examined.

The discussion above shows that alignment can be examined from many angles. Some have focused on the process of achieving alignment while others have focused on the content, ie, of how well firms have aligned their IS with organisational strategy. These have attempted to measure strategic alignment (or fit), often based on various typologies. For example, Atkins (1994) developed a survey using three different models to measure strategy and to assess alignment (Ansoff, 1965; McFarlan, 1984; Miles & Snow, 1978). Firms were identified first in terms of their position on McFarlan’s strategic grid, then in terms of their position with the other two models, to determine whether this matched their position on McFarlan’s grid. Lefebvre et al (1992) used Porter’s typology and found encouraging evidence that this classification scheme was applicable to small firms. However, they also concluded that small firms were not as strategically oriented as larger organisations, which makes the identification of strategy more difficult.

One of the most comprehensive attempts to quantify alignment was Chan et al’s (1997) investigation into strategic alignment and its effect on organisational performance. Chan et al (1997) developed four survey instruments to measure each of the following constructs: business strategy, IS strategy, IS effectiveness and business performance. Venkatraman’s (1989b) STROBE instrument was adapted for the business strategy instrument. A similar instrument was developed by Chan to assess IS strategy. As both instruments used the same eight dimensions of strategy, the two instruments were used to compute strategic fit. Chan found that alignment was a better predictor of performance than the individual measures of strategy, and thereby demonstrated a positive relationship between strategic alignment and business performance.

Luftman et al (1999) reported enablers to alignment based on interviews with executives. His top five enablers being: senior executive support for IT; IT involved in strategy development; IT understands the business; Business – IT partnership; and, well prioritized IT projects. Furthermore, Reich & Benbasat (2000) identified five major factors that influenced the social dimension of IT alignment: shared domain knowledge between business and IT executives, IT implementation success, communication between business and IT executives, connections between business and IT planning processes, and strategic business plans. These two studies show that alignment is influenced by a broad range of factors and that we are yet to reach a consensus. Importantly, both IT and non-IT managers influence alignment. They both make important contributions so must work as a partnership. Furthermore, not all of the factors identified by Luftman et al (1999) and Reich & Benbasat (2000) seem applicable to small firms. In particular, most small firms do not have IT executives or an IT department, thus it seems that the CEO is likely to play an even more significant role as an influence on alignment.

There is also some debate about how alignment or fit can be measured. Matching and moderation are two of the many ways of measuring alignment (Hofacker, 1992). The matching perspective is commonly based on the difference between two measures. Alignment is thus the level of similarity between the measures. The moderation perspective assumes that fit reflects synergy, for example, between IS and business strategy. Alignment is calculated as the interaction between the two measures, where, for example, IS strategy has a different impact on a high value for the business strategy compared with a low value. The greater the value of IS strategy, the higher the effect of business strategy on business performance. Chan et al’s (1997) results supported the moderation approach. Bergeron et al (2001) explored six perspectives of alignment and found support for both the matching and moderation approaches in part of their model but concluded that more research was needed on the different perspectives.

## IT and small firms

There has been relatively little study of the strategic use of IT in small firms, with many studies indicating that the conditions are not ripe for IT to be used strategically in small firms. For example, small firms do not perform adequate planning of the use and operation of IT (Lees & Lees, 1987). However, despite many limitations, computer use in small firms has become more sophisticated, particularly in terms of hardware, software and end-user computing. There is also evidence to suggest that as the technical sophistication has grown in small firms, so too has the sophistication of the managerial practices (Raymond & Pare, 1992). Bergeron & Raymond (1992) concluded that IT can be used as a strategic weapon by small firms to maintain their competitiveness and attain a favourable position within their sector of activity.

Levy et al (1998) identified firms where IT was used strategically, but such firms were a minority. They identified four types of small firm regarding IT. Their ‘innovation’ type firms provided examples of IT alignment; ‘IS are an integral and tightly woven part of the business strategy’ (p. 6). In other small firms they recognised a lack of alignment. For example in their ‘efficiency’ firms, ‘there is no recognition of the role of information in supporting the achievement of business strategy’ (p. 5).

Apart from Bergeron et al (2001), few prior studies have examined factors that influence the alignment of business strategy and IT strategy in small firms. However, many have studied influences of IT success in small firms. Although their results are far from conclusive, two factors have received much support: the role of the owner/manager in small business computerisation, and external expertise (DeLone 1988; Thong et al 1996; Yap et al, 1992). Monsted (1993) shows that these two factors were related as many small business owners rely on advice from consultants and a range of informal sources. Hence these factors may well influence small firm IT strategy and thus IT alignment.

The above discussion shows that IT success has been examined in the small firm context. Also, recent studies of large firms indicate that IT alignment may be crucial for IT success, ie, that IT alignment could influence IT success. However, few prior studies have focused on IT alignment in small firms, so a potentially major influence on IT success has, so far, received relatively little consideration in published studies. Thus this study aimed to provide further evidence about small firm IT alignment, with an emphasis on factors that may influence a small firm’s level of IT alignment. In particular, the study aimed to focus on the content aspect of IT alignment, rather than the process. In other words, IT alignment was considered as how well the content of the business strategy matched the content of the IT strategy. The process of aligning business strategy with IT strategy was not explored.

## Research model

The research model is depicted in Figure 1, where three variables influence IT alignment, ie, IT Sophistication, CEO Commitment to IT, and External IT Expertise. The dependent variable was IT alignment, which was viewed as the fit between business strategy and IT strategy, similar to Chan et al (1997). Chan et al (1997) found that an appropriate level of IT infrastructure was associated with the capability to align IT strategy and business strategy. IT infrastructure refers to numerous resources, including processes, technology and people (Raymond & Pare, 1992). Many of these elements have been linked with IT alignment. For example, Reich & Benbasat (2000) found that IT planning processes had an important influence on IT alignment. This supports the conclusion by Earl (1993) that the most common objective for IT planning was to align IT with business needs. King et al (1989) argued that firms with extensive resources at their disposal may gain a competitive edge by deploying them in support of or to strengthen their business. Garsombke & Garsombke (1989) found that the level of IT sophistication was related to the level of IT organisational learning. Also, shared IT and business knowledge was an important influence on alignment (Reich & Benbasat, 2000). All these various aspects of IT can be considered part of the variable ‘IT Sophistication’. Hence, the research model includes ‘IT Sophistication’ as an influence on IT alignment in small firms, as shown in Figure 1. This leads to the first hypothesis:

## H1: A small firm’s IT Sophistication is positively related to their IT alignment.

Prior studies based on large firms indicate that senior managers have a significant influence on IT alignment. For example, Luftman et al (1999) identified senior executive support for IT as the most significant enabler of alignment. Reich & Benbasat (2000) found that executives influenced the social dimension of alignment through shared domain knowledge and communication between business and IT executives. Senior managers are likely to influence alignment in small firms as the owner often plays a critical role in the strategic orientation and planning practices of small firms (Berry, 1998; Storey, 1994). These are similar roles to those identified by Luftman (1996), including defining and communicating strategy, technological visionary, business visionary and prioritizing IT projects. As a result, top management have key roles to play in different alignment perspectives, for example, when IT drives strategy and when strategy drives IT (Henderson & Venkatraman, 1993). Other small firm literature supports the inclusion of CEO commitment as an influence on alignment. For example, management support has been found to influence personal computing use in small firms (Igbaria et al, 1997) and IT adoption (Thong & Yap, 1995). Thus CEO commitment has been shown to influence many aspects of IT in small firms. ## H2: CEO commitment to IT is positively related to IT alignment.

Brouthers et al (1998) argue that external parties influence decisions within small firms, particularly if such parties have ‘power’ over the firm. This influence has been confirmed in an IT context by Iacovou et al (1995) where trading partners strongly influenced the adoption of EDI by small firms. Furthermore, IT consultants and vendors have been shown to have a significant influence on IT success in small firms (Thong et al, 1996) and on personal computing use in small firms (Igbaria et al, 1997). Thong’s analysis indicated that IT success was most likely to occur when external IT experts worked as a team with senior management. This is precisely one of the factors identified by both Luftman et al (1999) and Reich & Benbasat (2000) that influence alignment, ie, IT and non-IT executives working as a partnership. However, in the small firm context, many firms have neither an IT manager nor an IT department. Instead, IT expertise is sought from outside the firm from both consultants and vendors (Thong et al, 1996). This indicates that external parties influence IT in small firms even if they have no formal ‘power’ through ownership over the firm. The research model includes the variable ‘external IT expertise’ as an influence on IT alignment in small firms, as shown in Figure 1, which leads to the third hypothesis:

## Business strategy

Models from large firms have formed the basis for some studies of strategy in small firms. For example, Reid (1993) used Porter’s (1980) generic strategies of cost leadership, differentiation, and focus. Typically, small firms seek relatively low cost forms of product differentiation, emphasising rapid response, quality of service and personal treatment of customers.

There is evidence to indicate that business strategy in small firms may not be formalised or planned, but emerges as a result of consistent business decisionmaking (Mintzberg, 1988). Thus it is not surprising that prior studies of small firms have used typologies to study business strategy rather than explicitly measure business strategy, as argued by Venkatraman (1989b). The instrument proposed by Venkatraman (1989b) was considered for this study but rejected as it is still under development for use in large firms. Chan et al (1997) had to add dimensions to Venkatraman’s model as well as substitute many questionnaire items to improve reliability. Furthermore, the instrument is very long (involving over 30 items for business strategy, plus another 30 items for IT strategy) and has yet to be tested in the small firm context.

Thus, various earlier studies of small firms were used to develop a measure of business strategy for this study (Barkham et al, 1996; Pratten, 1991; Reid et al, 1993; Cambridge SBRC, 1992; Hall, 1995). These studies identified nine key factors that contribute towards small firm competitiveness. The items and their sources are exhibited in Table 1.

## IT strategy

The study aimed to focus on realised IT strategy, rather than intended IT strategy. In the absence of existing instruments relevant to small firms, this study adopted a similar approach to Chan et al (1997). Thus the IT strategy instrument was developed in terms of the IT support provided for business strategy. Nine items were used to measure IT strategy in parallel with the nine business strategy items. For example, IT support for pricing strategy was measured by asking to what extent their current systems assisted in reducing cost, which paralleled the business strategy measure asking to what extent the firm attempts to be ahead of competitors by cheaper pricing of products. In both cases respondents were asked to indicate their level of agreement to statements on a 1 to 5 Likert scale.

## IT alignment

Venkatraman (1989a) provided a classification for the concept of fit, in which he distinguished six interpretations of ‘fit’. Different approaches require different mathematical models and have different theoretical implications (Bergeron et al, 2001; Venkatraman, 1989a). The matching and moderation perspectives have been used by a number of researchers, and other perspectives are still in their exploratory stages and require further development. Bergeron et al (2001) having examined Venkatraman’s six perspectives on fit suggest the systems perspective of fit is richer than a bi-variate approach but did find support for the matching and moderation approaches on some pairs of variables. They concluded that more research on alternatives was needed and authors must clearly specify the approach they use. Chan et al (1997) used a combination of approaches, and their results supported the moderation model rather than the matching model. Hoffman et al (1992) also favoured the moderation model following their study of the effect of the organisation structure–technology fit on performance. They claimed that the moderation model was less ambiguous and more widely applicable, compared with matching. The moderation approach attempts to explain variations in performance by examining specific strategies and IT factors in pairs. For example, the congruence between specific dimensions of business strategy and specific dimensions of IT strategy were examined. Given the weight of evidence in favour of the moderation approach, this study modelled fit as moderation, ie, as the interaction between business strategy and IT strategy.

## IT sophistication

In the small firm context, IT sophistication is multidimensional and includes both IT Use and IT Management (Raymond & Pare, 1992). IT sophistication was measured using questions that drew heavily on the instrument proposed by Raymond & Pare (1992). Three aspects of technical sophistication were included: types of technology (identify items from a list of eight applications); sources of software (rank six possible sources); and, decision level targeted (rank three levels in terms of importance). Five aspects of IT Management were included: availability of IT staff; person in charge of IT; formalised IT strategy; number of years using computers; and, stage of IT development (select one from a list of three).

## CEO commitment to IT

The importance of executive participation and top management support is well advocated by many IS researchers in both large and small organisations (DeLone, 1988; Yap et al, 1992). This study measured CEO commitment to IT in three ways: personal IT use (Cragg & King, 1992), familiarity or knowledge of common software packages (adapted from Magal & Lewis, 1995), and participation in IT selection and implementation (adapted from Cragg & King, 1992). Thus the study included measures of both ‘executive participation’ and ‘executive involvement’ (Jarvenpaa & Ives, 1991).

## External IT expertise

Small firms depend on external IT expertise in the form of consultants and vendors (Thong et al, 1994). The questionnaire asked respondents to rank eight sources of advice, based on Hackney & Kawalek (1996) and Monsted (1993). In addition, the study sought the type of advice from both consultants and vendors, based on Thong et al (1994).

## Research methods

A mail questionnaire survey was used to gather data to test the three hypotheses. As suggested by Dillman (1978), the questionnaire was refined in three stages: pre-testing with academics and research students, pretesting with managers in small firms, and pilot testing with a mailing to 50 randomly selected managers in small firms. The resulting questionnaire was arranged in an A5 booklet style divided into various sections and the main questions used are shown in Appendix 1.

The choice of sampling universe was influenced by prior research studies. Following Delone (1988) and Storey (1994), it was decided to focus on firms with between 50 and 150 employees, since those much smaller or much larger are likely to approach IT issues differently. The manufacturing sector was selected as they can provide a range of levels of IT sophistication (Cragg & King, 1992). In the absence of any official lists, various commercial databases were reviewed and one of the most accurate and useful was that available from Duns and Bradstreet, which had also been used by Cambridge SBRC (1992). A total of 2272 UK addresses fitting the criteria of size and being a UK manufacturer were available and 50 were randomly selected for the pilot study. Balancing the resource limitations of the project and the need for a sufficient number of responses, a further 1400 were randomly selected for use in the main UK wide mailing.

Personalised cover letters and addresses were used, as well as a pre-printed ‘freepost’ return envelope (Dillman, 1978). 256 usable questionnaires were returned. This was an 18% response rate, similar to the Cambridge SBRC in 1992. Non-response was examined using time trend extrapolation (Amstrong & Overton, 1982). The first 30 and last 30 responses were compared on 22 major variables. Only two variables proved significantly different: ‘Intensive Marketing’ strategy and ‘IT support for Intensive Marketing Strategy’. This suggested that non-response was not a significant factor that could affect the conclusions about the variables being studied.

Nearly 70% of the firms were independent companies, with 30% as a subsidiary of another organisation. Mann– Whitney tests on 22 questionnaire items found no significant differences between these types of firm so it was concluded that both types could be combined for further analysis.

Slightly more than half of the firms were engineeringbased while others considered themselves as nonengineering-based. Again, Mann–Whitney tests identified only two variables that differed across these types of firm, which suggested that the engineering and nonengineering firms were similar and could be treated as one sample.

Most firms were more than ten years old (83%), with many (37%) founded between 11 and 20 years ago. Thus most of the firms were mature companies. About half (58%) of the firms had between 50 and 100 full-time employees. 11% had fewer than 50 employees. (This small group outside the original range specified was retained on the assumption they had recently downsized from just over 50, and the small group with just over 150 employees was similarly retained.) 46% of the firms had annual sales over 5 million pounds. About a third of the firms had used computers for between 5–10 years, and another third for 11–15 years. Thus the sample contained many firms with considerable experience with computers.

Nine items were used to measure business strategy and nine parallel items to measure IT strategy. Their means are reported in Table 2.

The highest rated items of business strategy were ‘quality service’ and ‘quality products’, as in prior studies of small firms (Cambridge SBRC, 1992; Pratten, 1991). Similarly, ‘pricing/cost reduction’ and ‘intensive marketing’ were ranked lowest, which is consistent with prior studies (Cambridge SBRC, 1992; Pratten, 1991).

While 68% of the sample had a written business plan, only about a quarter (26%) had formalised their IT strategy. This is not surprising as most of the past literature indicates that IT planning, if it exists, is carried out informally in small firms (Lefebvre & Lefebvre, 1988).

## Identifying IT alignment

Before being able to test the three hypotheses it was necessary to identify those small firms with a high degree of alignment. For each firm, the IT alignment on each strategy was measured by multiplying the rating of that Business Strategy (BS) item with the rating of the corresponding IT Strategy (ITS) item. A high rating for BS and a high rating for ITS would result in a high alignment score. On the other hand, a low rating for BS and a low rating for ITS would give a low alignment score.

## Factor analysis on the strategy items

As the instrument was new, it seemed possible that some of the nine strategy items were correlated with each other or that some underlying dimensions existed. The full set of all Pearson correlations between the 18 business strategy and IT strategy variables are shown in Appendix 2. It is clear that there are high correlations within each set of nine, but much less between the two parallel sets, suggesting there could be some underlying latent dimensions.

Factor analysis was used to search for such dimensions. The items met the conditions for tests of factor analysis with a Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin score of 0.725 and a Bartlett sphericity of 509. The factors were orthogonally rotated with varimax rotation (Kim, 1975). Separate factor analysis solutions were created for the business strategy items and for the IT strategy items. Based on eigenvalues greater than one, both sets of data produced three factor solutions, and all nine items loaded high on at least one factor. Items with factor loadings greater than 0.5 are reported in Appendices 3 and 4. For IT strategy, one item ‘IT support for Quality Product’ loaded high on two factors.

## Alignment based on the three factors

For both business strategy and IT strategy, each respondent’s ratings of the separate items that constituted each factor were averaged to obtain the respondent’s score for that factor (Singleton et al, 1993). For example, for both business strategy and IT strategy separately, the factor 1 scores were computed as (production efficiency + quality service + quality product)/3. The corresponding strategy factor scores were then multiplied to create moderation scores for each factor, and their means over the whole sample are reported in Table 5.

The ‘quality-oriented’ alignment had the highest score, indicating that SMEs in the sample had both focused on this strategy and perceived that their existing IT infrastructure provided support for this strategy. The degree of IT alignment was not high for the other two orientations of product and market.

## Cluster analysis

The degree of variation in alignment between the factors had not been predicted and clearly there was also considerable variation from firm to firm. This raised the question of whether there were groups of small firms with similar profiles in terms of alignment. Maybe there were some with low alignment on all three factors, some low on two factors but highly aligned on quality and some with other combinations of alignment. Thus it seemed important to identify any significant groups before the three hypotheses could be tested. Measurement issues also encouraged analysis by types of firms rather than use correlation and regression analysis. Importantly, several measures were dichotomous or categorical. Furthermore, it seemed possible that some ratio variables could have non-linear relationships with alignment.

Cluster analysis was used to group the firms since this technique produces clusters or groups of highly similar entities based on some specified variables. In this case, cluster analysis was used to form similar groups based on the three measures of alignment, based on Hair et al (1995) and Ketchen & Shook (1996).

This study used the cluster analysis routine in SPSS for Windows (release 6). Ward’s hierarchical clustering routine was used initially and the distance between objects was measured using squared Euclidean distance. The dendrogram and the change in the Ward’s coefficient suggested that a two cluster solution was appropriate.

The stability of the cluster solution was tested by splitting the data into halves. One-way ANOVA was used to analyse the alignment scores of the three factors for the two clusters. The results are shown in Table 6.

Table 6 shows that the mean values for the alignment scores of each factor on each cluster were significantly different at the 0.001 level. This confirms that two distinct clusters had been identified. The data indicated that SMEs in Cluster 1 had higher IT alignment on each factor than firms in Cluster 2. For this reason, Cluster 1 was labelled the ‘Aligned’ group and Cluster 2 as the ‘Not-aligned’ group.

Like the variation in alignment scores, this result had not been predicted. The very clear and significant separation into just two groups was a surprise, but the fact that no other groups emerged with combinations of low and high alignment on different factors considerably simplified the subsequent analysis. Thus, there was no need, at this stage, to pursue any further analysis based on three separate alignment factors. There was also no ambiguity in labelling the groups ‘Aligned’ and ‘Notaligned’ and using these two groups to test the three hypotheses.

Having successfully identified two distinct groups, other variables were examined to gain insights into the nature of the companies. This provided further evidence that the two cluster solution was meaningful (Aldenderfer & Blashfield, 1984). Chi-square tests showed the groups were different on three of the seven variables. On average, the aligned firms were older, more likely to have a written business plan, and had invested more on IT. (Appendix 5). Analysis of the business strategy items showed differences between the two clusters. The Non-aligned group focused their business strategy on the ‘quality-oriented’ business strategies, while the Aligned group pursued a diverse business strategy, emphasising all strategy areas except the ‘intensive marketing’ strategy.

## Hypothesis testing of the variables that influence IT alignment

Having clearly identified two groups of firms with different levels of IT alignment, it was now possible to explore the key objective of the research. This was to test the variables that may influence IT alignment, in particular: IT sophistication, the CEO’s commitment to IT and external IT expertise. To test the hypotheses, the aligned and not-aligned groups of firms were compared for each variable.

## CEO’s IT commitment and IT alignment

The CEO’s IT commitment variable was assessed using three measures: personal IT use, knowledge of software and type of participation with IS implementation. Similarly, for type of participation, the percentages in a combined category for each stage are shown, followed by the mean of the count of the number of stages in this category. Chi-squared tests were used to calculate the significance of the differences in most of the measures and these were all based on the recorded counts in the original categories. The significance probabilities are shown in the final column of Table 9.

The results in Table 9 show no significant difference between the groups for the level of CEO personal IT use. Rather similar to Table 7, there is a very significant difference (over 99%) between the aligned and non-aligned groups in the knowledge of technologies in terms of the number of technologies respondents felt they were knowledgeable about. ## Discussion

This study found some support for Hypothesis 1, which proposed a relationship between alignment and aspects of both IT sophistication and IT management, through the variables type of technology, IT growth stage and number of years using IT. This provided evidence of the aligned firms having greater IT maturity, which supports findings from large organisations (Lederer & Mendelow, 1988). Importantly, this maturity referred to more than just technical sophistication, supporting Fuller’s (1996) argument that SMEs undergo organisational learning related to the utilisation of IT.

The study also provided some support for Hypothesis 2 which proposed a relationship between the CEO’s commitment to IT and IT alignment. The CEO’s knowledge of software was greater in the aligned firms. The evidence suggests that software knowledge could be important for IT alignment. This supports the findings of Reich & Benbasat (2000) where an appreciation of the technology influenced alignment, particularly through aiding communication with IT specialists.

The support for Hypothesis 2 was limited since none of the items that examined CEO participation were significantly higher in the aligned firms. This evidence suggests that software knowledge rather than participation could be important for IT alignment. Jarvenpaa & Ives (1991) argued that executive ‘involvement’ was more strongly associated with the firm’s progressive use of IT than executive ‘participation’. They considered executive involvement to reflect the degree of importance placed on IT by the chief executive, while executive participation refers to the CEOs substantive interventions. Possessing a good knowledge of software could fit into this concept of executive involvement as knowledge would help a CEO decide what software was important for their organisation, and thus make alignment more likely. Future research could study how CEOs acquired their software knowledge. More importantly, this finding deserves further examination as it suggests that researchers of small firms have yet to fully clarify the concept of CEO commitment. Prior studies have focused on participation, eg, Thong et al (1996) focused on CEO participation in numerous parts of a project’s life cycle.

There was little support for Hypothesis 3 which postulated a relationship between external IT expertise and IT alignment. This was contrary to expectations based on Thong et al (1996). This unexpected finding could be a reflection of the aligned firms having greater IT maturity. For example, they could have had more time to learn about the capabilities of different types of software. Also, the more IT mature firms would have had more years to learn how IT could best meet their needs. Furthermore, more mature firms are more likely to be able to rely on their own knowledge to tailor IT to their needs, especially as engaging outside experts is fraught with problems. However, Soh et al (1992) argued that the quality of the external assistance was crucial, rather than merely the presence of external expertise. This study did not gather data on the quality of the assistance. Similarly, it did not examine whether there was proactive client participation (Gable, 1991), which would reflect a partnership between the firm and IT expertise (Luftman et al, 1999; Reich & Benbasat, 2000). This reinforces the need for research to examine the processes associated with alignment. Such studies may need to control for IT maturity.

## Implications for practice

The study suggests that IT alignment is possible within small firms and is related to IT maturity. The study indicates that small firms have built their technical capability over the years to meet their firm’s needs. The study also suggests that there is no easy route to alignment, for example by appointing an IT specialist, or by the CEO being responsible for IT. This suggests that it is important for small firms to expect to learn from their use of IT so that opportunities can be recognised and priority given to initiatives that help IT support their business strategy. Important resources include hardware and software, as well as staff skills and knowledge.

This study found that the CEO’s software knowledge was associated with the ability to align IT with business strategy. This suggests that CEOs, or other senior managers, need to be aware of existing and new technologies. This scanning may be more feasible and effective than the CEO’s direct participation in IT projects. Senior managers need to provide the strategic perspective to guide the direction for their firm’s IT.

## Limitations and research opportunities

Although considerable effort was taken to ensure that all the principal aspects of business strategy for SMEs were included, the IT alignment instrument was exploratory in nature. Future research could aim to validate this instrument.

This study focused on the content aspect of IT alignment. In other words, IT alignment was considered by measuring how closely the business strategy content matched with IT strategy. Although some influences on alignment were studied, the process of aligning business strategy with IT strategy was not explored. This provides another research opportunity, for example, studying planning processes that link IT to business plans.

Much past research on IT and small firms suggested that SMEs lack strategic planning, including IT planning. However, this study provided evidence of small firms with a high degree of IT alignment, suggesting that a large proportion of small manufacturing firms have undertaken some IT planning. Blili & Raymond (1993) argued that SMEs must adopt some kind of framework for planning information technologies. Further research could examine these IT planning processes, with a view to understanding how they may support IT alignment. This study showed that alignment is not simply associated with the existence of a formal IT strategy.

A further limitation of the study concerns cause and effect relationships. A cross-sectional study such as this cannot prove cause and effect relationships. Also, there are potentially other factors that could influence IT alignment. For this study, some of these factors were controlled or excluded. Future studies could use different variables and different methods. For example, case studies or some type of field experiment could permit the study of influencing factors and alignment. Relationships between factors are also worthy of study, as examined by Reich & Benbasat (2000).

## Conclusions

This study has examined three hypotheses concerned with the achievement of IT alignment in small firms and, in so doing, has increased our understanding of IT alignment within small firms. Using a measure of IT alignment specifically developed for this study, it was possible to identify two groups of small firms. One group of firms had achieved some success in aligning their IT strategy with their business strategy, while IT alignment was lower in the other group. By systematically testing the three hypotheses on these two groups of firms some association between the three variables and IT alignment was confirmed. The evidence suggests that the major factors which influenced IT alignment were: IT maturity, technical IT sophistication, and the CEO’s software knowledge. On the other hand, the CEO’s personal involvement in IT planning and personal IT usage seemed to have relatively little influence on IT alignment. Similarly, the different forms of external expertise seemed to have little effect on achieving IT alignment. These latter two findings are rather surprising and indicate the need for further research on the processes associated with IT alignment in small firms to clarify the concepts of CEO commitment and external expertise.
