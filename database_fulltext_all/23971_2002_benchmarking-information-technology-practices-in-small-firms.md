# Benchmarking information technology practices in small firms

PB Cragg

AFIS, University of Canterbury, New Zealand

Much attention has focused recently on benchmarking, a practice by which firms learn from other firms to help improve their own business processes. Numerous benchmarking tools exist for small firms and they typically cover a range of activities, including strategic leadership, quality, and performance. However, most pay no or little attention to IT. Thus, this study aimed to identify IT practices in small firms that could be included in benchmarking tools. The study commenced with a review of existing literature to identify major areas of IT management relevant to small firms. This literature guided multiple-case study research of four engineering firms to identify IT practices. A second phase of the study evaluated evidence from 30 small engineering firms with between 20 and 100 employees. Six practices differentiated IT leaders from IT laggards. Three practices reflected senior management involvement and the other three reflected technological capability.

## Introduction

Benchmarking has been used increasingly during the last 10 years and many see it as a natural evolution of total quality management (TQM). For example, Zairi (1992) claims that much of the first part of the TQM journey was about raising awareness and recognising problems and opportunities, while the second leg utilised benchmarking to optimise operations through finding and implementing better practices (Zairi, 1992).

An important aspect of benchmarking is the focus on external rather than internal standards. For example, Andersen and Pettersen (1996) consider learning from the best within your industry as ‘competitive’ benchmarking. Cox and Thompson (1998) recognise that benchmarking can help firms catch up with competitors, so they support benchmarking where it is applied appropriately, and particularly when firms focus on business activities that are critical to their success. However, Cox and Thompson (1998) provide a sobering view of benchmarking as they argue that benchmarking can ‘only ever hope to achieve second best’ (p 19). Thus benchmarking is inappropriate under many circumstances, particularly if firms are seeking to be leaders based on unique competencies.

The growth in benchmarking activity has spawned a number of benchmarking tools to help firms identify how they stand relative to similar firms. Firms can use these data to then decide what aspects, if any, they may wish to improve. These benchmarking tools cover a wide range of topics. For example, the DTI’s benchmarking index includes leadership, quality systems, impact on society, and business results (DTI, 1998). However, this and other benchmarking tools give little attention to IT. This is surprising considering the e.business revolution and the concerns of governments regarding small firm competitiveness. Thus, the aim of this research was to identify appropriate items that could be used to benchmark IT in small firms. The paper starts with an examination of existing benchmarking tools, followed by an analysis of IT practices in small firms.

## IT content of current benchmarking tools

Numerous benchmarking tools exist that are aimed at evaluating many aspects of a business. Some of these tools are based on the business excellence model, which is the basis for annual awards by the European Foundation for Quality Management (EFQM, 1999). They devised a reduced version of the model for small firms (EFQM, 1998), and a self-assessment version is available (EFQM, 2002).

The business excellence model has a total of nine variables or criteria. Five of these criteria are considered as ‘enablers’ and four as ‘results’, where ‘enablers’ influence ‘results’. The enablers refer to business practices; thus, the business excellence model considers that business practices influence business results, as depicted in Figure 1.

While the model emphasises the acquisition, understanding, and management of information that may lead to improvement or change, the model makes little direct reference to IT. However, under the enabler termed ‘resources’, one question refers to ‘how information resources are managed’, which is concerned with the availability of data. Another question refers to ‘how technology and intellectual property are managed’, including the exploitation of technology and its support for the business (EFQM, 2002).

Other benchmarking tools are based on the ‘business excellence model’, including the DTI’s UK Benchmarking Index (DTI, 2000) and the Australian Quality Council’s self-diagnostic tool (AQC, 2002). The criteria behind the USA’s Malcolm Baldrige Award influenced the development of the ‘business excellence model’ (Baldrige, 2002). Some other benchmarking services in the UK are based on the CBI’s PROBE tool, including versions for small firms called Microscope and PILOT (WLTC, 1997; Prabhu and Yarrow, 1998). These are based on a ‘world class manufacturing model’ that links business practices to operational and business performance (Hanson et al, 1996; Voss et al, 1998).

The IT coverage of these benchmarking tools is summarised in Table 1, showing that they give some attention to IT. Their emphasis is on the data resource rather than management processes. The EFQM instrument would seem to be the only one that addresses IT management, albeit through the broader topic of ‘technology’. For example, one question refers to how the organisation ‘identifies and evaluates alternative and emerging technologies in the light of policy and strategy’ and how the business ‘harnesses technology in support of improvement in processes’ (EFQM, 2002).

Other aspects of some of these tools could be used to reflect a firm’s approach to IT. For example, under ‘strategy and planning processes’, there is a question on ‘how the organisation uses its resources and assets to maintain and increase its value into the future’. And under ‘innovation process’, ‘how the organisation acquires, evaluates and implements creative ideas to accelerate business performance’ (AQC, 2002).

The data in Table 1 indicated that existing benchmarking tools currently pay some attention to IT.

Importantly, their focus on the use and availability of data and information provides a relatively simple view of IT compared with the strategic role espoused by authors like Earl (1989) and Scott Morton (1991). Furthermore, although the base models are well respected, they have been interpreted in various ways to create different benchmarking tools. This has resulted in an inconsistent treatment for IT, even for tools based on the same model. This indicated a research opportunity, which seemed particularly important considering the enabling role for IT and the rapid growth in e.business.

## IT management in small firms

Raymond and Pare´ (1992) argued that IT management was a major dimension of IT sophistication in small firms. They argued that IT management had two dimensions: functional sophistication and managerial sophistication, as detailed in Table 2. Functional sophistication related to ‘the structural aspects of the IS function and to the IT implementation process’ (Raymond and Pare´ , 1992, p 8). Managerial sophistication referred to ‘the mechanisms employed to plan, control and evaluate present and future applications’ (Raymond and Pare´ , 1992, p 8). Raymond and Pare´ (1992) reported low levels of sophistication for IT management in their sample of manufacturing firms. For example, most firms had no internal IS specialist, and informal planning processes.

Studies of IT in small firms have included some of the above elements of IT management, particularly when examining IT success factors. For example, Thong et al (1997) confirmed that both CEO involvement and external IT expertise significantly influenced IT success in small firms. Their analysis indicated that IT success was most likely to occur when senior management and external IT experts worked as a team. They identified external expertise as having the greatest influence on IT success and argued that both consultants and vendors had important roles to play (Thong et al, 1996).

Both management support and external support have also been found to influence personal computing use in small firms (Igbaria et al, 1997). Furthermore, the CEO often has a significant role in the adoption of IT by small firms (Thong and Yap, 1995). A small firm that is likely to adopt IT will most often have a CEO who has a positive attitude towards IT adoption, who is innovative and who is knowledgeable about IT (Thong and Yap, 1995).

Other research provides clear evidence that some aspects of IT management are weak in small firms. For example, small firms do not perform adequate planning of the use and operation of IT (Lees and Lees, 1987; Montazemi, 1988). Also, many small firms have little internal IT expertise and often rely on informal help from friends (Zinatelli et al, 1996). Furthermore, Palvia et al (1994) argued that the computing environment in firms with 50 or less employees is fundamentally different from medium-sized firms, where there was often a formal MIS department and a community of end-users. Levy et al (1998) have identified firms where IT was being used strategically, but such firms were a minority. More than half of their sample of firms were classified as ‘efficiency’ firms, where ‘there is no recognition of the role of information in supporting the achievement of business strategy’ (Levy et al, 1998, p 5). This indicates a lack of IT leadership. Levy et al (1998) also provided evidence of IT management practices being related to IT use, ie, that Raymond and Pare´ ’s (1992) two dimensions of IT sophistication were linked; sophisticated IT use was likely to be associated with sophisticated IT management.

Thus, there is evidence in the literature about some IT management practices in small firms. However, many of the items raised by Raymond and Pare´ (1992) have not been studied in depth, including internal IS personnel, the role of the IS function, and processes associated with planning, control and evaluation of IT. Overall, our understanding of IT management in small firms is weak.

## Research objectives

The benchmarking literature indicates that many management practices are important for business success. However, existing benchmarking instruments pay little attention to IT. Thus, the aim of this study was to identify IT practices that could be used to benchmark IT in small firms. This meant that the study aimed to identify some IT practices that would differentiate firms on the basis of their ‘excellence’. IT impact was chosen as the dependent variable rather than ‘firm performance’, based on Wiarda and Luria (1998). They advised researchers to ‘zero in on the shops that are good at F the one or two measures you most want to target’ (Wiarda and Luria, 1998, p 92). This advice was based on their experiences with benchmarking studies involving thousands of firms in the USA. They argued that firms should not be selected on firm performance, as high performing firms are unlikely to have the best practices across the whole firm. Thus ‘IT impact’ was used as the dependent variable, and viewed as the impact of information system on the performance of the business, as depicted in Figure 2.

Thus, the research objective was to identify IT practices that differentiated firms in terms of IT impact, with the aim of these practices being used subsequently in IT benchmarking studies. The study fell short of exploring the consequences of such IT practices. For example, the study did not aim to examine the links between practices and business performance, as implied, for example, by the business excellence model.

As our understanding of IT practices in small firms is weak, this encouraged the current study to adopt a case study approach to identify IT practices in small firms. Thus, the first stage of the study used existing literature and four case firms to help identify IT practices in small firms. Some of these practices were then examined in the second stage of the study to identify which IT practices differentiated the firms, based on IT impact.

## Stage 1: case research

The case research aimed to identify IT practices. Initially, a grounded approach was used to collect and analyse case evidence. This was followed by an analysis guided by the literature from both small and large firms.

IT practices were likely to vary by firm size, industry, and country, based on the experiences of Voss et al (1998) and Sohal et al (1999). Thus, it was important to focus the current study on similarly sized firms (20–100 employees), within one industry (engineering of metal products), and within one country (UK). Ideally, the study wanted to examine IT practices in some small firms that could be considered to be IT leaders. The IS literature suggests that ‘IT leaders’ do exist, and some firms, particularly some large, North American firms, have been the focus of considerable research, eg, American Airlines, American Hospital Supply, Otis Elevators, and Xerox. However, no such list exists for small firms. In the absence of data on IT excellence, some measure of overall firm performance had to be used to select the case firms for this stage of the current study. Although these ‘excellent’ firms were not expected to excel at IT, they seemed more likely than other firms, ie, less excellent firms, to have at least adopted some good IT practices. Thus, this part of the current study was of IT practices in successful firms, rather than IT practices in firms known to be excellent in IT. Four firms from the metal products sector were selected. All were manufacturers, independent, and had between 20 and 100 employees. One firm had recently won a ‘Small Business of the Year Award’, and another had been shortlisted for the same award. The awards had used many criteria to assess excellence, including recent financial performance.

Semi-structured interviews were conducted with one senior manager in each of the four firms. In addition, any IT specialist was interviewed. The questions and the analysis were based on McKinsey & Co’s Seven S’s, as described by Pascale and Athos (1982) (see Appendix 1). This framework was selected as it provided a broad view of the concept of management and had proved to be useful when examining IT maturity (Galliers and Sutherland, 1999) and business excellence (Peters and Waterman, 1982).

The prior literature on small firms was used to devise a set of open-ended questions, plus prompts to cover the range of the Seven S’s. This resulted in questions on the following topics: computer use and users, firm structure, IT plans, IT responsibilities, recent IT projects, external experts, internal personnel and IT skills, firm strategy and strengths, and internal communication. In addition, a short, paper-based questionnaire was used to gather data on IT impact, firm performance, IT use, and IT management.

The case evidence identified numerous practices for each firm. The within-case analysis involved sorting the practices for each firm into each of the Seven S’s. The cross-case analysis combined the results for the four firms for each of the Seven S’s.

Furthermore, six different frameworks were used to identify IT practices. Two of these were based solely on the small firm literature (Hall, 1992; Storey, 1994), two on the IT in small firms literature (Yap et al, 1992; Pollard and Hayne, 1998), and two on the large firm IT literature (Cortada, 1998; Galliers and Sutherland, 1999). The cases were then re-examined using each of the six frameworks. Each identified some IT practices, which were then classified according to the Seven S’s model. For example, Hall (1992) proposed six ‘hallmarks’ for small firm success. One of these hallmarks was the need for firms to manage the overall focus of the business. This hallmark was used in the case analysis to suggest the following IT practice: senior managers provide clear focus and direction for IT (strategy). Each of the six frameworks are outlined in Appendix 2. The resulting IT practices are discussed in the next section of the paper.

## Significant IT practices

The analysis provided two sets of IT practices for small firms. The first set was based solely on the case data while the second set of IT practices reflected the six frameworks. Some IT practices were identified during both phases of the case analysis, while other IT practices were only identified during one phase. The two sets showed many similarities. For example, both identified IT alignment, the involvement of senior managers for IT leadership, and rigorous evaluation. Also, the shared values were similar, with both the cases and the literature emphasising a focus on customer service, quality, and continuous improvement.

Importantly, both the cases and the literature complemented each other as they both identified some different practices. For example, the cases indicated the importance of shared IT leadership within the senior management team, and also the involvement of the full management team in an ongoing process of regular meetings, with specific individuals taking on important roles and responsibilities. The literature indicated the importance of building partnerships with stakeholders, particularly external IT experts, and the need for firms to take action.

A major area of difference between the two sets of practices concerned the use of external IT experts. The literature indicated external IT consultants to be important. The cases showed little reliance on external experts. Instead, they indicated a need for both IT and project management expertise, but from within the firm.

Thus the two analyses complemented each other. The cases informed the study, and the literature acted as a check on the case analysis. Table 3 provides a summary of the major findings presented for each of the Seven S’s (Pascale and Athos, 1982).

## Towards benchmarking

Stage 1 of the current study identified many IT practices. The objective for stage 2 was to identify IT practices that could be suitable as benchmarks. Various factors were taken into consideration in the process of selecting specific IT practices for further study from the many IT practices identified in stage 1:

1. For it to be useful in a benchmarking study, the practice must differentiate firms, based on IT impact.

2. The instrument should have a wide coverage rather than focus on one aspect, eg, focus solely on IT strategy. For this reason, stage 2 retained the Seven S’s framework.

The above criteria were applied to each section of Table 3. This process resulted in a total of 11 research propositions, which are outlined below. Stage 2 of the study tested these 11 propositions with the objective of identifying practices that differentiated firms, based on IT impact. The concept of IT leaders and IT laggards proved useful here (Lacity and Feeny, 1996). Firms with high IT impact were considered as IT leaders, while firms with low IT impact were considered as IT laggards.

## Proposition 1

IT leaders view IT strategically. The cases and the literature provided many examples and arguments of the growing role for IT being used strategically. However, managers in small firms are very busy and cannot take risks with IT. Thus, while some firms view IT as part of their competitive strategy, some other firms have been inclined to see the role for IT as primarily operational. It was expected that firms that viewed IT as strategic would be more successful (Hall, 1992; Cortada, 1998; Pollard and Hayne, 1998; Galliers and Sutherland, 1999, and all four cases).

## Proposition 2

IT leaders have senior managers who share responsibility for IT. Much literature has argued that senior managers need to provide IT leadership. This is particularly difficult in small firms, as most small firms cannot afford the luxury of appointing a manager with considerable IT experience. The cases showed that many managers were capable of providing IT leadership. However, small firms structure this leadership in different ways. It was envisaged that small firms would take the following four approaches to providing IT leadership: IT leadership was shared by two or more managers; one manager took responsibility for IT; an employee had been given the responsibility for IT (rather than a senior manager); no one within the firm was responsible for IT (Storey, 1994; Galliers and Sutherland, 1999, and all four cases).

## Proposition 3

IT projects are managed internally within IT leaders. The case firms showed a preference to manage their own IT projects from within the firm. Other literature indicates that small firms need external experts. Thus this proposition aimed to re-examine this topic. It was envisaged that IT leaders would appoint at least one person from within the firm to take an active role in IT projects, while IT laggards would delegate project leadership to an external expert (Hall, 1992; Yap et al, 1992; Storey, 1994, and all four cases).

## Proposition 4

IT projects are rigorously managed within IT leaders. The cases provided evidence that some small firms devote much effort to their IT projects. It was envisaged that some small firms would take a less rigorous approach to IT projects. (Storey, 1994; Cortada, 1998; Pollard and Hayne, 1998; Galliers and Sutherland, 1999, and all four cases).

## Proposition 5

New systems are bespoke within IT leaders. Some of the case firms had bespoke systems, while others had customised their IT systems to different degrees to meet specific needs. The literature shows that many small firms rely heavily on packaged systems. Thus alternatives to bespoke systems were: new packaged systems are customised to meet the firm’s specific needs; no or only minor customisation of packaged systems (Hall, 1992; Pollard and Hayne, 1998, and all four cases).

## Proposition 6

IT leaders employ someone within the firm with formal IT training as an IT specialist. The case firms had taken different approaches to providing internal IT expertise so that their IT best met the needs of the firm. Different approaches were: employ someone with formal IT training; appoint an existing manager to the role of internal IT expert; appoint an existing employee without significant prior IT training as the internal IT expert (Yap et al, 1992; Cortada, 1998; Pollard and Hayne, 1998, and all four cases).

## Proposition 7

New IT knowledge and ideas are sought and valued within IT leaders. The cases showed that some small firms actively seek new ways to use IT. It is envisaged that some small firms would devote little time to these types of activity (Cortada, 1998; Pollard and Hayne, 1998; Galliers and Sutherland, 1999, and all four cases).

## Proposition 8

Senior managers within IT leaders actively lead IT. IT was a topic of significant discussion by managers in some of the case firms, at frequent informal and formal discussions. It was envisaged that such active involvement would not be common in other small firms (Hall, 1992; Yap et al, 1992; Storey, 1994; Galliers and Sutherland, 1999, and all four cases).

## Proposition 9

IT leaders have at least one person with hands-on IT development skills. Some of the case firms had people with hands-on development skills. This allowed them to fix some problems and also to build small systems that were specific to their needs. This is relatively new to small firms. Many others have no person with hands-on IT development skills (Yap et al, 1992; Cortada, 1998; Pollard and Hayne, 1998; Galliers and Sutherland, 1999, and all four cases).

## Proposition 10

IT leaders possess considerable IT project management experience. All the case firms had gained considerable project management experience over the years. Some of this related to IT projects as three firms had over 10 years with IT, and managers in the other had significant experience at another firm. These firms with project management experience seemed more likely to be able to manage IT projects well and reap benefits from such projects, compared with firms that had little IT project management experience (Pollard and Hayne, 1998, and all four cases).

## Proposition 11

Important core values influence IT within IT leaders. The managers in the case firms showed real commitment to values like customer service, continuous improvement, and quality. This style of management benefited IS as these ideas were reflected in IT decisions by applying these shared values to the IT function. Also, IT was used to support such shared values. It seems likely that in many small firms, such core values do not exist or fail to influence decisions about IT (Hall, 1992; Storey, 1994; Cortada, 1998; Galliers and Sutherland, 1999, and all four cases).

## Stage 2: the practices of IT leaders and IT laggards

Stage 2 of the study tested the 11 propositions with the objective of identifying practices that differentiated IT leaders from IT laggards, and were thus likely to be useful for benchmarking. Thirty engineering firms were interviewed in Stage 2. All firms were in the metal sector, independent (rather than being a subsidiary of a larger firm) and had between 20 and 100 staff. The 30 firms were not a random sample of all small engineering firms in the region. Instead, the study used an existing database to identify 15 firms that were likely to be leaders and 15 firms that were likely to be laggards. The small firm database resulted from a prior study of small firms in North East England using the PILOT instrument (Prabhu and Yarrow, 1998). However, as PILOT included no questions related to IT, the database could only be used to identify firms based on operational performance and non-IT practices (eg, innovation, product development, and organisation and culture). Thus, the PILOT database was used to identify one set of 15 firms that had previously been very high on both practices and performance, and another set of 15 that had been low on both practices and performance. Furthermore, much of the PILOT data was 3 years old. Thus, the database could not be relied upon to present the current state of some firms. This prevented the study from examining leaders and laggards based solely on PILOT data. However, the PILOT data were considered sufficiently indicative of likely leaders and laggards for the study to proceed, assuming that recent evidence was gathered.

## Data collection

Most of the evidence was gathered by recorded interviews with a senior manager who took some responsibility for IT. The interviews were based on open-ended questions for all of the 11 propositions (see Appendix 4). In addition, each manager completed a short questionnaire to gather data on the dependent variable. Although the same person was used to provide data for both the independent and the dependent variables, the data were gathered using separate and thus independent methods, ie, open-ended questions for the independent variables, and a questionnaire for the dependent variable.

The significant part of the self-report questionnaire is shown in Appendix 3 as a set of eight items that were used to measure ‘IT impact’, ie, the impact of an information system on the performance of the business. The eight items came from two overlapping instruments that have been validated in small firms by Thong et al (1996) and King et al (2000), with reliability coefficients of 0.82 and 0.87, respectively. The eight items covered the impact of IT during the last 3 years on: costs, company image, staff productivity, competing, quality of decision making, sales revenue, profitability, and internal integration (Appendix 3). The scale was from 1 to +3. This untypical approach was in response to feedback when conducting the case interviews. Interviewees found that it made more sense for the low end of the scale to be a negative value as it indicated a negative impact. The responses for the eight items were summed, giving a possible maximum score of 24 and a minimum of 8. The mean firm score was 11.93. Scores ranged from 2 to 19, with a standard deviation of 4.71. The high variability was expected as the sample had been selected in a non-random way, ie, as a mix of previously high and low performing firms. The frequency distribution of IT impact scores was bimodal, with peaks at scores of about 16 and 8. These data implied two sets of firms, one set of firms with high IT impact and another set with low IT impact. The sample of 30 was therefore split at the median into two groups of 15 firms based on IT impact. The 15 firms with the highest ‘total IT impact scores were considered ‘IT leaders’, and the lowest 15 were considered as ‘IT laggards’.

A series of open-ended questions were used to gather evidence for the independent variables associated with each proposition. This evidence was used to classify each firm for each proposition. Some of the propositions were relatively easy to examine. For example, proposition 2 sought evidence on who took responsibility for IT.

Similarly, it was relatively easy to classify firms for propositions 3, 5, 6, 9, and 10. Classifications for the other propositions were based on a number of indicators. For example, in determining whether firms viewed IT as strategic or operational (proposition 1), each manager was asked about the firm’s major strengths and how IT contributed to these strengths. For proposition 1, firms were classified as strategic when IT’s contribution to the firm was high. A firm was classified as operational when there was evidence of IT concentrated in one part of the firm, IT was viewed as a necessary evil, and there was little expenditure on IT. A similar approach was adopted for propositions 4, 7, 8, and 11. Further details are provided in Appendix 4.

Very few firms delegated the responsibility for IT projects to an external expert (Table 6). Most firms preferred to manage IT projects themselves and carry out their own investigations.

Most firms invested much time and effort into IT projects (Table 7), including examining what systems were available and evaluation of options by senior managers.

Many leaders either went for bespoke systems or significantly customised packaged systems (Table 8). Having systems tailored to the needs of the firm was associated with IT success.

Leading firms had a greater tendency to employ someone with formal IT training (Table 9). However, many leaders did not employ an IT specialist. Firm size was a significant influence as few firms with less than 50 employees felt they could justify employing an IT specialist, but had successfully developed in-house expertise.

IT leaders were more active in seeking out IT knowledge, often through magazines, exhibitions, seminars, and talking to other firms about how they used IT (Table 10). Many managers were open to new ideas, which were often then discussed within the firm. Seeking IT knowledge was associated with IT success.

Active leadership from senior management was common in leading firms and this was associated with IT success. Many senior managers managed an IT project from initiation through to implementation, including visiting vendors and other firms (Table 11).

About half of the leaders had at least one person within the firm with hands-on development skills (Table 12). This allowed them to fix problems quickly and to customise systems. These firms wanted to be independent of IT suppliers. This was associated with IT success.

Although some managers gained IT project experience in larger firms many firms had been using IT for well over 10 years (Table 13). Thus, most firms had considerable IT project management experience.

Many firms took ‘quality’ and ‘customer service’ very seriously, often as their customers were large firms who expected high quality (Table 14). Many firms used IT to support this culture. Some firms aimed to be the best; being an IT leader was part of this strategy. Firm values influenced both new initiatives and the approach to managing IT. ‘Survival’ was a reality for some firms.

## Synthesis

Using the success rates as a guide, the above evidence indicated that the most successful tactics were reflected in propositions 1, 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9, ie, with success rates of 69%, 67%, 71%, 76%, 72%, and 73%, respectively, while propositions 2, 3, 4, 10, and 11 had success rates of 56%, 54%, 54%, 50%, and 54%, respectively. Thus, some tactics seemed clearly more successful than others, ie, to have a success rate of 67% or over, compared with others with success rates of 56% or less. The group of tactics with high success rates were then analysed further as they would seem to best differentiate leaders from laggards. These six tactics were:

P1. IT leaders viewed IT strategically (69% success).

P5. IT leaders either went for bespoke systems or significantly customised packaged systems (71% success).

P6. IT leaders had a greater tendency to employ someone with formal IT training (71% success).

P7. Managers in IT leaders were more active in seeking out IT knowledge (76% success).

P8. Active leadership from senior management was common in leading firms (72% success).

P9. Many leaders had at least one person within the firm with hands-on IT development skills (73% success).

Propositions P1, P7, and P8 reflect senior management involvement with IT. Managers of some small firms take IT very seriously. They view IT strategically, they seek out IT knowledge and they lead IT initiatives. Thus, IT leaders have managers who demonstrate significant personal commitment to IT. The cases provided evidence to indicate that this attitude to IT is reflected in greater IT success.

Propositions P5, P6, and P9 reflect technical capability in terms of systems and expertise within the firm. IT leaders are more likely to have systems that have been tailored to their needs, employ an IT specialist, and have someone with IT development skills. Thus IT leaders develop superior technical capability, and this is reflected in greater IT success.

The above discussion showed that IT leaders managed some aspects of IT differently from laggards. However, other aspects of IT were managed similarly by leaders and laggards, as reflected in the results for propositions 2–4, 10, and 11.

P2. Both leaders and laggards used a similar range of tactics regarding who was responsible for IT.

P3. Very few firms delegated the responsibility for IT projects to an external expert.

P4. Most firms invested much time and effort into IT projects.

P10. Many firms had considerable IT project management experience.

P11 Many firms took ‘quality’ and ‘customer service’ very seriously and this influenced IT decisions.

These findings indicate that IT laggards employed many good IT management practices. For example, firms were willing to invest time into IT projects (proposition 4), and system selection was managed internally (proposition 3).

## Discussion

A distinctive aspect of this study was that the second stage examined a broad range of management practices in small firms. Six practices were identified that differentiated IT leaders from IT laggards based on IT impact. Three of these practices reflected senior management involvement, while the other three reflected technical capability within the firm. Thus the study’s first major contribution is that it indicates that two factors, senior management involvement and technical capability, differentiate IT leaders from laggards. This finding has numerous implications for practice and theory as discussed later.

The senior management involvement factor sheds light on the complex nature of the concept of IT management (Mata et al, 1995). The current study shows that IT management includes managers working as a team, providing vision and leadership, and showing a willingness to explore new opportunities. Managers in firms that are IT leaders view IT in a similar way to quality, ie, as a way of doing business. Although many managers have recognised the potential of IT, it takes time for others to be convinced and for appropriate processes and systems to evolve to fit the specificity of the particular firm. Thus the case evidence supports the view of Mata et al (1995) that IT management capability could be difficult to transfer to another firm, and that it could be a source of competitive advantage.

The study found that technical capability was one significant factor that differentiated IT leaders from laggards. This is another important contribution of the study as little prior research has recognised the importance of technical capability within small firms. Thus the study has identified a new variable of significance. Important aspects of technical capability are: system customisation, specialist IT skills, and system development expertise. Many other researchers have reported aspects of technical capability, but none have indicated its significance. For example, Montazemi (1988) found a correlation between the number of systems analysts within the firm and user information satisfaction. However, Yap et al (1992) found no such relationship. The current study has indicated that technical capability is a key variable and worthy of further study.

Another major contribution of the study relates to how small firms develop their technical capability. Prior studies had indicated that external IT experts, ie, both consultants and vendors, provided an important source of expertise and guidance to small firms (Yap et al, 1992; Thong et al, 1996). However, prior research also shows that small firms are averse to consultants and reluctant to seek external help (Frank, 1993; Cragg and Zinatelli, 1995). The case firms showed a preference to rely on internal IT expertise rather than external experts. An extreme example was one firm that sent six staff, including the Managing Director, to a training course on Microsoft Access. The firm wanted to build its own system to help manage customer jobs through from initiation to completion. Thong et al (1997, p 271) argue that such low reliance on external experts ‘will result in less effective IS’, and indicated that the best scenario was where both top management support and external experts work as a team. However, firms within the current study had shown they were able to do well without engaging an IT consultant. Instead, they preferred to manage IT projects themselves, including all contact with vendors. Their success was in part due to them gaining IT knowledge over the years. Many small firms have been using computers for over 15 years, including major system upgrades. Also, the sample was of engineering firms, where many have developed good project management skills over the years. For some, each customer order can in effect be a major project that requires careful management. Furthermore, many leaders had an internal IT expert who was expected to take a significant role with IT projects. Some of these experts had their own informal, support network to turn to. Thus many of these firms have staff with good project management skills and high IT knowledge. Therefore many factors have significantly eroded the grounds for employing an external consultant. They have also significantly reduced the IT knowledge gap between them and vendors, and the need for training and ongoing support from vendors. Thus small firms have built their technical capability and made consultants and vendors much less important to them.

The study provided other indications that IT in many small firms has evolved during the last 10 years, even for laggards (Cragg and Zinatelli, 1995). Some IT tactics are now common practice across small firms as evidenced by many IT laggards as well as IT leaders employing many good IT management practices. Firms were willing to invest time into IT projects, and system selection was managed internally. This showed that small firms have learned how to manage IT through experience.

## Implications for theory

A distinctive aspect of this study was that the first stage identified a broad range of management practices in small firms. These were summarised in Table 3 based on the Seven S’s model. All of these areas are worthy of indepth study in small firms.

The study has implications for the IT content of current benchmarking tools. The current IT content has a bias towards the availability of information and thus plays down the strategic potential of IT. The current study has identified items that could be used to help benchmark IT in small firms, covering two major dimensions of senior management involvement and technical capability.

The study also has implications for research into IT success in small firms. Firstly, it confirmed the importance of senior manager involvement as an important factor. However, this study suggests that changes may be required to the instruments that have been used to measure senior management involvement in small firms. For example, the instruments used by Yap et al (1992) and Thong et al (1996) focus on the CEO’s involvement. The current study found that many non-CEOs took significant IT responsibilities, sometimes jointly with another senior manager. Focusing solely on the CEO’s involvement would have provided a very misleading picture in many firms. It will be more appropriate to refer to senior managers rather than the CEO in many populations of small firms.

Furthermore, existing CEO involvement instruments reflect the stages of the systems development life cycle. For example, Thong et al (1996) refer to the CEO’s attendance at project meetings, involvement in information requirements analysis, reviewing consultant recommendations, decision making, and monitoring the project. However, the current study indicated that IT projects were managed similarly by both leading and lagging firms. Instead, the current study indicated that the content of an instrument designed to capture senior management involvement should reflect some or all of the following types of involvement: viewing IT strategically, being enthusiastic about IT, regularly discussing IT issues, sharing responsibility for IT projects, researching new uses for IT, and seeking new IT knowledge. A focus on systems projects may fail to adequately reflect the proactive leadership role that many senior managers now take.

The current study has another implication for studies of IT success in small firms as it identified technical capability as an important factor. IT leaders were found to be more likely to have systems that have been tailored to their needs, employ an IT specialist, and have someone with IT development skills. Thus IT leaders develop superior systems and superior IT staff capability, and this was reflected in greater IT success. This is a new factor for researchers as it has not featured in recent studies of IT success in small firms. Subsequent research could aim to confirm the importance of this success factor and explain how small firms can best build and exploit such resources.

The reported low reliance on consultants and vendors questions the inclusion of external IT experts within studies of IT success. It seems likely that external IT expertise is still a success factor and that Thong et al (1997) are correct in that projects are more likely to succeed in the presence of high external expertise. However, the general reluctance to employ consultants, and the move towards employing internal IT experts, suggests that future studies may find less impact on IT success from vendor support and consultant expertise. Alternatively, future studies may need to refine instruments that measure external IT expertise to reflect new roles. For example, small firms still rely on vendors for software provision and some customisation, but less so for training and support. Furthermore, assistance from consultants may only be present in sufficient quantity for research when it is provided free or substantially subsidised, as was the case in Singapore (Soh et al, 1992; Thong et al, 1996).

The current study provided evidence that IT sophistication in small firms has improved over the years. Raymond and Pare´ (1992) modelled IT sophistication as two dimensions: IT usage and IT management. Further research could aim to confirm these two dimensions and their significance to IT success. In addition, further research could examine the interaction of these two dimensions. For example, do IT practices vary across the competitive scenarios of Levy et al (1998)? For example, do successful ‘efficiency’ firms manage IT differently from successful ‘innovation’ firms?

## Implications for practice

The study has implications for managers of small firms as the major findings indicate that firms that wish to be IT leaders must have senior managers who take IT seriously. These senior managers take responsibility for IT, are enthusiastic about IT, and seek to explore new opportunities for IT as part of business strategy. Senior managers must also be willing to invest in people as well as technology to enhance internal IT skills and expertise.

Thus, they may employ an IT expert and use these skills to help develop systems that meet the firm’s needs.

The study also confirmed that there is no best way for small firms to manage IT. For example, some leading firms employed an IT specialist while others did not. Similarly, some leading firms had managers who shared responsibility for IT while in others IT was the responsibility of one manager, who was supported by peers. Thus managers have to pick a set of tactics that suit their firm’s needs.

The findings also have implications for policies aimed at supporting small firms. Some small firms are already IT leaders, while some are laggards. It seems likely that some laggards are so far behind in their thinking and attitude to IT that they will never catch the leaders. This has implications for policy as any support may need to target certain types of small firm. For example, a policy that encouraged small firms to employ an internal IT expert is most likely to benefit laggards. However, such an individual could struggle to make a difference in the absence of senior management involvement.

## Limitations

Voss et al (1998) and Sohal et al (1999) found that practices varied by firm size, industry, and country. Thus the leading practices identified by this study may only apply to firms in the metal sector and to independent firms with between 20 and 100 employees. Other sectors, particularly service firms, may be much more sophisticated in their use and management of IT (DTI, 2000). Also, world class standards are dynamic; therefore, it must be recognised that best practices may be temporary (Cortada, 1998). In time, some best practices will become standard practices as other superior practices emerge. Furthermore, different firms have different IT strategies (Levy et al, 1998). Thus some leading practices may make most sense only to those firms that wish to use IT strategically. Further research could examine if practices vary by type of firm and, for example, by IT strategy.

As well as limitations due to the sample of firms used in this study, the research approach also had limitations. The study had to make the most of a relatively weak prior literature on the IT management practices in small firms. Much of the evidence relied on data from one individual within each firm. Importantly, the initial analysis used a grounded approach based on the Seven S’s of Pascale and Athos (1982). Another lens and researcher combination could have reached different conclusions. Also, the research used the Seven S’s to take a broad look at IT practices. This broad perspective may have missed something important that may only be revealed from the intense study of one or more parts of the Seven S’s. Furthermore, the second part of the study selected those IT practices that seemed most likely to differentiate IT leaders from IT laggards. It therefore limited its scope to a subset of all possible IT practices. Another study could examine a broader range of practices. Finally, the second part of the study used a set of open-ended, interview questions to gather data for each of the propositions. Another study could adopt a more rigorous approach to data collection, based on some of the indicators used in the current study.

The major data collection effort involved only 30 firms. As a result, simple correlation techniques were used to identify which IT management practices were successful. Thus further research with larger samples is needed to confirm this study’s major findings. Techniques like factor analysis could then be used to confirm that senior management involvement and technical capability were two factors.

## Conclusions

This study found that many of the self-assessment tools used to benchmark small firms omit IT practices. The impact on small manufacturers of modern processes such as e.business suggests that good IT will be an essential enabling resource. Thus this study focused on the IT practices of small firms with the aim of providing appropriate items to benchmark IT.

The first stage of the study used existing literature and four case firms to identify significant IT practices. The second stage of the study evaluated 11 research propositions to identify six practices that differentiated IT leaders from IT laggards, based on IT impact. Three of these six practices reflected senior management involvement and the other three reflected technological capability. These items could be incorporated into benchmarking tools for small firms.

The study is the first to report the IT practices of leading small firms. Evidence showed that many firms have senior managers who demonstrate significant personal commitment to IT, and this was reflected in greater IT success. The other significant influence on IT success was technical capability, which has received very little attention in prior research. Many small firms have built their in-house IT expertise and use this to customise their systems. Firms showed a preference to develop and rely on their internal IT expertise rather than use external experts.

## Appendix 1: The Seven S’s

## (Pascale and Athos, 1982, p 81)

Strategy: Plan or course of action leading to the allocation of a firm’s scarce resources, over time, to reach identified goals.

Structure: Characterisation of the organisation chart (ie, functional, decentralised, etc).

Systems: Procedural reports and routine processes such as meeting formats.

Staff: ‘Demographic’ description of important personnel categories within the firm (ie, engineers, entrepreneurs, MBAs, etc). ‘Staff ’ is not meant in line-staff terms.

Style: Characterisation of how key managers behave in achieving the organisation’s goals; also the cultural style of the organisation.

Skills: Distinctive capabilities of key personnel or the firm as a whole.

Super-ordinate goals: The significant meanings or guiding concepts that an organisation imbues in its members.

## Appendix 2: The six frameworks used to identify IT practices

## Hall (1992)

Hall proposed six ‘hallmarks’ for business success after examining a group of 30 successful firms. The hallmarks are presented as features that differentiate the seven ‘fast-growth’ (over 100 employees within 10 years since formation) companies from the other successful companies. The hallmarks are offered as the ‘principles for success for fast growth’ (p 12). The six hallmarks are:

Focus/direction: Fashioning and managing the overall focus and direction of the business.

Customerising: Continually delighting customers.

Partnering: Working in partnership with people who affect the business.

Personality: The character of the business.

Quality: A commitment to providing product quality and customer service.

Systems: Establishing systems to provide information empowers people to make key decisions.

## Storey (1994)

Storey (1994) examined many years of research to identify three main factors that significantly contributed towards small firm growth. His factors were:

The background/resources of the entrepreneur (including motivation and education).

Firm characteristics (eg, having more than a single owner, and having owners in middle age).

Strategy (eg, a willingness to share ownership, the ability to identify niches, the introduction of new products, and the ability to create teams of managers).

## Pollard and Hayne (1998)

They used a two-round modified Delphi approach with a broad range of firms to identify the following top 10 key IT issues facing small firms:

Using IS for competitive advantage.

Improving IS project management practices.

Improving effectiveness of software development.

Building a responsive IT infrastructure.

Aligning the IS organisation enterprise.

Coping with degree and rate of technological change.

Planning and managing communication networks.

Facilitating and managing business process redesign.

Educating the user.

Recruiting and developing IS human resources.

## Yap et al (1992)

Their study of user information satisfaction included a broad range of small firms. The following factors were significantly correlated with user information satisfaction (IS success):

Consultant effectiveness (experience, capability, effectiveness during feasibility study).

Vendor support (for hardware/software, amount of and quality of training, adequacy of documentation).

CBIS experience (years).

Sufficiency of financial resources (amount allocated to CBIS implementation).

CEO support (support of CEO, attendance at meetings, involvement in feasibility study and decision making).

User participation (attendance at meetings, participation in feasibility study and decision making).

## Cortada (1998)

Cortada has indicated the following IT best practices based on his own personal work as a consultant, research undertaken by colleagues at IBM, and research by others:

Align IT with business strategy through continuous IT and business planning, earmarking funds for strategic projects, and involvement from many.

Select IT initiatives that will reap value to the business, through taking a customer focus, involving many users, and using IT to do things differently.

Monitor IT initiatives based on strategic objectives through rigorous project control techniques.

Frequently experiment and innovate, which includes searching for IT applications that do things easier, faster, cheaper, and provide competitive advantage.

Apply continuous improvement techniques through measuring the performance of processes, analysing problems, setting goals, and going for them.

Invest in employees through training and education. Take the customers’ views of service seriously.

## Galliers and Sutherland (1999)

This paper adopted the Seven S’s to propose a revised stages of growth model for IS. Their model indicated seven stages of growth and outlined the characteristics of each based for each of the Seven S’s. The descriptions for the most mature stage are outlined below:

Strategy (maintain comparative strategic advantage, monitor futures, interactive planning).

Structure (centrally coordinated coalitions).

Systems (inter-organisational systems, new IS based products, external/internal data integration).

Staff (IS director member of board of directors).

Style (business team).

Skills (all senior management understand IS and its potentialities).

Superordinate goals (interactive planning).

## Appendix 4: The open-ended questions used in stage 2 for propositions 1, 4, 7, 8, and 11

Proposition 1: whether firms view IT as strategic or operational. Questions used included: What are the firm’s major strengths? How does the firm compete with others? How does IT contribute? What sort of an influence have computers had on the success of the firm? How important is computing to the firm? What role do computers play in new initiatives by the company? Indicators of IT as strategic were: evidence of IT planning by senior managers, IT planning as part of business planning, major investment in IT infrastructure, IT projects designed to provide competitive advantage/reap value to the business, seeking to use IT innovatively, clear links between firm objectives and IT initiatives, clear links between the use of IT and important objectives for the firm, eg, customer satisfaction and quality, IT viewed as crucial to the firm’s success, managers regularly scanning their environment for IT initiatives. Indicators of IT as operational were: evidence of IT concentrated in one part of the firm, many managers ignoring IT, IT viewed as a necessary evil, little expenditure on IT.

Proposition 4 on IT project management. Questions used included: How were major IT decisions reached? Who was involved? In what ways? Indicators of active management were: evidence of time devoted to explore and agree on requirements, thorough evaluation of alternatives, clear focus and direction from senior managers, planned and controlled implementation, project led by at least one senior manager. Poor project management was evidenced by little time devoted to requirements analysis, few options explored, support from probably only one senior manager, and no project plan.

Proposition 7 about seeking new IT knowledge. Questions included: How does the firm try to keep knowledgeable about computers and new developments? How does the firm learn about IT? Indicators of high evidence of highly active firms included: senior managers seeking ideas through reading IT magazines or attending seminars, also other staff receiving IT training and education. Indicators of low activity included: little attempt by managers to keep up with IT, eg, by little reading, education or training.

Proposition 8 about IT leadership. Questions included: How is planning undertaken for new computer initiatives? (explore how business planning is carried out and how IT planning is carried out). Indicators of active leadership included: frequent informal and formal discussion of IT issues amongst the senior management team, senior managers seeking new ideas about IT, senior managers recognising IT’s strategic potential, senior managers being excited about IT, senior managers leading IT projects, senior managers supporting others who have significant IT responsibilities. Indicators of inactive leadership included: managers taking little interest in IT, IT issues rarely being discussed amongst senior managers unless in an emergency, one manager possibly having taken on the role of IT leader and other managers leaving IT to that manager.

Proposition 11 about shared values. Questions included: Does the firm have any guiding principles or goals? (cheapest, quality, customer service, empowerment, best in the world, easiest to do business with). In what ways does this philosophy influence decisions about IT? The questions were seeking evidence of the firm having important core value, and these values significantly influencing IT decisions, eg, seeking to use IT to support customer service.
