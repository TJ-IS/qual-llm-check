# The development of two tools for measuring the easiness and usefulness of transactional Web sites

AM Aladwani

Department of QM and IS, College of Administrative Sciences, Kuwait University, Edailiyah, Kuwait

The worldwide diffusion of Electronic Commerce shifts the exchange relationship between buyers and sellers from the face-to-face model to the face-to-screen model; and it is, therefore, important for organisations to consider the Web attributes that attract users. Yet, conceptualising, empirically testing, and refining tools for measuring the easiness and usefulness of transactional Web sites are lacking. The present study tries to fill this gap in the literature by describing the development of two tools for measuring perceived easiness and usefulness in a Web context. The findings of exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses reveal that the two instruments demonstrate sound measurement properties and would be useful to organisations interested in setting up an electronic business, and to scholars interested in Web research. European Journal of Information Systems (2002) 11, 223–234. doi:10.1057/palgrave.ejis.3000432

## Introduction

The electronic commerce literature is replete with stories about organisations from all over the world and from various types and sizes integrating Web technologies with their businesses (Westland & Clark, 1999; Brynjolfsson & Kahin, 2000; Currie, 2000; Turban, 2000; Peter& Olson, 2001). With the help of Web technologies and applications, an organisation can provide its customers with not only general information about its operations, but also the chance to perform and close online business transactions. Organisations are becoming aware of the potentialities of Web technologies for their work, hence, spending large amounts of money on these applications. However, the current Internet revolution, fueled by the worldwide diffusion of Web technologies and their applications, has presented an intriguing predicament for most organisations. Most organisations are asking how should we attract Web users to our Web site, prompt their interest in our Web, and close successful transactions with them?

Perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness are probably two of the most widely cited correlates of technology acceptance and usage. Ease of use and usefulness are primary issues in the context of the Web, inasmuch as it is easiness and usefulness that inspire Web users to enjoy visiting and shopping from Web sites (Gefen 2000a; Aladwani, 2001; Moon & Kim, 2001). Hence, the answer to the above posed question could be partially answered by encouraging organisations to develop easy to use and potentially useful Web sites. As the exchange relationship between buyers and sellers shifts from the face-to-face model to the face-to-screen model, it is important for organisations to consider the Web attributes that attract Web users. However, Web site easiness and Web site usefulness are complex concepts; thus, their measurement is neither simple nor straightforward. The problem is exacerbated by the lack of agreement among researchers concerning how best to conceptualise Web site easiness and Web site usefulness and by the paucity of systematic and empirically derived tools for measuring the two concepts. Today no multi-item scales are available to measure users’ perceived Web site easiness and Web site usefulness focusing on the steps involved in a Web transaction. The provision of such scales will further enhance management’s ability to exploit the full potential of the Internet.

The goal of the present investigation is to develop two multi-item instruments to measure perceived easiness and usefulness of transactional Web site from the perspective of Web users. The proposed instruments would be valuable to researchers and practitioners interested in designing, implementing, and managing Web sites. The rest of the paper is organised in the following manner. The next section reviews past research relevant to perceived ease of use and usefulness in information technology and more specifically, Web site easiness and Web site usefulness. The following section and its various subsections describe the measurement and the various steps involved in scale development. The last section discusses applications of the proposed scales.

## Existing instruments of perceived easiness and usefulness

The interest in perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness is well documented in the literature, and there is ample evidence of their increasing importance. Information technology researchers have always been eager to understand what leads to the acceptance of information technology. Therefore, they have examined the relationship between perceived ease of use and usefulness and the acceptance of Personal Computers (Igbaria et al, 1997), CASE tools (Premkumar & Potter, 1995, Group Support Systems (Chin & Gopal, 1995), Expert Systems (Keil et al, 1995), End-user Applications (Chau, 1996), Windows OS (Karahanna et al, 1999), Workstations (Lucas & Spitler, 2000), and MRP II systems (Gefen, 2000b), to name a few. More recently, there have been several empirical investigations examining perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness in the context of Web technologies and applications (Gefen & Straub, 1997; Karahanna & Straub, 1999; Teo et al, 1999; Gefen & Straub, 2000; Tan & Teo, 2000; Aladwani, 2001; Moon & Kim, 2001). However, the easiness and usefulness of transactional Web sites remain vastly undefined concepts.

The literature has paid little attention to conceptualising Web site easiness and Web site usefulness in general. For example, Aladwani (2001) used single-item scales to tap Web site easiness and usefulness and to examine the importance of the two variables for the willingness of potential customers to use Internet banking. The author found that the studied banks rank service easiness was the second most important driving force of Internet banking. Moon and Kim (2001) developed two nine-item scales to measure perceived easiness and usefulness of the Web. The authors used items like ‘Learning to operate WWW is easy for me,’ ‘My interaction with WWW is clear and understandable,’ ‘Using WWW enables me to access a lot of information,’ and ‘Using WWW enables me to access the newest information’. The authors found that both easiness and usefulness predict behavioural intention to use the Web. Gefen and Straub (2000) used a six-item scale and a five-item scale from Davis (1989) to measure Web site easiness and Web site usefulness, respectively. The authors used items like ‘ABC is easy-to-use,’ ‘It is easy to become skillful at using ABC,’ and ‘ABC enhances my effectiveness in book searching and buying’. They found that Web site easiness has no effect on intended purchase.

Anandarajan, Simmers and Igbaria (2000) developed a two-item scale to measure Internet easiness (‘The internet is user friendly’ and ‘The internet is easy to use’) and a seven-item scale to measure Internet usefulness. The authors found no relationship between easiness and time and frequency of Internet usage and between usefulness and frequency of use. Furthermore, they found no relationship between the two constructs and Internet attitudes. Tan and Teo (2000) adopted the three-item complexity (ie ease of use) scale and the six-item relative advantage scale (ie usefulness) that were developed by Moore and Benbasat (1991) to measure Web site easiness and usefulness, and to examine their impact on Internet banking adoption. The authors found that easiness has no effect on attitudes. Karahanna and Straub (1999) used a single-item scale to measure e-mail ease of use, and a two-item scale for e-mail usefulness. They found no support for the original predictions of the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM). Teo, Lim and Lai (1999) borrowed items from Davis (1989) to develop a scale for perceived Internet easiness and usefulness. Unlike Karahanna and Straub, Teo et al found support for TAM’s predictions. Hu, Chau, Sheng and Tam (1999) developed two six-item scales based on the work of Davis (1989) to measure telemedicine easiness and usefulness, respectively. The authors used items like ‘I would find telemedicine technology easy to use’ and ‘using telemedicine can enable me to complete patient care more quickly’. They found that easiness has no effect on either usefulness nor attitudes.

Gefen and Straub (1997) adopted Davis’s (1989) perceived easiness and usefulness scales to measure e-mail ease of use and usefulness and to explore their effect on e-mail acceptance. The authors found no significant relationships between easiness and usefulness and usage. Segars and Grover (1993) replicated Davis’s (1989) study using e-mail and v-mail samples and found that the original theory had unsatisfactory model-data fit. Adams, Nelson and Todd (1992) conducted a similar investigation and reached the same conclusion.

Past research, although significant, suffers from at least two problems. First, there is a great deal of variability in how Web site easiness and Web site usefulness are conceptualised and operationalised. Moon and Kim (2001), for example, measured the ease and usefulness of browsing the Internet using two nine-item scales. Aladwani (2001) measured the ease and usefulness of Internet banking using two single-item scales. Hu et al (1999) measured the ease and usefulness of telemedicine technology using two six-item scales. Karahanna and Straub (1999) measured easiness using a single-item scale, and usefulness using a two-item scale. Variability in definitions, as reflected by the variable number and nature of indicators tapping the two constructs in the different studies, may confound the efforts of organisations to evaluate and improve their Web sites. In every study, the items of Davis’s instruments had to be altered drastically to suit the new context. Instead of taking an instrument and trying to fit it to the new context, a better approach would be to develop valid and reliable easiness and usefulness instruments within the specific context of transactional Web sites.

Second, most of the scales used by past research represent adaptations of the perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness constructs as defined and measured by Davis (1989) in the context of computer, not Web, technology. Although Davis’s instrument is extremely important and does have its advantages in being applicable to a wide range of organisational contexts, its transportability across technologies does not have strong support from the literature. For example, Hu, Chau, Sheng and Tam (1999), after failing to find a significant relationship between ease of use and usefulness and attitudes towards telemedicine technology, state that ‘This might reflect limitations of TAM’s applicability with respect to technologies. . .’ (p. 105). In a similar vein, Segars and Grover (1993) affirm that there is ‘no absolute measures for these constructs (easiness and usefulness) exist across varying technological and organisational contexts. . .’ (p. 525). The performance of Davis’s easiness and usefulness instruments has generally been below expectations when it comes to predicting other variables, eg behavioural intention and acceptance, in a Web context. Eighty percent of studies that were reviewed above (excluding the study by Aladwani who did not carry out formal hypothesis testing procedures) failed to support the original predictions of TAM. Consequently, there may be a need for a new set of instruments developed to suit the context of transactional Web sites.

The previous discussion highlights the fact that the easiness and usefulness of transactional Web sites lack a clear definition and their measurement is still in its infancy. Despite the importance of easiness and usefulness in Web acceptance research, no comprehensive instrument, to measure both constructs in the context of transactional Web sites existed.

## Instruments development and validation

Information systems researchers have developed several instruments (eg Doll & Torkzadeb, 1988; Webster & Martocchio, 1992) following frameworks developed by well-known psychometric researchers (eg Churchill, 1979; Babbie, 1992). The number of steps involved in scale development as suggested by these frameworks varies. However, three generic steps are common in all scale development models (Aladwani, 1996):

1 Conceptualisation, which focuses on content validity and involves such activities as defining the construct of interest and generating a candidate list of items from the domain of all possible items representing the construct.

2 Design, which focuses on construct validity and reliability analysis. It pertains to the process of refining the sample of items from the previous step to come up with an initial scale, deciding on such operational issues as question types and question sequence, and testing the initial scale that has been developed in the conceptualisation stage.

3 Normalisation, which focuses on the effort to normalise the developed scale. It involves the important steps of subsequent independent verification and validation. Unfortunately, this step is omitted in many scale development efforts.

In the conduct of these three scale development steps, several analytical techniques can be used, such as factor analysis and reliability analysis, as it will be described in detail in the following paragraphs.

## Conceptualisation: delimiting the domain

Conceptualisation involves defining the construct of interest and generating a sample of items representing the concept under consideration. To delimit the domains of perceived easiness and usefulness of transactional Web sites, it would be helpful to understand the consumer purchasing process and the steps involved in Web purchasing. Consumers, whomever they may be, tend to follow the same purchasing steps (Hawkins et al, 2000; Schiffman & Kanuk, 2000; Peter & Olson, 2001): need awareness, information search, and product/service evaluation and purchase. Need awareness is the first stage in the consumer purchasing process, the one on which all other stages depend. It can be internally generated by the difference between the desired state and the actual condition, or externally stimulated by the marketer through advertising. After need awareness, the second consumer purchasing stage is information search, the process through which the consumer actively looks for, and collects facts about different product/service and supplier options. A successful information search activity leaves the consumer with possible sources (ie alternative product/service and supplier options) to fulfill his or her recognised needs. Next comes the process of product/service evaluation and purchase, which entails: (1) establishing criteria for the measurement of the ideas generated for alternative product/service and supplier options, and (2) rating alternatives against the evaluation criteria developed earlier. It considers the consequences of each alternative as well as the likelihood of success of each (ie fulfillment rating). Having evaluated the alternatives, the consumer must then select the best alternative and perform the actual buying of the product/service.

The above model incorporates several logical and important phases, which can be adapted from the brick and mortar setting to help us understand some aspects of consumers’ purchasing behaviour over the Internet. Many of the basic processes remain the same. The fundamental difference between the traditional purchasing process and the new Web purchasing process is that the exchange relationship is now mediated by a technology interface using a PC, notebook, PDA, WAP, or other Web-enabled devices. Therefore, a well-designed Web site should address the different phases of the consumer purchasing process through built-in functions for generating interest in the Web (ease of, and usefulness for recognition), for facilitating navigation within the Web site (ease of, and usefulness for navigation), for providing relevant information to customers (ease of, and usefulness for gathering information), and for closing successful business transactions (ease of, and usefulness for purchasing).

After defining the domains of the easiness and usefulness of transactional Web sites, it is now possible to generate sample items representing the two concepts under consideration and covering their domains. In order to ensure content validity, the instrument needs to draw representative items from a universal pool (Cronbach, 1971). Based on an extensive review of the literature, a questionnaire was developed consisting of 64 items, 32 of which dealt with Web site easiness and a similar number of items dealt with Web site usefulness, covering the four Web purchasing steps as identified in the previous paragraph. During the review and item creation processes, an attempt was made to screen the pool first hand for duplicate and ambiguous items. This initial quality control process proved to be helpful in increasing content validity of the scales. After finishing from constructing the pool of 64 items, two information systems researchers were asked to examine the items written for each construct and its dimensions and make any changes deemed necessary. The two experts made no recommendations to add/delete items, but suggested minor wording changes for some of the items. ## Design: construct validity and reliability

The scale design phase focuses on construct validity and reliability, operational issues investigating whether the scales chosen are true constructs describing the event or merely artifacts of the methodology itself (Campbell & Fiske, 1959; Cronbach, 1971). The process started by arranging the selected items in a questionnaire format in preparation for data collection. The items were arranged in random order to reduce bias. The response options for the question items, anchored on a five-point Likert scale, range from (1) strongly disagree to; (5) strongly agree. The respondents to the questionnaire were students enrolled in 11 different sections of an introductory information systems class at a business school.

The researcher followed the same procedure in the 11 data gathering sessions, which took place in the same Internet–connected computer lab. All personal computers in the lab had similar hardware configurations, operating systems, and Internet browsers. Before administering the questionnaire, the participants were asked to read a brief overview of the study, and instructed them to search for, access, and navigate through the Web site of a well-known bookstore for some time. No time limits were imposed, but in every session the students completed the exploration task within 10 min from the start. The exploration task is deemed important for the success of the experiment to eliminate possible confounding effects resulting from the fact that some participants may already be familiar with the Web site and would be more likely to find it easy and useful than others who have never visited an online bookstore before. The researcher then administered the Web site easiness and Web site usefulness instruments to the participants and instructed them to search for and request any MS Office book that can be used for the same course they are enrolled in, but to stop before actually paying for it.

For all sessions the author was present to answer questions and provide any necessary assistance. Three hundred and ninety-five Web users enrolled in 11 different sections of an introductory information systems class at a business school that participated in this study; and usable responses were collected from three hundred and eightyseven. No rewards were offered for students’ participation in the study. Of the students that participated, approximately 69% were females and 31% were males. The average age of the respondents was 19.7 years. Of the 387 respondents, a majority was business majors. Twenty-four percent (24%) of the participants describe themselves as having some Internet knowledge, 45% say they have moderate knowledge of Internet, and 31% of the participants say they have advanced knowledge of the Internet.

The sample of 387 Web users was first split randomly into two groups. The first split sample was used for initial evaluation of the scales (the design phase), whereas the holdout sample was used for re-testing the revised scales (the normalisation phase). Using the first split sample of 193 Web users, two factor analysis procedures were conducted: one for the Web site easiness instrument and the other for the usefulness instrument. First, the 32 items representing Web site easiness were factor analysed to explore the dimensionality of the construct. An iterative procedure was followed, that began with submitting the items to a factor analysis procedure with varimax rotation. There are no accepted ‘absolute’ standards for the cutoffs; the choice is based on judgment, purpose of the study, and prior studies. Since the goal is to examine the most significant loadings in interpreting the factor solution, a cut-off point of 0.70 for item loadings and eigenvalue of one were used. After the first iteration items’ loadings were examined and items that did not meet the loading cut-off, or loaded on more than one factor were eliminated. The remaining items were then resubmitted to another round of factor analysis. The process went on until a meaningful factor structure was reached. At the end of the exploratory factor analysis procedure, four factors with eigenvalue of one or greater and 12 items remained (Table 2).

## Normalisation: verification and validation

To re-test the Web site easiness and Web site usefulness models, a series of tests using the holdout sample (194) were conducted. Reliability levels of the scales were first examined. Reliability scores were 0.90, 0.84, 0.91, and 0.88, for ease of recognition, ease of navigation, ease of gathering information, and ease of purchasing, respectively. The overall reliability of the 12-item easiness scale was 0.90. Reliability scores for usefulness for navigation, usefulness for gathering information, and usefulness for purchasing were 0.82, 0.84, and 0.88, respectively. The overall reliability of the 10-item usefulness scale was 0.87. These scores are above the reliability cut-off points suggested by Nunnally (1978).

Finally, the overall validity of the Web site easiness and Web site usefulness instruments was further examined by conducting a confirmatory factor analysis using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), also known as latent variable analysis. In SEM, one must develop a model based on priori information about the data structure (Anderson & Gerbing, 1988). Since the factor structures of the two instruments have already been validated, the instruments were assessed in a confirmatory mode. Confirmatory factor analysis is a statistical technique used to test a hypothesis regarding the multi-dimensionality of a theoretical construct. The full data set was used, and multiple fit criteria to test the hypotheses were employed. The test of the overall validity of the fourdimension model for Web site easiness produced the following results: $\chi ^ { 2 } / \mathrm { d f } = 3 . 7 8$ Goodness of fit index $( \mathrm { G F I } ) = 0 . 9 2 5 .$ Adjusted goodness of fit index (AGFI) = 0.885, Comparative fit index (CFI) = 0.941, and Root mean square residual (RMSR) =0.048. The SEM analysis for the three-factor model for Web site usefulness produced the following fit values: $\chi ^ { 2 \% }$ of freedom = 1.34, Goodness of fit index $( \mathrm { G F I } ) = 0 . 9 7 9 .$ Adjusted goodness of fit index $( \mathrm { A G F I } ) = 0 . 9 6 3$ , Comparative fit index $( \mathrm { C F I } ) = 0 . 9 9 4$ , and Root mean square residual (RMSR) = 0.029. These values met the acceptable levels suggested by the literature indicating the models fit was appropriate. ## 4. Conclusions

The present investigation empirically evaluated and verified two multi-dimensional instruments for measuring perceived easiness and usefulness of transactional Web sites. The Web site easiness instrument consists of 12 items and has four dimensions: ease of recognition (2 items), ease of navigation (3 items), ease of gathering information (4 items), and ease of purchasing (3 items). The Web site usefulness instrument on the other hand consists of 10 items and has three dimensions: usefulness for navigation (3 items), usefulness for gathering information (4 items), and usefulness for purchasing (3 items). The findings of an extensive development and validation process using multiple statistical techniques including exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses provided evidence for the excellent psychometric properties of the proposed instruments.

There are several points of caution that warrant a mention before discussing the implications of the findings. One potential limitation of the present study is that a convenience sample was used in the development and validation of the instruments. The student subjects used in the present study may not be representative of the population of potential buyers in general. Another limitation is that the results cannot be generalised to all types of Web sites. The study focused only on online bookstores. Therefore, careful use of the results should be made, especially as to their applicability to other transactional Web sites. Yet another possible limitation of this study is that the perceptions of Web site easiness and usefulness may change over time and the present investigation does not take this fact into account.

The extensive testing and refinement procedures provide evidence of the validity of the two instruments. Nevertheless, instruments are always subject to further improvement and fellow researchers are encouraged to do so. Several research endeavours may be based on this work. One is to continue to refine the models and instruments using a wider sample of potential buyers. It is possible to conduct a detailed examination of each dimension of the Web site easiness and usefulness instruments and perhaps even develop a detailed instrument for the dimension of interest. It may turn out that some dimensions are more meaningful for certain Web sites, organisations or industries, and further insights into these may be of interest. Another area for research would be to develop an elaborate model to investigate the nature of the relationship between Web site easiness and/or Web site usefulness and users’ actual buying behaviours. The corroboration of a positive relationship between the two should encourage higher investment into improving Web site easiness and usefulness. Yet another opportunity for future research would be to examine the perceptions of Web site easiness and usefulness over time through longitudinal studies.

The proposed instruments would be useful for organisations. The instruments provide important tools for assessing the easiness and usefulness of Web sites. The Internet is currently the playground for millions of Web sites varying widely in terms of easiness and usefulness. The proposed instruments and their sub-scales might be used to assess the easiness and usefulness of a given Web site. This could be carried out at the aggregate easiness/usefulness level using the full instrument, or at a specific easiness/usefulness dimension level, for example, using a scale of one of the three factors of Web site usefulness. This assessment may provide early warning signals to the organisation. In case it finds itself falling short in any of the factors, an organisation may perform a more point-by-point examination and carry out needed remedial steps. Moreover, an organisation may want to evaluate the relative importance of the easiness and usefulness factors and the specific attributes in its own working context. Although the present investigation provided their relative importance based on its own sample, each organisation is peculiar based on its operations, management practices, customers, etc. The same analysis would facilitate considering the identified dimensions and attributes explicitly in Web site planning, analysis, development, and implementation.
