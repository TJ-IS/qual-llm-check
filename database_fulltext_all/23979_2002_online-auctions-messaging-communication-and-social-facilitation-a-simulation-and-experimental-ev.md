# Online auctions, messaging, communication and social facilitation: a simulation and experimental evidence

S Rafaeli and A Noy

Graduate School of Business, University of Haifa, Mt. Carmel, Haifa, 31905 Israel

This is a study of social facilitation effects in online auctions. We focus on the growth in online auctions, and the emergence of instant messaging and communication availability technologies. These two trends merge to provide a collaborative online social framework in which computer mediated communication may affect the behaviour of participants in online auctions. The interaction between buyers and sellers in traditional, face-to-face markets creates phenomena such as social facilitation, where the presence of others impacts behaviour and performance. In this study we attempt to replicate and measure social facilitation effects under the conditions of virtual presence. Does social facilitation apply to online auctions, and if so, how can it influence the design of online settings? We developed and used a simulated, Java-based Internet Dutch auction. Our findings indicate that social facilitation does indeed occur. In an experimenta examination, participants improve their results and stay longer in the auction under conditions of higher virtual presence. Participants also indicate a preference for auction arrangements with higher degrees of virtual presence. Theoretically, this study contributes to the study of social facilitation, adding evidence of the effect when the presence is virtual.

## Introduction

Auctions are social processes (Smith, 1989). This is a study of the social process of auctions, as affected by the system design of online implementations. Individual and group behaviour of participants in online arrangements has become the focus of increasing study. Much of this focus has been on the psychological aspects of participants’ cognition, choices and actions. However, beyond the individual level of analysis, online behaviour is influenced by others as well. With the advent of online communication technologies, forums and contexts, participants in online activities such as auctions are more likely to be affected by the proximity and availability of others. In other words, the social aspects of auctions are likely to be brought to the fore. More attention needs to be paid to group and social-psychological inputs that may impact personal online behaviour such as auctions.

The following study attempts to understand behaviour in online auctions from a social perspective. Namely, how can the design of online auction systems harness the expression of others’ presence to impact the behaviour of individuals in online auctions? This study calls upon the theory of social facilitation to inform the design and process of online auctions. We report on the development of a simulation for online auctions, used in an experimental setting, which allowed varying levels of access and communication between auction participants. The differential outcomes of participation under different levels of social facilitation serve as a test of the theory.

## Online auctions

Online auctions are one of the most dominant examples of online behaviour. Auctions in general, and online implementations of the auction format in particular, are of research interest for a variety of reasons (Kagel, 1995). They represent a renaissance of dynamic pricing regimes, and a challenge to prevailing fixed pricing arrangements. In auctions the seller sets the rules but the buyer determines the price, creating a novel division of responsibilities and power. Game theorists have turned their attention to auction behaviour, as auctions are considered by some as pure markets (Vakrat & Seidman, 1999). The estimated value of business conducted in auction mode as a proportion of total business continues to increase (Sarkar, Butler & Steinfield, 1997; Chui & Zwick, 1999; Jap, 2001). What about the personal and social aspects of auction behaviour? As Herschlag and Zwick (2000) indicate, ‘the social impact of electronic exchanges has received little direct treatment’. The social impact on electronic exchanges has received even less attention so far. The present study is an exploratory attempt at theoretically framing and examining just such an impact.

On the psychological level, clearly, auctions generate much excitement. However, auctions are a social phenomenon, not just economic and psychological. In their online incarnation, auctions occur in a new, less explored configuration, where participants have unprecedented opportunities to communicate and interact with each other. In particular, online auctions have two characteristics that set them apart from the more traditional forms of auction. First, online auctions have less face-to-face proximity for buyer and seller, as well as less face-to-face proximity among buyers. Second, online auctions occur on the Internet; this medium enables computer–mediated point-to-point as well as group communication systems. The Internet has broken wide open the geographic and temporal maps of who we communicate with, when and where. As a result of instant and persistent communication channels, the social context of communication has been redrawn (Rafaeli, 1988; Walther, 1992; Walther & Burgoon, 1992; Reeves & Nass, 1996; Erickson et al, 1999; Jones & Rafaeli, 2000). We propose to harness the theory of social facilitation for a deeper understanding of the behaviour of auction participants due to the availability of others.

## Social facilitation

Since the late nineteenth century, if not earlier, the impact of the surrounding social environment on individuals’ behaviour, most notably one’s motivation, has been the focus of psychological study. Early work on telecommunication by Short et al (1976) identifies ‘evidence that the other is attending’ as a critical feature in the promotion of socially meaningful interaction. Eggins and Slade (1997) indicate that responses and rejoinders serve several beneficial purposes in conversation. Evidence of the presence of others builds and sustains relationships, expresses a willingness to maintain and prolong contact, and tacitly indicates interpersonal support, encouragement, and acceptance of the initiator.

One of the oldest and most time-honored constructs in social-psychological theory is the notion of social facilitation, originally understood as the tendency of people (and other animals) to perform simple or welllearned tasks better when others are present. As coined by Zajonc (1965), social facilitation theory predicts that the presence of others affects behaviour and performance. The ‘Drive Theory’ of social facilitation indicates that in the presence of others, people are expected to perform better on simple tasks, and to yield weaker results in complex tasks. The underlying psychological processes that cause such reaction to the presence of others may involve evaluation apprehension, concepts of personal space, defensive reactions to threat, as well as interest in attaining social desirability (Guerin, 1993). Research has examined the distinction between the effects of ‘mere presence’ social facilitation and effects that can be attributed to richer qualities and expressions of the other. Issues such as distraction, social presence, choking, anxiety, crowds and the size of audience needed to achieve the social facilitation effect have also been discussed in this literature. Baumeister (1984) and others have indicated the connection between social facilitation and pressure. For instance, sports teams tend to perform better before a friendly audience (an effect popularly known as the home team advantage), while for important games and high pressure the presence of a home crowd lowers the success rate.

Social facilitation has been taken beyond the psychology laboratory into ‘real world’ studies and economic behaviour. In full compliance with predictions derived from the tradition of social facilitation research, individual behaviour in real marketplaces is affected by the presence of others. Sommer et al (1992) documented social facilitation effects on shopping behaviour, where shoppers accompanied by others spent more time in the store and purchased more as compared to lone shoppers. Similarly, consumption of drinks in pubs and coffeehouses was affected by the presence of others. With groups present, people were found to purchase more and stay longer (Sommer, 1965; Geller et al, 1986). Sommer and Sommer (1989) summarise that the presence of others prolongs the stay and increases the purchase.

Can social facilitation carry over to situations where the social facilitators (onlookers or audience) are not in close physical proximity? Rafaeli (1988; 1989; 1990); Lombard and Ditton (1997) review some literature showing that this is indeed possible. There is a human need for interaction and even merely mediated hints at the availability of such interaction are desirable and affect behaviour. The effects documented so far are both motivational and cognitive in nature. Guerin (1993) suggests that, psychologically, we carry an audience around with us. People tend to carry out social behaviours even when alone. Aiello and Svec (1993) demonstrated the utility of employing a social facilitation framework to the study of computer monitoring of work performance. The physical presence of an observer watching a subject work on a complex task (as a supervisor might ‘look over an employee’s shoulder’) was examined. The experimental four conditions involved the electronic presence of computer-based work monitoring (as a supervisor might monitor an employee’s work remotely via computer) and a control condition of subjects working alone without any monitoring of their work. The ‘in person’ and the electronic monitoring regimes impacted task performance equally.

## Objectives

Most social facilitation experiments use real audiences while testing the behaviour of real subjects. The increased use of CMC (Computer Mediated Communication) and widespread availability of instant messaging and other synchronous or asynchronous communication options for auction participants raises the question of the possible role of virtual audiences. Sproull et al (1996) have shown that even when synthetic facial emulation is used in interfaces, it can result in increased arousal. This is the mediating mechanism through which social facilitation operates. Nass et al (1995) have shown social behaviour in mediated contexts. Perceptions of human interactions in computer-mediated experience are not necessarily positive (cf Bonito et al, 1999). Moon and Nass (1996; 1998) and Nass et al (1999) have investigated the attribution and personality aspects of reactions to computers, reporting generally that virtual representations are accepted as social, and that people react to computer mediated social phenomena as they would to real people in real proximity. The present study focuses on the social facilitation impacts on participants of online auction caused by the presence of a virtual audience.

We use the online Internet auction context for testing the effect of the virtual presence or availability of an audience on real human subjects. We tried to evaluate whether performance of subjects was influenced by the virtual presence of an audience by simulating different levels of presence. A consistency between consumer behaviour during traditional shopping (Sommer et al, 1992) and Internet shopping might be expressed by the duration customers elected to stay in the shop (in our case, remain in the auction site). Another finding of previous studies is that customers buy more, while in groups. In summary, the availability of cues and contacts regarding relevant others, termed here ‘presence’, has been shown to affect behaviour. Virtual presence is the online instantiation of such presence.

The present study used an interactive experiment and questionnaire to assess the following propositions derived from Social Facilitation theory:

H1: With higher levels of virtual presence, bidders in online auctions will perform better.

H2: With higher levels of virtual presence, bidders in online auctions will stay longer in the auction. H3: Participants in online auctions will prefer situations were there is a higher level of virtual presence of other bidders.

## Method

This section describes an experiment that was conducted during the summer of 2000, the different setups and manipulations, and the technological framework that was used to run this experiment.

## Participants

Sixty-five students (45 men and 20 women) were recruited to participate in the experiment. The overall nature of the experiment was participation in a series of online auctions. Participants were MBA (graduate level) students in Israeli universities, for whom the subject of auctions was part of the curriculum. Participation was voluntary, and no class credit was offered or awarded. The participants were told that they would take part in a commercial competition in which only the best performers will be rewarded. At the start of the experiment, participants’ names were recorded and their pictures were taken by a digital camera. Participants were told that names and pictures might be used in the experiment. Each participant was then seated alone near a networked personal computer. Instructions were provided online from this point, and participants were randomly assigned a navigation path (URL) that directed them to their randomly assigned experimental condition. No time limit was imposed, and participants were free to read instructions and proceed at their own pace, with the exception of the Dutch clock described later. We made an effort to insure that participation took place in an empty room with no live audience or distractions. The experiment was constructed entirely online to reduce experimenter availability or interference.

## Experimental procedure

The participants were randomly assigned to one of four types of scenarios, with each scenario consisting of two to three competitions. Participants were told that the reward was given to the best performer in any single competition. As will be explained later, the initial competitions were of different, contrasting natures, while the third competition was a repeat of one of the earlier two. Gender was balanced between scenarios and assignments. After each competition, participants were asked to complete a computerised questionnaire. The questionnaire was intended to serve as a manipulation check, to verify participants’ awareness of other bidders, and to asses the difference between experimental groups on this awareness. At the completion of two sessions, the participant was asked whether they wished to participate in a third session in order to increase their chances of winning. If so, they were asked whether they wanted to participate in the first or second type of competition to which they have just taken part. After completing the second or third session, according to the selection, the participants finished the experiment and were released. The entire interaction with participants was done through pre-composed, onscreen instructions.

The participants used a PC connected to the Internet. The experimental module was loaded into the participants’ Java enabled Microsoft Internet Explorer 5.0 browser. The experiment was executed on different types of PCs with no systematic or detected differences in performance between them. On the server back end, an Apache web server was used to send the HTML pages, process CGI scripts and to collect data from the Java applet that implemented the Dutch auction. Experimental and questionnaire data were later analysed and the results are reported below.

The experiment consisted of two or three sessions with a competition and a questionnaire following each session. Participants were instructed to maximise wins in each competition. Every competition consisted of 10 rounds of auctions. In every auction the participants had a chance to bid on a box of chocolate candy at the lowest possible price, using a decreasing price (Dutch) auction set of rules and mechanism. The box was announced to have a market value of \$9. In every auction the same product (only one), was ‘sold’ to the first bidder. The ‘Dutch’, decreasing price mechanism is such that being a first bidder is to offer highest price. The winner’s profit in each auction was the difference between the market price, which was displayed on screen and remained a constant \$9 per unit, and the winning bid. The profit in the competition was accumulated in all 10 rounds (auctions) of the competition. After completing the 10 auctions, a table containing the accumulated profits of all bidders was displayed to the participant.

## Manipulation

Four manipulation conditions were used to construct participation scenarios. These manipulations were titled Condition 1 to Condition 4. The manipulations were devised to increase the virtual availability of others in the auction process. Included in the social availability manipulations were: (1) knowledge of how many bidders participate in the auction; (2) knowledge of who these bidders were, by name and picture; and (3) direct communication access to these bidders. All of the social availability manipulations were synthetic, ie not real but simulated, and therefore uniform for all participants.

Condition 1 was the basic setup. Participants were presented with the Dutch auction decreasing price clock, the data about the auction, experimental messages and the operational buttons as described in Figure 1.

In this setup, the participant has no information about the number and identity of the other participants in the auction except for a serial number identifying the winner in each auction (ie, when a certain bidder wins an auction, his number appears at the bidder name field; eg, Bidder #3).

In Condition 2, the participant was provided with additional information. In this condition, participants were informed of the number of participants in the auction. This number was fixed to seven including the participant him or herself for all auctions in all sessions.

In Condition 3, participants were shown pictures of six other participants. These were the ostensible ‘competitors’ against which the bids and auction were held. The pictures were displayed in a 50 by 70 pixels rectangle at the bottom of the participant’s screen. An identifying name label accompanied every ‘contestant’ picture. Upon completion of a round, the name and picture of the ‘winner’ for that round (auction) were also displayed in the Winner field. An emphasised text label displaying: ‘Your competitors’ accompanied the six pictures.

In Condition 4 participants were given all the previous information, ie numbers, names and pictures of ‘competitors’, as well as a highlighting of the name and picture of each round winner. In addition, there was a simulated online chat mechanism between the bidders of the auction. The other bidders were seen as sending messages, and the participant could read and participate by sending his own messages. This was, of course, a simulated chat session in the sense that, unknown to the participants, there were no real competitors. Figure 2 shows this setup.

## Scenarios

The participants were randomly assigned to one of four scenario types. In every scenario the participant executed two sessions. For each participant there was one session (competition) with low virtual presence of other bidders (Condition 1 or Condition 2) and one session with high virtual presence of other bidders (Condition 3 or Condition 4). To control for order effects, a random half of the participants were exposed to the lower level of virtual presence first, and half of them were presented with the high level of virtual presence first. We hypothesised that the appearance of the faces (in Conditions 3 and 4) was a significant attribution to display that can contribute to the performance. Based on this assumption, we arranged for each participant to perform two sessions (to economise on subject time): one with low virtual presence (Condition 1 or 2), and one with high virtual presence (Condition 3 or 4).

We assumed, and therefore did not test that:

1. The appearance of the number of participants (Condition 2) is not sufficient to cause a significant change in the behaviour (over Condition 1).

2. The availability of an online chat (Condition 4) is not sufficient to cause a significant change in the behaviour (over Condition 3). The four scenarios were; The four scenarios were:

1. 1->3 (Condition 1 and then Condition 3, first low then high social presence)

2. 3->1 (Condition 3 and then Condition 1, first high then low social presence)

3. 2->4 (Condition 2 and then Condition 4, first low then high social presence)

## 4. 4->2 (Condition 4 and then Condition 2, first high then low social presence)

After completion of the second session each participant could choose to repeat one of the two conditions he/she has tried before. At this stage each participant was already familiar with two variations of the auction: low level condition (Condition 1 or Condition 2) and a high level condition (Condition 3 or Condition 4). The repetition allowed participants to reveal the preferred condition subjectively believed to be the most beneficial in improving performance. The preferred condition was also elicited by the questionnaire.

Several behavioural variables and decisions made by the participant were recorded during the experiment. We used this data later during the analysis stage. The following variables were recorded during the experiment:

1. Number of wins — counts the number of ‘wins’ in the competition out of the 10 auctions, and the difference between the number of wins in the first session and the second session.

2. Accumulated profit — accumulates profit in the 10 auctions of the competition, and the difference in the accumulated profit between the two sessions.

3. Time of stay in the auction — records the time that participants stayed in the competition (10 auctions) and the difference in that time between the two sessions. It was measured on the server side from the load of the applet, and is expressed in minutes. The time of stay measure excludes time spent on familiarisation in the first round.

4. Maximal win — records the maximal profit in one auction out of the 10 auctions of the competition, and the difference between the maximal profit of the two sessions.

5. Number of errors — it was assumed that participants will make some errors in their bidding decisions. An error was a wrong bidding decision in which the participant bids at a higher price than the market value. In this case a negative profit (loss) was accumulated. This parameter recorded the number of error in each session and the difference in the number of errors between the two sessions.

6. Third session decision — this parameter records the decision that the participant made: whether to participate in the third session or not, and if he/she chose to participate, what type of competition (high or low virtual presence) was chosen.

After the completion of each session, the participant was required to complete a computerised questionnaire. The questionnaire data helped to:

1. Check if the simulation successfully implemented ‘virtual presence’. As the questionnaire was presented after every session, we did not want to directly ask the participants about the simulated participants since half of the sessions did not present any description of the participants.

2. Supplement the performance findings by checking what the participants thought about their performance and the influencing factors.

3. Receive feedback about the setup conditions and the display format.

The questionnaire consisted of four parts each on a seven-point scale:

1. A question regarding clarity of the instructions.

2. Five questions regarding satisfaction with the display structure, with the presentation of the competitors and with the auction format.

3. Seven questions about participant perceptions of their own performance and influencing parameters during the auction.

4. The fourth part included seven parameters. The participants were requested to rank the factors according to their influence.

## Experimental apparatus

The technological framework for the experiment consisted of the following components:

1. A World Wide Web (WWW)-online Dutch auction applet with additional procedures to implement the social presence manipulations.

2. A mathematical simulation of bidders.

3. CGI (server-side processing) driven instructions and explanations, forms and questionnaires that were activated from the experiment Internet site and managed the experiment.

4. Auction administration forms that were activated from the experiment Internet site and used by the site administrator.

The Dutch auction applet was based on a Java package created by Ball (1997). This applet was modified and enhanced to serve this experiment. Modifications included adding the pictures and chat system. The software uses several simulation modules in order to simulate the behaviour of the bidders including simulation of Chat (see Figure 3).

## Results

The following section presents the results of the experiment and the questionnaire. Our data support the hypothesis that social facilitation can affect bidding behaviour in online auctions. Overall, participants’ performance was improved when a higher level of virtual presence of the other bidders was made available. The participants stayed longer in the auction site when they were exposed to a higher level of virtual presence. They indicated subjective preference for socially facilitated contexts by choosing to participate in later auctions where the virtual presence of the other bidders was maximised.

Furthermore, the questionnaire data provided a manipulation check. This data suggests that the social facilitation manipulation did indeed ‘take’. It showed that the participants thought that they were facing real bidders over the Internet.

The performance of the participants in the auction and the answers of the questionnaire were analysed both within group, and between groups. These analyses compared the performance and answers of groups that were exposed to a low level of virtual presence, and groups that were exposed to a high level of presence. Analysis was performed for the change in performance and responses of participants who completed two or more sessions with different levels of virtual presence.

We did not contrast all possible differences between groups. Instead, we selected only comparisons between groups in the same scenario. Theoretically a participant could participate in each of the four conditions. However, participants were allowed to participate in only two sessions due to time limitations and due to our two assumptions:

No systematic reason was uncovered for differences in accumulated wins not matching those of the number of wins. The accumulated wins in Condition 1 exceeded the accumulated wins in Condition 2 and even those in Condition 4. A possible explanation for these findings may be in Social Facilitation theory itself. Winning a single auction is relatively easy. But accumulating a large profit may prove more difficult and cannot be related to practice, learning or other strategic capability of the participant. Social Facilitation theory differentiates between easy and complex tasks.

One of the findings here is that there is an increase in performance in easy tasks while there is a degradation in complex tasks. This is a clear indication of the need for further research. Namely, would it be possible to parse out contradictory effects of audience presence in reducing performance in complex or prolonged auctions, following the predictions of social facilitation theory? Another explanation is related to a winning strategy that might be used by the participants. They might prefer winning, in the sense of gaining a small profit, over taking a chance of loosing by waiting too long in a bid for higher potential profit, allowing other participants to submit a winning bid. The lack of impact on cumulative profit may also be due to the fact that participants are not shown their cumulative profit until the end of the 10 auctions. Cognitively, participants may find it easier to mentally track how many wins they’ve had, rather than to keep a mental running total of accumulated profits. Hence, participants optimise number of wins, not accumulated profit. Future studies may want to show a running total of cumulative profits, and this may affect the outcomes observed.

## Conclusions

How can we account for the behaviour of participants, to better predict and better arrange online shopping and auction environments? It is clear that online auctions, with the attendant psychological stress, are here to stay. It is also clear that instant messaging and the ability of auction participants to talk to each other are a powerful computer-mediated reality. Should designers of auction arenas be aware of the possible interaction between users, and the impact of such interaction on the behaviour and performance of bidders?

The findings of the current study support the existence of social facilitation effects in online bidding behaviour. Participants achieved higher scores and stayed longer in the auction activity when exposed to a higher level of virtual presence (of the other bidders). When given a choice, participants preferred to bid in an auction with a higher level of virtual presence.

Despite previous studies of social facilitation, there has been only limited work on social facilitation in commerce, and in computer-mediated situations, where the presence of others is virtual. This study is a call to attention for researchers and designers to heed the potential implications of such connections. In discussing virtual/online communities and business, Hagel and Armstrong (1997) suggest that such communities constitute a ‘tremendous vehicle for increasing loyalty to a vendor’s products’. Because people ‘want to connect to each other’, say Hagel and Armstrong, ‘enetrepreneurs ‘can turn the intrinsic cultural appeal of communities into a real business proposition’. However, even Hagel and Armstrong conceded that compelling real world examples of this power of communities are lacking. This study provides an early, experimental and empirical pointer to a social-facilitation mechanism through which such impacts may be achieved.

The manipulation employed in previous non-mediated social facilitation research included various setups, locations and activities. The inspector was located in front, behind, or even in a different room (Fridlund, 1991). The usage of computers in social facilitation studies was motivated by the need for participants to stay alone while doing the task. When the computer implemented the inspection (Aiello & Svec, 1993) the participants’ achievements were affected.

In contrast with the Fridlund (1991); Aiello and Svec (1993) studies, in the present study participants did not interact face-to-face or even see the real experimenters. Instead, as all interaction was done online, effects on participant behaviour can only be attributed to virtual presence. Nevertheless, findings indicate that the resulting behaviour was identical to real presence. The participants achieved higher scores in the auctions and stayed longer in the site, when there was a higher level of virtual presence.

This result could not be explained by drive arousal due to the competition condition under which the participants were placed, because competition levels were constant across the different experimental conditions. Participants remained in a state of competition in all conditions, both while there was a low level of virtual presence and while they had been exposed to a higher level of virtual presence (by the figures of the other bidders and online chat). This alternative explanation can justify the change in the time of stay but cannot justify change in the achievements. So, it is concluded that change in the achievements and in the time of stay is explained by social facilitation.

One of the limitations of the present research is that the measurement of duration and activation of the auction, including both the time of actual participation in the auction, and the time participants spent analysing the auction data. Future work should attempt to better distinguish these measures. Another limitation of the research is the use of the decreasing Dutch auction format as opposed to the more common increasing English auction. Dutch auctions are characterised by dropping price mechanisms as well as by short decision time periods. In this type of auction social presence effects may actually be underestimated as bidders have only one opportunity to bid. In a multi-stage process, where bidders can sequentially correct their bids over time, performance might be influenced even more.

These limitations of time and pressure in the experimental paradigm might explain the absence of a systematic matching for the accumulated wins and number of wins parameter. Another possible expansion of the current research is a direct measurement of the linearity of the effect of bandwidth on the hypothesised relation. Does the available bandwidth moderate or directly affect the relation between social facilitation and auction behaviour outcomes? Initial indications, based on media richness theory (Daft & Lengel, 1984) may suggest that this is the case. We hypothesised that presenting the images of the participants would cause a significant change in the performance, but did not verify it by establishing all possible comparisons along the bandwidth continum suggested by the conditions in this study. The bandwidth hypothesis goes beyond the scope of this study, and is certainly suggested as a topic for further study.

The questionnaire was used as a manipulation check and supplement for the performance findings. Tables 3 and 4 report a consistent direction in the differences between experimental conditions. Although not all questionnaire items yielded statistically significant differences on all contrasts, participants noticed the influence of other bidders when present. Based on discussions with participants following the experiments, and this questionnaire data, we concluded that the participants really believed that they were participating in a real auction playing against real bidders and not a computer simulation. The participants preferred the auction with a higher level of details about bidders, in correlation with their response in the experiment. The results of this study support the intuitive trend currently practiced in the field by online auction operators. There are various tools and technological innovations that help Internet users to collaborate and communicate using various methods. From a business standpoint, the results can contribute to the design of real implementations. Many companies and technology innovators develop and sell frameworks for collaborative work. Internet sites are still measured by the number of Internet users that visit the site. The social facilitation effect on time of stay may be important in relation to commercial promotions that attract users.

From a theoretical standpoint, the importance of the results is in the extension and articulation of the ‘presence’ term. The term was used in previous social facilitation studies, and even earlier, in the earliest of telecommunication studies (see Short et al, 1976; Rafaeli, 1988) to document social–psychological effects on the personal use of information systems. Here again, we see that the potential of computer mediated systems is not just in bridging space and time. The bridging potential may be even more powerful in the interpersonal space, or in harnessing the interpersonal potential. This extension opens an opportunity for further virtual presence studies. The contribution of the present study is in both social psychological and information systems research. This study suggests ways in which the design of online auctions needs to be informed by the social nature of the auction process.

There is much folklore about fake or ‘shill’ bidders, ‘pools’ and ‘rings’ in auction settings. Smith (1989) reports that in traditional auctions, both sellers and buyers often engage in ‘teamwork’, attempting to impact the outcome. Smith terms such arrangements ‘sharp practices’. He does, however, indicate that such practices are rarely initiated by the auctioneer, and are more typical of buyers and sellers. On the other hand, the social facilitation paradigm investigated here suggests that the auctioneer, designer and maintainer of the online auction system may have an interest in laying the CMC infra-

structure for such teamwork. The design for studying online auctions proposed here uncovers some theoretical background in Social Facilitation theory that could help shed light on this ‘teamwork’ social process. Auctions are not just a social process. These findings point to dynamics between participants in auctions that can be harnessed by both system designers and actors in the economic arena. The results presented indicate that the virtual presence of others enabled by computer mediated communication could cause a behaviour change.
