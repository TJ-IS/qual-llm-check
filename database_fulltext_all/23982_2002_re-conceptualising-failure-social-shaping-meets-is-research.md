# Re-conceptualising failure: social shaping meets IS research

M Wilson<sup>1</sup> and D Howcroft<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Management, UMIST, Manchester, UK; and <sup>2</sup>Information Systems Research Centre, University of Salford, Salford, UK

The aim of this paper is to illustrate the interplay of the social studies of technology (SST) approach with IS research to further our conceptualisation of failure. It is intended that this cross-fertilisation of disciplinary backgrounds will produce a critique of traditional conceptions of information technology and help to further our understanding of the IS development, implementation and use process. We begin by providing a commentary on the IS failure literature, highlighting the variety of descriptions and noting the lack of consensus regarding how success/failure is constituted. We then go on to delineate the contribution of the SST approach to enlightening our study of failure, since it has as its core concern an understanding of the dynamics of the society–technology relationship. This approach is applied to a case study where the intention is to demonstrate the moving line between success and failure among different groups as well as over different periods of time. The study shows how the success/failure factors can be equally applied to construct an account as and when required, depending upon how legitimacy is ascribed to different ‘voices’. The SST themes are then revisited to enable a deconstruction of the stages that were undertaken before failure was finally declared. Finally, conclusions are drawn on the contribution of SST for aiding our understanding of how failures occur within their social and organisational context.

## Introduction

Despite the negative press it receives, failure can be said to have played a pivotal role in shaping the dynamics of information and communication technologies. Friedman with Cornford (1989) formulates the drive to innovate as a reaction to commonly occurring failures of information systems in organisations in his influential account of the history of information systems (IS) development. The 1990s witnessed an increase in the volume of work by academics and practitioners dealing with this phenomenon (eg, Sauer, 1993; Myers, 1994; Fortune & Peters, 1995; Drummond, 1996; Flowers, 1996; Vaughan, 1996; Lyytinen and Robey, 1999). Yet, a key omission in much of this writing is some recognition that failure may not be unilaterally agreed. This, we argue, is due to the existence of differing interests and perspectives, which give rise to distinct subjects of study (as to what is examined regarding failure/success), as well as the way in which that study takes place. On the one hand, definitions and causes of failure are not clearly distinguished; however, on the other, many accounts of the demise of IS fail to problematise the success/failure dichotomy (Mitev, 2000). We argue that developments in the social shaping of technology can shed some vital light on this murky subject.

From an IS perspective, the issues concerning failure can be divided into the following: empirical questions related to cause and effect issues; conceptual problems concerning the nature of IS failure; and normative issues related to the tools and techniques developed to improve the record of IS failure (Sauer, 1993). Arguably, much of the literature written in response to the failure phenomenon has largely concentrated on normative issues, namely the development of new tools and techniques. The present paper aims to contribute to conceptual aspects associated with an understanding of IS failure, although we pass comment on empirical questions relating to causal issues.

In the first section of this paper, we provide a commentary on the IS failure literature, distinguishing between definitions and causes. This precedes the establishment of the critical view provided by Social Shaping of Technology (SST) in its comprehension of the complexity of organisational life and its questioning of the unitary nature of organisational goals. The potential contribution of the SST approach to enlightening our study of failure is then provided, where notions such as interpretative flexibility, stabilisation and irreversibility, and translation and persuasion are discussed. This approach is then applied to a case study where the intention is to illustrate the moving line between success and failure among different groups as well as over different periods of time. Finally, conclusions are drawn.

## A commentary on the information systems failure literature

In this section, we begin by providing an overview of the IS failure literature. The first section shows a variety of descriptions, indicating the lack of consensus and suggesting that a definition of success is difficult to achieve. These are summarised in tabular form to assist our case study illustration of the point that such variety potentially exists even within one organisation. The section that follows identifies the way in which causes of failure tend to be portrayed in the literature, illustrating that there are numerous perspectives when attributing cause, which may coexist and/or may be differently applied according to the context and ‘worldview’.

## Defining failure

Tales of IS failure are legendary. Despite the fact that failures ‘may be downplayed as aberrant deviations from the norm’ (Robinson, 1994, p 1), the available statistics on success rates in IS development tell another story (for example, Lyytinen & Hirschheim, 1987). Yet, on closer inspection, it is evident that these statistics all refer to different phenomena: cost over-runs, schedule over-runs, cancellations, operating at a loss, and so on. Typically, in the IS literature lists of factors are presented against which success and failure can be measured. Based on the work of Drummond (1996), Dutton et al (1995), Flowers (1996), Fortune & Peters (1995), Lyytinen & Hirschheim (1987), Jones (1995),

Mitev (2000), Morris (1996) and Sauer (1993), we have amalgamated and summarised these in Table 1.

Based on the above, it appears that achieving a consensus on a definition of failure seems implausible. To an extent, the various definitions of IS failure expose more general problems of technology evaluation (for example, how do we measure success?), not to mention the issue of differing or contradictory perceptions of success and failure (DeLone & McLean, 1992).

Perhaps in contrast to common belief, ‘failure’ does not mean the system needs to have been abandoned altogether, or is even ‘falling apart’, but is simply not used in the way intended (Laudon & Laudon, 1998). This failure to meet expectations is a key theme in much of the failure literature, most notably with Lyytinen & Hirschheim (1987). Hence, failure does not hinge on the technical functioning of the system, and can occur even when the technical system has performed as its designers intended (Dutton et al, 1995).

## Construction of IS failure

In this section, we briefly summarise the critique developed within the IS literature as to how causal explanations of failure are attributed in organisations. We concur with other authors that attributing cause is related to one’s perspective of failure and is probably influenced by one’s view of how to improve the situation. This would suggest the need for a different conceptualisation.

As an alternative to the typical response that construes improved tools and techniques as a means of remedying the situation, Sauer (1993) suggests we begin by recognising that the level of support for a project is a crucial factor and that we should conceive of failure as a process rather than a single, discrete event. During this process and for political reasons, there is a practice of attributing responsibility for failureFespecially with large, public catastrophes. This can be perceived as just another way of blaming operators of systems for problems and failures and it is the result of a politic that is ‘biased towards the most powerful’ (Dutton et al, 1995, p 45).

Within any alternative conceptualisation of failure, due recognition should be given to the organisational context. Arguably one cannot decide how widespread failures are without taking into account the substantial subjective element entailed in evaluating a system as a success or failure. Yet evaluations are purportedly the means by which failures come to be ‘discovered’. Much of the literature would have us believe that when evaluating IS, organisational members will list a number of factors that then add up to a failure or a success. However, we argue that it is possibly the case that a decision is made as to whether or not a system is a failure and this is then followed by the construction of a list enumerating factors that constitute the failure/ success. In the case of failure, the next step is the attribution of causes once failure is declared. By its nature, this is a rationalisation process whereby causes ‘fit’ the failure factors. This means that, contrary to common notions, attribution of failure and the search for causal explanations is in itself a process that deserves examination. In discussing the construction of failure, Robinson (1994, p 1) notes that it is the setting of objectives of the project that is the key to its achievement:

[Failures] mayy express exactly the same dynamics, motives, interests and logic as successes. The difference between the two comes about when the system does not meet the goals set for it by the actors who define it as a failure.

The above discussion on the causes of failure is intended to stress the problematic nature of the phenomenon. One substantial limitation with these approaches is that, given the multifarious descriptions of failure, almost any project is potentially vulnerable to the attribution of failure. Yet this is clearly not the case in practice, since despite their flaws (such as project over-runs, over-budget spending, poor quality design, etc) many systems are still deemed a success. This then begs the question, is there a further conceptualisation that can improve our existing understanding of IS failure?

## Social nature of information systems failure

Given the above, we suggest that the social studies of technology (SST) approach, which conceptualises technology as socially shaped, has a role to play in deepening our understanding of the complexity of IS failure and in providing us with additional insights. In this paper, we treat SST as a ‘broad church’ (Williams, 1997) and so employ elements developed within different models from a variety of writers. According to Mac-Kenzie & Wajcman (1999), although the SST approach was seen as being novel in the mid-1980s, it has now become almost an orthodoxy in the treatment of technology in general. However, it is evident that apart from a few notable exceptions (such as Bloomfield & Best, 1992; Boland & Schultze, 1996; Monteiro & Hanseth, 1996; Sahay & Robey, 1996; Mitev, 2000), this is not the case in IS research.

To begin with, according to the SST approach, there exists an underlying problem for the process of evaluation of IS: the problem of technological determinism. Specifically, the assumed objectivity of techniques used to evaluate systems is part of a broader set of notions that entail an assumption as to the ‘neutrality of technology, yet this must be rejected if we hope to arrive at a more sophisticated understanding of sociotechnology.

This means that in IS research when we are examining the social context of the technology, it becomes evident that the sponsors of an IS will view ‘success’ as the survival of their project, despite the fact that this is based upon a consensual view of organisational life whereby all groups are assumed to adhere to a common set of goals (Robinson, 1994). In contrast, Pinch & Bijker (1987) suggest that different ‘relevant social groups’ will not only define a technological problem differently but also that there will be disagreement over definitions of what constitutes success and failure. From the social constructivist approach (Bijker et al, 1987; Bijker & Law, 1992) we may infer that organisational politics are played out not only during the initial creation and implementation, but also in the way that certain stories of these events come to dominate. By employing this social constructivist approach, it becomes possible to identify who contrives things such that their perspective is recounted as ‘the’ story in recognition of the possibility that one person’s success may be another person’s failure or even disaster. ‘History’, so the adage goes, ‘is told by the victors’ (Robinson & Wilson, 2000). This would suggest that we may gain insights from accessing stories other than those constructed by the victors.

As a means to achieve this, we may have recourse to one of the primary tools of SST, deconstruction. This enables us to unravel the differences between official accounts of technological development and alternative perspectives on that process. Hence, in describing the social construction of failure we are including the actual process of the failure’s construction as well as the (official and unofficial) account of that process. After the fact, the process is represented in documents that may be the sole visible trail of evidence. This implies that it is only by conducting a longitudinal study that we will have access to the process. Such a study would then permit an analysis of the dissonance between documentation arising from de facto decision-making and non-legitimate, alternative accounts.

## Explanatory concepts for information systems failure

## Importance of failure and the value of interpretative flexibility

In addition to using the tool of deconstruction, what other contribution can be gleaned from using SST? To begin with, a social shaping approach would not use the deceptively neutral terms, ‘failure’/‘success’, as this does not indicate for whom the technology presents itself as such. Failures are of particular interest for understanding technological development since the controversy that surrounds them tends to reveal processes that are more easily hidden in the case of ‘successful’ projects (Bijker & Law, 1992). Likewise, compared to accounts of ‘successes’, failure studies often make it easier for the researcher to elicit more complex explanations from actors and to disentangle how technical and social issues are constructed and delineated (Akrich, 1992).

A further concept advanced within the SST school of thought that can be effectively applied to the issue of the success/failure of IS is that of interpretative flexibility (Pinch & Bijker, 1987). Interpretative flexibility is useful for understanding how problems and solutions associated with a technology present themselves differently to different groups of people. Consequently, it allows us to see how and why a particular technology’s success/ failure is open to more than one interpretation, and prompts us to ask why, in particular circumstances, one interpretation becomes the dominant account. This approach thus challenges the assumption that design stems from the logic of the technology itself.

## Stabilisation and irreversibility

Other useful SST concepts include irreversibility and stabilisation, both of which encourage a view of success/ failure as a process rather than an event. Irreversibility describes the evolutionary process in which a network passes from a state of flux and divergence to one of strong stabilisation and the disappearance of problems through closure. Closure can be deemed to have taken place when the technology renders itself indispensable to other actorsFan ‘obligatory point of passage’ (Callon & Law, 1982) in a larger network of organisational practices. Further, the degree of ‘irreverisibilisation’ means the degree to which it becomes impossible to return to a situation where the next step is only one possible option among others (Callon, 1986; Coombs et al, 1987), possibly because ‘alternatives become unthinkable’ (Bloomfield et al, 1997). It also refers to the extent to which later translations are pre-determined, and how decisions become more and more dependent on previous ones.

Combined with the observation that IS constitute a technological innovation, and the innovation in organisational behaviour required to complement the system, the implication of stabilisation for IS is that although the technical application may be deemed to be finished, or even a success, in one setting, this may not be the case in another. Hence, the stabilisation of an artefact can differ from location to location. Success cannot be taken for granted. The take-up of the technology, on the part of end-users, has to be ‘won’ by the sponsors. Further, in order to achieve irreversibility, end-users must be persuaded to abandon previous systems of working, through the establishment of the IS as an obligatory point of passage. One way or another, they must be denied a choice. Thus, irreversibility does not necessarily imply that consensus has been achieved, simply that the dissenters have been denied a legitimate voice or forced to accept the situation.

## Strategies to achieve stabilisation: translation and persuasion

As discussed, the agnostic term ‘stabilisation’ is presented as a means by which the success or failure of an IS may be described. From an implementation perspective, stabilisation entails, among other things, translation (Callon et al, 1986): that is, the effective persuasion of pertinent actors, especially end-users, that it is in their interest to use the technology in the prescribed manner, and that the technology is the answer to their ‘problems’ (Bloomfield & Best, 1992). This translation might involve issuing a polemic against alternative, competing translations, and/or establishing that a certain technological development and organisational procedure is necessary or beneficial. In health care, for example, considerations can only be considered legitimate if they are seen to further patient care (Bloomfield & Vurdubakis, 1997). In the decisions determining which problems are relevant in the development of technologies, the social groups involved with the artefact and the meanings that those groups give to the artefact all play a crucial role (Pinch & Bijker, 1987).

## Legitimacy and power

Following on from this is the notion of a moral order being built into the very design of the technology (Akrich, 1992; Latour, 1992). It is also suggested that organisational and economic interests will be reflected in the particular configuration of an IS (Shaiken, 1986). As such, these systems are likely to support and enhance the interests of a minority who already are in more powerful situations (Markus & Bjorn-Anderson, 1987; Hirschheim & Klein, 1989). Thus IS can be used to translate the desire for change held by the most powerful players in the organisation.

We believe that SST is a useful approach for conceptualising the role of technology, but it has been criticised elsewhere for its limited commentary on social structure and power relations (Kline & Pinch, 1999; Wajcman, 2000). In particular, Winner (1993) has criticised Social Construction of Technology (SCOT) for their almost total disregard of ‘irrelevant’ social groups (the excluded and marginalised), while Walsham (1997) notes that Actor Network Theory (ANT) pays little attention to the broader social structures that influence the local. However, in the IS field, there has been a move that conceptualises systems design and implementation as a process of social contention and political, as well as technical, determination (Franz & Robey, 1984; Noble, 1984). In relation to our understanding of failure, this implies that the exercise of organisational power also affects which accounts or evaluations come to dominate and have the status of legitimacyFimplying that non-orthodox views may be ignored or silenced (at least for a while, anyway). So, while interpretative flexibility means that different views of the systems are likely, understanding how one set of ideas can become the orthodoxy and thus ‘valid’ is a function of organisational political reality. Further, the emphasis on failure/success as a process rather than an event has meant that we can concentrate on how stabilisation is achieved (or not, as the case may be) and the practices adopted by sponsors to configure the decision space and mobilise support in order to persuade others to ‘pass this way’, that is, to use the IS as they would have them do.

We suggest that these tools will be helpful in answering research issues arising from the previous two sections, taking into account the social context of IS development and use. Specifically:

<sup>\*</sup> How is failure variously perceived?

<sup>\*</sup> What are the differences between legitimate and alternative accounts of failure?

<sup>\*</sup> How are these different accounts constructed and how do they change over time?

<sup>\*</sup> What role does power play in mobilising these different accounts?

These issues are now explored in the case study that follows.

## Applying a social shaping approach to IS: an illustration

In order to highlight the usefulness of an approach to failure that emphasises the subjective and socially constructed nature of technological outcomes, we include a summary of findings from a UK National Health Service (NHS) case study. The role of the case study is to provide something of the rich detail of lived experiences of IS as well as exploring the research issues detailed above. The objective of the inclusion of the empirical work is to illustrate how the conceptual tools of the SST approach aid our understanding of how different perspectives are simultaneously possible and to show how the same phenomena may be differently interpreted over time. However, in keeping with a political perspective, we also illustrate how the system stabilises through the process of ascribing legitimacy to different ‘voices’ over time.

## Research method

## Investigation, data collection methods and position in the field

The precise focus of the case study is directed to the care planning function of the Zenith Nurse Management System and its users at the Eldersite Hospital in the North of England.<sup>1</sup> A case study illustration is included since it illuminates the issues discussed above and enables us to ask penetrating questions and capture the richness of organisational behaviour (Gable, 1994): this was particularly relevant given the size and diversity of the organisation in question. The investigation centred on examining the role of IS in changing the way people carried out work in the NHS. The period spent in the field totalled 10 months and used multiple techniques of data collection. Since the research was primarily descriptive, individual interviews were the primary source of data collection, enabling the respondents to propose their own insights as a basis for further enquiry. In the main, these were one-off, hour-long interviews. In addition, multiple interviews with key players such as the Project Nurse and the Assistant Project Nurse took place. All the interviews were semistructured and taped throughout. In total, 20 interviews were carried out. The transcripts from the tape recordings of the nursing staff were organised according to topics or issues raised in the interviews and were analysed according to themes that emerged from the literature review as well as those that emerged during the data collection period. Formal structured methods for identifying themes were not used; rather during the process of analysis, there was a movement from a more open-ended approach (in common with some of the ideas from Grounded Theory (Glaser & Strauss, 1967)) towards a more closely directed one.

In addition to interviews, the study also entailed informal evaluations of the nursing information system (NIS) as well as an analysis of the various texts and representational practices associated with IS training and use. Indeed, much of the story that unfolds below was pieced together through ‘Benefits Realisation’ and update reports and correspondence written by members of the Nursing Implementation Team or the IT manager. The examination of reports highlighted the way the evaluation and training procedures were aimed at persuading the users of the so-called benefits of the system.

## People and groups

The interviews and observations concerned two main groups of people: those involved in implementing the NIS (the sponsors), and those expected to use the system (the nurses on the wards). There were 100 wards at the hospital, with the system going live on 27 of these; interviews took place on seven of the ‘live’ wards. A cross-section of those members of staff who were deemed to be affected by the introduction of the computer system was devised and included different wards (specialities), various grades, and both positive and negative views of the system. ## The Zenith system story

In order to illuminate the ebbs and flows of the implementation of this system, we begin by presenting a timeline of events to be used as a reference against which the failure/success ascription moves (Table 2). The story is then developed more descriptively below.

## Background

Developed by a small software house, the Zenith Nurse Management System is a database system that comprises three main functions: care planning, rostering, and workload assessment. The main focus of the study is the care planning function since it directly relates to the delivery of care and pertains to the work of nurses on the ward with the majority of users interacting with this part of the system. The care planning function consists of a database of Care Libraries that can be edited individually and free text added to produce a printed and standardised document. These are intended to replace the hand-written notes used by nurses in the recording of their intended care delivery for patients through the previous manual Kardex system. As is the case with many tales of IS in the NHS, the story of Zenith began with the desire and perceived need for standardised health-care practice and methodological financial management (Keen, 1994).

## Training and implementation

It was felt that by encouraging the nurses to create a part of the system using their knowledge and expertise, it would more appropriately match their needs and thus lead to a better system. Yet, from the outset, nurses from Eldersite were not involved in the original design of the Zenith system; instead a lot of resources were put into developing and maintaining support mechanisms throughout implementation: link nurses, user groups, regular meetings with the Directorate Nurse Manager and monthly Implementation Group meetings.<sup>3</sup>

In addition, key nurse-users were signed up for the training programme as part of the implementation, thereafter to act as the Zenith representative on the ward, at which point it was envisaged the technology would gradually stabilise. Despite involvement in these training programmes, according to the Project Nurse, it was a recurrent event for nurses to approach her individually after the meetings to tell her how things were ‘really’ going. As far as many of the nurses on the wards were concerned, the training they had received was inadequate.

Benefits Realisation sessions were also carried out by the Project Nurse, to ‘up-date’ the information users on the so-called benefits of the system. Enrolment to the system can only be achieved if the users are convinced that it is beneficial to them:

Once they see the benefits of the rostering then we can start, we can look at the benefits of the care planning side of it, and hopefully get them to work on that in the same way.

## Failure to meet expectations and resistance

Despite attempts at participation, many nurses felt they were not ‘really’ consulted about what they wantedF they had not been asked, nor listened to. During the process of enrolment, the nurses who had participated had been given an impression that they would have some control over the system, whereby they would be able to alter the system to ‘suit their needs’. Unfortunately, however, there were instances when the nurses were simply misled by the Nursing Implementation Team as to the nature of the system they would be receiving and what they received was very limited.

Nurses who had been incredibly enthusiastic about the promise of new technology, gradually suffered diminished motivation. Eventually, even those who had been keen to use the system became disillusioned with its poor performance, as one disappointed staff nurse remarked:

Initially I thought it was a good idea but I got really fed up with it, yes. Everything was duplicated, it took you twice as long to do everything and it was very difficult really.<sup>5</sup>

On some wards, there had been a period when negative attitudes had actually diminished because of increasing confidence of usage, but the system was generally seen to detract from the job of nursing, even by those who did not avoid using it:

It doesn’t improve things. It doesn’t make it any better for the patient. The discharge rate has not increased. The average length of stay has not shortened because of it. The patients don’t feel their treatment and the way that they’re dealt with is any better. In fact if anything, we’ve had comments like: ‘All you seem to be doing is spending time on the computer typingFyou don’t seem to have any time for us’.<sup>6</sup>

## Achievement of failure

However, in spite of a catalogue of negative opinions concerning Zenith, these were not formally recognised until months before the sponsors abandoned the system. A report prepared by the Project Nurse entitled, Evaluation and Future Implementation of the Zenith Nurse Management Information System, finally acknowledges the nurses’ opinions, which, by being reported, are thereby legitimised. The great difference with this report as compared with either of the previous Benefits Realisation reports is that the problems are actually listed, and at this point, outnumber the benefits.

According to the Project Nurse, the looming decision of whether to carry on with the system had convinced management of the benefits of withdrawal and therefore, we can assume this promoted the less partisan report from the project team. The impetus to make a decision about the continued implementation of Zenith in part arises from the software suppliers’ demand for payment of the outstanding structured support fee. Eldersite were, in fact, in dispute with these suppliers and were receiving no external software support at this time. Whereas previous Benefits Realisation reports were intended to convince its audience of the need to continue with the project, this one raised the question of whether it was worthwhile to continue, and mobilised the nurses’ views to do so. Following the Evaluation and Future Implementation report, a new system of regular auditing of the performance and problems of the Zenith system was established to be effected by the clinical nurse specialist on each ward. Consequently, yet another report was produced, this time based on a specially organised Zenith Workshop that was initiated by the project team in order to encourage the voicing of discontent. It is at this stage that problems with the system, as opposed to just the users, are brought out. Failure is finally declared, described as an outcome of the Zenith Workshop:

The overall opinion is that the Zenith Nurse Management Information System had failed to produce the expected benefits.<sup>7</sup>

Later that year, coinciding with the end of the 3-year roll-out period of the RMI project, in a Sign Off report from Eldersite, the decided failure of the NIS is described as non-achievement and is deemed to have failed in the following ways:

<sup>\*</sup> It did not prove as ‘user friendly’ as originally anticipated, which was particularly important to a user community unused to computers.

<sup>\*</sup> Its implementation was not robust. In particular, its reliability was not satisfactory.

<sup>\*</sup> There were software ‘bugs’ in key functions that reduced user credibility.

<sup>\*</sup> Certain functions were not delivered within the time scales originally promised. This was particularly a problem with reporting functions. High quality timely outputs were essential to retain the system credibility.

This report of the problems with Zenith does not contradict the problems highlighted by the nurses. However, according to some of the nurses, the same problems had been brought to the attention of the systems project team 3 years previously. At that point, they were deemed to be ‘glitches’ that would be ironed out, or more negatively posed as unwillingness of the part of the nurses to ‘give the system a chance’. Whatever case had been made for the benefits of the Zenith system, it was eventually withdrawn, five years after the resource management project began. At the early stages of the project, the Nursing Implementation Team identified a key obstacle to the enrolment that proved to be ultimately intractable:

There have been many problems with the installation and implementation of the Zenith system, the most difficult to overcome is the one of culture change, the bringing together of nurses and information technology.<sup>9</sup>

## Case study analysis and discussion

In order to accentuate the value of the SST approach, we begin by operationalising the failure criteria provided by much of the literature into descriptive and causal factors and apply these to the case study. First, these lists show the descriptions and causes of failure (represented in Table 3), which are informative and useful as far as they go. However, we then go on to list reasons why the system may equally be interpreted as a success (Table 4)Foften using the same descriptions and causes, but from a different viewpoint. We aim to highlight the limitations of this failure-factor identification and go on to discuss how this dualism is possible and why, over time, one perspective comes to dominate over another.

## Perceptions of failure and success

First, when we ask the question ‘Why was the Zenith system a failure at the hospital?’ this can be easily answered, if we take on face value the Sign Off report from the case study<sup>10</sup> where the factors of failure are enumerated. The system had been unpopular with a majority of end-users, the nurses, from the time of its arrival and installation on the wards since they found it did not assist in the delivery of care, and in some case was deemed to detract from effective hands-on patient care. Yet, the sponsors’ response (ie, the legitimate response) had been to discard such objections as due not to the system being a failure, but rather due to the nurses inability to ‘see the benefits’. However, in a Sign Off report, the official version of events and criteria for evaluating the NIS are presented to the end-users and the decided failure of the IS is described as a nonachievement.<sup>11</sup>

## Stabilisation and irreversibility

For a technology to stabilise, the relevant social groups must be persuaded that they need to ‘pass this way’ to solve their problem, or accomplish their task. The nature of such an obligatory passage point may vary, but in the case of the Zenith system, the passage point entailed care planning via the automated system. However, an alternative route to recording nursing care was kept openFthe manual Kardex system. The care planning was not a substitute for all the other documentation that had preceded the installation of Zenith. For the passage point to be effective, every item of information should have been recorded on the automated care plan. Given the necessity, due to shortage of time, to choose between giving hands-on care or writing records, nurses elected care. It is the official record (that is, in the eyes of the sponsors of the Zenith project), but unofficially (and openly in defiance of management), the Kardex remains the record. ## Translation

The process of stabilisation rests crucially on the ability to translate successfully other’s interests to one’s own, thus aligning the various interests. With the case of the Zenith system, nurse-users’ and sponsors’ needs were translated into one and the same solution and inscribed into the technical system.. However, as we have seen, their needs were not ‘one and the same’. The users used the system in an unanticipated way, instead of following the prescribed programme of action. Latour (1991) describes this as an anti-programme. (See Monteiro (2000) for further discussion of how this applies to IS.) Crucially, nurses felt the need to improve the quality of patient care they were providing that had been frustrated by lack of time and resources. This had been translated by the Government generally and on a local level to be solved by freeing nurses up from unwieldy recording activities, in turn translated as solved by computerisation. In addition, computerisation was translated as the solution to concerns about professional status on the part of nurses: they would be taken more seriously if they used the latest technology. In the event, computerisation increases the time to record care delivered and so many nurses opted to reject the technology. Therefore, the Zenith system is not successfully translated as the solution to nurses needs and so does not become an obligatory point of passage through which all nurses must pass if they are to be satisfied with their professional status and delivery of patient care.

## Persuasion

The evaluation process entails the schooling of users as to the properties and values the system possesses. In the early stages, resistance and hostility were treated by the implementers as understandable, but, nevertheless, irrational responses to the system and the evaluation process played a pivotal role in its attempt to overcome such a response. By the time of the first Benefits Realisation session, the users were to be encouraged by the ‘fact’ that the worst aspects of training and change had been overcome. The emphasis now involved the education of potential users as to rewards as well as showing the benefits to existing supporters. If it is the case that evaluations can be used to prevent the abandonment of a system that otherwise would be deemed a flop, then there is an element of this recuperation strategy in the Benefits Realisations carried out. As a prelude to the session, the Project Nurse and the IT manager tried to persuade the nurses that the benefits of the Zenith system were undoubtedly there to be had:

The Nursing System will save time, taking over much of the mundane administration, as it can analyse information quicker therefore (sic.) providing excellent information support at all levelsy there are benefits to be gained, it is achieving them that is the main problem.

The ‘organizational benefits’ of the Zenith care planning system were produced using the systems suppliers’ own Benefits Realisation methodology. Within this, it was believed that setting the wrong targets would result in failure. We may note these generic benefits attend to managerial concerns and this highlights the role of evaluation as a political resource, elevating the views of certain groups over others. Further instructions were given by the suppliers on how to make qualitative benefits into hard, financial gains that could be assessed in pure monetary terms. Formal evaluations such as these can be perceived as a political resource to the extent that they circumscribe the legitimacy of opinionsFhere, claiming that if only nurses would open their eyes they would ‘see’ these benefits awaiting them. This was deemed necessary to overcome feelings on the part of nurses that using the nursing system was not of immediate use to them and may even have been detrimental to patient care. One bone of contention was who used the information and for what purposes. The Project Nurse admitted privately that the information was not really for the users and that the Director of Nurse Managers had most use for it. Yet it is precisely the issue of who the technology is intended to serve and for what purposes that is at the heart of the Benefits Realisation and evaluations, for users must be persuaded that it is in their interest to use the system.

In relation to the Sign Off report, the situation is reversed and the implementation team no longer act as advocates for the system and aim to persuade the readers of the report of the advantages of abandoning the system and thereby condone non-usage. The important thing to note here is that there is no record (verbal or written) of deterioration in capacity of the system itself between the period when Zenith was a success and the time of the declaration of failure. It is possible that end-users’ familiarity and competence even improved during this time. So, not only did the items cited as Zenith successes (Table 4) have to be obfuscated in later reports describing the failure of the system, but also, its negative attributes needed to be highlighted. In other words, success and failure were legitimately ascribed to the same system over time. In simple terms, measurement of success meant seeing ‘the cup half-full’ whereas failure meant seeing ‘the cup half-empty’. So, for example, in an earlier report, it was noted that the Zenith project was going well since 45% of patients on the wards where the care planning system was implemented were receiving an automated care plan and 27 wards were now live; yet later, in the Sign Off report it was noted that 55% of patients were leaving hospital without a care plan ever having been created and the system had been implemented on just over a quarter of wards.

## Legitimacy and issues of power

In keeping with other IS writings on the political nature of organisations, the study also shows that organisational power is exerted in the establishment of which perspectives are the legitimate ones at any given time and this was related to the hierarchy and authority structure within which the actors operated. Above, we discussed the opportunism with respect to the evaluation process that was evident, especially on the part of the system sponsors. Zenith was a success for its sponsors for a good proportion of its life, before becoming a failure. Although the system had been a failure in the eyes of many nurses for some time, it was only when management and the implementation team decided to construct it as thus that the system was officially dubbed a failure. Hence, failure is here more appropriately viewed as a process rather than an event (Table 5).

However, despite wielding the legitimate power in the organisation with respect to the care planning system, the systems sponsors did not manage to persuade the nurses to adopt the technology (due in part, perhaps, to the continuing existence of the dual system). This was because the nurses implicitly challenged the legitimacy of who would act in accordance with improving the delivery of high-quality patient care. This highlights the dissonance between the assumed priorities of management and those of the nurses: the latter displayed an unwillingness to conform to a system that they suspected was introduced to further the auditing practices associated with the unpopular governmental Resource Management Initiative and so they successfully resisted it. Once a decision to withdraw the system seemed likely, the official failure of Zenith was achieved through a number of activities including organised workshops legitimising the collective voicing of problems with the systemFwhere supporters were notably absent. This was followed by the creation of a reporting system dedicated to the collection of data on problemsFdata that were later mobilised to show evidence of failure.

## Conclusion

In the first part of the paper, we have demonstrated the value of the cross-fertilisation of IS and SST literature, by developing a means for furthering our understanding of IS failure. Following a commentary on IS failure, we highlighted the problematic nature of the ‘factor/flaw identification’ literature. Then, building on the work of authors who seek to move beyond developing more tools and techniques to remedy the situation we pointed to the need to re-conceptualise IS failure. We found the SST approach particularly attractive since it has as its core concern an understanding of the dynamics of the society–technology relationship. As a consequence, it has a number of analytical tools offering support for conceptualising success and failure that can be fruitfully applied in IS research. These tools included interpretive flexibility, stabilisation and irreversibility, translation and persuasion, and legitimacy and power. The resultant research issues were then explored in a study of an NIS. Using official and unofficial accounts, the story of the demise of this system was recounted. The analysis and discussion began with an application of the factors for success and failure as tabulated in the second section. This exercise was intended to demonstrate howFin theoryFthe factors equally could be applied to produce an account of a success or failure. The core of the analysis and discussion deconstructed the stages of failure achievement by revisiting the SST themes outlined earlier.

A number of rich insights emerged and represent the contribution of the paper. First, the points made regarding perceptions of failure/success relate to the dual concepts of relevant social groups and interpretive flexibility and tell us that it is not necessary for a technology to have changed in order for it to be perceived differently over time. The ascription of failure/ success to a given technology was a social accomplishment and depended on the perspective of the subject. The case study illustrates how the existence of multifarious criteria for attributing failure enables a failure to be constructed as and when required. It allows us to comprehend why the same IS can be simultaneously constructed as a success or a failure to different groups at one time, and further to this, how the same people reinterpret that system over time, viewing the system positively or negatively, depending on factors beyond the technology itself. This is in contrast to a traditionally technological determinist view, which would have us believe that the technology ‘speaks for itself ’. A commentary on legitimacy and power was provided by illustrating how formal evaluations serve as a political resource. Organisational power can be exerted to circumscribe which perspectives are legitimised at any given time, thereby configuring the decision space. This legitimised view or bias can then be mobilised in support of its supporters.

Next, in relation to the stabilisation process, the study revealed that if an alternative to the proposed technology remains in place, an obligatory point of passage cannot be successfully constructed. Stabilisation will falter and the process of irreversibility does not take place and the technology is thereby liable to failure. As for the concept of translation, we have seen that it is not sufficient for sponsors of a technology to set up a translationFit has to have meaning for (be accepted by) the users, else it will not be effective, despite attempts at persuasion and enrolment. This implies that translations are always contingentFtheir success cannot be assumed Fresistance is always possible. Further to this, the account of persuasion given confirms quite simply that evaluations stress positive aspects of a technology in order to make people accept that technology. Yet, even the same evaluation documents can be altered to accentuate flaws and ‘put a different slant on the story’ as part of an argument to abandon the project. This also confirms our view that failure is best understood as a process rather than an event. In sum, the paper builds on the valuable contribution of Sauer (1993) and others who recognise the importance of organisational context; this adds to Lyytinen & Hirschheim’s (1987) understanding of stakeholder perspectives, by indicating that perspectives shift over time and by emphasising the role of power and politics in the mobilisation of bias.

Despite our best intentions, there are a number of limitations to the paper. First, SST as an explicit approach for understanding technology is a broad approach and we have focussed only on a selection of the tools that it provides. Future research could broaden this agenda by applying other SST tools. This may be realised in connection with the application of other social theories that have been applied within the IS field (such as Structuration Theory or Critical Theory) and their different contributions could be compared. There was no intention here to set up a direct polemic against these or other valuable theories that have also offered insight into IS phenomena. Secondly, and inevitably, we undoubtedly lose something of the rich detail of the lived experience of technology in presenting the case study as a linear narrative. Specifically, we have been unable to do justice to the complexity of the ways in which individual perceptions of failure and success shifted as members of the groups were ‘infected’ by the opinions of others.

Limitations aside, the insights detailed above may well be of value to IS researchers and practitioners. Hopefully, attention will continue to be directed away from the development of more tools and techniques and towards understanding the social context of IS development and use. Practitioners should be made aware of the potential for almost any project to be vulnerable to the attribution of failure and hence must acknowledge the highly political process surrounding IS development.
