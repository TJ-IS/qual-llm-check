# Do I really have to? User acceptance of mandated technology

SA Brown<sup>1</sup>, AP Massey<sup>1</sup>, MM Montoya-Weiss<sup>2</sup> and JR Burkman<sup>3</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Kelley School of Business, Indiana University, Bloomington, IN, USA; <sup>2</sup>College of Management, North Carolina State University, Raleigh, NC, USA; and <sup>3</sup>College of Business Administration, Texas Tech University, Area of ISQS, Lubbock, TX, USA

Extensive research supports the notion that usefulness and ease of use are primary drivers of user intentions to adopt new technology. However, this research has been conducted primarily in environments in which adoption was voluntary. When technology use is mandated, as it is in many organizations, we expect that the underlying relationships of traditional technology acceptance models will be different. In this paper, we discuss our current understanding of technology acceptance, as well as the notion of mandated use. We then discuss a field study conducted in the banking industry to examine technology acceptance models in a mandated use environment. The results indicate that there are, in fact, differences in the underlying relationships of technology acceptance models in this mandatory use situation. The paper concludes with a discussion of the implications for research and practice

## Introduction

Extensive research supports the notion that usefulness and ease of use are primary drivers of user intentions to adopt new information technologies (IT) (eg, Davis, 1989, 1993; Davis et al, 1989; Mathieson, 1991; Adams et al, 1992; Szajna, 1994, 1996; Taylor & Todd, 1995; Davis & Venkatesh, 1996; Lou et al, 2000; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). However, with few exceptions (cf Hartwick & Barki, 1994; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000), this research has been conducted in environments in which adoption was voluntary (eg, students’ use of word processors). Likewise, research on which models of technology acceptance have been based, namely the theory of reasoned action (TRA; Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980), focused entirely on volitional choices (eg, smoking cessation, brushing teeth) and were intended only to explain volitional behaviour. However, many of the behaviours in organizations, particularly those related to technology, are not volitional (Ram & Jung, 1991). For example, the decision to implement a new IT architecture within a firm results in individual users having limited, if any, control over the implications of this decision. It may mean, for example, that only certain software will be available in the future (eg, Lotus Notes rather than MS Exchange), resulting in forced compliance with decisions made by others.

An extensive body of research on the technology acceptance model (TAM) (eg, Davis, 1989; Davis et al,

1989; Mathieson, 1991; Taylor & Todd, 1995) has developed, tested, and supported relationships among beliefs, attitudes, behavioural intention, and usage behaviour. While the relationships have been consistently supported in volitional contexts (see Ajzen, 1991, for a review; see also Sheppard et al, 1988), it is not clear if the same relationships will hold when the behaviour is mandatory. That is, when individuals must perform specific behaviours, the importance of their beliefs and attitudes as antecedents to the performance of those behaviours is likely to be minimized. They might not like performing the mandated behaviour, but they do it anyway, because they are required to do so.

The primary issue explored in this paper is whether the well-documented TAM relationships are appropriate in explaining technology acceptance in mandatory use environments. A mandatory use environment is defined here as one in which users are required to use a specific technology or system in order to keep and perform their jobs. In this environment, we contend that the traditional notion of ‘use’ (ie, physically using the technology) is not the appropriate dependent variable (DeLone & McLean, 1992). Since employees must use the system to perform their job functions, there are no alternatives to actual use. This does, however, raise the question: if individuals must use a system, why do we care about the antecedents to mandated use? As Zuboff (1988) demonstrated, while employees may use the technology, their job satisfaction, feelings toward their supervisors, and loyalty toward the organization can be severely and negatively affected. A further motivation for understanding mandated use lies in the desire to minimize sabotage and unfaithful appropriation of technology, and the resulting costs to organizations associated with such behaviour. A number of studies, for example, have demonstrated that employees will use a technology to perform (and keep) their jobs, but they may also engage in alternative destructive behaviours, which may or may not be intentional (Markus, 1983; Leonard-Barton, 1988; Zuboff, 1988; Davis et al, 1992).

In the following section, we discuss mandatory vs volitional use of technology and the role that attitudes play in a mandatory use environment. We then briefly discuss TAM and related variations of the model that have been used to help explain usage of technology. The models are then compared using data collected from a field study conducted in a mandatory use environment. The paper concludes with a discussion of directions for future research in mandatory use environments.

## Theoretical background

A voluntary use environment is one in which users perceive the technology adoption or use decision to be a wilful choice; a mandated environment is where users perceive use to be organizationally compulsory (Hartwick & Barki, 1994; Agarwal & Prasad, 1997; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). While seemingly a clear distinction, a number of issues surrounding the notion of mandatory vs volitional usage behaviour have been raised in the literature. Some research suggests that there is a continuum of voluntariness (eg, Moore & Benbasat, 1991; Hartwick & Barki, 1994; Karahanna et al, 1999), such that a given adoption decision may seem more or less voluntary to the individuals involved. As examples, results reported by Karahanna et al (1999) and Agarwal & Prasad (1997) indicate that there can be wide variability in user perceptions of voluntariness, even when the system and setting are held constant.

Furthermore, Hartwick & Barki (1994) refute the assumption that there will be little variance in technology use when use is mandated. Instead, they suggest that usage behaviour is variable since employees can vary their extent of use. While our perspective is not entirely inconsistent with this, we do depart on one key point. We agree that usage can be variable in a mandatory environment, but that variability is a function of the degree to which technology is integrated into one’s job function (Melone, 1990). Thus, measuring use in a mandatory environment results in a value that is necessarily highly correlated with job function and not necessarily with any positive or negative effect toward the system.

Hartwick & Barki (1994) also propose that, even in mandated environments, technology use is still fundamentally volitional, ie, users can still choose to use a technology or not. In the case of stand-alone or personal software applications this may well be true. For example, while an organization may mandate the transition from one application to another (eg, Word-Perfect to MS-Word), some users may choose to continue using the previous application despite the fact that it is no longer supported by the organization. However, in the case of a broad scope system or application (eg, the implementation of an ERP system), we propose that employees do not have a decision regarding use. In these types of mandated situations, the system must be used to complete one’s own job tasks that are also tightly integrated with the tasks of multiple other job performers. Here, and as noted above, a high correlation with job function makes ‘working around the technology a non-option. We believe it is empirically and theoretically important to isolate the ends of the continuum, ie, examine a system that is truly mandatory, critically important for performing one’s job, and highly integrated across job functions. As such, we propose that mandatoriness is composed of multiple components, two of which are system necessity to perform one’s job and interdependence of system use.

## Mandated use and attitudes

When use is mandated, a ‘user’s only freedom of choice, assuming that he or she does not want to leave the organization, is how wholeheartedly to accept the innovation’ (Leonard-Barton, 1988, p 604). Employees who do not wholeheartedly accept the innovation can delay or obstruct the implementation, and resent, underutilize or sabotage the new system (Markus, 1983; Kimberly, 1987; Leonard-Barton, 1988; Zuboff, 1988). We believe these reactions are a result of the positive or negative attitudes employees form toward the technology.

Ajzen (1988) describes attitudes as a predisposition to respond favourably or unfavourably to a stimulus. In the IS literature, user responses have typically been evaluated via user satisfaction. As DeLone & McLean (1992) suggest, user satisfaction is ‘probably the most widely used single measure of IS success’ (p 69). Research in this area has measured satisfaction as an overall, single-item construct (eg, Ginzberg, 1981) as well as a multidimensional construct incorporating components of the system outputs and perceptions of the relationship with the IS staff (eg, Bailey & Pearson, 1983; Ives et al, 1983). If we consider user satisfaction to be an attitude, then the stimuli that have been examined include the system output, the IS staff, data quality and security, and participation in the development process (Melone, 1990). In many ways, user satisfaction research to date has assessed the attitude toward the outputs of a system and the IS group, rather than that of using the system. We propose that a neglected stimulus is attitude toward using the system. Thus, when an organization mandates use, attitudes will likely take on heightened importance and thus warrant consideration.

## Technology acceptance

Technology acceptance research has relied upon models that incorporate attitudes, beliefs, and behavioural intentions. The majority of research in this area has been conducted to assess, validate, and extend TAM (eg, Mathieson, 1991; Taylor & Todd, 1995; Venkatesh, 1999; Lou et al, 2000). At its core, TAM states that perceptions of usefulness and ease of use will influence an individual’s intention to use IT, which will ultimately influence actual usage behaviour. According to TAM, perceived usefulness is also influenced by perceived ease of use because, other things being equal, the easier the system is to use, the more useful it will be. Across many empirical tests of TAM, perceived usefulness has consistently been shown to be a strong determinant of behavioural intention, with standardized regression coefficients typically about 0.6 (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000, p 187). Similarly, the ease-of-use–intention relationship has been about 0.2 (eg, Davis et al, 1989; Venkatesh, 2000; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000).

Recently, Venkatesh & Davis (2000) examined an extended version of TAM in both volitional and mandatory contexts. The basic TAM relationships (ie, intention–usage, usefulness–intention, ease-of-use– intention, and ease of use–usefulness) were supported with standardized regression coefficients consistent with past findings. Based on these findings, it seems that, at face value, TAM fares well in both volitional and mandatory contexts. However, we believe that like Hartwick & Barki (1994), Venkatesh & Davis may have captured voluntariness based only on one dimension: necessity. Specifically, Venkatesh & Davis (2000) neither discuss the role that the mandatory technologies had in the day-to-day performance of employee jobs nor the degree to which use was integrated across users. Venkatesh and Davis do, however, report the mean voluntariness measures for both sets of users: 1.2–1.5 for the mandated situations, and 6.2–6.7 for the voluntary situations. This provides prima facie evidence that the systems were indeed perceived by the users to be mandatory and voluntary, respectively. We do not, however, get a sense of the degree to which usage was integrated across users. This begs the question, when a system is the only way to perform one’s job functions in a highly integrated work environment, how explanatory is TAM?

In the original formulation of the model (Davis, 1989; Davis et al, 1989), TAM included attitude. Through studies conducted in volitional environments, it has been demonstrated that the explanatory power of the model is equally good and the model is more parsimonious without the mediating attitude construct (eg, Davis & Venkatesh, 1996; Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). Thus, it has become the norm to exclude the attitude construct from TAM. However, we propose that in a mandated environment, excluding the attitude construct from TAM will not provide an accurate representation of the phenomenon. As discussed earlier, we contend that attitude will be a critical factor in understanding mandated use environments because it represents the degree to which users are satisfied with the system (Melone, 1990).

When adoption is voluntary, attitudes have been shown to correlate with behavioural intention (eg, Davis et al, 1989), and subsequently with use (Sheppard et al, 1988). However, while we believe attitude is a key construct in mandated use environments, an employee’s attitude toward using a technology and his or her intention to use that technology will likely be unrelated. That is, attitudes will not be associated with behavioural intention, as employees will intend to use the system (in order to keep their jobs) regardless of whether they have positive or negative attitudes toward it.

The theory of planned behaviour (TPB; Ajzen, 1991) was developed, in part, to account for situations when performing a behaviour was not entirely under the volitional control of the actor. Together with attitude, subjective norm and perceived behavioural control are said to contribute to an individual’s behavioural intention. Subjective norm reflects the degree to which referent others want the individual to perform a particular behaviour, while perceived behavioural control captures an individual’s perception that he/she has the skills, opportunities, and resources necessary to engage in a particular behaviour. Taylor & Todd (1995) presented the decomposed theory of planned behaviour (DTPB) in which they found attitude, subjective norm, and perceived behavioural control each contribute to behavioural intention. Further, they demonstrated that both the TPB and DTPB explained behavioural intention better than TAM. Consistent with those results, we anticipate that subjective norm and perceived behavioural control will have significant influence on behavioural intention in a mandated use environment. We differ, however, in that we anticipate that the relationship between attitude and behavioural intention will be nonsignificant.

Model 2 presents the original formulation of TAM, which includes the mediating attitude construct. Lastly, Model 3 incorporates perceived behavioural control and subjective norm as additional constructs influencing behavioural intention. ## Research method

The context for this research is a \$5 billion multi-bank holding company (BHC) formed in the early 1990s. Among the comprehensive range of financial services offered by BHC are commercial and retail banking trust, brokerage, correspondent banking, and insurance. Our interest is in the commercial and retail banking services provided through the 30 bank affiliates that BHC acquired during the 1990s. Crossing three Midwestern States in the USA, the affiliate banks employ approximately 3000 people in nearly 130 office locations.

The target markets, number of banking offices, and total assets vary significantly across the affiliate banks. The tradition of each affiliate is in community banking, exemplified by close and long-standing customer ties. Throughout the acquisitions, BHC purposefully supported each affiliate’s retention of its autonomy. As such, each affiliate continued to operate under its own original name, president, board and charter. Marketing and sales decisions in terms of product and service offerings were also left to the individual banks. Through this independence, the affiliates were able to maintain their community-banking heritage.

Historically, BHC outsourced most of its data processing needs, while the various affiliates were free to choose whatever platforms and banking systems they felt met their specific needs. In an effort to improve internal efficiencies, BHC created an IS subsidiary responsible for data processing and operations. Subsequently, the IS subsidiary developed a plan for standardizing the hardware and software platform across all bank affiliates. The newly formed IS subsidiary was charged with providing support, installing Windows-based PCs and LANs, developing a WAN infrastructure, converting the affiliate banks to a standardized computer banking system (CBS), and training employees to use the new CBS platform. This study focuses on the mandatory adoption of CBS in the bank affiliates.

## Instrument development

We developed a survey to capture each of the model constructs shown in Figure 1. The items used to measure each construct are presented in the Appendix. The items measuring perceived usefulness, ease of use, and behavioural intention were derived from Davis et al (1989) and Moore & Benbasat (1991). The items measuring perceived behavioural control and subjective norm were derived from Taylor & Todd (1995). The attitude items were drawn from Ajzen & Fishbein (1980).

## Data collection

We conducted a mail survey of all employees in the four most recently converted BHC bank affiliates. In total 195 surveys were distributed and 107 usable responses were received, resulting in a good overall response rate of 55% (Babbie, 1990). Table 1 provides details regarding the job categories of the respondent pool. The majority of respondents were from among employees whose primary function is to interface directly with customers, ie, tellers and lenders who must use CBS on a daily basis in order to perform their job tasks. Managerial (eg, loan officers) and support staff use CBS on a daily or weekly basis for report generation or problem resolution. Table 2 provides details regarding the distribution of respondents by bank.

Perceived usefulness was measured with six items with an alpha value of 0.98. The five-item scale measuring ease of use had a Cronbach alpha reliability of 0.88. Perceived behavioural control, measured with a twoitem scale, had a Cronbach alpha of 0.88. Subjective norm was measured with a two-item scale and had a Cronbach alpha of 0.85. The attitude scale consisted of four items with a reliability score of 0.95. Behavioural intention was measured using three items and a resulting alpha of 0.91. The confirmatory factor analysis is presented in Table 3, while the descriptive statistics are shown in Table 4.

In addition to our survey instrument, we conducted multiple semi-structured individual interviews and focus group interviews with various stakeholders (ie, senior affiliate managers, operations managers, and day-to-day system users). The objective of the interviews was to ascertain the stakeholders’ perspectives regarding CBS and the conversion processes.

## Mandatory adoption at BHC

In this study, all signals indicated that use of the CBS was mandatory. Employee perceptions are typically based on the messages from senior management, the availability of alternatives, or any number of signals that employees receive (Moore & Benbasat, 1991; Hartwick & Barki, 1994; Karahanna et al, 1999). For each affiliate undergoing conversion to CBS, senior managers were present at the conversion kick-off meetings in which the cutover date was announced. During the 60 days prior to cutover, upgrades to the existing LAN or WAN infrastructure were made and data were mapped from the banking system currently in place at each affiliate into the CBS format (eg, customer records). During this period, it was repeatedly emphasized to employees that they would begin using CBS on the cutover date. Employees also received week-long training sessions according to job function. Most importantly, it was clear that there were no alternatives to using CBS. On the weekend of the cutover, the old system was completely removed and the new system completely installed. At that moment in time, employees had no other options. As one end-user said: ‘You had to be ready Monday. The system changed and how I did my job changed. There was no going back.’ It is important to note that due to the nature of the transactions performed in their jobs (ie, financial transactions) and security restrictions, it was not possible for a bank employee to perform their work manually and have another employee enter it. Nor was it possible to perform job functions in a manual or alternative automated environment. All transactions had to be associated with the person who performed them and entered into CBS at the time they were performed. In order to perform their job functions, they had to use CBS.

## Discussion

In this study, we compare TAM with and without the attitude construct and the related TPB model in terms of their contribution to understanding technology acceptance in a mandatory use context. The results show a somewhat different pattern of relationships in the mandated environment than we have seen in prior research on voluntary adoption contexts. We discuss the implications of each of the three models in the following.

First, when we consider TAM without the mediating attitude construct (Figure 2) we see that the basic relationships (usefulness–intention, ease-of-use–intention, and ease-of-use–usefulness) were supported. However, unlike past research findings, our results show that ease of use was the primary determinant of behavioura intention, with perceived usefulness a significant secondary determinant. This is in contrast with much prior research in voluntary settings that suggests perceived usefulness emerges as the primary antecedent to behavioural intention (Davis, 1993; Venkatesh, 1999). particularly when use is extrinsically motivated (Davis et al, 1992). If we believe that behavioural intention is important under conditions of mandated use, these results would suggest that organizations should emphasize ease of use since it is the primary concern of the employees. TAM has been shown to assist in making accurate predictions of behaviour in situations where people can exert a choice (adopt or reject), or in situations where users can vary the extent of use (voluntariness along a continuum). However, our results suggest that when there is no choice (except to leave the organization) TAM relationships provide limited explanations of acceptance, and perhaps misleading guidance to organizations. In fact, our interviews revealed that, in general, end-users did not perceive CBS as easy to use. As examples, a loan officer commented, ‘Compared to our old system, it isn’t as easy to record a customer’s information and process a loan application.’ Another operational manager observed, ‘I’ve had a lot of problems generating the reports I need. It just isn’t clear to me how to pull data together from different sources and generate a report using CBS.’ A teller added, ‘I’ll just have to muddle my way through using the new interface.

Secondly, when we consider TAM with the mediating attitude construct (Figure 3), our results show that this model also does not fare well when usage is mandated. Specifically, the relationship between attitude and behavioural intention, which is assumed to underlie the model, is not significant. Further, while a central tenet of technology acceptance research is that perceived usefulness is a key driver of behavioural intention (Venkatesh, 1999) this is not the case in the mandated environment. And, while Venkatesh (1999) suggests that intrinsic motivation is a key factor in elevating the importance of ease of use in explaining behavioural intention, our results suggest that extrinsic motivation (ie, being told one must use a technology) may play an equally important role. Thus, the relationships we have come to expect when using the original formulation of TAM (Davis et al, 1989) do not replicate when usage is truly mandated. Given that the relationship between attitude and behavioural intention is not significant, it indicates that the feelings employees have about using the system are not influencing their intention to continue using it. In our study, in order to perform their job functions, employees were using the system, and we can assume they would intend to continue using the system as long as they planned to continue working for the bank.

The results of Model 2 (Figure 3) show us that usefulness is the key antecedent of attitude. In volitional settings, perceived usefulness is the key antecedent of behavioural intention (eg, Davis et al, 1989; Venkatesh, 1999). In mandatory usage settings, perceived usefulness continues to be important, but for a different reason: to encourage positive attitudes about use. Our interviews revealed that perceptions of usefulness and consequently attitudes varied. Senior managers described usefulness in terms of productivity gains or efficiencies. For example, one manager noted that, ‘CBS will provide some efficiencies for us by providing data processing services that we could never afford’. Conversely, endusers such as loan officers and tellers described usefulness in terms of how CBS impacted customer service. One end-user noted, ‘In the past we’ve prided ourselves on providing timely, accurate, and excellent customer service. With CBS, we’ve gone from excellence to mediocrity.’ A loan officer commented, ‘In training, we were told ‘‘yes, CBS will work like your current system [referring to home equity loans]’’. Now, they are saying ‘‘no, it won’t work that way’’. Our old system worked just fine. The way it changed just isn’t good for the customer.’

Finally, results of Model 3 (Figure 4) show that the relationships between perceived usefulness–behavioural intention and attitude–behavioural intention continue to be nonsignificant. The results indicate that perceived behavioural control and subjective norm influence intentions and explain over half the variance in behavioural intention. Users intend to use the system because they are mandated to do soFperceived behavioural control and subjective norm serve to reinforce this intention. Specifically, perceived behavioural control reflects beliefs regarding access to facilitating conditions needed to perform a behaviour (Triandis, 1980). In our study, employees were provided time away from their regular jobs to undergo training on the functionality and use of CBS. As evidence, a senior manager noted, ‘All our employees, from the president on down to tellers, were given time away from their regular duties for training. Training varied based on job function, but it was anywhere from 2 to 5 days. Training is intended to increase the confidence or selfefficacy of an individual in performing his/her job functions using a new system (Bandura, 1977, 1986; Compeau & Higgins, 1995). Both factors (facilitating conditions and self-efficacy) contribute to beliefs about perceived behavioural control, reinforcing behavioural intentions. Similarly, subjective norm reflects what an employee believes others (peers or superiors) think he/ she should do. Taylor & Todd (1995) suggest that the link between subjective norm and behavioural intention may be due to perceptions of the real consequences associated with use (or non-use). Not using CBS, as mandated by superiors, would result in an employee not performing his/her job functions (with the potential to negatively impact the work of others), thus likely leading to dismissal from the organization. As one loan officer commented: ‘It was pretty clear when they gave me time-off to go for training, [management] thought this was important. I’m willing to accept change to do my job. But, it doesn’t make sense to me when our old system worked just fine.

In concert, our results show a different pattern of relationships in a mandated environment compared to volitional contexts. The key findings of our study are the relationship between perceived usefulness and attitude and the absence of a relationship between attitude and behavioural intention. The real question concerns the implications of the usefulness–attitude relationship in a mandatory use situation. In a volitional situation (such as the case of choosing among alternative software applications), optional training, informal ‘push’ announcements concerning availability, or minimal management involvement may be sufficient. That is, users themselves will determine whether or not they intend to adopt the technology in question. The results of adoption (or lack of adoption) may not affect others or the organization as a whole. Clearly, in a mandated adoption situation, it is possible to have an ‘effective’ information system without positive attitudes, or satisfaction, on the part of the users (Melone, 1990). However, the consequences of negative attitudes are potentially profound in a mandatory situation. Attitudes can have a significant influence on an individual’s perception of the work environment and organization (eg, Zuboff, 1988). For example, even though employees must use the technology, negative attitudes about the technology may influence users’ interpretations and attributions related to the reasons for installations of the technology (Pratkanis, 1988). Our focus group interviews revealed that affiliate employees believed that CBS represented BHC’s attempt to reduce the autonomy of the affiliates and potentially the size of the workforce. As an example, one senior affiliate manager commented, ‘I don’t see how standardization across all affiliates won’t affect our ability to introduce new products and services for our customers.’ Regardless of whether or not this was BHC’s intent, the point remains that negative attributions can arise, leading to negative repercussions. Some examples of negative consequences include sabotage or employee separation from the organization.

Thus, in a mandatory setting, the organization must work to engender positive attitudes toward the technology and its use. This is particularly true if the technology alters or tightly links the work activities of multiple users. If the formation of initial attitudes is driven by perceptions of usefulness (and to a lesser degree ease of use), mechanisms must be put in place to support this relationship. Examples of supporting mechanisms include training, formation of user groups, formal announcements, testimonials and managerial support. The need for such mechanisms is particularly pertinent when the system replaces an existing one that users are familiar and satisfied with (as was the case with CBS). Using such mechanisms to convey how the new systems will enhance one’s performance seems essential to the formation of positive attitudes. In our study, a review of the CBS training programme and materials revealed an exclusive focus on technical system characteristics (input and functionality). There was no holistic view of CBS or an overarching model of how all of the pieces (modules) fit together relative to the bank affiliate’s typical operations and needs or a vision of why this was important for each affiliate. When attempting to engender positive attitudes, training programmes will need to go beyond knowing which ‘button to push’. Instead, users need to know why they are pushing this button, how pushing this button will change how they perform their jobs, and how pushing this button will impact others’ job performance. It is worth noting that, following this study, the CBS training programme underwent a significant revision and BHC formed a CBS user’s group, consisting of representatives from each of the converted affiliates. The user’s group meets periodically to discuss technical and/or operational problems and has played an advisory role for affiliates undergoing conversion to CBS.

In sum, in a volitional setting, when perceptions of usefulness are low, the result is simply the intention (and subsequent behaviour) not to adopt. Conversely, when use is mandated, but perceptions of usefulness are low leading to negative attitudes, the consequences may be far reaching. The results of our exploratory study suggest that behaviour in a mandated setting is more complex than technology ‘use’. More research is needed to incorporate various attitude–behaviour response modes that may provide a richer characterization of mandatory environments (Melone, 1990). In addition, while this study shows a strong usefulness–attitude relationship, there are likely additional antecedents that will assist in explaining how users form initial attitudes and how those attitudes can be modified over time.

## Limitations

This study examined the efficacy of well-documented technology acceptance relationships in a truly mandatory use context. Our results suggest some changes in construct relationships. However, the study is not without limitations. First, our study was limited to members of one overall organization, using one system.

While that means variability is not due to the system in question, it does raise the issue of generalizability. The fact that respondents came from four different subsidiary banks, each with its own culture and experiences, does provide some support for the generalizability to other organizations. Recall that each affiliate retained autonomy and operated under its original name, president, board and charter. In essence, then, these subsidiaries represented four distinct banking organizations. Future research is needed to examine mandatory use in industries other than banking, as well as in a cross-section of organizations.

Secondly, the focus of this study was limited to the core technology acceptance constructs. Future research is needed to expand the models to include constructs such as personal innovativeness (Agarwal & Prasad, 1998) cognitive absorption (Agarwal & Karahanna, 2000), and experience and job relevance (Venkatesh & Davis, 2000). Further, additional new constructs, as well as new conceptualizations of existing constructs, may be needed to further reflect the nature of adoption when use is mandated.

## Future directions

The results of this study highlight a number of directions for future research. First, in mandatory environments, the current conceptualization of perceived usefulness may not be appropriate. Consider Davis et al’s (1992) definition of extrinsic motivation: ‘performance of an activity because it is perceived to be instrumental in achieving valued outcomes that are distinct from the activity itself, such as improved job performance, pay or promotions’ (p 1112). This definition has been used as the basis for developing the commonly used items for assessing perceived usefulness, which emphasize job performance. If, however, the valued outcome of a mandated adoption is not better job performance, but rather it is to keep one’s job, then the items currently used will not adequately reflect this component of usefulness. If, as we have argued, behavioural intention in the mandatory situation is really an intention to keep one’s job, then the usefulness construct should be expanded. It is quite likely, in fact, that if the measures of perceived usefulness are expanded to more completely capture the domain of the construct, usefulness may become a direct antecedent to behavioural intention in the mandated environment.

Second, in order to truly understand the nuances of voluntary and mandatory system use, we must better understand the nature of voluntariness and mandatoriness. We propose that mandatoriness of systems be assessed using multiple dimensions, two of which are system necessity to complete one’s job and interdependence of system use. As shown in Figure 5, we propose that as both the degree of necessity and interdependence increase, the systems of interest become more mandatory, and thus attitude becomes the dependent variable of interest. Future research should be directed at assessing necessity and interdependence, and their interaction. Likewise, additional measures of mandatoriness need to be developed to capture the complexity of the construct.

Third, while the conceptualization of mandatoriness may be one reason that our results differ from Venkatesh & Davis (1999), it is possible that some contextual factors might be influential in our results. One such factor concerns the power and political issues associated with implementation (eg, Markus, 1983). The purpose of Venkatesh and Davis was not to assess the importance of resistance or other negatively perceived user behaviours. In our study, on the other hand, there is qualitative evidence that the system implementation was perceived to be a harbinger of power balance changes.

Markus (1983) suggests that a political view of implementation is associated with a changing balance of power in the organization. Recall that while the affiliate banks had been acquired by BHC, they had been allowed to keep their presidents, products and systems. The move toward a standardized, integrated, and centralized system was perceived by the affiliates as the beginning of a loss of autonomy. Similar to the view of the accountants in Markus’s study, our interviews revealed that the members of the BHC IS group could not understand the resistance on the part of the affiliates. Resistance is the result of an interaction between the system design features and the organizational distribution of power. Ease of use and usefulness are factors inherent to the system, and thus represent design features. Subjective norm and perceived behavioural control are factors inherent to the person. Thus, current technology acceptance models account for system and individual factors, but do not incorporate factors associated with the distribution of power. We could refer to these as factors inherent in the environment. According to Markus (1983, p 438) ‘Resistancegenerating conditions are mismatches between the patterns of interaction prescribed by a system and the patterns that already exist in the setting into which the system is introduced.’ Bloomfield & Coombs (1992) further point out that a relational view of power is likely to produce greater insights than one that takes a more possessive (eg, who has power) approach. Finally, Cendon & Jarvenpaa (2001) indicate that a major source of power is in the resources that are highly valued in the organization. In the information economy, access to information and control of information systems will certainly be associated with organizational power. Thus, to capture the extent to which resistance is an influential factor in technology acceptance, we need to assess the degree to which the patterns of interaction are impacted. As such, items that capture factors associated with changes in reporting relationships, work relationships, and information access would seem to be most appropriate.

In terms of current models of technology acceptance, we propose that the environmental factors would be antecedents to both attitude and intention. The more the balance of power changes, the greater the likelihood that attitudes toward using the system will be negative. Users may perceive that their power diminishes with use of the system. In an extreme case, we might expect that intention to use the system will also decrease, as a means of exerting power. There is no reason to believe that the environmental factors will impact factors inherent to the individual (ie, subjective norm, perceived behavioural control) or factors inherent to the system (ie, ease of use, usefulness). Future research is needed to explore how environmental and political factors impact technology acceptance attitudes and behaviours.

Finally, this study contributes to the on-going debate in IS regarding the ‘right’ dependent variable (eg, DeLone & McLean, 1992). If an employee’s attitude is not related to his/her intention to use technology, what does it influence? One possibility is to examine the intention to turn over as a result of the system implementation. Under situations of forced compliance, individuals rationalize their behaviour based on the rewards of compliance or the consequences of noncompliance (Festinger, 1957; Beckmann & Irle, 1985). When individuals are forced to use a system, they are likely to rationalize their actions based on the consequences associated with it, eg, performance evaluations, promotion/firing. This in turn suggests that individuals do not need to alter their beliefs about the technology in order to align beliefs with actions. They can continue to believe that the behaviour (ie, using this technology) is not positive, yet still perform the behaviour. Thus, in mandatory use contexts, intention to perform a behaviour may be related more to beliefs about the associated rewards and punishments than to beliefs about the technology itself. Further, the behavioural intention in question may very well be the intention to avoid punishment or receive rewards. Thus, future research should attempt to uncover the rewards and punishments that the employees perceive will result from their use or non-use of a mandatory system. It is possible that the intention to avoid punishment or receive rewards may far outweigh the intention to use the technology.

For practice, our results suggest that the antecedents to attitude toward use and behavioural intention are different in a truly mandated environment. Organizations wishing to foster positive employee attitudes toward a new system should focus on expounding the benefits of use (ie, usefulness). Simultaneously, facilitating conditions and the positive influence of peers and superiors should enhance perceived behavioural control and subjective norm as determinants of an employee’s intention to use the system and not leave the organization. As discussed above, more research is needed to understand the impact of attitudes and intention on issues such as employee productivity, satisfaction and turnover. Perhaps the most important idea for practice is that the mandatory environment is different from the volitional environment, and should be treated as such.

In conclusion, it appears that attitudes matter more than intentions when technology use is mandated. Our results suggest that TAM and related variations of the model do not generalize directly to mandatory use situations. It is important to identify the boundaries of existing theoretical models and to identify the factors important in different contexts. From a practical perspective, examining intentions and their antecedents may cause organizations to focus on the wrong factors when attempting to motivate technology acceptance and use. This research proposes that attitudes should be the focus in mandatory use environments. As more and more organizations implement broad-scope or packaged systems (like ERP systems) that are tightly integrated within and across job functions, further research is needed to increase our understanding of the relationships among beliefs, attitudes and behaviours in such settings.
