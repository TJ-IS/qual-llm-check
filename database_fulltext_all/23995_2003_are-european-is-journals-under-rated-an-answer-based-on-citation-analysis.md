## Abstract

A concern that the quality of European IS journals has been under-rated by existing studies was recently addressed in the IS community. In this study, an objective method based on a Citation Analysis was employed to re-examine the quality of four established European IS journals as compared to 11 other wellrecognized IS journals. Extensive citation data were collected to derive eight indices as the measures of journal quality. Our research results indicate that the quality of the four leading European IS journals is quite comparable to that of other well-known IS journals reported in prior studies.

The evaluations of research quality are influenced by attitudes concerning the journals publishing the research articles (Cooper et al., 1993). In addition, publications in prestigious journals have significant influence on academic peer recognition, departmental and institutional rankings, tenure and promotion decisions, and the merit increase in faculty compensation. Hence, scholars are understandably interested in assessing the relative quality and prestige of the journals in their fields of inquiry (Garfield, 1979; Johnson & Podsakoff, 1994).

The quality rankings of IS and IS-related journals have been evaluated and reported by many previous studies (e.g., Gillenson & Stutz, 1991; Holsapple et al., 1993, 1994; Walstrom et al., 1995; Hardgrave & Walstrom, 1997). However, a concern was addressed recently that the quality of some leading European IS journals has been either excluded or under-rated in these previous studies; thus, there is a need to re-examine the quality of European IS journals as compared to other well-recognized IS journals.

## Quality evaluations on IS research and journal publications

Disciplinary reviews in IS have addressed a variety of worthwhile issues over the past two decades. Athey & Plotnicki (2000) examined the research productivity of each IS faculty and individual institution based on the number of publications appearing in a set of top-tier IS journals. Hamilton & Ives (1982) explored several issues, including the principal sources of information used by IS researchers, the extent to which IS discipline relies on the accumulation of previously published knowledge, and the delay time between the publication of IS articles and their citations. Gillenson & Stutz (1991) studied the academic value of writing books in the IS discipline. In addition, faculty opinions on the rankings of IS and ISrelated journals were collected and analyzed (Walstrom et al., 1995; Hardgrave & Walstrom, 1997). Moreover, Holsapple et al., (1993) and Nord & Nord (1995) proposed first and second tiers of IS and IS-related journals based on their relative quality.

Among previous studies on quality rankings of IS and IS-related journals, one of the approaches employed (e.g., Holsapple et al., 1994) is a Citation Analysis. Since ‘research firmly based upon one or more past scientific achievements, y as supplying the foundation for its further practice y (Kuhn, 1996, p.10)’, the number of citations that a target journal receives has been considered an objective measure of its academic impact or influence (Brown & Gardner, 1985; Salancik, 1986). As further maintained by Cote et al. (1991), analyzing citations received by a target journal is quite useful in identifying a journal’s quality and its contributions to others.

Therefore, this study employs Citation Analysis to evaluate the quality of four European IS journals (i.e., European Journal of Information Systems, Information Systems Journal, Journal of Information Technology, and Journal of Strategic Information Systems) with a comparison to 11 other well-recognized IS journals selected from the journal ranking lists reported in six previous studies. Details on journal selection, research methodology, and study results are reported in the next two sections, followed by a section on discussing these study results. Conclusion is given in the final section.

## Research methodology

## Journal selection

Four European IS journals (see Table 1) were preidentified based on the concern addressed in the beginning of this paper and a suggestion given by a highly regarded European scholar. These four European IS journals are compared to 11 other ‘well-recognized IS journals’ selected based on journal ranking results reported in six previous studies (Gillenson & Stutz, 1991; Holsapple et al., 1994; Walstrom et al., 1995; Hardgrave & Walstrom, 1997; Whitman et al., 1999; Mylonopoulos & Theoharakis, 2001).

Note that it becomes increasingly difficult to determine if a journal is European or not solely based on its publisher or editors. As a result of global networking, a publisher may have its operations in multiple regions, and a journal’s editorial board may include scholars from more than one continent. Hence, whether a journal is classified as European or not could be very much based on the general perception of scholars in the field. The four European IS Journals included in this study are in fact identified based on the comments of two highly regarded European scholars.

The selection process for well-recognized IS journals was carried out in the following manner. First, an arithmetic mean of each journal’s rankings from six previous studies was used as the ‘aggregate ranking score’ to create a sorted joint ‘journal ranking list,’ which includes 114 journals. Since this joint ranking list is quite long, only the top 60 journals were preselected (see Appendix A). Then, to confine our study to the quality of ‘IS only’ journals, two additional criteria were used to reach our final selection. They are: (1) only those journals with an IS focus are selected, that is, journals with a multidiscipline or non-IS nature (e.g., Decision Sciences, Academy of Management Review, Management Science, etc.) are excluded; (2) the citation data of the selected journal must exist and be sufficient. In the end, 11 wellrecognized IS journals were selected from the joint journal ranking list. Adding the four preidentified European IS journals, altogether 15 IS journals were evaluated in this study (see Table 1).

## Data collection

Primarily, all citation data made to each target article published in the 15 journals must be collected. Thus, a list of target articles published in each journal from 1995 to 1998 was compiled. Altogether there are 2937 target articles analyzed in this study. The number of references (i.e., citing counts) provided in each target article was also recorded and then summarized for each journal year.

A second list of citations (i.e., cited counts) made to each target article by other published articles was also compiled. To ensure a reasonable citation history for analysis, a 2-year lag time was used to collect the cited counts since the modal elapsed time between IS article publication and citation was found to be approximately 2 years (Hamilton & Ives, 1982); in other words, cited counts were collected from those articles published from 1997 to 2000.

All citation data were collected from the Social Science Citation Index (SSCI) and the Science Citation Index (SCI), the two primary sources of citation data that cover a broad range of publications. Our data collection shows that altogether 7725 citations were made to the 2937 target articles. Among all target articles, further classifications were made for those target articles that received either no citations or at least 20 citations. The number of self-citations (see definition in a later section) for each target article was also recorded. Finally, all annual aggregates for the above citation data were compiled for each of the 15 journals. Appendix B summarizes the collected citation data for each journal.

## Quality index derivation

Based on the collected citation data, eight citation-based indices of journal quality were derived since journal quality is a multifaceted concept and could be reflected by different measurements (Zinkhan & Leigh, 1999; Mylonopoulos & Theoharakis, 2001). Results of the citation-based indices for each journal are reported in Appendix C. A brief description of each quality index is provided below.

Citations Per Article: This index is the average number of citations received per target article published in a particular year. It is an appropriate index to assess journal quality in terms of its academic contribution, since it eliminates the size (i.e., number of articles published) effect of a journal (Garfield, 1979).

Un-cited Ratio: This ratio is the percentage of the target articles published in a particular year that have never received any citation. The higher the ratio, the less reputation the journal that publishes the target articles.

20+ Citations Ratio: This ratio is the percentage of the target articles published in a particular year that have received at least 20 citations. A high value of this ratio indicates the leadership role played by the target journal in the field.

Annual Mean Citations Per Article: This index provides a normalized quality measurement of the target article based on the number of years elapsed since its publication (e.g., since citation data are collected up to year 2000, a target article published in 1995 will have 5 years elapsed since its publication). This index is derived by dividing the Citations Per Article (see aforementioned definition) by the number of years elapsed since publication of the target articles; thus, the time effect on the number of citations received by the target articles will be removed.

Cited-to-Citing Ratio: Knowledge flows among journals are considered a fundamental measurement of a journal’s scientific status and prestige in the social exchange model (Zinkhan et al., 1992; Johnson & Podsakoff, 1994). As noted, journals that emphasize basic research are likely to be cited more often than they cite others and they are valued as ‘knowledge sources’; in contrast, applied journals are more likely to cite others than they are cited and they are perceived as ‘knowledge storers’ (Zinkhan & Leigh, 1999). Given such connotations, the Cited-to-Citing Ratio of a journal could indicate that the journal is a knowledge source if its value is high, a knowledge storer if its value is low (Zinkhan & Leigh, 1999).

Self-Citations Ratio: If any author of the citing article is one of the authors of the cited article (i.e., the target article), it is a self-citation. The Self-Citations Ratio is derived for each year by dividing the number of selfcitations made to the target articles by the total number of citations received by the target articles published in that specific year.

Current Article Impact: This index measures the frequency that the articles in the journal have been cited over the most recent 2-year period (Garfield, 1979). It eliminates the citation advantage of those journals that are published more frequently (Kerin, 1996). The index for each reference year is derived by dividing the number of citations made to the target articles published during the most recent 2 years prior to the reference year by the number of the target articles published during the same time period. The higher the index value, the better academic currency impact the journal has.

Cited Half-life: This index measures the degree to which the journal’s archives continue to affect new knowledge production (Garfield, 1979). Knowledge use, measured by rate of the citations made to the articles, normally declines as the articles age. Hence, in general, the articles that have been frequently cited over a long period of time are considered distinctive and the journals that carry a large number of such long-lasting articles often receive academic recognition for their quality and impact (Zinkhan & Leigh, 1999). This index for a reference year is determined by the number of years back from the reference year that account for 50% of the total citations made in the reference year to the journal’s target articles. For example, the index value for EJIS in the year 1998 is 3.00 (see Appendix C); it means that half of the citations that were made to the EJIS target articles by other articles published in year 1998 cited the EJIS target articles that were published in either 1996, 1997, or 1998; the other half cited the EJIS target articles published in 1995.

## Test of significant differences

Before calculating the test statistics for mean differences across the groups, we employed Barlett’s test of sphericity to examine whether there is a significant, intercorrelation among the citation-based indices. The results are significant, which suggests that there exists an intercorrelation among the indices. Hence, it is appropriate for this study to conduct a multivariate test instead of several separate univariate tests (Hair et al., 1992; Cooper & Schindler, 2000).

Two sessions of multivariate test were conducted to examine the significant differences in the scores of the citation-based indices among the journals included in this study. The first session compares the scores of the eight indices between the group of 11 well-recognized IS journals and the group of four European IS journals. The second session compares the scores of the eight indices among all 15 journals.

Note that since Current Article Impact and Cited Halflife are based on the year when the target articles were cited rather than the year when the target articles were published (see Appendix C), two separate multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) were conducted in each session of the multivariate test. The First MANOVA includes six indices – Citation Per Article, Un-Cited Ratio, 20+ Citations Ratio, Annual Mean Citations Per Article, Cited-to-Citing Ratio, and Self-Citations Ratio; the Second MANOVA includes the remaining two indices – Current Article Impact and Cited Half-life. Since the citation data were collected for four years (i.e., 1995–1998), there are four samples for each journal in the First MANOVA. Nevertheless, there are only three samples for each journal in the Second MANOVA because the indices of Current Article Impact for the year 2000 are not available (see Appendix C).

It is also worth noting that with regard to the Uncited Ratio, two European IS journals – EJIS and ISJ – have higher quality than some wellrecognized IS journal that was included in several previous studies.

## Discussion

Questionnaire Survey and Citation Analysis are the two major research approaches used by IS scholars to analyze journal quality and ranking. In the past, five of the six journal quality studies employed the Questionnaire Survey by collecting data from academicians and practitioners to evaluate and rank IS and IS-related journals (Gillenson & Stutz, 1991; Walstrom et al., 1995; Hardgrave & Walstrom, 1997; Whitman et $a l . ,$ 1999; Mylonopoulos & Theoharakis, 2001). Only one of the six previous studies (i.e., Holsapple et al., 1994) employed Citation Analysis to evaluate the journal quality.

Both Questionnaire Survey and Citation Analysis have some weaknesses. Data collected through Questionnaire Survey are primarily a reflection of the participants’ perceptions about the journals. Thus, the survey approach may be affected by the subjective stances the participants choose to adopt (Mylonopoulos & Theoharakis, 2001) and by some inherent measurement biases (Cooper et al., 1993). In fact, four of the five previous studies employing Questionnaire Survey had collected data from the participants in North America only.

On the other hand, while Citation Analysis is viewed as more objective than Questionnaire Survey, Citation Analysis may have some biases as well. Most past Citation Analysis research was based on the references provided in the articles published in a given set of ‘base journals’ (Cote et al., 1991), which in fact confines the scope of analysis (Tahai & Meyer, 1999). Since the European IS journals are relatively new (i.e., EJIS, ISJ, and JSIS started in the early 1990s) to the IS field, they were not available to be considered as the ‘base journals’ in the previous Citation Analysis studies (Holsapple et al., 1994).

Therefore, a rigorous approach that is immune to the above weaknesses should be employed to re-examine the IS journal quality and ranking. In contrast, the Citation Analysis conducted in this study did not use the references from a predefined set of ‘base journals’ as a primary source to pinpoint the journals to be evaluated. Rather, we directly identified 15 IS journals to be evaluated and collected the citations made to the articles published in these journals. Hence, the potential bias due to the selection of ‘base journals’ did not exist in our study. In fact, this approach can be easily extended to cover and evaluate any journals that are of the study interest.

Our study results show that the well-recognized and the European IS journals are significantly different in Cited-to-Citing Ratio and Self-Citations Ratio. As compared to the 11 well-recognized IS journals, the four European IS journals have a relatively lower value in Cited-to-Citing Ratio. It might be because that the European IS journals have published a more applied research than the basic research. However, its actual cause deserves some further research and ought to be investigated by a separate study. Such study, if conducted, would require a detailed content analysis on each article published in the journals and an examination of each journal’s publication policy $( \mathrm { e . g . } ,$ references limit, themes of research subjects) that may have some impact on the resultant Cited-to-Citing Ratio.

Secondly, our results show that European IS journals have a relatively high Self-Citations Ratio. This could be due to the fact that most researchers seek publication in the journals that are published in nearby geographic regions. Researchers tend to be more confident in and comfortable with publishing their studies in the journals that they really know. Similar phenomenon has been observed in the management discipline that European researchers publish in European journals more often than in North American journals (Collin et $a l . ,$ 1996). In addition, there are fewer European IS journals than American ones. These reasons may help justify the high Self-Citations Ratio in European IS journals. However, a firm interpretation can only be supported with additional investigations.

By and large, our study does show that the four European IS journals are second to the top three IS journals (i.e., MISQ, ISR, and CACM) as ranked by virtually all previous studies. To be specific, our research results are consistent with early findings in the management discipline (Collin et al., 1996). That is, researchers from both Europe and North America tend to cite more North American journals than European ones. Thus, as long as such phenomenon continues, it will be quite difficult for European journals to outrank their North American counterparts. Nevertheless, further research is needed to explore the underlying reasons (e.g., availability of European journals to North American researchers and vice versa).

## A note on possible violations of MANOVA assumptions and their effects

Properties of the statistical tests in MANOVA are derived or obtained by making three assumptions (Hair et $a l . ,$ 1992; Sharma, 1996): (a) each observation is independent of other observations, (b) the data come from a multivariate normal distribution, and (c) the covariance matrices for all groups are equal. Among these three assumptions, the first one is considered the most basic and critical assumption to MANOVA analysis (Hair et $a l . ,$ 1992). In this study, this independence assumption is quite true since the citations made to a particular target article published in a particular year are highly independent of those citations made to any target articles published in any years.

The eight citation-based indices derived in this study may not be multivariate normal since the indices demonstrate some moderate univariate non-normality. That is, skewness and kurtosis values of three indices, Citations Per Article, 20+ Citations Ratio, and Cited-to-Citing Ratio, are between 2.0 – 4.5 and between 5.0 –

21.0, respectively. Violation of the normality assumption may affect the power of test statistics (Olson, 1974). However, in the First MANOVA (which involves Citations Per Article, 20+ Citations Ratio, and Cited-to-Citing Ratio) of both the first and second session of multivariate test, significant results were found (see Tables 4 and 6).

In each MANOVA conducted in this study, Box’s Test was employed to test the equality of covariance matrices of the citation-based indices and some significant results (at P ¼ 0.05) were found. These significant results produced by Box’s Tests suggest a rejection of the null hypothesis and thus it might not satisfy the multivariate assumption on the equality of covariance matrices. However, the significant results of Box’s Tests may be caused by violation of the normality assumption for this particular test (Stevens, 1972; Harris, 1975) and violating the assumption on equality of covariance matrices does not appreciably affect MANOVA if the groups in MANOVA are of an approximately equal size (Holloway & Dunn, 1967; Olson, 1974; Hakstian et al., 1979; Hair et al., 1992) as in the second session of multivariate test of this study.

Violating the assumption on equality of covariance matrices for unequal group sizes in MANOVA may affect the significance level of the test or Type I error (Sharma, 1996). In the first session of multivariate test where group sizes were unequal (i.e., a group of 11 well-known IS journals vs a group of 4 European IS journals), by examining the determinants of covariance matrices (Sharma, 1996), we found that the larger group has more variability. Thus, the actual significance level of the test is less than the nominal significance level (Holloway & Dunn, 1967; Hakstian et al., 1979).

In the case that the actual significance level of the test (e.g., P ¼ 0.02) is less than the nominal significance level (e.g., P ¼ 0.04), there is no need to be concerned about the significant results found in the test. Therefore, the significant results found in the First MANOVA of the first session of multivariate test (see Table 4) are not affected by the violation of the assumption on equality of covariance matrices. However, readers should be aware that the insignificant results (based on the nominal significance level P ¼ 0.36, see Table 4) found in the Second MANOVA of the first session of multivariate test might result from violating the assumption on equality of covariance matrices.

## Conclusion

In this study, the Citation Analysis was employed to compare the quality of four leading European IS journals with that of 11 well-recognized ones in the IS community. Our study results suggest that the quality of four European IS journals is quite comparable to that of 11 well-recognized ones. Our findings also show that European IS journals have higher Self-Citations Ratio and lower Cited-to-Citing Ratio as compared to the 11 well-recognized IS journals. Further research is needed to investigate the underlying reasons why such gaps exist.

With our pairwise comparison results, the four European IS journals are only second to the top three IS journals – MISQ, ISR, and CACM – with respect to some quality measurements. It is worth noting that, in terms of the Uncited Ratio, two of the four European IS journals carry higher quality than some well-recognized IS journal reported in previous studies.

Our study justifies the recent concern that the quality of European IS journals has been either ignored or underrated by the IS community. This study suggests that the European IS journals should be included on any wellrecognized IS journal list for academic evaluations and tenure and promotion decisions.

The Citation Analysis conducted in this study provides an effective method for eliminating potential measurement biases and the objective criteria for assessing journal quality. The research methodology employed herein could be easily applied for journal quality evaluations in any other disciplines. Additionally, the very methodology and the citation data collected also allow researchers to examine objectively IS knowledge contributions from within and across other disciplines. Analyzing the citations made to IS articles allows us to evaluate objectively knowledge contribution the IS discipline has made to advancing the body of knowledge and to examine where we stand among the larger scientific community.

One seeming limitation of this study is the lack of equality of covariance matrices in the data for multivariate tests. However, as observed in our study, this phenomenon could be caused by departures from the normal distribution of data. Finally, the only result in this study that might be affected by violating the assumption on equality of covariance matrices is the insignificant results found in the Second MANOVA of the first session of multivariate test.

Finally, this study collected only 4-year citation data since most European IS journals started publishing in early 1990s and their citation data were not available until 1995. Thus, a replication of this study to cover a longer period of citation data (e.g., 10 years) and to investigate whether the time span impacts citationbased indices of journal quality would be of important value.
