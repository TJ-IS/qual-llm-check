# Development of a measure for the information technology infrastructure construct

Bruce R. Lewis<sup>1</sup> and Terry Anthony Byrd<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Wayne Calloway School of Business and Accountancy, Wake Forest University, Winston-Salem, NC, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>401 Lowder Building, Department of Management, College of Business, Auburn University, Auburn, AL, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Terry Anthony Byrd, 401 Lowder Building, College of Business, Auburn University, Auburn, AL 36849, USA. Tel: +1 334 844 6543; E-mail: tbyrd@business.auburn.edu

## Abstract

The goal of this research was to operationalize the information technology infrastructure (ITI) concept by identifying its underlying dimensions and establishing a valid and reliable measure for the construct. In order to accomplish this task with the highest degree of certainty, two approaches were sequentially applied: exploratory factor analysis and confirmatory factor analysis. In evaluating the confirmatory factor model, a step-by-step process was employed, assessing unidimensionality, reliability, discriminant validity, and nomological validity. The dimensions of ITI were determined to be: Chief Information Officer, IT Planning, IT Security, Technology Integration, Advisory Committee, Enterprise Model, and Data Administration. Researchers may now make use of this validated ITI measure to conduct investigations of ITI and other organizational measures. Implications of the ITI measure with other organizational initiatives such as electronic commerce and knowledge management are explored.

## Introduction

For more than a decade, information has been acknowledged as a valuable corporate asset comparable in importance to capital, plant, and personnel (Jackson, 1987; van den Hoven, 1999). Today’s large expenditures on information technology (IT) indicate that business managers are aggressively applying this principle (Strassmann, 1999). In the late 1980s and early 1990s, IT outlays became a prominent feature on the balance sheet of most companies (Sullivan-Trainor, 1989), with IT expenditures accelerating at a rate exceeding general economic growth (Keen, 1991; Informationweek, 2000). It has been estimated that corporate America spent in excess of one trillion dollars on IT since 1990 (Grover et al., 1998). International Data Corporation, an IT industry group, reported that spending on IT exceeded 316 billion dollars in 1997 and was expected to grow at better than 10% in future years (Judge, 1998). However, many organizations have become somewhat leery of further IT investments (Hackney, 1999). As a result of the magnitude of IT expenditures, the effective application of information and its underlying technologies has become a crucial organizational goal (Mahmood & Mann, 1993).

An organization’s structural IT characteristics not only provide the foundation for managing information assets but can also offer tangible benefits and a continuity of business practices (Kettinger et al., 1994). Kettinger and his colleagues suggested that one of these defining structural capabilities is the technological infrastructure. The components of an organizational IT infrastructure (ITI) include networks, databases, practices, and applications (Duncan, 1995). Rockart et al. (1996) reflected the ideal goals of an ITI in referring to an ‘IT infrastructure’ of telecommunications, computers, software, and data that are integrated and interconnected so that all types of information can be expeditiously – and effortlessly, from the users viewpoint – routed through the network and redesigned processes . . . Because it involves fewer manual or complex computerbased interventions, a ‘seamless’ infrastructure is cheaper to operate than independent, divisional infrastructures.’ Duncan (1995) described ITI as a set of shared, tangible IT resources forming a foundation for business applications. Weill (1993) noted that ITI was a foundation for capability across business and/or functional units.

Davenport & Linder (1994) referred to ITI as that part of the organization’s information capacity intended to be shared. They concluded that an ITI is a firm’s institutionalized IT practice – the consistent foundation on which the specific business activities and computer applications are built. The institutionalized practice is in the form of shared IT resources that follow formal or informal rules and policies established to govern the behaviors of data, hardware, and software that may be developed or maintained by the organization. The institutionalized nature of ITI has at least a couple of implications. First, the formal and informal rules and policies dictated by the ITI serve as the foundation for innovation in business strategies and value chain activities (Allen & Boynton, 1991; Duncan, 1995). Second, the institutionalized nature of ITI offers a layer of protection from the indiscriminate implementation of incompatible technologies in an organization. In many of today’s firms, decisions about IT applications are made at the individual department or business unit, thus possibly promoting the implementation of incompatible data, software, and hardware. Institutionalizing the rules and policies implied by the ITI can serve as a guide for these individual departments and business units and improve the chances of creating enterprise-wide connections and compatibility among the departments and business units. Based on the above descriptions from the literature, ITI in this study was defined as

The organized, shared IT resources of hardware, software, communication technologies, data, core applications, and practices that provide a unique technological foundation (1) for widespread communications interchanges across an organization, (2) for the design, development, implementation, maintenance, and management of present and future business application, and (3) to support innovation within the organization.

Researchers and practitioners alike have taken note of the potential value of an organization’s ITI. In fact, the growing strategic value of the ITI is almost undeniable. IT infrastructure expenditures account for over 58% of an organization’s IT budget and the percentage is growing at 11% a year (Broadbent & Weill, 1997). McKay & Brockway (1989) called ITI the enabling foundation of shared

IT capabilities upon which the entire business depends. In addition, ITI provides support for global business strategies and may serve as a catalyst for the globalization process within a company (Peppard, 1999). Many have called IT infrastructure the new competitive weapon and see it as being crucial in developing sustained competitive advantage (Davenport & Linder, 1994; Boar, 1997).

Unique characteristics of an IT infrastructure determine the value of that infrastructure to an organization. Duncan (1995) wrote, ‘One firm’s infrastructure may make strategic innovations in business processes feasible, while the characteristics of competitors’ infrastructure may likewise cause their inability to imitate the innovation rapidly enough to mitigate the first mover’s advantage.’ (p. 38). In surveys featuring the issues most important to IT executives, the development of a responsive ITI is always ranked at or near the top (Niederman et al., 1991; Brancheau et al., 1996; Boar, 1997). Given the importance attributed to ITI within organizations and the complexity of the topic, it is essential that sound research be undertaken in order to understand the concept in depth.

The basics of academic research, theory construction and a longitudinal focus, are inseparable from measurement (Bagozzi, 1982). Consequently, DeLone & McLean (1992) called upon the Management Information Systems research community to develop validated measures. However, MIS as an academic field has been criticized for inadequate construct development and insufficient attention to measurement (Straub, 1989). The investigation described herein was undertaken specifically in response to these concerns relative to the ITI. The goal of this research was to operationalize the ITI concept by identifying its underlying dimensions and establishing a valid and reliable measure for the construct. The research followed the outline given in Figure 1 and presented in the rest of the paper.

## Theoretical considerations

An extensive content analysis of the current MIS literature focusing on enterprise-wide IT technologies and practices was executed. We searched databases that contain periodical indices in business and social science (ABI/INFORM and Wilson DSOC, respectively) using the above terms. We also reviewed top academic journals (e.g., Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly, Journal of MIS, European Journal of Information Systems). Books and monographs that had the stated criteria were also included. The time period was 1975 to the present.

Scanning the literature through the search, we found and used relevant research works on enterprise-wide technologies and practices to identify themes associated with ITI. The themes that were found included chief information officer, IT planning, IT security, technology integration, IT advisory committee, enterprise model, information integration, and data administration.

Using the sources from the literature, explicit characteristics of each dimension were extricated and noted.

The characteristics provided a foundation for items used on the measurement instrument. The objective was to determine various aspects of each dimension and to represent them in the questionnaire. The questionnaire was pretested using seven IT managers in various organizations. Respondents remarked on the dimensions and the items used to measure them. They were asked to note any dimensions or items that should be added, deleted, or modified. They also commented on each item’s meaningfulness and readability. Refinements to the instrument were made based on their suggestions.

A pilot test of the instrument was then undertaken with 11 members of a chapter of the Society of Information Management (SIM). These respondents were asked to evaluate the questionnaire in similar manner to the pretest respondents. In addition, these 11 respondents were asked to give an overall impression of the instrument. Wording and format refinements to the instrument were made based on comments from the respondents. The eight themes that emerged are discussed below.

The Chief Information Officer (CIO) dimension represents a senior executive responsible for corporate-wide information technology policy, planning, management, and acquisitions. Synnott & Gruber (1981) coined the term ‘chief information officer’ to give a title to the top executive that oversees the convergence of IT and administers the corporate information resource. The research literature suggests that CIOs must possess both technical and business knowledge for innovation success (Synnott, 1987; Earl, 1989; Armstrong & Sambamurthy, 1999). Therefore, items referring to the CIO should relate to both technology and business.

The IT Planning dimension reflects an inclusive information systems/technology planning process that supports business goals, encompasses both central and distributed technologies, and features a mechanism for assessing the potential of new technologies. The scope of IT planning is broad. The activities associated with IT planning include organizational requirements and, hence, do not focus on the development activities of a single information system (Segars & Grover, 1998). The perspective of IT planning is at the highest level of management. This means that business goals as well as technology issues are included in the process. The time frame of IT planning is much longer than lower level planning in the organization. Organizational members involved in IT planning must look far into the future to determine and attempt to combat competitive threats (Das et al., 1991). Items used for this dimension should reflect these characteristics.

The IT Security dimension depicts a comprehensive security program that includes access control and data security, and a security awareness effort. Even with several high-profile IT security breaches in recent years, IT security continues to lag behind other issues in the minds of IT and business managers (Parker, 1983; Loch et al., 1992; Straub & Welke, 1998). IT security in an organization varies between the risk of a disaster and the risk of computer abuse. IT managers should be concerned about both catastrophic loss and the illicit or unauthorized penetration of computer resources with equal concern (Hoffer & Straub, 1989). Both of these divisions should be measured as indicated by the items in our study.

The Technology Integration dimension delineates a comprehensive and integrated approach to information technologies, including computing, networking, telecommunications, and office automation. Technology integration is one of the challenges of organizations in managing the various heterogeneous platforms and networks across and within functional departments. While some companies have started to produce integrated platforms and networks in some organizations, many companies still have a major paradigm shift in moving to integrated infrastructures. The value of interoperability and interconnectivity among technological platforms and communications networks is almost universally recognized in today’s organizations (Byrd & Turner, 2000). Items developed to measure this dimension focused on the integration of various technology resources like corporate-wide networks, communications, and office automation.

The Advisory Committee dimension refers to policy oversight boards that deal with systems and technology issues and include end-user and senior management participation. The presence of advisory committees consisting of IT personnel, senior management, and users should have a significant effect on the use of the IT resources in organization (Armstrong & Sambamurthy, 1999). An IT advisory committee is an ideal mechanism for the exchange of information among these three stakeholder groups. Participation in the advisory committee is a crucial measure of how representative the process of the management of IT is within a firm. Items in our study reflect the existence of and participation in the advisory committee.

The Enterprise Model dimension illustrates a comprehensive approach featuring documented business processes, a development methodology, inventories of facilities and information, corporate-wide technology standards, and the use of automated development. Enterprise models can help identify potential systems projects for the organization and rethink business processes (Goodhue et al., 1992). The enterprise model is often the result of ‘information engineering’ (e.g., Martin, 1989) that involves its planning, analysis, design, or construction using a methodology and a set of formal techniques. Information engineering applies structured techniques to an enterprise as a whole, or to a large sector of the enterprise (Martin, 1989). Since an enterprise is so complex, information engineering has been primarily associated with automated techniques in developing and constructing an enterprise model. The items in our study display these characteristics of the enterprise model dimension.

The Information Integration dimension focuses on integrated data and applications systems, with the data shared between users. Where technology integration is more concerned with physical connectivity, information integration is more about compatibility, the ability to share any type of information across any technology component (Duncan, 1995). At one extreme, only simple text messages can be shared, while at the other extreme any document, process, service, video, image, text, audio, or a combination of these can be used by any other system, regardless of manufacturer, make, or type. The integration of information in an organization is so important now because information is now used as a competitive weapon (Powell & Dent-Micallef, 1997). Traditional functions need to be blended together in a seamless process with a free flow of information and timely execution of processes among the functions (Fan et al., 2000).

The Data Administration dimension features a data administration function, utilizing a data dictionary and defining shared data that is based on a corporate data architecture with integration of data between applications and policies on data ownership. However, a major problem in today’s organizations in assembling coordinated, organization-wide responses to competitive confrontations is the lack of data integration because of the lack of commonly defined data elements and codes in the IT infrastructure (Goodhue et al., 1992). Many companies still have disparate database systems with different names and identifiers for such common entities as customers, products, and suppliers. Empirical evidence has revealed that common data definitions result in a smooth flow of accurate and quality data between units of an organization (Jain et al., 1998). Items to measure this dimension focus on issues like the extent of data standards, data dictionary, data integration between applications, and policies on data administration.

## Data collection

The final version of the instrument (see Appendix) was sent to an industry-stratified random sample of senior IT managers in 470 Fortune 1000 companies. Typical titles for these jobs included VP of Information Systems, CIO, and Director of MIS. The industries encompassed manufacturing, banking, finance, insurance, retail, transportation, utilities, and health services. The respondents were asked to indicate the actual extent of implementation in their organization for each ITI item on the instrument and their opinion of the extent to which each of the ITI items should be implemented, that is, their judgment regarding the importance of the ITI item. Both responses were on a six-point scale (in categories ranging from ‘1’, meaning ‘not at all’, to ‘6’, meaning ‘very great extent’). In this paper, the first scale is referred to as the currently implemented scale and the second is referenced as the should-be-implemented scale.

After two mailings, a total of 150 questionnaires were returned, representing a 32% response rate. In all, 51% of the respondent group were CIO’s. A total of 65% of the respondents reported that they had over 10 years experience in information systems, with a mean IS experience in the respondent group of 21.6 years. The most common industry of the respondents was manufacturing, which was twice as predominant as any of the other industries, and represented a finding typical of the Fortune 1000 firms in the population. Companies in the return sample were relatively large (52% of the firms reported more than 5000 employees and 50% reported revenues over one billion dollars), again reflective of the Fortune 1000 composition.

Stone (1978) noted that examining the components that make up an overall measure is a legitimate method for evaluating construct validity. In this study, factor analyses were applied sequentially to assess construct validity by determining and verifying the underlying dimensions of ITI. To begin, an exploratory factor analysis was performed on data from the currently implemented scale to determine the components of ITI. This was followed by a confirmatory factor analysis on data from the should-be-implemented scale to verify these dimensions.

## Initial measurement assessment

A varimax-rotated, exploratory factor analysis was first conducted on data from the currently implemented scale to initially assess the construct validity of the instrument The sample size of 150 was deemed adequate for exploratory factor analysis because Cattell (1978) suggested that three to six times as many respondents as variables as the experientially indicated desirable lower limit. Our ratio was above the 3 that is deemed acceptable. The result was a solution with eight factors, each with eigenvalues greater than 1.0. This solution explained 67% of the systematic covariance among the items. Items were dropped from further analysis if their factor loadings were less than 0.45 or if they loaded on two factors with loadings greater than 0.45. The final 39- item, eight-factor model is reported in Table 1. The rotated factor solution met the following three criteria: simplicity (Harman, 1976; Sethi & King, 1991), interpretability (Kachigan, 1982; Lederer & Sethi, 1992), and a reasonable percent of variance explained (Bernstein, 1988; Straub, 1989).

Reliability was assessed by computing the internal consistency, Cronbach’s a coefficient, for each of the dimensions determined from the exploratory factor analysis (Cronbach & Meehl, 1955). The reliability coefficients for each of the factors are presented in Table 1. These a coefficients for all eight of the factors were above 0.7, with six above 0.8, thus indicating acceptable reliability (Nunnally, 1967).

At this point, based on the exploratory factor analysis on data from the currently implemented scale, the measurement instrument exhibited acceptable reliability and validity. Next, these eight dimensions were analyzed via confirmatory factor analysis on data from the shouldbe-implemented scale. In order to verify that the responses on the second scale (should-be-implemented) differed from the responses on the first scale (currently implemented), a multivariate analysis of variance was computed on the vectors of item means from the two scales. The Wilks’ Lambda test statistic for this procedure was significant at less than 0.0001, indicating the two vectors were not equal, providing evidence that the means of the items were different between the should-beimplemented and currently implemented scales.

In utilizing this approach to confirm factors for the strategic IT planning construct, Segars & Grover (1998, p. 148) wrote, ‘y the analytical framework of confirmatory factor analysis provides an appropriate means of assessing the efficacy of measurement among scale items and the consistency of a pre-specified structural equation model with its associated network of theoretical concepts.’ In a similar fashion in this study, the items reported in Table 1 were hypothesized to uniquely measure an associated factor and the systems of factors to determine an overarching construct called ITI.

In evaluating the confirmatory factor model, the stepby-step process suggested by Hair et $a l .$ (1995) was employed. The factors were evaluated in isolation, then in pairs, and then as a collective network, using the EQS program for structural equation modeling. Skewness and kurtosis were examined and these tests showed no major departures from normality in the data. Hair et al. (1995) suggested that three issues should be considered in evaluating the confirmatory model: covariance vs correlation matrices, sample size, and identification.

The variance/covariance matrix is especially useful any time a true ‘test of theory’ is being performed, as the variance/covariance matrix satisfies the assumptions of the methodology and is an appropriate form of the data for validating causal relationships (Hair et $a l . _ { \ l }$ , 1995). The variance/covariance matrix for the sample data was used in this study.

Sample size is also an important consideration in any statistical method because it provides a basis for the estimation of sampling error. Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) usually requires a sample size of at least 100. Although there is no ideal sample size, a general consensus seems to have developed that between 100 and 200 is a ‘good sample size’ (Hoelter, 1983). In the present study, the sample size of 150 fell in the middle of this range.

Identification is the inability of the proposed model to generate unique estimates. Hair et al. (1995) suggested that the identification problem can be tested by reestimating the model in multiple runs, using different starting values. In this study, the starting values for the coefficients were changed and the models rerun several times to test for identification problems. The results converged at about the same point each time and, thus, provided evidence of no identification problems in the data.

From a theoretical standpoint, the measurement properties of a construct should be evaluated using a variety of techniques. Taking an operational view, the following subset is considered of prime importance (Venkatraman, 1989; Sethi & King, 1994): unidimensionality, reliability, discriminant validity, and nomological (i.e., predictive) validity.

The eight ITI factors from the exploratory factor analysis (Table 1) were the starting point for the confirmatory analysis. Each set of items for the hypothesized factors (e.g., planning, information integration, etc.) were examined separately on data from the shouldbe-implemented scale, as suggested by Venkatraman (1989), to locate any mis-specifications in individual factors. An initial examination of the overall confirmatory model of these eight factors showed that the information integration and data administration factors were very highly correlated and represented one factor. Therefore, these two factors were combined and seven factors, instead of the eight from the exploratory factor analysis, were analyzed in this stage of the process.

## Unidimensionality and reliability

Unidimensionality (and convergent validity) has been defined as the existence of one latent trait or construct underlying a set of measures (Anderson et $a l . ,$ 1987). In this study, the measurement properties of each ITI factor were examined separately with the objective of isolating and locating any errors in specifying each factor. Items were retained in a factor if their standardized loading exceeded 0.40. Items with a loading below this cutoff were dropped from further consideration. Based on these criteria, five items were dropped: application systems integration, distributed facilities, business continuity/ disaster recovery planning, training for end users, and user support for distributed facilities. The remaining items that satisfied the unidimensionality criteria for each factor are shown in Table 2.

In assessing the fit of the remaining items on their respective factors, the indications were that each set of items measuring each factor may very well define one latent trait of ITI. The goodness-of-fit indicator (GFI) was well above the recommended cutoff of 0.9 for all of the factors. The adjusted $\chi ^ { 2 \prime } \boldsymbol { \mathsf { s } }$ for all the factors ranged from less than one to five, all in an acceptable range (Gerbing & Anderson, 1988; Bagozzi et al., 1991). The root mean square residual (RSMR) was at or below 0.05, which is excellent for organizational research. The reliability for each factor was at or above 0.7 and in line with Nunnally’s (1967) recommendation. Overall, the fit indicators seemed to suggest that each factor was capturing a significant amount of variation in the latent dimensions of the ITI construct.

## Discriminant validity

Discriminant validity is inferred when measures of each factor converge on their respective true score, which is unique from the scores of other factors (Churchill, 1979). Following Bagozzi et al. (1991) and Venkatraman (1989), discriminant validity for the factors of the ITI construct was assessed by testing if correlations between pairs of factors were significantly different from unity. Such a result implies the presence of discriminant validity between the pairs of factors (Sethi & King, 1994; Segars & Grover, 1998). This test was performed on all possible pairs of factors in the model. Table 3 reports the results of 21 pairwise tests among the seven ITI factors. The significant results of all the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ tests attested to strong discriminant validity in the model.

## Full ITI confirmatory model

The full model, including all seven factors with all their items, was assessed at this point. Table 4 reports the model fit summary for the full model (shown in Figure 2). As can be seen in this table, the indicators provided evidence of a good fit for the data. The GFI – a measure of overall fit in programs like EQS – for the full model was 0.89, which is extremely close to the cutoff of 0.90 and above those reported in similar organizational IT studies (e.g., Sethi & King, 1994; Segars & Grover, 1998). The adjusted $\chi ^ { 2 }$ was less than 2, another indication of a good fit. The RMSR was 0.06, which is below the recommended 0.08, providing further evidence of a good fit for this model. An examination of the distribution of the standardized residuals showed them to be symmetrical and centered around zero. In addition, convergence to a solution in EQS required only six iterations, the last an indication of a well-fitting model. Therefore, since all these model indicators were in satisfactory ranges, the model appeared to be a reasonable fit for the data.

Figure 2 shows the full confirmatory model, including the correlations between all of the factors in the ITI construct. Correlations among these factors were statistically significant with moderate magnitude, suggesting the existence of a possible higher-order model featuring a construct that is made up of seven factors. According to the theoretical development in this study that construct was, of course, ITI. Segars & Grover (1998) declared that the efficacy of such a higher-order model can be tested using a comparative methodology for such structures.

## Investigation of a higher-order confirmatory model

The comparative methodology contrasts a baseline model with a model featuring the second-order construction. In this study, the baseline was the ITI model in Figure 2, with the model fit indicators given in Table 4. The alternative, second-order model is shown in Figure 3, with model fit indicators presented in Table 5. Marsh & Hocevar (1985) advised that the second-order model may be assessed through the ratio of $\chi ^ { 2 \prime } \boldsymbol { \mathsf { s } }$ of the baseline model to the second-order model. The ratio should approach 1.0 if the second-order model sufficiently captures the relations among the factors in the model. The ratio in the current study between the baseline model and the second-order model was greater than 0.99 and approaching unity. This indicated a good fit, with no over-fitting. Segars & Grover (1998) suggested that in such cases the second-order model is a truer representation of the model structure (seven paths in contrast to 21 correlations) and that the second-order model can be accepted over the baseline model.

## Nomological validity of the confirmatory model

The data from the currently implemented scale were also used to examine nomological validity. One question on the questionnaire asked the respondents to respond to the following on a 6-point scale: To what extent are your company’s information systems/ technology efforts effective? This question provided an indication of the success of ITI in the respondents’ companies. The question was used in a structural equation model with a causal link between the second-order ITI construct and the performance indication as measured by this question. The results of the test of this relation showed that the ITI factor was statistically significantly related to the performance question with a moderate magnitude of 0.49. Although not a very robust test of the predictive power of the second-order factor, it does add to the evidence that the factors found in this study are probable measures of ITI. We also note that these factors are in reasonable agreement with the IT services described by Weill & Broadbent (1998) in their book.

## Discussion

## Implications

The necessity to share information in ways that make it easier to access and use productively has become an imperative in most commercial endeavors. The quality and extent of information sharing, storage, and processing is directly related to the capabilities provided by an organization’s information technology infrastructure. ITI is the cornerstone for the development of business applications as well as the backbone for electronic communications within an organization and beyond. A sound ITI is, likewise, a requirement for engaging in global business enterprises, where the transmission of information and knowledge throughout the organization is essential (Rockart et al., 1996; Peppard, 1999). Further, strategic organizational initiatives, such as business process reengineering, electronic commerce, and knowledge management, are dependent on a solid ITI. A valid and reliable measure of ITI capabilities is essential if IT resources are to be aligned with these and other major organizational undertakings.

There is evidence from several successful implementations of business process reengineering (BPR) that ITI capabilities are instrumental. For example, the development of the ITI at Wal-Mart was a catalyst in its successful transformation to the world’s largest retail organization, allowing it to overtake both Sears and K-Mart (Furey & Diorio, 1994). Wal-Mart’s unique procurement and distribution process could not have been accomplished without the extensive capabilities of its ITI. Broadbent et al. (1999) provided clear evidence that ITI is fundamental to the success of BPR. They studied four firms, two from the petroleum industry and two from the retail industry. In both industries, the firms with high ITI capabilities enabled BPR, while the firms with lower ITI ratings exhibited ITI that either hindered or had no effect on BPR. These findings illustrate the importance, not only of ITI, but the requirement of getting an accurate measure of the ITI capabilities in a firm.

Electronic commerce is also an area where ITI has been shown to be central to successful implementation. The implosion of the ‘dot-coms’ has been blamed on the lack of a robust ITI (Straub & Watson, 2001). In fact, Straub and Watson concluded that a key research question to be addressed is ‘Which ITI capability leads to favorable outcomes for electronic commerce organizations?’ This question assumes that the capabilities of ITI have been identified and assessed. Research on which of the capabilities are keys to favorable outcomes for electronic commerce organizations can be conducted with the use of measurement instruments like the one developed in this study.

Knowledge management is another organizational concept that has been touted as a key differentiating factor between firms (Drucker, 1995; Randall et al., 2001). For example, the Right First Time program, a knowledge management initiative at Hoffmann-LaRoche, a Swiss pharmaceutical firm, is designed to reduce the time to market new drug approvals. Delays in this process are very costly and losses can run up to a \$1 million a day. Through the Right First Time program, Hoffmann-LaRoche was able to entrench the knowledge repository within organizational processes with the support of an ITI that consisted of capabilities such as technology integration and data administration (Weill & Broadbent, 1998). Again, we can see the link between ITI capabilities and the success of a premier organizational initiative. The Hoffmann-LaRoche case illustrates that the ability to assess the readiness of the IT infrastructure can alert managers to possible barriers to successful implementations. This action can save firms time and money, and focus them on eliminating ITI deficiencies before starting

BPR, electronic commerce, knowledge management, or other enterprise-wide business initiatives. As these examples show, ITI just might be, as some researchers claim (Davenport & Linder, 1994), the foundation on which the business is sustained.

## Research directions

Business managers have taken note of the importance of ITI. MIS researchers must pick up the challenge created by this high level of interest among practitioners and further investigate how an appropriate ITI is best implemented and aligned with other initiatives within organizations. From the examples cited above, it is evident that ITI can be a source of sustained competitive advantage. As such, the understanding of ITI must be expanded through more research in this area. MIS researchers can utilize the model and measurement instrument developed in this study to explore relations between ITI and other organizational characteristics. As an example, the dimensions of ITI documented in this article could be used as a framework in investigating the ITI planning process, the linkage of which with business strategy is a fundamental success factor (Hackney, 1999).

The factors for ITI uncovered in this study should be improved in further studies. For instance, only three items were retained to measure the IT security factor. Future studies must add to these items to reflect possible facets that were missed in our analysis and that might have been created since our analysis. IT security is taking on new importance with the ubiquitous nature of the Internet and the integration of interorganizational systems. New areas of concern for security are bound to develop as more and more of these types of innovations are created in the future. The same is true of most of the other factors presented in this study. As additional studies are completed in this critical area, we must consolidate the findings and develop meaningful theories to help guide practitioners in establishing and managing their ITIs and in using them to support the key business initiatives in their organizations.

## Study limitations

As with any empirical investigation, weaknesses in the methodology and data will be present, and this study is no different. Two, in particular, should be acknowledged. First, the number of observations upon which the analyses were performed was in the barely acceptable range. Although we cited evidence that our sample size is minimally adequate, we recognize that other researchers might take exception to our small sample size. Second, the survey data utilized in this study were collected exclusively from firms in the United States. Even though these large American companies may be representative of others throughout the world, this is by no means certain. As a result of these limitations some amount of skepticism is appropriate with regard to our findings. However, at the very least, the components of ITI and the measurement instrument developed in this study provide a good starting point for further investigation of the ITI construct.
