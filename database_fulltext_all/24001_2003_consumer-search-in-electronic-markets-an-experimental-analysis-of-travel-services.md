## Abstract

Electronic markets are frequently touted to be highly efficient. The prevailing hypothesis is that very low information search costs will enable buyers to expand their product information search and comparison beyond what is feasible in conventional markets. The aim of this paper is to evaluate the relative efficiency of product search in current travel service markets. We report the results of our analysis based on an experimental setting with 92 subjects. Our conclusions tend to disagree with previous predictions about the efficiency of electronic markets. The electronic markets observed were found to be at best as efficient as their conventional counterparts. Our results suggest that poor availability of product information and a lack of systems integration severely limit the efficiency of consumer search in present electronic markets.

## Introduction

Information systems (IS) are central to the infrastructure of electronic commerce. They intermediate transactions between buyers and sellers, facilitating the creation of electronic markets. Malone et al. (1987) investigated electronic marketplaces built on interorganizational IS (Barrett & Konsynski, 1982). They grounded their study on transaction cost economics (see e.g. Williamson, 1975, 1979, 1981) and maintained that diminishing costs of information technology (IT) will lower transaction costs and, thus, favor markets over hierarchies as the organizational structure of economic activity.

Bakos (1991a, b) proposed that electronic markets will fundamentally change the balance of power in the markets, since buyers will face little difficulty in obtaining product information. Low search costs enable buyers to search extensively for product information and eventually make better procurement decisions. Stigler (1961) established that high search costs are a major source of the inefficiency observed in most markets. Thus, efficient information dissemination should render electronic markets as relatively efficient mechanisms of trade.

Bakos (1997) investigated the strategic impacts of electronic commerce on consumer markets for both homogeneous and heterogeneous products. He collectively termed the mechanisms underlying electronic markets as electronic market systems. Bakos proposed that markets for homogeneous goods will sharply converge towards efficient equilibrium price. The effects of low search costs will be more gradual in markets for heterogeneous goods, while the prices will become less dispersed. However, limited empirical evidence has been presented in support of the proposed positive effects of electronic markets.

Our work follows several other studies concerned with the effects of electronic commerce on consumer search in differentiated markets. Bailey (1998) analyzed prices for books, compact disks, and software in the Internet and conventional outlets, and found evidence that prices on the Internet were, on average, higher. Brynjolfsson & Smith (2000) demonstrated that substantial price dispersion existed in electronic markets for books and compact disks while the average prices were somewhat lower. They have also provided evidence suggesting that even pricesensitive book buyers have preferences with regard to online sellers (Smith & Brynjolfsson, 2001). Clay et al. (2001) respectively found that many on-line book buyers may not be engaging in search despite its potential benefits. Degratu et al. (2000) examined on-line grocery markets and found mixed support for the hypothesis of price sensitivity being lower in on-line than in conventional channels. Lynch & Ariely (2000) used laboratory experiments to study the sale of wine through electronic channels to show that the amount of product information provided to customers can affect price competition and increase customer loyalty. Lee (1998) found that prices of used cars sold via electronic auction (AucNet) tended to be higher than comparable prices in conventional auction markets. Shankar et al. (1998) surveyed travelers to show that prior positive experience with a brand can decrease price sensitivity in electronic markets. Clemons et al. (1999) found substantial price dispersion for domestic airline tickets offered by on-line travel agents in the U.S. Anckar & Walden (2002) found online reservation of travel services to be a complex task, often exceeding the capabilities of even an educated customer.

While the increasing performance/price ratio of IT is a fact, it is less clear if enhances in data transmission alone will substantially change consumer behavior. The purpose of this work is to provide evidence of the functioning of electronic markets in the travel industry. Based on our experiment, we have tested (1) the effect of electronic markets on the extensiveness of consumers’ information search process, (2) the expected increase in efficiency of consumer information search in electronic markets, and (3) the efficiency of electronic travel markets relative to conventional markets. The paper begins with a review of previous literature on consumer search and market efficiency. We then present the arrangement of our experiment and the results of our analyses. Finally, we discuss the impact of our findings and the limitations of our study.

## Consumer search in electronic markets

In this work, electronic markets are defined as systems of exchange facilitated by information technology. Following Bakos (1991a, b, 1997, 1998), we will limit the discussion to the role these systems have in bringing together buyers and sellers. Besides the market-making function, markets have other roles, like transfer of payments and goods. While these transactions are likely to affect the market price, they are often bilateral in nature, and therefore we exclude them from this study. Bakos (1991b) distinguished the concepts of the electronic marketplace (or electronic market system) and electronic markets. He offered that the latter term is used more generally to depict the economic governance mechanism; see for example, Malone et al. (1987, 1989). Presently, electronic marketplaces (Bakos, 1991b) are increasingly embedded in the global information infrastructure, the Internet. As a whole, they constitute electronic consumer markets. We therefore prefer to use the more general expression.

It should be pointed out that early studies of electronic markets focused on business-to-business markets. Copeland & McKenney (1988) suggested that SABRE, the first global reservation system, may have increased competition in U.S. airline markets. Bakos (1991a) maintained that electronic markets tend to favor buyers, since they lower search costs. However, in these studies the term buyer referred to businesses, essentially travel agencies using the SABRE terminals.

## Consumer search

The economics of information theory is based on the premise that buyers do not fully inform themselves about the alternatives available in the markets because of high search costs (Stigler, 1961; Ratchford, 1982). The theory implies that both the benefits and costs of search are related to the number of alternatives considered. A frequency distribution of prices is assumed in the market of N alternative brands or sellers. If the buyer considers a subset noN, the expected utility of the chosen alternative increases with n (Stigler, 1961; Ratchford, 1980; Hauser & Wernerfelt, 1990). Thus, it is more likely that good alternatives are included in the subset of products considered as the size of the consideration set increases.

Alba et al. (1997), while discussing the merits of interactive home shopping (IHS), posited that the vast number of alternatives available to consumers is a significant benefit of IHS compared with other retail formats. Bakos (1997) suggested that declining search costs in electronic markets will enable consumers to engage in more extensive pre-purchase search, that is they will be able to consider a larger set of alternatives. Since search costs in electronic markets are widely expected to be lower in electronic markets, we propose the following hypothesis.

H1: Consumers using electronic markets construct larger consideration sets than consumers using conventional markets.

The economics of information theory further implies that, in terms of the benefits of search, the returns are sharply diminishing. Every additional alternative examined offers smaller potential increase in benefits than the previous ones. The cost of searching for an additional alternative tends to increase. As it takes progressively more effort to locate new offerings, a point is reached at which the expected cost of considering additional alternative exceeds the potential increase in benefit.

Empirical evidence indicates that consumers relatively quickly reach the point where the perceived cost of search is higher than the expected benefits. Consumers rarely visit more than one or two shops even when buying high-ticket consumer durables (e.g. Newman & Staelin, 1972; Wilkie & Dickson, 1985). Crowell & Bowers (1977) have shown that, in particular, the cost of a consumer’s time determines to a great extent how much search is feasible. If electronic markets lower the cost of search as proposed by Bakos (1997), consumers should, ceteris paribus, spend less time for search in electronic markets.

H2: Consumers use less time for search in electronic markets than in conventional markets.

The value of time is a controversial topic. Clearly, the opportunity cost of time varies from consumer to consumer. Furthermore, Leclerc et al. (1995) have shown that the value of a consumer’s time is not constant but depends on contextual factors. When controlling the cost of time, consumers using electronic markets should be more efficient. Thus, they should be able to generate more alternatives per unit of time.

H3: Consumers using electronic markets find more alternatives per unit of time than consumers using conventional markets do.

## Market efficiency

The theory of price competition dates back to at least Cournot (1838), who implied that price, along with quantity produced, is a decision variable in a duopoly analysis. In his review of Cournot’s work, Bertrand (1883) incorporated buyers as actively searching agents with Cournot’s model. Stigler (1961) established in his seminal paper on the economics of information theory that high search costs are reflected in the markets as wide price dispersion. High search costs, thus, enable sellers to price their offerings monopolistically.

Market efficiency and its effects on individual consumers have received considerable attention at least since Oxenfeldt’s (1950) original work. Various operational definitions as well as empirical measures have been developed to examine this topic. Ratchford et al. (1996) divide the measures of market efficiency into four broad categories: (1) measures of price dispersion, (2) price– quality correlation, (3) measures of potential losses to consumers, and (4) measures based on the concept of an efficient frontier. Most consumer markets are quite heterogeneous and, thus, product quality should be taken into account while measuring market efficiency.

Bakos (1991a, 1997) suggested that relatively low search costs in electronic markets enable buyers to extend their pre-purchase search, which will increase price competition and, thus, swiftly lead to less dispersed and lower prices in markets for homogeneous products. He maintained that this effect persists in differentiated markets, even though it is more gradual.

H4: Electronic markets are more efficient than conventional markets in terms of price dispersion and price level, ceteris paribus.

## Methodology

In this section, we describe the methodology of the study and the conduct of the data collection. We chose to conduct an experiment since we aimed to compare the electronic and conventional markets based on the benefits and efforts information searchers face. Previous studies of electronic markets have used more indirect measures. The markets are expected to reflect the information search costs; high search costs are associated with high price dispersion. However, the market structure and life cycle of products sold are known to affect the market, and it is often difficult to separate the causes of observed market characteristics.

We chose to study the travel industry for the following reasons. First, the travel industry is one of the industries that are expected to be strongly affected by electronic commerce (Lewis et al., 1998). Second, it accounted for 11% (World Travel & Tourism Council, 2000) of GDP across the global economy in 1999 and, hence, possible changes in the industry should interest both academia and practitioners. Third, travel services, like services in general, are experience products; they do not lend themselves to physical examination before purchase. Electronic markets should not be disadvantaged as an arena for product comparison.

We arranged an experiment to compare the cost and benefits of information search and product comparison in Finnish electronic and conventional markets for travel services. Early adoption and high diffusion of information and telecommunication technology should enable Finnish consumers to use electronic market mechanisms fairly effectively.

We chose the method of experiment, since it was imperative to control the task characteristics to be able to compare the efforts and rewards of information search in electronic and conventional markets. Furthermore, while the experimental setting limited our ability to generalize our findings, it enabled us to compile a rich data set describing the search processes.

## Experiment

The experiment comprised two tasks in which the subjects were asked to arrange conference voyages to (1) Hawaii in January 2001 (HICSS) and (2) Brisbane in December 2000 (ICIS). They had 1 week to complete the tasks. The experiment was designed to allow every subject to use both electronic and conventional markets. They were randomly assigned to two groups. The first group used the information sources of electronic travel markets for the first task, while the second group acted as the control group. For the second task, the roles of the groups were switched. This arrangement allowed us to crosscheck our findings and to further control for random differences in our subjects’ tastes and involvement.

We instructed our subjects to arrange (1) flights to and from the conference destination, (2) accommodation for the duration of the conference, and (3) local transportation. The journeys were to be designed for one person and had to cover at least the conference dates. The subjects were further instructed that they should try to minimize the travel costs while simultaneously satisfying some goals (presence in the conference destination during the conference schedule, accommodation in single rooms). Every subject completing the assignment was awarded 10/100 points for the course grade. To further motivate the subjects, they were awarded additional points for every FIM 1000 (in Euro 168, in USD 180) they could save starting from the total approximate price of FIM 15,000 for Hawaii and FIM 17,000 for Brisbane.

## Subjects

The experiment was one of the assignments of a university-level course in electronic commerce. Thus, all the subjects were undergraduate or graduate business students. The assignment was optional yet the majority, altogether 92 students, participated in the study.

Weber & Roehl (1999) have constructed a profile of people searching for and purchasing travel services on the World Wide Web. Their profiles are based on the eighth GVU (the Graphics, Visualization & Usability Center at Georgia Tech) survey data. Weber and Roehl found that on-line and off-line information searchers deviated on several accounts. Typical on-line information searchers are well educated. They often hold a managerial or professional position, their work is related to computers, and they have relatively high income.

Our subjects were homogeneous in these background factors, because they were both well educated and technically knowledgeable. As education and technical orientation have both been related to the selection of information sources used for product search, it is reasonable to expect that our data are likely to favor the electronic markets.

## Results

In this section, we report the results of the statistical analyses. We first compare the number of options the subjects considered and relate it to the expected benefits of electronic markets. Then we analyze the search effort of our subjects in both electronic and conventional markets, and relate the effort to the quality of decisions. Finally, we analyze the efficiency of electronic travel markets relative to the conventional Finnish travel markets.

## Size of the consideration set

The size of the consideration set is, in theory, directly linked to the efficiency of the search, since large consideration sets are more likely to include good options than small ones. Efficient information search in electronic markets is usually thought to increase the number of alternatives consumers consider before purchase. We operationalized the concept of the consideration set as the number of alternative travel services the subjects considered while searching. We formulated the first hypothesis to be tested as follows:

H1: Consumers using electronic markets construct larger consideration sets than consumers using conventional markets.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathrm{H1} _ {0} \colon \mu_ {E} \geqslant \mu_ {T}, \\ \mathrm{H1} _ {1} \colon \mu_ {E} <   \mu_ {T}. \end{array}
$$

We scrutinized the data to ensure that the tested variables are normally distributed or do not depart too markedly from normality. For the independent samples ttests the a-risk was controlled at 0.05 when $\mu _ { E } = \mu _ { T } .$

The results of the independent samples t-tests can be found in Table 1. These results provide substantial support for H1: the number of options considered was significantly higher for those subjects who used the information sources of electronic markets. Our data suggest that people tend to construct larger consideration sets while using electronic markets.

The number of alternatives considered is often directly linked to the quality of the decision, as discussed earlier in this paper. ## Time costs of search

To make inferences on the effect that electronic markets have on consumer behavior, further tests are needed to control the costs of search and quality of decisions. We next consider how the information sources used affected the costs of search. As previously discussed, the opportunity cost of time is often considered to be the major cost consumers have to sustain while searching for product information. Should the electronic markets be more effective than their conventional counterparts, consumers would use less time on search when no other search costs exist, or they are relatively small compared to the opportunity cost of time. We formulated H2 as follows:

H2: Consumers use less time, ceteris paribus, for search in electronic markets than in conventional markets.

$$
\begin{array}{r} \mathrm{H2} _ {0} \colon \mu_ {E} \leq \mu_ {T}, \\ \mathrm{H2} _ {1} \colon \mu_ {E} > \mu_ {T}. \end{array}
$$

We compared the mean times used for searching for flight seats and accommodation for both travel destinations. The results of the independent samples t-tests are presented in Table 2. We found that subjects assigned to electronic markets used significantly more time to search for flights to both destinations and for accommodation in Hawaii. Search for accommodation in Brisbane also took more time in electronic markets, yet the difference was more modest. The tests provided no support for H2. Our subjects used significantly more time searching for travel services in the electronic markets. These results conflict with the propositions related to the efficiency of electronic markets.

## Productivity of search

One possible explanation for the result of the previous analysis is that consumers use more time to search in the electronic markets, since the benefits of search are higher. Thus, consumers using electronic markets find more alternatives per time unit and this is reflected in the quality of the procurement decision. We formulated H3 as follows:

H3: Consumers using electronic markets find more alternatives per time unit than consumers using conventional markets do.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathrm {H3_ {0} : \mu_ {\mathrm{E}} \geq \mu_ {T}}, \\ \mathrm {H3_ {1} : \mu_ {\mathrm{E}} <   \mu_ {T}}. \end{array}
$$

To test the relative efficiency of information dissemination of electronic and conventional markets, we constructed a productivity of search measure by dividing the number of alternatives considered by the time (minutes) used for the search. Table 3 presents the results of the independent samples t-test used to test for the differences in productivity in electronic and conventional markets. As can be seen, search in the electronic markets produced only slightly more options per time unit. The differences are very small; the only statistically significant difference observed was obtained for the search for accommodation in Hawaii that was more efficient in electronic markets. We conclude that hypothesis H3 found only limited support in our data. Most observed differences between the electronic and conventional markets were not statistically significant.

The productivity measure is a linear approximation of the benefits of search. The marginal benefits of search are often found to be sharply decreasing. Thus, the productivity measure is likely to be biased in favor of the electronic markets, unless the prices are more dispersed there than in the conventional markets.

We formulated the second part of H4 as follows:

H4b: Electronic markets are more efficient than conventional markets in terms of price level.

$$
\begin{array}{r} \mathrm{H4b} _ {0} \colon \mu_ {E} \leq \mu_ {T}, \\ \mathrm{H4b} _ {1} \colon \mu_ {E} > \mu_ {T}. \end{array}
$$

Table 5 presents the pairwise comparisons of mean prices of flights and accommodation. Independent samples t-tests suggest that electronic markets and conventional markets for travel services are equally efficient for services related to Hawaii. While the mean price for accommodation was lower in electronic markets, the prices were also more dispersed. This resulted in a low t-value in the test and led us to conclude that there is no statistically significant difference in the mean prices between conventional and electronic markets. The conventional markets of flights to Australia are surprisingly more efficient, judged by the mean price level. On the other hand, the mean price of accommodation services in Brisbane was lower in the electronic markets. The accommodation costs reported in Table 5 are the collective prices reported. To control for the duration of staying at the destination, we also tested for the differences in accommodation costs per night, yet the reported results persisted.

Airline seats are considerably differentiated products. Their prices vary as a function of flight class, time spent at the destination, seller, and time of buying the ticket. To ensure that variation in price dispersion and price level does not reflect quality differences in the services offered through electronic and conventional markets, we next constructed linear regression equations to control quality of air travel. As we screened our data, we found that all reported flights were in economy class, and there was little deviation in most well-known quality factors such as the number of legs in flight. The time spent at the destination is one of the most important quality factors for flights and the reported flights diverged substantially on this factor. Thus, we included it in our regression models to explain the variation observed in flight prices. Our models are of the following form:

$$
Y _ {i} = \beta_ {0} + \beta_ {1} X _ {i 1} + \beta_ {2} X _ {i 2} + \varepsilon_ {i},
$$

Where: $X _ { 1 }$ are the days spent at the destination, and $X _ { 2 } = 1$ , if the flight was found in electronic markets and 0 otherwise.

We present the last part of H4 as follows:

H4c: The price level is lower in the electronic markets when the quality of services is controlled.

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathrm{H4c} _ {0} \colon \beta_ {2} \leq 0, \\ \mathrm{H4c} _ {1} \colon \beta_ {2} > 0. \end{array}
$$

The a-risk is controlled at 0.05 when $\beta _ { 2 } = 0 .$

As can be seen from Tables 6 and 7, controlling for quality did not appreciably change the result of the analysis. While no statistically significant differences were observed in the markets for flights to Hawaii, the difference between markets for flights to Brisbane persisted. We feel confident that the observed relatively high mean price and somewhat larger price dispersion in electronic markets do not result from higher quality services available on electronic markets. Thus, the electronic markets involved in our study appear to be at best as efficient as the conventional markets.

One possible explanation for the lack of increase in decision quality is that travel agencies actually make good recommendations. Even if they offer a limited set of options to the customer, these merely constitute a subset of alternatives known to the agency. The travel agency filters available options into a set of alternatives matching the needs of the customer. Better knowledge of available options is part of the value that travel agencies can add to the customer because information disparity exists between the agency and the customer.

## Sources of inefficiency

We asked our subjects to keep a diary during the assignment work. After analyzing the diaries, we concluded that only a few subjects found low-priced flights on the Web. Finding the flight prices was difficult to begin with, since many travel agents and airliners required registration before disclosing price information. The most frequent difficulties encountered while searching for flights were reported as follows: finding price information (16 reports), finding flight availability (16), and need to register to Web services before product information was disclosed (10). Similarly, the most frequent difficulties encountered while searching for accommodation were locating hotels on the Web (6) and finding room availability (6).

Although price information was hard to find, most subjects were eventually able to find sufficient information since they spent a great deal of time to construct the consideration set in the electronic markets. A few subjects were able to find flights to either destination using Webbased services alone. In a feedback session, these subjects stated that it was relatively easy to find expensive flights in the electronic markets while only a few inexpensive alternatives were to be found. Travel agencies seemed to hide their best offerings behind their counters, to be offered to those customers who contacted them personally.

The critical phase of search proved to be finding out if the services offered were still available. Most subjects had to use complementary information sources, for example, send E-mail to the seller, to find out whether free seats were available for flights considered. Many travel agencies and travel service providers evidently require their customers to place a reservation before disclosing information on the availability of the services.

## Discussion

Our findings are rather disillusioning. The observed electronic markets bear little resemblance to those envisioned by Bakos (1997), and were not found to be more efficient than conventional markets. On the contrary, higher mean prices of flights were observed. The observed price dispersion was somewhat larger in electronic markets, yet not large enough to be statistically significant. While the difference in flight prices to Hawaii can be explained by sampling error, the price difference in flight prices to Australia was sufficiently large to be statistically significant. Thus, our analyses provide evidence to question the expected high efficiency of electronic markets (Table 8).

We conclude that the relatively high numbers of alternatives considered while searching the electronic markets are related to searches leading to dead ends. That is, the subjects had to iterate the search process as they learned that the initially preferred alternatives turned out to be either unsatisfactory or unavailable. A number of subjects reported that they experienced difficulties in finding information on prices and the availability of services in the electronic market. Law & Leung (2000), while investigating airlines’ on-line reservation services on the Internet, reported that a higher number of airlines provided on-line flight schedule information than flight availability information. They also observed some regional differences in provision of flight availability information, which they attributed to the high cost of integrating on-line Web services to airlines’ central reservation systems. In our data, flight availability information could be found in the Web, yet many sellers tended to display all flights to a destination regardless of the availability of seats, which hampered product comparison. Many sellers required either registration, making reservations, or that they be contacted before disclosing price information. Thus, it seems that sellers deliberately create obstacles to information search and price comparison.

It is likely that our analysis reflects the present market structure where the same sellers account for most of the market share of both electronic and conventional markets. Thus, the present IT-assisted market systems do little to enhance market efficiency if the underlying market structure is not changed. Alba et al. (1997) suggested that the search costs related to IHS systems are relatively low and tend to decline with experience using the interface. These low search costs may encourage some consumers to switch to electronic markets. However, they also point out that other consumers may find it too tedious to screen the large numbers of alternatives available, unless there is reason to expect that the added alternatives are systematically different from those in the conventional markets with a different distribution of expected utilities.

The observed Web-based travel service outlets are apparently designed for the needs of business customers willing to pay relatively high prices. Furthermore, we conclude that present electronic travel markets are mostly used for marketing purposes. They share characteristics with travel advertisements and catalogues. Rudimentary information about the services offered is provided on the Web, while much of the attribute information is omitted. The efficiency of product comparison is effectively limited, as consumers are not informed about the price or availability of a service before trying to make a reservation.

Sellers should face few technical obstacles in providing consumers with price information through the Web. Price information is likely to be missing when sellers are unwilling to disclose information. On the other hand, there are at least two explanations for the absence of information on the availability of flight seats that are complementary to each other. The airliners are reluctant to reveal the number of free seats on their flights out of fear that this information will be used by competitors to their advantage. Providing information on the availability of flight seats is possible on two conditions only. (1) The seller is also the provider of the services and, thus, owns the inventory system or (2) the seller has managed to integrate its system to inventory systems of various travel service providers. It should be obvious that providers of travel services are best positioned to supply customers with information on available services. Furthermore, seamless integration of reservation systems is a task of nearly Augean proportions. Considering the amount of work needed for extensive integration of systems, it is unlikely that the efficient electronic markets of travel services envisioned will emerge in the near future.

The design of the experiment may also have affected the results obtained. We did not include travel services of very low complexity, for example, a one-leg flight, in our experiment. The exact effect of product complexity on consumer search is, however, difficult to estimate. Judging from the consumer information processing perspective (e.g., Srinivasan, 1990), low complexity products lend themselves for relatively easy comparison since the amount of data needed for evaluations is low. While the direct effect of high product complexity is an increase in search costs, product complexity may also increase the benefits of search. High product complexity and product heterogeneity are often related, and some product-related uncertainty is needed for the search to be beneficial (Moorthy et al., 1997). Schaninger & Sciglimpaglia (1981) found that consumers process more information for heterogeneous than for homogeneous products. Duncan & Olshavsky (1982) reported that consumers searched more when they perceived large differences between brand features and prices. The decision problems in the experiment were largely predetermined. In reality, according to Simon (1957, 1987), decision-making is often hampered significantly by limited human cognitive ability:

The capacity of the human mind for formulating and solving complex problems is very small compared with the size of the problems whose solution is required for objectively rational behavior in the real world (Simon, 1957, p. 198)

Thus, not only objectively rational problem solving, but also problem formulation is often beyond human cognitive capabilities. Building on bounded rationality, championed by Simon (1955, 1957), Bettman et al. (1998) have proposed that ‘due to limited processing capacity, consumers often do not have well-defined existing preferences, but construct them using a variety of strategies contingent on task demands.’ In this context, the search process is not characterized by the evaluation of products against an existing set of decision criteria. Rather, search is necessary for the consumer to be able to identify the procurement criteria and their relative importance.

Another possible bias relates to the travel decision context, which is difficult to replicate in an experiment. Travel decisions are very much affected by forces outside the individual, such as role and family influences, reference groups, social classes, and culture and subculture (Moutinho, 1987). Consumer decisions in general are contingent on the social environment (Bettman et al., 1990); the need to justify the decision to others has been found to lead to more coherent decision strategies. More specifically, the social setting that characterizes the consumption of a product also influences the information search (Stayman & Deshpande, 1989). The composition of the traveling party heavily influences the behavior of leisure travelers (McIntosh & Goeldner, 1986). Leisure travel products are often consumed jointly with others and the travel activities reflect both the direct and indirect influence of all those traveling together (Chadwick, 1987).

Our data are limited to markets of a few services at one point in time. Markets differ spatially, temporally, and as a function of services offered. We have attempted to expand our ability to generalize the results by comparing the electronic and conventional markets of different service categories (flights and accommodation), as well as compiling services associated to different travel destinations into our data set. Our subjects constitute a convenience sample, and as such they deviate from the population of consumers of travel services in many aspects. Our data allow us to make inferences of consumer behavior in electronic markets in the near future. Actual consumers of travel services are, in general, less knowledgeable in technical matters. Thus, our analyses are more likely to favor the electronic markets than the conventional markets.
