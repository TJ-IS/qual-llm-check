# Identifying software project risks in Nigeria: an International Comparative Study

Anja Mursu<sup>1</sup>, Kalle Lyytinen<sup>2</sup>, HA Soriyan<sup>3</sup> and Mikko Korpela<sup>4</sup>

## Abstract

We report a study where software project risk factors were solicited in Nigeria. The study repeats a research design, which has been used in a Delphi study in the U.S., Finland, and Hong Kong to rank software development risks. The study produces a rank-order list of software risk factors and compares results with the earlier study, showing significant differences. Obtained rankings signal the importance of the infrastructure related and socio-economic software risks in developing countries like Nigeria. This demonstrates the importance of understanding the broader socio-economic context in identifying and managing software risks. The study also shows that some risks in Nigeria are located on a different scale. The choice of contingent mitigation strategies of project managers play a less important role, because the risks are so deeply engrained with the poor quality of basic infrastructure services. ## Introduction

Difficulties faced in turning around successfully Information System (IS) projects are still considerable in spite of advances in technology and garnered development experience. In the IS literature, the analyses of IS failures abound with cost over-runs, project delays and unmet user needs (Barki et al., 1993; Cule et al., 2000). Consequently, the importance of preventing failures through proactive planning and by engaging in some sort of software risk management has been recognized since the 1970s (Alter & Ginzberg, 1978; Lyytinen & Hirschheim, 1987; Charette, 1989; Boehm, 1991; Cule et al., 2000).

The first step in managing successfully software development risks is to identify the risk factors associated with the project. By a risk factor we mean a contingency that can form a serious threat to the successful completion of an IS development project (Barki et al., 1993). Accordingly, over the years several articles have been published identifying, classifying and comparing software risk factors (McFarlan, 1981; Boehm, 1989; Barki et al., 1993). Many of these are founded on anecdotal evidence, or they cover a narrow portion of the development process (Keil et al., 1998). There have been but a few systematic attempts to identify software project risks by rigorously inquiring opinions of those who have extensive experience in managing projects (Keil et al., 1998; Schmidt et al., 2001). There is a dearth of rigorous risk studies, where investigators ask (Schmidt et al., 2001): (1) What are typical risk factors that software project managers face?

(2); Which of those factors do software project managers consider more deserving of their attention? (3) Which countermeasures are most effective in mitigating risk, given a particular set of risk factors?

Recently, Schmidt et al. (2001) probed what risks software project managers typically face, and how they rank them in terms of importance in three different socio-economic contexts: the U.S., Finland, and Hong Kong. The study was based on a rigorous Delphi methodology. In this paper we repeat this risk study in a radically different context, Nigeria in West Africa, to increase our understanding of the risk variation, and the impact of socio-economic context on software risks. The goals of the study are to compile a list of risk factors in Nigeria and to determine which of those risk factors are deemed the most important by the project managers.

By repeating the earlier study we expect to observe some germane differences in detected software risks due to radically different socio-economic, cultural and infrastructural environment in Nigeria. This is more compelling because ISD practices and software risks in Africa have remained largely an unexplored terrain. At the same time, the development literature suggests that IT has a potential to become a major driver for socioeconomic development in Africa (e.g. Heeks, 1996). Thus, the importance of understanding and influencing factors that impede effective exploitation of this potential is critical. So far, only problems of IS implementation have been identified in developing countries (e.g. Waema, 1996; Heeks, 1999), while a more systematic analysis of software risks has remained void of rigorous inquiry.

This paper provides answers the two questions in the context of a West African country, what are typical risk factors that software project managers face in this context? and which of those factors do software project managers consider more deserving of their attention? To answer the first question, we collect through Delphi methodology a list of most common software risk factors in Nigeria. To answer the second question, we carry out a ranking exercise trough Delphi-based survey, which determines which of the common factors are considered most important. In addition, we compare these results with the results of a previous study conducted in three industrialized countries: the U.S., Finland, and Hong Kong. Overall, the paper provides an original contribution to the empirical investigations of risk management factors and their dependency on socioeconomic context.

The reminder of the paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we present the used Delphi method and illustrate the research context. In the following section, we present the main findings of the Delphi study by identifying a list of common risk factors, and ranking them. We also compare the obtained rankings with the previous study. Finally, the paper concludes with a discussion of limitations and implications of this study for future research.

## Research method and context

## Research method

This is a repeat study that seeks to improve the generalizability of risk factor research across varying socioeconomic contexts, and also to develop insights why and how such risks factors vary from one context to another. Hence, it is important to follow a similar data collection and analysis procedure across different contexts to increase the generalizability and comparability of the results. Therefore, we followed the canons of an earlier Delphi survey method (Schmidt et al., 2001). The Delphi survey method was chosen as a research method due to its wide applicability for fast data collection from experts in some domain, and its inherent rigor in data collection and analysis procedures. The followed Delphi survey process was divided into three phases (Schmidt, 1997; Keil et al., 1998; Schmidt et al., 2001) after the initial panel of experts had been chosen (see below). Along with the Delphi study, we conducted several case studies on software development in Nigeria (e.g. Mursu et al., 2002). Based on interviews and field observations, we could better triangulate likely sources of identified risk factors.

In phase one, participants of the study brainstorm various risk factors to elicit as many items as possible for further analysis and comparison. Therefore, each respondent is asked to name at least six risk factors, and a short description of the factor. In our case, the step resulted in 131 separate risk factors. We removed duplicates from the list and combined similar and close factors. The number of risk factors was thereby reduced into 51 factors. To guarantee for better generalizability (so that the ranking would start at least always with the same common set of factors), we merged the obtained list of factors with the original list of Schmidt et al. (2001). Thereby, the number of risk factors on the list increased to 72 factors. These factors were then grouped into a set of categories (14) to enable better overview and organization of the list. This resulting final comprehensive list was then reviewed and validated by each participant for possible omissions, completeness, relevance and coherence.

In the second phase, the long list was narrowed down to a more manageable size by asking respondents to choose from a randomized list of items at least 10 factors he/she considers most important. These items were not ranked at this time. The criterion used for narrowing the list was that each item selected had to be regarded important by over half of the panelists. During this step, we ended up with a list 19 risk factors called a common list of risk factors.

In the third phase, the respondents were asked to rank the common list of risk factors in their order of importance to yield a ranked list of risk factors. Here, multiple rounds of rankings may be necessary to reach an acceptable level of consensus. The level of consensus was measured by Kendall’s coefficient of concordance W (Siegel & Castellan, 1988). Accordingly, the ranking process is ended either when the respondents reach strong consensus (sufficiently high W), or the consensus does not change from one round to the next (i.e. $W _ { i } – W _ { i - 1 }$ is either negative, 0 or less than a define threshold). In our study we conducted two rounds, which did not yield a significant value for W that would show high level of consensus.

We stopped the study here for two reasons. First, we lost a majority of our respondents between the first and the second round (the number of rankers dropped from 11 to five). Moreover, the result of the second round did not improve the achieved level of agreement considerably, and thus met the second stopping criterion. The value of Kendall’s W was 0.256 for the list obtained in the second round which denotes a weak significance at P ¼ 0.20. W was 0.142 in the first round with a significance P between 0.05 and 0.10. Due to the type of metric we used we can order the ranked risk factors by their average ranks, but we cannot interpret that this reflects the final order of importance. Moreover, it should be observed that a high or significant value of W does not mean that the orderings observed are true representations of the level of consensus. In other words, a high degree of agreement of an ordering does not necessarily mean that the order agreed upon is an objective one. Kendall suggests that the best estimate of the ‘true’ ranking of the N objects is provided, when W is significant, by the order of the various sums of ranks $R _ { i } ,$ or the average rankings $\overline { { R } } _ { i }$ (Siegel & Castellan, 1988).

## Research context

Nigeria is a developing country in the Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). It is the most populous country in Africa; almost 20% of the SSA population lives in Nigeria (World Development Report, 1998/99). It has a tropical climate, 250–400 or more recognized ethnic groups, and 350–400 languages, while English is recognized as an official language. It has been independent since 1960 (Metz, 1992). Nigeria is regarded to be ‘one step ahead’ in SSA, because of her sheer size, improved health-care system and the volume of educated workforce. Still, most of the problems found in all parts of the SSA are known in Nigeria as well: the climate impact, the net outflow of capital and major inadequacies in the infrastructure. For example, 92% of firms located in Nigeria have installed private generating capacity (World Bank, 1994).

Since the findings in Nigeria can be ‘an antecedent’ for the rest of SSA (Daini et al., 1992) excluding South Africa, it was deemed highly appropriate to conduct the Delphi study in Nigeria. One reason is the growth of the IT sector in Nigeria. In 1988, more than 200 companies offered computer-related services in Nigeria (UNIDO, 1989), while in 1994 the number had grown to more than 500 (Alabi, 1994). By the turn of the century, the number of IT service companies had reached 1000. Most of them concentrate on hardware sales, training courses, etc. We estimate that probably 20% of them, between 150 and 200 companies, develop software or provide system development services (Soriyan et al., 2002).

Project managers representing 11 software companies in the main commercial centre, Lagos, participated in the survey. These companies were selected from a list of member companies of the Computer Association of Nigeria. We tried to keep the sample relatively diverse by having both large and medium size companies. The selected companies are probably more advanced than software companies in Nigeria on average. All companies were software companies (no in-house departments), so there is no specification about the total employees and IS employees in the Nigeria column (see Table 1). The companies were mostly Nigerian in that only two companies were owned by foreign companies. Almost all companies, except one, produced tailored software for client companies, which include banks, oil companies, etc. The software dealt mostly with business applications. One company developed software packages for purchasing. All companies had e-mail addresses.

All panellists were regarded as high-level IT-experts in these companies and had several years of experience in IT field with varying development experience. We had 39 respondents at the start. ## Risk factors in Nigeria

## The comprehensive list of risk factors

The combined list of risk factors obtained from Phase 1 is presented in Appendix A. These risk factors are organized following the same classification as Schmidt et al. (2001) used. We have added one additional group: that of a socio-economic context. The reason for this expansion is that we identified several risk factors that differed considerably from the factors included in the earlier study. Although Schmidt et al. (2001) define their first group, corporate environment, to contain changes in the political environment, none of the risk factors included in that group focus on the socio-economic context. In contrast, most of the risk factors in our list within that group focused on the general socio-economic context rather than on the immediate corporate environment. Therefore, we decided to form a new group called a socioeconomic context.

## A list of common risk factors in Nigeria

For the third phase, the list of over 70 factors was reduced into a list 19 common risk factors. This list of actors can be interpreted to represent the most important barriers to successful software development in Nigeria. We also asked the respondents to indicate the relative importance of each risk in addition to the final ranking in order to control the validity of the ranking. The analysis of the list shows that the variance between the average ranks of importance between the risk items is not lower than with Schmidt et al. (2001) study, which indicates that the scope of critical risk areas is relatively broad in Nigeria. The final ranking for the reduced 19 risk factors is shown in Table 2. Table 3 relates the factors of this study with the factors observed in the earlier study.

Overall, we can see that the list includes six risk factors which are unique to the Nigerian context and did not show up in the earlier Schmidt et al. (2001) study. These risk factors are (5) ‘Under funding of development’, (6) ‘Import of foreign packages’, (10) ‘Energy supply’, (15) ‘IT awareness in the country’, (17) ‘Huge capital requirements’ and (18) ‘Erratic and unreliable data network’. These can be seen to reflect: (a) the lack of capital and adequate resources to develop even mundane services (items (5), (17)) within organizations; (b) the poorly developed public infrastructure that is critical to any reasonable computing service (items (10), (18)); (c) low level of education and literacy of the computing capability and services (item (15)); and finally (d) the threat of foreign services (item (6)).

The last one is hardly a risk factor in general for developing any application, but can be interpreted here to indicate sampling bias (only software houses included) and reflect a business risk for participating software companies. The poor public infrastructure services include thus poor communication network and electricity supply, and reflect the low maturity of computerized activities in Nigerian organizations. Here, the lack of national IT policy and low level of general infrastructure investments establish high barriers for successful software development. In addition, the unstable political situation creates negative conditions for software development. As one manager bluntly phrased it: ‘The government is the biggest buyer in the community, and when the government stops operating it has a ripple off effect to all other business as well.’

The impacts of poor infrastructure for ISD are pervasive. First, companies need to buy alternative sources of electricity that also brake down easily. As one interviewee described, ‘Even when the generator works, fuel is sometimes impossible to procure except on the black market. When NEPA (National Electric Power Authority) does provide power, it is often so bad that computers re-boot on their own.

The same holds with reliable telephone service. One manager told us that when they tried to get six lines to their office, they spent ‘two years and \$1.500 per line to get them’. Yet, they are happy if they have three lines working at the same time despite the fact that they have a person halftime looking after the telephone lines.

Of the remaining 14 risk factors most of them have been recognized in other socio-economic contexts, and are widely regarded as the general critical software risk factors. The only two other factors that did not show in other risk studies deal with ‘inadequate user training’ (12), and ‘lack of skilled personnel’ (4). Although such factors were shown in the longer lists in industrialized countries they did not show up in the reduced lists. The reasons for the lack of educated people vary, however, considerably in different contexts. In Nigeria this shows again the chronic problems in education and the paucity of resources to develop and use applications. In contrast, in Hong Kong the very high number reflected the high turnover between the organizations and the flee of skilled workers from the colony before the Chinese turnover.

The remaining factors are similar to those identified across industrialized countries (see Table 3) and can be said to reflect general common risk factors for software development. These deal with managing and obtaining right requirements, interacting with the users and gaining their commitment, managing the time, resources and people within the project properly; introducing technology efficiently; and following an appropriate process and management approach. There are, however, some important differences how these findings should be interpreted.

First, nearly all studies indicate user relationships as an important risk factor thus suggesting chronic problems with user interactions across multiple socio-economic contexts. In Nigeria, this is mostly due to low level of IT literacy – not because of novel user demands. The main reason why the user relationships risks were emphasized in the earlier study, however, reflects mostly the changed role of applications in users’ work. Consequently, in industrialized countries users’ expectations have become more ambitious and diverse. Therefore, applications need to be built primarily by focusing on how to add value to user experience and operations. In Nigeria, instead, the focus was still on automating clerical and business operations where expected user input was low. Inadequate user training in Nigeria refers to this observation.

Second, some critical aspects in the project management were identified in Nigerian list (e.g. ‘Choosing wrong development strategy’) that are also observed in industrialized countries. This highlights inadequate basic project management skills among Nigerian project managers. The maturity of IS development in Nigeria is still lower than in industrialized countries, and stresses the importance of putting basic project management practices in place (cf. Schmidt et al., 2001). The reasons for the difficulties to manage effectively projects in Nigeria can be again found from the short history of software development and poor management education. Our results indicate that improving basic project management skills can play an extremely critical role for successful ISD process, and it should be emphasized in education.

As in the earlier study, we observed only few risk factors that relate to technical aspects of software development like the choice of programming languages, operating system compatibility or user interfaces (cf. Schmidt et al., 2001). These problems are, however, often also created by inadequate technical skills of the available personnel and can thus become more visible in Nigeria.

## A ranked list of risk factors in Nigeria

If we now look at the relative importance of the rankings of each risk factor, the following risk factors stand out in Nigeria: (1) ‘Misunderstanding the requirements’ (Avg rank ¼ 4.4); (2) ‘lack of effective development methodology’ (Avg rank ¼ 4.8); (3) ‘Lack of required skills and knowledge’ (Avg rank ¼ 5.8); and (4) lack of skilled personnel’ (Avg rank 7.6). Thus, understanding the requirements, obtaining adequate control over the development process, and the chronic lack of skills and personnel are the most critical areas that have to be heeded also by Nigerian project managers.

## A comparison of ranked lists of risk factors

We can compare in more detail the risk factor rankings of our study with the rankings obtained in the Schmidt et al. study (2001). It is obvious that many risk factors obtained in any country-based or organization-based study are situation dependent due to cultural bias, anchoring effects and differences in learning. Therefore, sampling from a single culture tends to leave us blind to some risk factors. Accordingly, it is impossible to generalize into one ‘top-10’ list of risk factors across different cultural settings. At the same time it is instructive to seek variations in risk factor rankings, and examine reasons for such variation.

When we compare the ranked list of factors in Nigeria with the earlier ranked lists of factors, some important differences emerge (see Table 3). Altogether, Nigeria ranked 19 items, while Hong Kong did it for 15 factors, Finland for 23 factors and the U.S. panel for 17 factors. All other countries except Nigeria reached some level of consensus in ranking, although the obtained rankings do not match particularly well with each other.

Among the U.S., Finland, and Hong Kong, there were 11 items that all these countries had in common. If we intersect this list with the Nigerian list, the number of common items reduces to only five, thus demonstrating that the risk management context is probably more distant in Nigeria from the three other countries than they are from one another. This also indicates that managing risks in developing countries can be a different type of exercise. Yet, risk factors observed in the Nigerian list are familiar to anybody who is proficient in the development literature (see e.g. Waema, 1996; Roche & Blaine, 1996).

We will first consider those five factors that all four countries included into their final rankings. These can be regarded as general, context-independent risk factors, which reflect the risky nature of any software development task, and its immediate development context. These five factors are: ‘Misunderstanding the Requirements’, ‘Lack of Required Knowledge/Skills in the Project Personnel’, ‘Changing Scope/Objectives’, ‘Lack of Top Management Commitment to the Project’ and ‘Failure to gain user commitment’.

‘Lack of Top Management Commitment’ was chosen as the most important risk factor almost by common consent by the IT experts in the U.S., Finland, and Hong Kong. Consequently, Schmidt et al. (2001) observe that top management must play a strong and active role in projects from the initiation through implementation despite variance in the socio-economic context and project managers must seek to actively manage this relationship. In Nigeria this factor, however, was not ranked so high. We believe that this is an indication of the less mission critical nature of the applications being developed in Nigeria and the less-developed competitive environment. This demands much less attention from management to IT-related topics.

Two other factors that received a high rating also in the other three countries were: ‘Misunderstanding the requirements’ and ‘Changing Scope/Objectives’. In Nigeria ‘Misunderstanding the requirements’ was ranked as the top item, in both rounds. Notably, in all countries it was included in the top 10 risk items. This shows that obtaining a good insight what one should deliver during the project has been and is an extremely difficult and complex task. One major source of failures is the belief that requirements are understood while in fact they are not. A closely related risk factor deals with ‘Changing Scope/Objectives’, which also was relatively highly ranked in all four countries. Many times this is a function of a project manager’s failure to grasp the requirements right in the first round; sometimes it is due to the fact that the project manager has failed to understand the inevitable volatility of requirements.

Another common risk factor, ‘Failure to gain user commitment’, was ranked much higher in the earlier study when compared with our study. This risk factor can be interpreted to reflect the universal difficulty in managing relationships with users in a productive manner. This problem seems to be universal, although the lower ranking of this risk factor in Nigeria again reflects the fact that applications are not as pervasive, and their development does not necessarily demand such close relationships with the users. Finally, the risk item ‘Lack of Required Knowledge/Skills in the Project Personnel’ reflects general problems in managing and obtaining critical skills and knowledge to carry out the development task.

Many of the remaining risk factors identified in the U.S., Finland, and Hong Kong relate to business strategy. They dealt with business processes, stakeholders, vendors, and consultants and the diversity and multiplicity of stakeholders and user communities was identified as an important new risk factor (Schmidt et al., 2001). Nigerian software companies, in contrast, did not identify this as a risk factor. The void of recognizing such factors in Nigeria can be seen to perhaps reflect weaker alignment between software development and business development, and lower maturation of the IT service markets.

## Analysis of ranking differences in light of Risk Management Theory

Altogether, we agree with Schmidt et al. (2001) that the four panels ranked the solicited risk factors differently. One way to analyze this further is to relate it to the level of control that the project managers feel they can exert over the risks (Marsh & Shapira, 1987). As March & Shapira (1987) suggest this is fundamental to understanding how managers actually approach risk.

Software risks can be accordingly divided into the outside risks (over which the project manager has no control), and inside risks (which can be monitored and controlled). Between these two ends lies a middle ground of risks over which project manager has limited or shared control. Table 4 classifies the Nigerian risk list using this classification. Typical for Nigerian software risks seem to be that risks outside the purview of a project manager are more prominent. This reflects inherent weaknesses, as observed above, in the infrastructure and socio-economic environment.

In contrast, Schmidt et al. (2001) observed in their study that outside risks (with no control) were not widely selected for ranking in industrialized counties. Moreover, those that were selected in this category did not rank highly. Consequently, in Nigeria, the number of outside risks was proportionally higher (68%) than in Finland (56%) and in the U.S. (59%). Their average ranking, however, was not significantly higher than in other countries. In Hong Kong, the number was also high (73%) which may reflect some of the fatalist features of Chinese culture and peculiarities of the Hong Kong business environment (fierce competition, high turn over). The comparison of risk factors in each category and in each country is presented in Table 5.

It is also important to note that risk items with No control or influence in Nigeria are more directed towards the broader socio-economic environment over which the pro-ject manager has literally no control. In contrast, in other countries organizational level factors that are more predictable are more prominent. Usually risks that were observed in Nigeria and which cannot be controlled at all are not considered as risks (March & Shapira, 1987), but constraints.

One interviewee made an observation that it is difficult to rank risks that are common to all contexts of software development on a par with the special ‘environmental’ risk factors in Nigeria. The socio-economic factors and changing requirements locate in two completely different scales, thus echoing the fact that the latter ones are not really ‘risks’. He phrased this as follows: ‘one has to do with risks involved in any ISD wherever it takes place, and the other set is particular to third world countries’. Here we are dealing with constraints that are so pervasive, and integral part of the local reality, so that Nigerians accept them as a part of life like ‘hot air’ or ‘bureaucracy’. Yet, these outside risks need to be taken into the consideration in risk management, even though project managers tend to rank these risks low in importance. Accordingly, project managers should be made aware how environmental factors drive the project’s risk profile (cf. Schmidt et al., 2001).

Overall, Schmidt et al. (2001) observed that participants had a tendency to rank highly risks over which they had some limited control or influence. This also holds in Nigeria as the number of items selected here is the largest and their average ranking is highest. This observation is in line with March’s & Shapira’s (1987) analysis of managers’ risk perceptions, which treat risks as malleable threats, which can be influenced at will. Finally, inside risk items were ranked lower on average than risks with limited control (Schmidt et al., 2001). This holds also in Nigeria. There was some variation between the countries, in that Finland selected a largest number of such items, while the U.S. panel ranked them higher than the other countries.

To explain observed ranking differences between countries based on cultural issues, one possibility is to use Hofstede’s (1980, 1991) theory and dimensions of cultural difference. This model enables to differentiate how cultures approach uncertainty and differences in distinct organizational behaviours (like power distance). Hofstede’s dimensions of cultural differences are: individualism, collectivism, power distance, uncertainty avoidance, and masculinity, femininity. If we apply Hofstede’s way to divide Africa to West, East, and South, Nigeria is part of the West, where an area equal in size and variety to Europe is considered as one culture. Most countries in Africa are colonial creations and usually several cultures can co-exist even inside one country. Therefore, we must approach with caution the generalization that West Africa is just one cultural entity even along Hofstede’s dimensions. In addition, how these features affect possible biases in the risk ranking is not possible to analyze further due to the nature of our data collection method, where we did not collect data on such independent variables.

## Conclusion

This paper reports results of a risk identification and ranking study conducted in Nigeria. The study repeats the research design of an earlier study carried out in the U.S., Finland, and Hong Kong. We asked a panel of Nigerian software development experts what are the most common risk factors in Nigeria and which of those factors they find most important. The goal was to obtain a richer understanding of the risk variation and the impact of socio-economic context on software risk identification and management, specifically to develop a list of common risk factors in Nigeria and to determine which of those risk factors were deemed the most important by project managers. We wanted to shed light on emerging theories of risk sources and variation by providing new empirical evidence of the impact of socio-economic context on software risk.

For the first time, a developing country was used as a data collection site. The results show that there are several important differences in identified risk factors and their ranking. In particular, in Nigeria the environmental and socio-economic risks emerged as a much more important class of software risk. At the same, we observed a small but important set of generic risks that are common to all socio-economic context. These deal mostly with task specification and understanding (requirements), and relationship management (top management and users).

Despite the rigor followed in collecting the data, it is difficult to estimate the extent to which our findings can be generalized throughout the SSA, or other developing countries. We believe, however, that our findings of the importance of infrastructure related risks, and the different profiles of user and project management skills provide valuable insights into assuming that significantly different risk profiles are common in developing countries. They are likely to be different unless they can successfully erect reliable and efficient energy and telecommunication infrastructures and improve their educational standards. We believe that the obtained list of risk factors provides a good starting point to derive such profiles and will help analyze further software development barriers in developing countries with a low maturity level of IT deployment.

The list of 51 risk factors identified in this study has aroused considerable interest among practitioners in Nigeria. It has already been used as a checklist by project managers when they assess software project risks. This study also forced participating project managers to reflect upon their work resulting in positive insights and new knowledge among the participants of the study about the role of various factors that may impede successful software development.

The major limitation of our study is in how the research was carried out by using the Delphi method. The used Delphi method’s suitability to Nigerian environment was not without problems. The deployment of this kind of a method demands efficient and fast data collection methods than is normally required in personal interviews or in the use of ethnographic methods. Reliable communication infrastructures like post, e-mail or fax must be available. Since this was not the case in Nigeria – together with some politically uneasy periods in the country – the study took more time and effort than we planned and we experienced some unexpected problems like high drop-out rates among the participants.

During our study we also observed that the concept of success or failure in ISD may have a broader meaning in a developing country. The magnitude of constraints that one faces in African country like Nigeria makes the ‘basic’ sustainability of information systems, F that is whether IT is used appropriately – a major issue in all development efforts (Mursu et al., 1999). Therefore, by focusing solely on risk factors as perceived by project managers is insufficient to promote successful systems development. There is a need for expanding the scope of studying software risks to a careful socio-technical analysis, which considers several critical drivers that affect ongoing IT deployment. One interesting starting point for future research would thus be to examine users’ and process owners’ viewpoints on software project risks and also use risks.
