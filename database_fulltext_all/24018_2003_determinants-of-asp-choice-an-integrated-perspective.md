# Determinants of ASP choice: an integrated perspective

Bandula Jayatilaka<sup>1</sup>, Andrew Schwarz<sup>2</sup> and Rudy Hirschheim<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>School of Management, Binghamton University, SUNY, Binghamton, NY, U.S.A.; <sup>2</sup>Department of Information Systems, E.J. Ourso College of Business Administration Louisiana State University, Baton Rouge, LA, U.S.A.

Correspondence: Bandula Jayatilaka School of Management Binghamton University, SUNY Binghamton, NY 13902 U.S.A. Tel: +1-607-777-6057 E-mail: jbandula@binghamton.edu

## Abstract

With the rise in the availability of scalable network technologies and resources, Application Service Providers (ASPs) have become increasingly more feasible sources for obtaining IS services. ASP research represents an opportunity to integrate previous research on outsourcing, applied in the area of ASP choice. Historically, outsourcing research has adopted many theoretical perspectives, including transaction cost, resource-based, resource-dependence, and knowledge-based. We apply these perspectives in the ASP context. When obtaining IS services through an ASP, factors other than cost and those explained by the resource-based and resource-dependent perspectives can become paramount. The knowledge-based perspective enables us to examine knowledge risk and integration requirements. Drawing upon past research and an exploratory study conducted with 35 respondents in 11 firms, an integrated, theoretical model is proposed, which combines the transaction cost, resource-based, resource dependency and knowledge-based perspectives. The model is used to explain the antecedent conditions useful for making decisions on IT sourcing through ASPs.

## Introduction

The Application Service Provider (ASP) market has grown dramatically over the past few years, with many experts believing that it will re-shape both the software industry and corporate computing. Although the term ASP is new, the concept of application hosting dates back to the 1960s (Bernstein, 1999). This application hosting has evolved into the business model for the ASP market. While the ASP market has begun to see its share of consolidations and has evolved from its inception (Terdiman, 2000), indications are that the concept of ASP is here to stay (Hirschheim & Lacity, 2000). Consider the following (Demsetz, 1991; Fonseca, 2000; Gantz, 2001):

 IDC estimates that the ASP market will be \$7.7 billion by 2004.

 The ASP Industry Consortium began with 25 members in 1999, but had more than 300 by the middle of 2000.

 In a recent survey of IT executives within America, the Information Technology Association of America found that nearly one-fifth of IT departments were already using ASPs, nearly 24% were planning to evaluate their use over the next year, and 19% expected that they would be using ASPs before the end of 2002.

While the optimistic projections vary in their potential dollar impact, the actual adoption rate of ASP services is relatively low. The ASP Industry Consortium, an advocacy group for the industry, recently found that only 8% of firms surveyed worldwide are currently using an ASP. Further, an IDS study found that 79% of the consumption for ASP services derives from the United States (Sridhar & Balachandran, 1997). Initial explanations of this slow growth include findings that the ASP space is ‘vastly more fragmented, complex, and risky than most business managers realize’ (Kern et al., 2002). Despite the slow adoption, there is ample evidence to suggest the need for academic attention. A related trend – the outsourcing of IT services – has been the subject of significant academic research (Gupta & Gupta, 1992; Loh & Venkatraman, 1992; Venkatraman et al., 1994; Poppo & Zenger, 1995), and specifically earlier research, which can help improve decision making, had examined determinants of IT outsourcing (Grover & Teng, 1993; Grover et al., 1996). However, only a few studies on ASPs can be found in the literature (Currie, 2000; Currie & Seltsikas 2001a; Surmacz, 2001). Thus, academics should respond and begin investigating the ASP market.

To this end, the objective of this research is to understand the determinants of choosing ASP services. To accomplish this objective, the research explores the determinants of ASP choice by adopting multiple perspectives. Historically, three theories/perspectives have been used to explain the determinants of outsourcing choice: Transaction cost theory (TCT), Resource-based theory, and Resource dependence theory. These three perspectives will be presented in the context of ASPs, along with a new perspective: the knowledge-based perspective. Although each of the perspectives provides valuable insights for understanding the choice of an ASP, each has shortcomings when applied to the ASP market. Therefore, our goal is to understand the ASP decision choice by taking an integrated perspective. We argue that this marks the first effort to examine the client-side motivations of engaging in a relationship with an ASP vendor. While previous research has attempted to explain selective sourcing in general, this paper attempts to elucidate the factors that firms consider in an ASP context.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. First, the concept of an ASP will be discussed and differentiated from application hosting alone. Next, selective sourcing research in general will be presented, including a discussion of how selective sourcing research is not generalizable within an ASP context. Then we offer each of the four theories used in the creation of the integrated model, followed by the results from a study designed to validate the factors found from the four views. After the conceptual explanations, we will discuss the integrated model and use a case study to demonstrate how the integrated model can be used to offer insights. Finally, the paper presents conclusions.

## Theoretical development

In developing a theory about the determinants of ASP choice, the first step is to clarify the concepts being studied. While an ASP can be thought of as the renting of packaged software applications and related services (Bennett & Timbrell, 2000), this definition limits the scope of ASP activities, for it considers only generally available software packages. It may be possible that a company develops a software package and hands it over to an ASP for deploying. Therefore, it is important that the definition of an ASP be expanded to include alternative sources of packages, other than those generally available through service providers. Thus, we define an Application Service Provider, as any vendor that offers, on a rental basis, IS application services, which it hosts and manages for its customers. At the most basic level, an ASP can be thought of as an extension of a conventional Internet Service Provider (ISP). Whereas an ISP will likely manage web and e-mail servers that are connected to the Internet (hosting web pages for businesses and individuals and routing e-mail messages), an ASP expands this model to include software programs. ASPs host a variety of applications ranging from simple stand-alone applications such as word processing to an integrated suite of modules such as an enterprise resource planning (ERP) package. ASP users then interact with these remotely hosted and managed applications via a web browser over the internet on an anytime, anywhere basis. In this model, users no longer buy software, rather they rent it, paying for the service on per seat or per use basis. Many commentators believe that the future will see the merging of the ISP, value added reseller (VAR), and traditional outsourcing vendor models into an ASP dominant world. This is different from mere hosting, because the vendor manages the complete deployment and management of the activities related to production. As such, a relationship with an ASP is treated differently from Business Service Provisioning, where the vendor provides a complete range of business services. The customer, an ASP, or another third party can develop the application. The application run by the ASP can be an off-the-shelf package such as widely available ERP packages, developed by the vendor to suit a specific industry or a customer base, or developed by the customer and handed over to the ASP. Within the area of IT outsourcing, ASP represents a selective sourcing arrangement between the customer and the firm providing the service.

Selective sourcing refers to the case where a firm hands over some portion of its internal functions to outside vendors (Gupta & Gupta, 1992). Those functions specific to a client company that can be performed more successfully by an internal IS department as opposed to by an external vendor are kept inside (Lacity & Willcocks, 1995) and those that cannot are outsourced. Typical candidate functions to be outsourced include: software development and maintenance, data communications network, support operations, disaster recovery, data center operations, integrated system development, training, and back-office clerical tasks (Apte & Mason, 1995). Some researchers such as Lacity & Willcocks (1998) have argued that selective outsourcing leads to a greater degree of success, since selective outsourcing provides more flexibility as well as more control over the vendors (see also Apte & Mason, 1995; Sridhar & Balachandran, 1997; Holohan, 2000). Moreover, it is generally thought that outsourcing vendors could have more resources for producing generic IS services and vendors may possess advantages due to economies of scale by the virtue of serving a larger customer base with a generic IS service. Hence, market costs of generic functions will be less than the production costs. Indeed, the finding that selectively outsourcing IS functions results in a higher frequency of success in cost savings confirms the results of Grover et al. (1996), who argue for the value of outsourcing in general. Other researchers have focused on the criteria for selecting functions to be outsourced (Quinn & Hilmer, 1994; Lacity & Willcocks, 1995). This selection can be made based on the strategic importance of the individual functions, where a company may outsource non-strategic IS functions in order to devote more internal resources to strategic IS functions (Quinn & Hilmer, 1994; Lacity & Willcocks, 1995).

While selective sourcing refers to sourcing of any IS function, ASP sourcing refers to only those services that are related to software applications. Thus, the criteria used for deciding on ASP sourcing may be different than for selective sourcing in general. For example, criteria associated with the handling of data, including the need for distribution of services, adequate bandwidth, and immutability, are all important in ASP sourcing. Additionally, in selective sourcing, the location where the applications are developed, maintained, and operated may completely belong to the customer; whereas in the case of ASP sourcing, a customer may have a lesser degree of control over such considerations, especially, since the vendor will be responsible for deployment. Hence, the client company may have less or no control over the deployment of the application. Nonetheless, within selective sourcing, it is important that firms differentiate between strategic and non-strategic IS functions and identify their core competencies and critical applications.

Despite the importance of the topic of application outsourcing, only little previous research examining selective sourcing in the context of ASPs could be found. While Currie & Seltsikas (2001b) recently examined the taxonomy of ASP services from the supplier’s perspective, we are interested in the client-side motivations for engaging in a relationship with an ASP. To our knowledge, no research has been carried out seeking to understand client-side reasons for entering into a relationship with an ASP vendor. This then provides the motivation for our work.

This research seeks to apply the IS community’s understanding of the selective sourcing literature to the domain of ASPs. By assimilating previous research, a model of the determinants of ASP choice will be built. To aid in the development of the model, we adopt a previous framework of the determinants of outsourcing practices proposed by Cheon et al. (1995). The new framework differs in two fundamental ways: (1) The original framework was a contingency perspective toward outsourcing in general. The revised framework is specifically oriented around ASP selection. (2) The original framework included agency theory as a theoretical perspective. The new framework replaces agency theory with the knowledge-based view.

Agency theory mainly focuses on governance mechanisms. Although agency theory had been applied to different organizational phenomena, the domain of agency theory is the relationship between the principal and agent (Eisenhardt, 1989). There are two streams of agency theory research: positivist agency theory research and principal–agent research. Principal–agent researchers focus on the determination of optimal contracts, while positivist agency theory researchers have focused on the contracts and conditions that can result in the agent behaving in the interests of the principal. While Cheon et al. (1995) took into consideration the major focus of agency theory, they have considered agency costs as a criterion for making an outsourcing decision. Except for the assumptions about risk aversion and information (as a commodity), the assumptions of transaction cost theory and agency theory cover the same domains (Eisenhardt, 1989). Although agency costs are incurred in obtaining ASP services, some of the costs due to risks may not be directly quantifiable. Especially, in relation to software applications, their use may entail knowledge that provides advantages to companies, and as discussed later, knowledge-view of an organization provides a perspective that is more suitable for assessing the risks. Further, the role of information regarding principal–agent relationships and agency costs are more comprehensively considered by resource- and knowledge-based perspectives.

Thus, our model represents an attempt to build upon the cumulative tradition of outsourcing literature within a new outsourcing context. As the next step to begin forming an integrated model of ASP choice, we discuss the four research perspectives and apply them within the context of ASP.

## Transaction cost theory

Research on IT outsourcing has used TCT to explain the determinants of outsourcing. The transaction cost view of the firm, developed by Williamson (1975a, b, 1981, 1985) argues that decisions are made by analyzing two types of costs: production costs, or the costs of capital, labor, and materials, and transaction costs, or the costs of monitoring, controlling, and managing transactions. However, analyzing these costs accurately is difficult in IT due to intangible factors. Williamson proposes a heuristic for estimating the costs, depending upon three factors:

1. Frequency of occurrence, or the number of times that a user initiates the use of an application. It could be occasional (once in a while) or recurrent (occurring regularly).

2. Asset specificity, or the degree of customization of the application. Asset specificity can be non-specific (the application is generic for every organization), idiosyncratic (the application needs to be highly customized to every organization), or mixed (some aspects of the application are customized, while others are generic)

3. Degree of uncertainty, or the ability of the organization to determine their future needs, given the effects of new technologies, changes in business needs, etc.

Given the three factors, Figure 1 depicts the transaction cost perspective of ASP choice. According to this perspective, when a firm decides about whether to use an application service provider or build an application inhouse, firms assess the frequency of occurrence, the asset specificity, and the degree of uncertainty.

Since Williamson argues that a high degree of uncertainty will result in the application being developed in-house, a low degree of uncertainty will be assumed for the remainder of the determinants of ASP choice. Table 1 displays the sourcing strategies in the context of ASPs.

Although Table 1 is applicable for IT outsourcing in general, there are several limitations of TCT when directly applied to ASP sourcing, including two major ones: (i) the difficulty of assessing use, cost, and value of resources for application services, and (ii) knowledge-specific requirements.

First, within an ASP context, the frequency of use is a critical factor in determining the cost of an application. A company will have to consider the frequency of use of an application when attempting to utilize resources competitively for application services. For example, frequent use of an application may require better servers that can be provided (and maintained) by an ASP vendor more economically than by a client company. On the other hand, the market might provide a cost-efficient source for less frequently used applications; for example, the cost of obtaining network services might drop if there is low network traffic. The frequency of use of an application may also determine the technical and managerial capabilities required for obtaining specific services. Of course, frequency of use is only important if the application is available. And lastly, economies of scale can have an influence on a vendor’s decision to either develop a specific application package or not.

Second, knowledge-specific requirements are concerning the problems of losing processes that have been created by a company to a third party. An application can be specific to a company and a vendor may not have enough economic benefit from developing it. However, a company can develop a specific package and turn over the hosting, maintenance and related management activities of it to an ASP. Thus, an ASP can run a company idiosyncratic application cost effectively. However, by turning over an idiosyncratic application to an ASP, a company may be compromising company-specific knowledge. Therefore, it may be difficult to explain the decision to obtain ASP services using TCT. Even if the three dimensions of asset specificity, frequency, and uncertainty were to be used, instead of the two-dimensional table as shown in Table 1, the ambiguities due to frequency of use and asset specificity can still arise.

This confirms the earlier findings on outsourcing in general. Theories on outsourcing based on TCT views an application as a commodity and obtaining IS services through the ASP (or vendor) as a transaction. TCT accepts the fact that firms will not exist without opportunism (Williamson, 1985), tantamount to stating that disagreements related to their objectives will exist between a vendor and a client company. Even if we assume that opportunism does not exist in firms (both vendor and client), there may be differences due to resources that exist in companies, especially when companies are attempting to focus on their competencies. While TCT assumes that the IT capabilities available in the market can be compared to those available within a firm, there may be disparities in performances and capabilities that are not readily comparable. Further, the value of IS applications in a company may not be easily measured as in the case of the other more tangible commodities and the use of a given application may differ in two different organizations. Therefore, it may be necessary to consider other attributes (or characteristics) associated with the use of a software application in a company. For such considerations, resource-based theory, which views software applications and systems as resources that provide efficiencies to a company rather than just commodities under economic transactions, might be more appropriate.

## Resource-based theory

A firm has internal strengths or capabilities that are sources of competitive advantage. Resource-based theory is built upon three key concepts: firm resources, competitive advantage, and sustained competitive advantage. The firm can be viewed as a collective of physical, human, organizational, and financial resources. According to Barney (1991):

Resources include all assets, capabilities, organizational processes, firm attributes, information, knowledge etc. controlled by a firm that enable the firm to conceive of and implement strategies that improve its efficiency and effectiveness (p. 101).

A firm achieves sustained competitive advantage by implementing strategies that exploit internal strengths, neutralizing external threats and avoiding internal weaknesses (Barney, 1991). Further, when we consider the external environment a firm’s competitors may imitate or acquire similar resources thereby threatening the competitiveness of the firm. Therefore, immobility of resources is an attribute that provides sustained competitive advantage. On the other hand, if all the firms possess similar resources then no firm may have a competitive advantage over another. Thus, the firm’s resources must be heterogeneous in order for the firm to sustain the competitive advantage. The essence of the theory is that information systems can be a source of sustained competitive advantage; given that a company can achieve resource heterogeneity, resource immobility, and the satisfaction of the requirements of value, rareness, imperfect imitability, and non-substitutability (Barney, 1991). From an IT perspective, IT can become a source of competitive advantage through cost reduction and enabling product differentiation, and four attributes of IT have been suggested as possible sources of competitive advantage. The four attributes are: access to capital, proprietary technology, technical IT skills, and managerial IT skills (Mata, Fuerst, & Barney, 1995). An IT application can become a resource that provides competitive advantage and several different activities must be performed for this to be possible. First, the application must be acquired or developed; second, the application must be deployed; and third the application must go into production. Each of these activities need IT skills and capabilities. When applying the resource-based theory to find determinants for ASP sourcing, these aspects of IT applications have to be examined. Developing an application through an ASP can be explained by considering the resource gaps as done by Cheon et al. and shown in Figure 2.

From an ASP viewpoint, the resource-based perspective requires analyzing the allocation of resources for developing capabilities that may be easily imitated or obtained from vendors. Three limitations can be observed: (i) problems with knowledge-specific requirements, (ii) market conditions, and (iii) lack of explaining direct costs.

First, similar to TCT, the resource-based perspective has problems with explaining decisions about knowledgespecific requirements. For production and operations of an application, running the software application and an IS can establish routines within a company. Routines can embody competitive advantage of a company. Therefore, it will be necessary to consider the routines embedded in an application and the competitive advantage gained through such routines by a company. Second, problems with the market need to be considered. By turning over the handling of necessary data, the company also relinquishes data administration, and the security of data. Depending upon the sensitivity of the data and the ramifications of losing compromising data, the market could impact the decision. Further, ambiguities and questions could arise when determining the imitability of routines and data when an ASP runs the applications for a client and the potential loss of those routines and data to competitors within the market. Third, the resource-based perspective does not examine the cost concerns that decision makers share. When making a decision, managers examine the direct costs and compare the costs to the market as well as other options. However, the resource-based perspective does not explain direct cost comparisons.

If a company obtains resources from the external environment, then a company must possess knowledge about the external environment. However, the possibility and efficiency of obtaining resources from the external task environment depends on its characteristics as the resource dependence theory explains.

## Resource dependence theory

While a resource-based theory of the firm focuses on the firm completing an internal analysis of capabilities, resource-dependency theory recognizes that all organizations are dependent upon the external environment. The choice of an ASP is a strategic choice intended to increase dependence of one organization upon another in order to obtain critical resources. To determine whether or not to increase the dependence of an organization upon another, organizations need to assess two elements: (i) the task environment and (ii) the resource.

The task environment has three dimensions (Pfeffer & Salancik, 1978):

1. Concentration, or the extent to which power and authority between the organization and the environment is dispersed.

2. Munificence, or the availability of resources.

3. Interconnectedness, or the pattern of linkage among the organization and the environment.

Assessing the resource decision (or deciding to invest in the ASP or build in-house) requires the examination of three factors: the importance of the application, the degree to which the ASP company has discretion over the use of the ASP, and the number of alternatives.

Firms must assess the environment to determine a strategy and determine their own resource allocation, given the environment. Firms take their resource allocation and their strategy, and from the alignment (or misalignment) of these two, determine if there is a need to obtain critical resources from an ASP to implement their strategy. Figure 3 depicts a resource dependence perspective on ASP choice.

Limitations of the resource dependence perspective can also be observed. For example, like the transaction cost and resource-based theories, the resource dependence perspective does not examine knowledge-specific requirements. Further, the resource dependence perspective also does not consider the role of the application resource in competitiveness and direct cost comparisons.

When companies consider their core competencies and define the critical applications within the corporation that support the core competencies, they approach an

## Knowledge-based view

The knowledge-based view argues that the competitive advantage of a firm is in the role of creating, storing and applying knowledge. From a resource-based perspective, knowledge is considered a resource, but a resource only at the firm level. The knowledge-based view can be treated as an extension of the resource-based perspective, where a finer analysis of knowledge is performed (Grant, 1996). This extension involves focusing on the knowledge required for the development, deployment, and use of IT applications. Furthermore, a firm will be able to use its knowledge more effectively if the resources required for creation, transfer, sharing, and re-use are applied and managed properly (Nonaka & Takeuchi, 1995; Nonaka & Teece, 2001). The firms that are better at utilizing stored knowledge will achieve competitive advantage over those that do not leverage the knowledge in their organizations. The ability of firms to leverage the knowledge in their organizations is dependent upon two factors (Grant & Baden-Fuller, 1995):

1. The efficiency of integration mechanisms, or the ability of the firm to access, transfer and apply the specialized knowledge available within it to the transformation of inputs into outputs;

2. The extent of capacity utilization of knowledge, or matching the firm’s product domain to the knowledge domain.

Nonetheless, there are times when the organization does not have the knowledge within it to achieve the desired result. It is then necessary to go outside of the firm to acquire the necessary knowledge to achieve the strategy desired. Figure 4 shows the ASP choice from the knowledge-based view of the firm.

From the knowledge-based view, the following arguments surrounding ASP choice can be observed.

1. A firm will enter into an ASP relationship when there is no firm-specific knowledge embodied within the application being used. The market can meet the knowledge integration requirements more effectively when no firm-specific knowledge is embedded in the application (Grant, 1996).

2. The greater the incongruity between the application development knowledge of the firm and IT knowledge required, the greater is the potential for an outsourcing agreement. This is an extension of the argument from the resource-based theory but focusing on the knowledge requirements specifically.

3. The greater the breadth of knowledge that a product requires, and the less the product specificity of knowledge, the greater the likelihood that an ASP will be selected. When a greater breadth of knowledge is required, it would be more likely that a firm can meet its need by integrating knowledge available in the market (Grant, 1996). Further, this is an extension of argument 1.

However, the knowledge-based view also has limitations. Similar to the resource-based and resource dependence perspectives, the knowledge-based view of ASP choice does not consider direct costs to the firm. Further, the knowledge-based view does not consider any resource requirements that a firm possesses when making a choice. For example, a firm contains resources that can be mobilized to develop an application. The knowledgebased view does not account for the resources within the corporation that are examined when the management is looking at developing an application in-house vs choosing an ASP.

In addition to the knowledge leveraging and IT knowledge requirements for an IT application, there are risks involved in exposing a firm’s application outside to an ASP vendor. For example, the use of an IT application within a firm may create tacit procedural knowledge. Such knowledge will remain within a firm if the application use is not exposed to external firms. On the other hand, if the firm obtains the services of a vendor to deploy an application, the firm will be exposing its procedural knowledge to a vendor, which may be tantamount to indirect sharing of its procedural knowledge. In this manner, a firm may expose itself to a knowledge risk when using an ASP. The knowledge perspective allowed us to examine the knowledge risk factor, which was not considered by other research on risks of ASPs (Kern & Willcocks, 2001).

## Model of the determinants of ASP choice

Each of the perspectives has its own focus, view of IT, and considers different factors that are important for ASP choice. (Table 2 displays the factors explained by each of the theories and their major focus, while Table 3 presents the factors that each perspective excludes when applied to ASPs.)

TCT mainly focuses on the cost aspects of ASP choice and the market use and outcomes have to be translated to market costs and compared against the firm’s hierarchical costs. Although the knowledge required for an application can be considered as embedded in the asset specificity assumptions of TCT (Dibbern et al., 2001), a company may use the market for efficient use of its resources and when the likelihood of opportunism is low. The resource-based and knowledge-based perspectives may be thought to provide a more comprehensive perspective for explaining ASP decision given their foci on the use of IT resources and knowledge associated with applications. A firm may evaluate the knowledge requirements and knowledge risks associated with seeking market resources for an application and search for the availability of resources in the market. However, the resource aspects themselves may not be adequate to justify an ASP choice. The comparison of market costs with internal costs is essential for justifying an ASP choice. It thus seems logical to combine the different factors from each perspective.

## Validating the factors

## Data collection

To investigate the methods that practitioners use when determining whether or not to invest in an ASP, an online questionnaire was developed and distributed to members of an Information Systems Research Center (ISRC) at an urban, southwestern university in the United States. There were approximately 25 member companies. The objective of the data collection was to ensure that all of the relevant factors of ASP choice had been discovered during the literature review. Thus, the data were used to confirm the factors found in existing models and not necessarily for testing the integrated model.

The ISRC membership list was chosen for two reasons: (1) The membership was a readily available sample source and (2) the membership included Fortune 500 companies, who had recently voted to include the topic of ASP as one of the top subjects that they were investigating with their firms. Thus, the sample was highly interested in the subject, was currently investigating ASPs within their firms, and the companies were readily available and open to be studied.

An invitation was e-mailed to all ISRC member firm representatives (typically two or three per organization). The representatives were encouraged to participate and answer questions about the use of e-commerce within their firms. In total, 35 respondents from 11 firms participated in the survey. The next most represented industry was the oil and gas industry, followed by the air transportation and aerospace industries. ## Data analysis

Respondents were asked: ‘What criteria do you use to decide whether to build an application in-house vs outsource the application to an Application Service Provider?’ All respondents were offered the ability to list five criteria that they use, but were not asked to list their responses in order of preference. In total, the 35 respondents offered 128 criteria.

All 128 criteria were examined to look for similar citations. A four-step process was followed to group the criteria into logical sets:

Step 1. Initial analysis of criteria: Researcher #2 read through the list of criteria, looking for similar criteria and created an initial grouping set and corresponding definition.

Step 2. Formal grouping of criteria: During this step, the criteria were analyzed in a more structured fashion by researcher #2. Each criterion was placed into the appropriate grouping set. For example, the criterion ‘cost’ was individually listed 15 times. ‘Cost’ was combined with similar citations such as ‘price,’ ‘money,’ ‘internal vs external costs,’ and ‘capital cost concerns.’ After logically grouping all ‘cost’ issues together, the cluster was examined and named to reflect the individual criterion. In this example, all cost criteria are represented in cluster #1, ‘budget and cost constraints, both initially and for the life cycle.’ In the same way, similar citations were then logically grouped together for the 128 criteria until 15 major criteria were found.

Step 3. Formal review of grouping. During this step, researcher #1 reviewed the grouping name, definition, and corresponding criterion. Researcher #1 validated the groupings made by researcher #2.

Step 4. Analysis of the formal groupings: After studying the criterion and groupings, they were analyzed to determine if they fit the factors found during the literature review.

In Table 6, the 15 criteria are listed, along with their frequency of occurrence.

In further examining the 15 criteria, eight major factors were found that helped to explain ASP choice. We categorized the criteria into eight major factors as follows. (1) The first criterion – budget and cost constraints – clearly is an economic cost consideration, which we see as a key factor influencing transaction cost considerations. This is the first of the eight factors. (2) When a company has time constraints to implement an application, it has to find the necessary resources within the given time constraints. If a company can find the necessary resources to develop and deploy an application, it need not opt for an ASP assuming all the other conditions are also favorable for an internal solution. Therefore, we regarded the second criterion – time constraints – as directly related to resource requirements and we placed time constraints in the category of resource requirements. This is the second of the eight factors. (3) A company would seek knowledgeable IT professional only after evaluating the knowledge requirements of an application. Therefore, we placed the third criterion – location of qualified IT staff – under IT application knowledge, which is our third factor. (4) The strategic benefits of a company directly fall under the need to be competitive. Therefore, the fourth criterion – criticality of application – is placed under a factor we label competitiveness. This is our fourth factor. (5) Companies also listed several criteria concerning the availability of resources in the external environment. The respondents expressed their concern about the characteristics of the ASP (fifth criterion), the security offered by the vendor (seventh criterion), and the support offered by the ASP (12th criterion). We felt these three criteria were related to the task environment and this became our fifth factor. Additionally, respondents listed the ‘availability of a relevant product’, the ‘availability of more specific functionalities from the ASP’, and the ‘ease of modifications and flexibility of the available applications’. Therefore, we decided to also include these three criteria (sixth, eighth, and 15th) in the task environment as they too come from the external environment. (6) Some respondents listed availability of other resources such as facilities (e.g. physical and computer), and which do not fall under the categories of direct financial and personnel resources, as important criteria. These were considerations of internal resources; therefore, we have placed the ninth criterion, the availability of internal resources, into a sixth factor called other resources. (7) In addition to IT application knowledge, the knowledge of the industry was also considered essential. If an ASP does not possess appropriate knowledge of the industry, the client company would face the risk of not receiving the necessary expertise from the vendor. Therefore, we placed the 10th criterion, the knowledge of the industry, under a knowledge risk factor. Similarly, the risk analysis of implementation and technology needed (the 13th criterion) is also directly related to knowledge risk so it is included in this factor. (8) The size and complexity of the application (the 11th criterion) is important from the point of view of the ease of integration with the other applications as well as with other activities done by the vendor or the client company. A similar concern is expressed by the 14th criterion, the compatibility of the application with existing infrastructure. Therefore, we placed both the 11th and 14th criteria under a factor called integration requirements.

The simple data collection created eight major factors that helped to determine ASP choice. The eight factors were then examined relative to the four outsourcing perspectives previously presented. An examination of the eight factors reveals that:

 The knowledge-based view of the firm was represented in IT application knowledge, integration requirements, and knowledge risk factors.

 The resource-based view of the firm was represented in the competitiveness, resource requirements, and other resources factors.

 The resource dependence view of the firm was represented in the task environment factor.

 The transaction-cost view of the firm was represented in the economic costs factor.

Thus, the data collection ‘validated’ the relevant factors of ASP choice and corresponded to the four views of the firm. The next step was to create a theoretically grounded, integrated model of ASP choice.

## Integrated model of ASP choice

While the factors presented previously independently help to explain the reason why a firm engages in a relationship with an ASP, as previously argued, each perspective has its own limitations. We believe there is a need to consider a more comprehensive set of factors to explain ASP choice. This we believe can be done by tying together the factors associated with each perspective to form an integrated model.

The integrated model of ASP choice involves four steps: (1) examining the knowledge within the corporation (knowledge-based view); (2) deciding upon the necessary resources (resource-based view); (3) examining the options available, that is, task environment (resource dependence view); and (4) weighing cost issues (transaction cost view). Each step embraces one or more of the identified factors as discussed above. All eight factors are involved in the model. More specifically, the model argues that the IT knowledge required for development and deployment of an application and the integration requirements such as networking defines resource requirements. The lack or possession of internal resources (i.e. other resources) will also influence resource requirements. Similarly, time constraints (i.e. resource requirement) will dictate the necessity for seeking external resources (e.g. an ASP). Ultimately, these resource requirements along with competitiveness, task environment, and economic costs are used as criteria in ASP choice. Additionally, risks due to knowledge exposure (i.e. knowledge risks) will influence the competitiveness of a firm. The eight factors were placed within the steps of a process model and the integrated model of ASP choice was created (Figure 5).

The integrated model posits that as a firm decides whether or not to implement an ASP solution, it will consider the factors suggested by the different views of the firm. Yet, this does not imply a sequential progression as suggested by the representation in Figure 5. A firm does not necessarily move from considering knowledge specific to resources to the task environment to the transaction. The only temporal restrictions in this model are that knowledge-specific areas need to be analyzed prior to examining resources. Beyond this constraint, the model does not prescriptively mandate the order of progression.

As an integrated model of ASP choice, the model offers a descriptive rather than prescriptive examination. The objective is not to highlight the factors that should be considered when deciding upon an ASP. Rather, this model suggests the factors that firms do consider when examining a selective sourcing agreement for applications. While not all firms consider all factors, the model does offer possibilities for firms to use this model as the basis for choosing an ASP. To demonstrate this fact, we conducted a case study with a firm considering a relationship with an ASP vendor.

## Case study: Xcetra Bank

## Data collection

To ‘test’ the factors in our integrated model, we undertook an exploratory case study in a small bank in the northeastern United States, Xcetra Bank. Xcetra Bank was started about 35 years ago and provided services to customers living in several cities around its location. For the purpose of this study, one of the authors interviewed the CIO and the network manager of the bank. Semistructured interviews were conducted for about one and a half hours inquiring about the use of external IT services in the bank. The data collection was focused upon the support services it obtains from a vendor for its credit card business. They have been offering a credit card with their name for about 8 years and about one and a half years ago decided to take a more active role in the credit card business.

## Data analysis

The interviews held at Xcetra were recorded and later transcribed. The transcriptions of the interviews were interpreted looking for the conditions and reasons that had led Xcetra to employ external service providers. The following explanation and short description of the case is based on the interpretations of the researchers based on the responses obtained during the interviews. In the following, we present relevant quotations that reflect the interviews at the bank. Following each response is an analysis using the integrated model.

## Case study background

Although Xcetra is a small bank compared to other large national banks, one of the services that it offered to its customers was a credit card bearing the name of the bank to account holders. Historically, Xcetra had been issuing these credit cards with its name to its customer through another financial institution, with the other financial institution managing the credit card business. However, this arrangement was causing difficulties: Xcetra customers received two separate statements: one for their bank accounts and the other for the credit card accounts, thus making it difficult to ensure consistent customer service. Xcetra management decided to issue their credit card and own more of the process. To provide the services to the customers and support the credit card activities, Xcetra sought services from external providers, ultimately selecting a vendor named Synergy as the service provider.

With the current arrangement, Synergy provides the services for transaction processing, backup processing, charge back processing, and for accessing the payment networks. When a customer wants to make a purchase using Xcetra’s credit card, the request is handled by an application in Synergy, which accesses Xcetra’s database to approve the transaction. At the end of the day, Synergy sends the batch of daily transactions to Xcetra, which completes the customers’ transactions and updates the accounts. When a customer wants to retract a charge made to the credit card, they call Xcetra’s call center. The call center would access Synergy’s application to process the charge back and make the necessary change. The connection between Synergy and Xcetra is structured, such that backup processing at Synergy provides the capabilities to process transactions completely even when Xcetra’s systems are down.

For the purpose of this study, Synergy is the ASP provider and Xcetra is the ASP customer, with the outsourced application being the one used for credit card processing. While the background of the case provides the historical background, it does not uncover the motivations for why Xcetra chose Synergy. The determinants will now be explored and analyzed using the integrated model proposed in the next section.

## Determinants of ASP choice at Xcetra bank

During the interviews, the CIO was asked directly why Xcetra sought an external provider for the credit card processing. The response was:

You really don’t have to but it’s just much more expensive to go directly. With the existing IT department we could not handle it without spending more for expansion.

From the perspective of the integrated model, one of the determinants of ASP choice for Xcetra Bank was the existence of a resource gap within the IT department. Thus, the resource requirement of the application could not be handled internally because of the lack of application knowledge required. Xcetra only wanted to manage the accounts while not allocating the resources for the transaction approval, for charge back systems, for accessing the payment networks, and for a backup system due to the cost of handling them internally. Xcetra has an internal IT department, but did not want to allocate the existing resources to the new credit card processing activity. Yet, it is not just the knowledge needed for the application that made the resources too expensive to be handled internally; there are also integration requirements that make resources too expensive. While Xcetra has outsourced the application for credit card processing to Synergy, they also use other vendors for providing services for customer relationship management. Concerned about the regulatory guidelines that guarantee that customer behavior data will not be compromised, Xcetra relied upon Synergy to integrate the new transaction software such that there was a strong firewall between its systems and the other vendor systems.

When further pressed for more details about the search for the appropriate knowledge and resource requirements, the CIO clarified the process by explaining,

And the criteria were that essentially, price is one of them, the other one was functionality, we have a lot of different credit-card products, 5 different credit card products, and each of them has special features on them. We have a platinum card it’s tied into home-equity loan the other loan processors cannot handle that. We have a MasterCard that has travel points associated with it. Without some credit-card processors you can’t handle that. So, the other primary criteria were you got to be able to sort of service all the products that we offer now.

From the perspective of the integrated model, the (economic) costs were a factor in deciding to move toward an ASP. Second, the features that an ASP can offer, or the presence of appropriate providers in the task environment was important. Lastly, a determinant for choosing the ASP was such that the bank could maintain its’ competitiveness through the use of the application. Moving to full credit card account management internally and using the services of an external provider for some resources gave Xcetra the ability to integrate the information and provide on-line services to the customers. This also made it easy for Xcetra’s customer services agents to gain access to the knowledge about customers’ accounts when the customers call with problems. As the CIO explained, this arrangement was created such that:

Synergy provides us with backup processing so if the link goes down or we go down they can do the authorization for us. Everyday we send them a big batch file that contains the balances of every single Visions member so that they can say, they can stand in for us

From the perspective of the integrated model, Xcetra decided that the competitiveness gained by the use of Synergy did not come with a knowledge risk. Instead, there was an expansion of competitiveness that came from moving the knowledge to the provider, for Synergy could then ‘stand in’ for Xcetra.

In summary, Xcetra sought the use of a service provider to meet the IT resource gap at a lower cost than it could internally. The employment of the service provider also allowed Xcetra to integrate the knowledge about the customers and provide better service at an acceptable cost. The security of the company data was guaranteed through the firewall, while the regulations assumed to be guaranteeing the elimination of risk from exposing some customer data to Synergy. Thus, the integrated model was able to identify the relevant factors for Xcetra Bank’s decision to choose Synergy as an ASP.

## Discussion and conclusions

Our integrated model of ASP choice differs from other approaches to looking at ASPs in that our model takes into account four distinct perspectives on ASPs. Each offers unique insights into ASP choice. The knowledgebased perspective offers a view about the organization: its specific application needs, and the general environment in which the application(s) would reside. The resourcebased view focuses on the resource requirements vs internal resources available of an organization. The resource dependence view focuses on the task environment of the applications choice – the characteristics of the potential ASPs, security issues, and suchlike. The transaction cost perspective focuses on economic costs: are the economic benefits of choosing an ASP worth the costs? Our model is an integrated model differing from Cheon et al.’s (1995) contingency approach. Whereas they suggest a ‘best sourcing option’ based on the adoption of a particular perspective consistent with the specifics of an organization condition; we offer no such contingency.

## Implications for practitioners

For practitioners, our model offers a way of thinking about the adoption of an ASP without having to view themselves in a contingency framework. This we believe is a preferred option since contingency frameworks require organizations to fit themselves into one or another category, and it is not uncommon for firms to experience difficulties undertaking such categorization. As an alternative to a contingency approach, our model works as follows.

(1) An organization – adopting a knowledge-based perspective – would consider the knowledge needed for a specific application, including availability and location of qualified and knowledgeable IT professionals (internally vs externally); the size and complexity of the application and the compatibility of the application with existing infrastructure; and the risks associated with the use of an ASP. This latter consideration involves knowledge about the industry (both internally and externally) and risk analysis of the technology needed as well as its implementation.

(2) An organization – adopting a resource-based perspective – would consider what would be necessary to maintain its competitiveness; what resources it could bring to the discussion, where these resources would come from (internal vs external), how solutions could be arrived at in a timely fashion (overcoming time constraints associated with application development and deployment), and so on.

(3) An organization – adopting a resource dependence perspective – would consider the task environment in which the application would be part, including the company characteristics of the ASP (credibility, financial viability, their experience); the availability of a relevant product; the security of the ASP; the functionality and quality of the application offered by the ASP; the support offered; the ease of modification to the application and the flexibility of the application(s).

(4) An organization – adopting a transaction cost perspective – would consider the costs and benefits of ASP adoption. Such costs would involve economic costs of the ASP product, management costs associated with the contract relationship, and switching costs to another vendor or back in-house if the transaction is terminated.

## Implications for researchers

Philosophically, the model assumes a functionalist view of the firm. This philosophical position suggests that decision-making is rational and that human nature itself is ostensibly predictable. The measure used to assess the veracity of the model is its predictive power: is the model able to capture enough of the variation of behavior accurately? While alternative philosophical positions are beyond the scope of this research endeavor, we note that:

(1) we have wed ourselves to the philosophical assumptions implicit within these four perspectives and (2) such a position is consistent with the dominant view of the firm within mainstream IS research (i.e. functionalism). Further research could adopt different philosophical positions, for example, interpretivism or radical humanism.

In the outsourcing literature, other perspectives have been used to a greater or lesser degree; for example, agency theory (Heckman & King, 1994; Wang et al., 1997), power and politics (Lacity & Hirschheim, 1993; Hirschheim & Lacity, 2000); relationship theories (Klepper, 1995; Kern, 1997); strategic management theories (Quinn & Hilmer, 1994), innovation theories (Loh & Venkatraman, 1992; Hu et al., 1997), institutional theory (Ang & Cummings, 1997; Jayatilaka, 2001), and game theories (Klotz & Chatterjee, 1995; Elitzer & Wensley, 1997). The four we chose were picked because: (a) they are compatible with one another and (b) the data collected in our study validated the factors found in theoretical generation of the integrated model.

## Limitations

Nonetheless, the research to develop the integrated model does not come without limitations. First, the data collected in the study reflected data from across the organization and was not limited to ASP decision makers. While we argue that individuals at all levels of the organizations share in the decision-making, some of the respondents in the data collection may not have been directly responsible for IT decision-making. Yet, we believe the views toward ASP choice collected from the respondents can be used as a surrogate for decision makers, since the majority of respondents were in highlevel IT decision-making positions. A second limitation is the limitation to the four views of the firm presented. Each of the models selected represent a functionalist view of the firm and reflect rational decision-making. By limiting the model to these four views, we have wedded ourselves to the assumptions and criticisms inherent in each perspective. Our exclusion of agency theory was based on the argument that it is more suitable for investigating governance mechanisms and ASP choice is more closely related to the resource (and also knowledge) needs and availabilities of a company. Although agency costs are not considered as important as in selective sourcing of overall IT activities, where the agency roles are more explicit, the management of relationships with ASP vendors might benefit from a consideration of agency theory.

While the model is theoretically sound, no empirical justification exists for the model. In this paper, we have not empirically tested the model but have shown how a conceptually/theoretically derived ASP choice process (represented by our model) reflects how at least one organization decided to choose an ASP. Of course, further empirical testing is needed, but this must be left for another research exercise.

Lastly, we have assumed that the results of our study are appropriate not just for the US but for Europe as well. We believe our results are valid for Europe based on the work of Kern et al. (2002), who suggest there is ostensibly no difference in ASP choice behavior between practitioners in US and Europe. A similar finding involving general IT outsourcing was noted by Apte et al. (1997).

In summary, this paper has offered an integrated model, which should help organizations in their decision process about ASPs. The four perspectives embraced in the integrated model each brings into focus different aspects of the ASP decision process. This we believe is the first attempt to provide such an approach.
