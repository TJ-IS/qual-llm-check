## Abstract

The article comments on, and critiques, a view of IS as an independent discipline. It argues that, rather than being a subject that is distinct and distinguishable from existing research disciplines, it cannot be seen as uniquely different, and to attempt to do so would be theoretically and practically unsafe. A number of suggestions as to how IS research might be conducted and assessed in light of this claim are made.

## Introduction

While I apologise for the appalling convolution in the title of this comment on Ray Paul’s opinion piece, it serves to entangle the two and move the debate on (well, surely (IS)<sup>4</sup> is the next step on from (IS)<sup>3</sup>?). As in the article preceding this, it is intended to provoke a response, and to highlight what I believe to be an important question in the nature of what it is to do research in this field we call ‘information systems’.

At this point, it may be relevant to state my association with Ray Paul and to make my personal position in the area clear. I have known and worked with Ray for the last 8 years, and at one point, he supervised my Ph.D. As dean of faculty, he is my boss, although I maintain a good relationship with him. Like most researchers within Information Systems schools and departments, I did not do my undergraduate studies within an IS environment (I used to call myself a psychologist), and did not originally join an IS department, although I have ended up working in one, being variously (and without having changed jobs) in a Department of Mathematics and Computer Science, then Computer Science and Mathematics, Computer Science, Computer Science and Information Systems, Information Systems and Computer Science, and then (note the diminutive) Information Systems and Computing. Perhaps I exaggerate a bit here, but this is not too far from the truth. In essence, however, I fell into an IS department without really choosing to do so. (Aside to Ray: yand I am very happy to continue working here.) What I am attempting to clarify here is that I have no motive to attempt to justify the existence of IS as a field, although this may become apparent as I continue. There, I feel better already.

What I will do in the rest of this response is to run through the points that Ray makes and place a different spin on them. To summarise my understanding of the points raised in the piece, they are as follows:

(i) measurement of ‘quality’ in research is not a true measurement of value;

(ii) a definition of IS as a field is given;

(iii) a critique is given of (the abuses of) methodology and theory in researching IS problems, and a claim made that there is no ‘body of knowledge’ in IS;

(iv) it is proposed that an IS provides a working model of an organisation;

(v) a claim is made that a subject (i.e. IS) that seeks to become a discipline can do so by doing what we have agreed to call ‘research’.

I will attempt to address some of these issues below, but before I do this, I think it will be useful to discuss information systems as a technology, before examining how it is taught and practiced.

## IS: discipline or domain?

Since becoming a member of an IS department, my mind keeps returning to the question of what Information Systems is (or is it are?). The problem is semantic as well as grammatical. It is also probably pragmatically problematic $( \mathrm { P } ^ { 3 } ? )$ , but I digress.

This question was originally motivated by my becoming a part of an IS department, and not really knowing what IS wasyor rather, I tried to think about what IS was not. Well, I did not think it included the arts: fine art (although it did include design), languages (although it did include the study of language), history (although it did include critical methods such as hermeneutics). If that was all it excluded, what then did it include? Anything that constituted, or examined, a system of information, possibly ranging from geography to particle physics – technology, science and the social sciences? What sort of department was I getting myself into? I think Ray’s opinion piece is an attempt to steer through this melange of confusion and to define what a department of IS does. However, this is not necessarily what IS is.

At its root, an information system is a system that contains information. This may be any such system, such as a system of paperwork (see, e.g., Sellen & Harper, 2002), but more commonly, we think of them as computer systems. In practical, everyday terminology, IS tends to refer to large-scale organisational computer systems. IS are not therefore an area of research, but things – they are a form of technology (although not necessarily a piece of technology).<sup>1</sup> Of course IS can be studied, but this discipline would be IS research. Ray provides an interesting perspective on this – by tying the information system to the model of an organisation, he is making a direct association between systems thinking (i.e. research) and the technology itself. However, again, a distinction needs to be made here: an instantiation of a model is not the same thing as the process of modelling.

What I am attempting to do here is to show that information systems are perhaps best described as a domain rather than a discipline. Rather than appear to be pedantic in my argument, this is an important consideration in bringing up a discussion on method and methodology, and on what the ‘body of knowledge’ in IS (i.e. IS Research, or ISR) is.

## Information systems research

If the focus of work in ‘ISR’ is on examining work systems, ISR methods and a body of knowledge are applied to understanding the nature of IS as a technology. We can therefore do research on ${ \mathrm { I S } } ,$ but is this the same as research in IS? If we are to break this idea of research in IS down, we end up asking the same sorts of questions that other academic disciplines end up asking – the central question being ‘how are systems organised?’ This question forms the core of a number of other academic disciplines: psychology, anthropology, sociology, organisational behaviour, computer science, operational research, mathematics, physics, economics, and so on. Moreover, depending on the sorts of questions that might be asked about an information system, we have a range of well-developed methods that can be applied to understand these: experiments, observational techniques, or computer (simulation) modelling, to name a few. Ray’s article does not dispute this – however, he argues that application of methods (and methodologies) directly from these disciplines and the measures of relevance required by these disciplines is inappropriate in IS research.

There are strong pragmatic reasons as to why research into information systems should not be judged on the same criteria as the existing research disciplines and paradigms and these, I think, are what Ray is appealing to. Yes, I would agree that to make an impact on IS design, the research turnaround occasionally needs to be rapid; yes, it is hard to do multi- or inter-disciplinary research because of the multiple bodies of existing knowledge; yes, to understand the problems of a technology, or how a technology is used in a setting, an appropriate research method may become apparent only after a significant period of study. Yet, to me, these justifications are problematic. Research is research – arguments about the need for a methodology and the significance of data cannot be determined with an appeal to the nature of the problem domain. Instead, I suggest that we concentrate on the value of the data (which would include its relevance and application to the domain) arising through the application of accepted research methods. This switch in emphasis is enough to satisfy the criteria set in existing research disciplines; the critical component in this being to make visible the limitations of the study (perhaps showing the nature of the research constraints), and justifying the need for the approach chosen.

## On division and self-reference

There is one further danger in separating information systems out as an independent discipline from existing disciplines, and this is with regard to its insularity from mainstream thought in other disciplines. As with all boundaries, social, physical, or political, the intellectual boundaries that IS seeks to constrain itself within as a discipline may focus the community on itself, rather than outside itself. This is all well and good when the focus of a research domain is centred squarely on a single topic of interest, but IS research is diffuse, looking at many aspects of information systems and covering anything from the economic to the psychological. It is hard to provide justification for this with evidence from other disciplines, but a simple review of publications within information science (composed largely of research librarians, incorporating research from information retrieval and library information systems) shows how it is disciplinarily self-referential. I will use, for example, the proceedings of the second conference of information seeking in context (Wilson & Allen, 1999 – a publication on information science plucked at random from my bookshelf). For example (and I have not performed a statistical analysis of this), many of the bibliographic references within this relatively small discipline are from within the information-seeking literature, even when referring to models of human activity. On inspection, several of the models developed and used in the proceedings appear to have been developed by information science researchers and independent of contemporary sociological and psychological research. This cannot be a good model to follow. This paper is not intended to critique information science (and would be a weak argument for doing so), but to provide a warning to researchers within information systems that disciplinary partition can lead to insularity, and that this intellectual insularity can have detrimental consequences.

The argument raised above could be challenged as being easily addressed, in that it is simply bad research not to address work from other disciplines. However, if we metaphorically turn around and look in journals and conference proceedings from sociology or psychology, we see little evidence of the material used or referenced from outside these domains. Herein lies a subtle difference: to a large extent these disciplines are narrowly focused and require little input from outside contexts. Moreover, there is a huge body of literature within this tight focus that encompasses what these researchers are attempting to do. Information systems research is unlike these disciplines – (rightly) it has no unified perspective on the way that we should examine a system of information, because of the huge range of reasons we might be investigating it. Similarly, we cannot develop a large or deep body of research in each of the areas because of the huge number of perspectives that IS research has on information systems themselves. However, by creating an artificial boundary and calling IS a discipline, we are undeservedly reifying it as a unified body, and, in a sense, giving permission for researchers and practitioners to stay within its confines just as other disciplines are wont to do.

## Practicalities – teaching and practice in ISR

The arguments made above denying IS its position as a discipline are, I hope, theoretically and practically persuasive. However, for those teaching the design of information systems and practitioners developing and implementing systems for the real world, it may appear to be navel gazing. In some ways, these are very academic arguments, but they do have a practical side to them. IS research and development needs to be carried out in a principled and effective way, drawing on an integrated set of resources and prior knowledge without its practitioners having to become interdisciplinary experts. While this is not an argument to negate my previous lines of reasoning, it does pose an interesting dilemma: is it possible to teach and practice IS as if it were a unified discipline?

The answer to this is of course, yes. There are a huge number of domains in which this is performed without a great deal of problems. Within universities<sup>2</sup> this can be clearly seen in departments teaching medicine and engineering, and to an extent, many other departments in which there is a ‘craft’ or ‘applied’ element.<sup>3</sup> What distinguishes these from IS is that they are either explicitly multidisciplinary domains of enquiry, or that they treat the methodologically applied component of research differently, with the relevance and utility of the research (as I have suggested in IS) to the domain being emphasised in its assessment. This turns the use of the term ‘discipline’ from one meaning a set of relatively rigid boundaries (i.e. an accepted and regulating set of rules, methods and a body of knowledge), to its everyday meaning – a set of skills and knowledge relevant to a domain. In this sense of the word, there are no impediments to developing an applied body of knowledge and set of skills to teaching and conducting IS research and development. What we need to continue is to bear in mind that this is rather different to an epistemologically and ontologically coherent approach to the subject that would make IS an academic discipline. Certainly, in the Kuhnian use of the term, no paradigm shift has taken place to make the understanding of information systems distinct from the other disciplines that I have argued compose it.

According to this argument, we do not need to appeal to artificial criteria (e.g., as set by research funding bodies) of what research in IS is, because such criteria already exist in the other formative disciplines that set our research paradigms. There is a pragmatic point to make here: outlets for publication (journals and conference papers) and that yardstick for practitioners, successful project completion, are what creates a – useful – illusion of IS as a discipline. These are judged and evaluated according to the concerns of those working within the same, or similar sets of practical constraints within the domain. While not a discipline, there can be a developed and developing resource set for those working within IS. If that is what works for you, run with it, but researcher, beware – when playing with mirrors, you might get cut. An illusion is not reality, and brings with it its own dangers.

## Do we need a discipline of IS research?

## Conclusion

So, we have a definition of an information system as a technology (but not a set of methods or a body of research). We have a viewpoint on what might constitute criteria for judging the acceptability of research findings – its value to the domain (and not primarily its rigour). We have a perspective on the body of knowledge that constitutes its parent disciplines (but not an independent body of knowledge of its own). This may be a very conservative perspective on what IS is, but it is the only one that I can feel truly satisfied with as being internally consistent.
