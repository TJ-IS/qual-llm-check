# Information systems: a subject with a particular perspective, no more, no less

Guy Fitzgerald

Department of Information Systems and Computing, Brunel University, Uxbridge, Middlesex, U.K.

Correspondence: Guy Fitzgerald, Department of Information Systems and Computing, Brunel University, Uxbridge, Middlesex, U.K.

European Journal of Information Systems(2003) 12, 225–228. doi:10.1057/palgrave.ejis.3000473

I respond to Ray Paul’s Opinion Piece (Paul, 2002) as follows:

The debate of ‘what is X, and is it a true discipline?’, where X is the subject on which the author of the statement currently teaches or practices, is perennial, especially where the subject area of X is relatively new and/or under threat. I have personally seen at least three distinct waves of interest in this debate within Information systems (IS) in my career. Perhaps Paul is attempting to create another wave. I personally hope not, for I have not seen much benefit from such debates within IS (or anywhere else for that matter), although there is always hope that this time it will be different.

I am, like others, sometimes embarrassed in discussions with people outside of our subject of what marks out or distinguishes IS and what it has contributed. I am usually able to say something about what I think IS is, but struggle when I try to identify any general guidelines, let alone rules or ‘universally applicable results’. My usual response is that IS is a new subject and has not yet had time to evolve and develop such important things. I talk about how long it has taken most other disciplines to be recognised and accepted as such. I also might add, if I am feeling particularly bullish, that there is indeed some evidence to suggest that a few guidelines are evolving, provided, of course, that we define the context of their applicability quite narrowly, and hope whomever I am talking to does not ask for evidence or examples.

However, as I walk away from the conversation, I have to admit to myself that I do not really believe this. I do not think that we are even close to being a ‘discipline’. Of course, applying traditional academic method, this actually depends on what we mean by ‘a discipline’. Jones (1997) addressed this problem directly and entitled a paper ‘It all depends on what you mean by discipliney’! In this he identifies three different interpretations from the literature, as follows:

(1) A normative interpretation, that is, ‘seeking to set out, often on the basis of some first principles, what topics are to be included within its boundaries.’

(2) A descriptive interpretation, more pragmatic than 1 in that it categorises ‘what it is that IS teachers, researchers (and in some cases, practitioners) doy The question here is not whether there is necessarily a common perspective, but what is included under the banner of IS, however diverse the practices may be in terms of their philosophical stance.’

(3) A control interpretation, that is, a way of controlling the above two aspects ‘it is through the operation of institutions, such as professional bodies, academies, accreditation systems, and academic journals that the loose collection of individuals in (2) are brought into alignment, with varying degrees of success, with the rules provided by (1).’

As a result of these interpretations, Jones feels that IS would not meet the requirements for a discipline. However, for others, and based on much the same interpretations of discipline, it has sometimes been concluded that IS is a discipline because it has topics, based on some principles, often those of applied computing (although there are others). It has a banner (a practice) and it has mechanisms of control such as associations, university departments, journals, codes of conduct, etc.

However, for me this is not what is meant by a discipline, although all these things may well be important parts of it. What is absent from this definition (although it might be argued to be included in 1) is the key concept of a ‘body of knowledge’, where body of knowledge is not just an agreement about what is ‘included under the banner of IS’ but as Paul suggests, means ‘some rules or laws or evidenced guidelines or even practical universally applicable results’. So we appear to have some of the trappings of a discipline, for example, mechanisms of control, but without the core body of knowledge or agreed theory. Perhaps this means that IS is an intellectual subject (actually the question posed in the title of Paul’s Piece) but not a discipline. IS can, and has been, be argued to have an agreed set of topics, etc. (Banville & Landry, 1989; Khazanchi & Munkvold, 2000) and all the trappings of Jones’ definitions. It is thus intellectual in that there is enough that distinguishes it as a subject and that generates significant questions and debates, with different stances and perspectives that participants can argue cogently about, that is it is an intellectual subject, but it has little or none of Paul’s body of knowledge. We might therefore conclude that it as an intellectual subject in search of knowledge.

But what is this ‘body of knowledge’ that Paul requires for IS. It is actually what is required to be a science, with immutable laws and rules, developed using the scientific methods of proof. As we know this can indeed produce great benefits and progress. But it is judging IS on scientific criteria when it is clear it is not a science. Paul tones down his criteria by saying these are rules, or evidenced guidelines, or practical universally applicable results, but in my view, to achieve even this, the only way would be by the application of scientific method. So perhaps this is suggesting that IS is more like Engineering rather than the physical sciences, that is an applied science. Engineering, though, is still based on science and the scientific method, unlike IS, which, whilst having some elements of applied science, is much more focused on human activity systems. This means that it is not a science, even in Engineering terms. The idea of anyone ever agreeing a body of knowledge in IS is questionable.

Suppose we did find something that the IS community agreed upon. It was once thought that participation of users in systems development resulted in better, more acceptable, more successful, systems. Indeed, a number of studies (although not of a scientific nature) appeared to come to this conclusion. Might it be a putative rule? Might it be the basis for a little bit of a ‘body of knowledge’ in IS? Unfortunately not, in my view. Firstly, it could never be in any way ‘universal’. We can always imagine some circumstances in which it was not applic able. For example, what about embedded systems that do not have any humans to participate? So we are forced to narrow the context in which it might be applicable–say business systems in which users are involved, which might be more accurate but unfortunately makes it much weaker as a rule. Secondly, of course there would be argument about what is meant by ‘better or more successful systems’. IS has never resolved the issue of ‘success’, the best it has achieved is to try and identify some surrogates of success, such as user satisfaction, but this is still highly contentious, and argued not to represent success. Thirdly, it could be that users might not want to participate in systems development for some reason, which again narrows its applicability. What happens if the system changes work patterns of users fundamentally or even removes employment from users, as happens sometimes. Fourthly, a business may find it too expensive to involve users, negating the economic success of the system. Thus there are likely to be many other factors that could also be taken into account, that help determine success, which would negate our rule. Fifthly, there are studies that find ‘successful’ systems that did not have high levels of user participation. And so on. There are many other factors that will negate our potential rule. We might also find that while IS academics may identify a rule, practitioners choose to ignore them, as by and large they did in this case. So if it was not a rule, could it have been an evidenced guideline? Well, no in that it will never be truly ‘evidenced’ but yes in that it could form part of a normative guideline, that is, it is an interpreted view of something a practitioner developer might consider doing, under appropriate circumstances, but it would not necessarily lead to success. It certainly would not be an agreed guideline by the whole IS academic community–there would always be dissenters, indeed there might be alternative competing guidelines. This puts a great deal of pressure and responsibility on the practitioner to weigh up the competing guidelines and identify the circumstances of appropriateness, etc. And this perhaps explains why they tend to ignore the IS academic community and their guidelines. Nevertheless, the production of guidelines is, in my opinion, an appropriate activity for IS, which has some small potential benefit, but is never going to lead to rules, in a scientific sense. So a clear distinction needs to be made between rules and laws, and more general, only possibly helpful, guidelines. Paul mixes these up in his test for a discipline.

So IS is not going to qualify as a discipline, in Paul’s terms (if we ignore guidelines), because it is not a science, and as we have seen a discipline involves science. This means, of course, that many other subject domains are also not disciplines, for example, history, geography, sociology, indeed most of the so-called social sciences. But what about economics? Economics, or at least macroeconomics, can be argued to have developed some laws and rules, for example, the laws of supply and demand that govern market behaviour. These are termed laws and it seems that most people accept them as ‘laws’ although they are clearly not universal. They have been empirically and statistically tested and have been seen to apply in enough cases to be considered as laws. Their testing has been conducted using scientific methods. This means, using our definition, that economics can be regarded as a discipline. Its research approach utilises scientific methods and yet its domain involves human activity, a rare mixture. However, it might be argued that economics has relatively few laws and that the majority of its body of knowledge is no more agreed or scientifically provable, than that of IS. Yet it has some, so could it be a model for IS?

I think not. Why because IS, for the most part, does not operate at the same level of granularity as macroeconomics. The unit of analysis is different. IS typically operates at the level of the application or the organisation rather than the economy or society, which makes it more like micro-economics than macro-economics. This implies that rules or laws are very unlikely to be determined or determinable because such laws involving human activity do not operate at this relatively low level of granularity. So for IS to try and imitate macroeconomics would probably be counterproductive. However, if some people want to attempt this, by applying a scientific method to discover rules and laws, then that is their prerogative, I just do not think it is likely to produce the desired outcomes.

So what is IS and what should it be striving to achieve? In my view, IS is a perspective, which places it between the technology and a number of other existing subject areas, such as management, psychology, sociology, computer science, etc. In IS, we stand with our backs to the technology, the computer, the machine or whatever, and look outward towards the world at large. However, computer science stands in much the same place but looks inwards towards the technology. In IS, the context is broad and includes important issues beyond the technology, including business, organisations, social issues and their impacts. The impact of technology is not just concerned with efficiency, that is, that it allows existing activities to be undertaken more quickly and cheaply, but that it is a significant change agent with an ’enabling’ quality. This wider perspective means that IS has to embrace a number of other associated topics and a wide variety of research approaches, some of which are at a considerable distance from the quantitative and positivistic methods of science. If IS is just a perspective in this way, it is partly defined by the technology and that is key to the perspective and cannot be ignored. I therefore exclude from IS those topics that do not have some relationship to technology, so the artefact at our back is of crucial importance. The looking outward embraces whatever subjects or disciplines may prove useful. So for example, in IS outsourcing research, with which I have been involved, the fact that it is outsourcing elements of technology or systems development is crucial. It is different to outsourcing cleaning services or catering or anything else. The analysis utilised some existing concepts and theories relating to economics and management, such as ‘make or buy’, transaction cost economics, core competencies, etc., but also some IS issues relating to technology, skills, the IS market place, IS development activities, etc. Thus, IS is a combination of these issues, and as such inter-subject (or as more usually termed inter-disciplinary–but it is not restricted to those subjects defined as a discipline), in that it might utilise ‘things’ from those other areas, where ‘things’ might be concepts, theories, methods, etc. For me, this perspective gives particular strengths. For example, in relation to the IS outsourcing research mentioned above, we found that IS outsourcing was not like all other forms of outsourcing, there were significant differences. We found that existing theories, from other disciplines, did not seem to apply, or at least not in the way that might have been expected. We found that some factors from our analyses might possibly have had some relevance or impact on the reference discipline, from which some of the possible explanatory theories were imported, and so on. Thus, the particular IS perspective is argued to be valuable.

I hope I have addressed the essence of Paul’s discussion about IS as a discipline and intellectual subject but it is difficult to know as his Piece veers all over the place taking swipes at various topics along the way. This is, of course, totally acceptable in an Opinion Piece. I will address a few of these swipes. For example, Paul complains that IS does not have its own particular research methods. Well, this seems to me to be a strength. As IS is not a discipline, we should expect it to employ research methods from other disciplines and apply them as appropriate to the type and objective of the IS research. Why should we not utilise the research methods of other disciplines? Why would IS have its own methods? What might these be that are not utilised already in other subjects or disciplines. I like the idea of competing and alternative research methods in IS. I think it is appropriate to have a pluralism, including both positivist and interpretative methods. Let the best methods emerge based on the best outcomes, but I suspect that this will not lead to any single appropriate research method in IS, even in the long term. Paul thinks that method pluralism has led to ‘a ‘pick and mix’ approach to the use of research methods. I agree that this is a danger so there needs to be a focus on the justification of the appropriateness of the research methods used in IS, and I believe that this exists in most IS papers. Paul seems to object to this justification, whereas I think it is critical. Where legitimate alternatives exist, it seems to me that we need to justify those we use very carefully. In particular, Paul seems to object to questionnaires, and I agree that some IS survey research is quite banal. We both object to poor or inappropriate methods, but unlike Paul I think there are circumstances where questionnaires can be appropriate, and indeed there are some good examples of survey-based research. Paul suggests that the researcher asks themselves if a survey will discover something they already know or not. He argues that if they already know it, then ‘why bother?’. Well this seems to negate the notion of confirmatory research, which is an important basis of the scientific method. If we test something we already know and it is confirmed, it helps evolve towards an accepted finding or a rule and then perhaps a theory. Just the kind of thing Paul appears to want in IS. If the researcher does not already know the answer to the question they are proposing, he asks ‘what questions can they ask to find out what they do not know?’. Paul appears to think that this is good enough to convince us of the ridiculousness of questionnaires. Can I not ask in a questionnaire a question I do not know the answer to? As part of the above-mentioned outsourcing research, Leslie Willcocks and I conducted a survey utilising a questionnaire. There were a whole range of questions that we did not know the answers to, such as what kind of IT outsourcing were companies undertaking. We found some we did not know about! How? Well the respondents told us about them. More importantly, we found that the majority of outsourcing in the sample was ‘selective’ and in quite small quantities, rather than ‘total’. This was an important and interesting finding at the time when the media was full of total outsourcing stories, giving the impression that this represented the activity of the majority of UK companies.
