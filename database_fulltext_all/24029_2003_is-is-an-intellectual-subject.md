# Is IS an intellectual subject?

European Journal of Information Systems (2003) 12, 229–230. doi:10.1057/palgrave. ejis.3000474

I respond to Ray Paul’s article asking whether information systems (IS) is an intellectual subject (Paul, 2002). In this short response, I concentrate on three issues: first, whether information systems can be seen as models; second, the issue of research approaches that might be appropriate for IS; and finally, the question whether all this matters? I will be brief on the first two issues, less brief on the latter – for it certainly does matter.

An IS is a representation of the real world using information technology, so IS are models. However, this might be a misleading statement, as they are not simple models. For example, they cannot be reduced to a mathematical formula. IS systems involve the messy and complex mix of information technology, people and organisations. Systems thinking, rather than technology or mathematics, is therefore an appropriate (but not the only) starting point for IS. Information systems should therefore not be seen as models in the sense that the term might be used in operational research nor IS people seen as merely model builders. Indeed, the complexity of the ‘models’ might explain the lack of success of many IS in practice. One only needs to think of knowledge management (KM). How easy to think (at a superficial level) of some sort of ‘model’ representing the knowledge of the organisation and its people using IT, at least as a possibility. Of course, we know how difficult such a ‘model’ is to implement. None of the KM applications I have seen or heard of begin to address the challenge. However, such issues make the subject exciting, interesting and an intellectual as well as a practical challenge: it is a very rich area. Seeing IS merely as models, therefore, may be strictly true, but may not help.

The second issue that Ray Paul raises concerns the number of research methods used in IS. But as information systems involve the messy and complex mix of information technology, people and organisations, then it is not surprising that researchers use different research methods to address different aspects of this complexity. Why is this a problem? Why should we use only one approach? Why think simplistically? What is important is that we use each approach correctly and appropriately. There are a number of qualitative and quantitative research methods appropriate to research different aspects of this complex domain. For example, Myers & Avison (2002) evidence the appropriate use of many qualitative approaches to the study of IS. Again, IS is a rich area for research and we should be comfortable ‘letting many flowers bloom’.

Regarding the comment that ‘Many referees will not let a paper be accepted for publication unless mention is made of some of these research approaches’ (Paul, 2002, p 175), surely the point is that, in order to assess any research paper, a referee and reader alike need to know the research approach used and how it was used. This should help ensure that it has indeed been used correctly and appropriately. Otherwise, how can we trust the results or the argument? I am such a referee (and PhD examiner), so be warned! Further, there is nothing wrong in using approaches from other disciplines if the users understand the issues fully. We are fortunate in IS to have as colleagues those who gained their PhDs in anthropology, history, sociology, and many other subjects (including operational research) as well as IS. However, as Baskerville & Myers (2002) show, IS can also be a reference discipline itself.

The final question that I wish to address is whether all this matters. I am sure it does. I think it naı¨ve to state that ‘We do not need to defend IS – it is not necessary’ (Paul, 2002, p 176). Ray Paul and his colleagues are indeed fortunate to belong to a ‘major teaching and research IS department (much due to Ray’s own efforts) with 40 academic staff and growing, and over 1000 students (including) 80 PhD students’ (Paul, 2002, p 174). Indeed, I am fortunate to be visiting research professor there. However, Brunel is the exception that proves the rule. In many business schools, for example, IS is a relatively small group when compared to the numbers in accounting, finance, marketing, strategy and so on. Similarly, in many computer science departments, the information systems academics are few compared to the number of computer scientists.

Most of us are working with few colleagues and with little power. This was struck home to me when I sent a message to the ISWorld listserv asking for arguments to use to ensure that IS remains on the MBA core at my own business school (Avison, 2003). I received over a 100 replies, many stating that they had the same problems at their schools. One lament says simply ‘This is a perpetual problem for us as well’, another ‘We are discussing the very same issue(s) now’, another ‘We at [named] University are dealing with the same issue right now’, another ‘I am struggling with just this problem’ and yet another ‘I would be most interested to see these [responses] as we constantly face this type of pressure’ and yet again ‘our case seems to be the same as yours y this is the trend’.

As a result of the overwhelming response, I was able to make a good practical case for inclusion, mainly based on the crucial practical elements that MIS is a core competence for today’s managers; MIS is a crucial issue for future managers; and taking MIS off the core curriculum could lead to reduced demand and a loss of prestige for the MBA. But the case would have been destroyed if there were missing features that make up an acceptable ‘discipline’: including academics using research approaches appropriately. It was also enabled by having an infrastructure supporting IS academics – in this case the ISWorld listserv – but also the Academy of Information Systems (and UKAIS, AIM, etc.), conferences, such as ICIS (and IFIP, PACCIS, ECIS, ACIS, etc.), journals like EJIS (and ISJ, JSIS, and MISQ), book series like that of Butterworth-Heinemann (and Wiley, etc.) and the rest.

The list below represents the start of such a case from a management/business point of view, which I am using at my own business school to ‘defend the subject’ (I am grateful to Steven Alter, Gordon Davis, Peter O’Connor, and Brian Pentland for their responses to my IS World email on this topic):

 IT and IS are of strategic importance in organisations today – we cannot afford not to know about technology.

 Companies use technologies to compete, it is a key competitive weapon.

 Business systems are dependent on and are integrated with IS.

 Business organisations spend enormous amounts of time, energy, and money (roughly half of their capital expenditures) on these systems.

 IT/IS failure and a lack of strategic alignment are crucial factors in business failure.

 IT/IS issues are therefore crucial organisational issues.

 Professional managers need to be confident with technology and be able to manage technology-oriented people and make technology-oriented decisions throughout their whole career.

 Managers must be aware of how IT affects everything else they do.

 Business professionals need to know their responsibil ities to ensure the success of these systems.

 Managers need to know about corporate IT, information and knowledge resources.

 IS is a critical component in value creation (and cost minimisation) in every functional area.

However, even though MIS is pervasive and important, it is widely misunderstood and difficult to manage well. It is the researcher and teacher in IS who will help improve practice in this key area.
