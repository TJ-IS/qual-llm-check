# Managing e-transformation in the public sector: an e-government study of the Inland Revenue Authority of Singapore (IRAS)

Chee Wee Tan<sup>1</sup> and Shan L Pan<sup>1</sup>

## Abstract

Continuous organizational transformation is becoming a common phenomenon as public agencies rapidly embrace new information technologies (IT) to streamline and re-invent their obsolete operations. However, due to the overwhelming extent of the necessary changes as well as the sheer diversity of stakeholders involved in transforming corporate functions, practitioners are finding it difficult to manage the level of dynamism required in the effort. In an attempt to shed some light on the phenomenon of managing e-transformation, this paper examines in-depth an e-government initiative. Through the study of how one organization in the public sector adapts to the dynamic customer relations brought about by e-transformation, the case serves to identify the governing factors for successful organization–stakeholder relations, and how these may have a bearing on the development of effective e-transformation strategies. An important contribution of this study is the proposal of a development model of relationships in organizations for an understanding into how organization–stakeholder relationships can be efficiently managed to bring about an effective overhaul of business processes. Keywords: organizational e-transformation; customer relationship management (CRM); stakeholder relationships; e-government initiatives

## Introduction

The prevailing dominance of information technology (IT) in modern economies has established a climate of change and re-invention within business organizations to counter dynamic consumer expectations (Davenport, 1998, 2000). As a result, persistent business transformations have become a norm as enterprises battle for competitiveness by seeking efficacies in their operational routines with the aid of IT. Managing organizational transformations, in other words, is becoming a strategic and integral part of corporate management activities. This trend, however, is not exclusive to commercial institutions; the public sector is also adapting to similar managerial challenges.

Indeed, the premise of public administration is undergoing identical transformation with the redesign of non-innovative bureaucratic governmental structures (Moon & Bretschneider, 2002) to accommodate an emerging generation of modernized public services or e-governments (Stratford & Stratford, 2000; Devadoss et al., 2002). More importantly, this recent form of public management within civil administrations emphasizes increased interactivity and greater sensitivity to the needs of their diverse stakeholders (Wimmer & Traunmuller, 2000; Ho, 2002). Nonetheless, the vision of the customer-centric government is often overshadowed by the need for radical reforms to fundamental but crucial organizational business processes (Aichholzer & Schmutzer, 2000). In particular, the increasing tendency to incorporate citizens as part of the managerial equation (Webler & Tuler, 2000; Lowndes et al., 2001) has re-focused attention on the extraction of customer value as a prerequisite for effective strategic planning purposes in e-governments (Burn & Robbins, 2001). This view is further reinforced in the work of Prahalad & Ramaswamy (2000), which foresees the future of organizations as intimately dependent on their capabilities in ‘harnessing competence in an enhanced network’ of stakeholders.

This research thus attempts to complement the existing pool of organizational transformation studies, which has conventionally revolved around intra-organizational considerations in private firms (see Hales et al., 1994; Klenke, 1994; Korpela, 1994), by exploring how customer relationship management (CRM) is strategized by a public agency as a corollary of business re-engineering efforts. Further, the study endeavours to tender reference for practitioners in managing customer relations within corporate re-inventions by describing, analysing and explaining how one public agency, the Inland Revenue Authority of Singapore (IRAS), has advanced in its business process evolution through the fusion of IT modernization with an incremental development strategy for CRM. By utilizing IT as a catalyst for organizational e-transformation as well as an enabler of dialogic communications with its various stakeholders (or customers), the IRAS has effectively engineered a turnaround from an unproductive, inflexible establishment to an efficient, customer-centric one. The electronic filing (e-filing) system developed by the IRAS stands as a testimony to this achievement.

E-filing is one of the first e-governmental transformation initiatives to be introduced by the Singapore government to revitalize public services. It marks a substantial step towards the migration of conventional practices to the virtual environment. Given the unique context associated with such a complete overhaul of the current operating procedures of an organization, and the diversity of the stakeholders involved, this case study offers a rewarding opportunity to uncover the governing factors for successful organization–stakeholder relations, and how these may have a bearing on the development of robust e-transformation strategies.

An important contribution of this investigation is the proposal of a development model of organization– stakeholder relationships to appreciate how they can be efficiently managed to bring about effective reforms in business processes. The proposed framework aims to lend focus to future efforts in organizational e-transformation while directing academic work in CRM (Pan and Lee, 2003) for the domain. The next section explores some related studies that have been conducted in similar areas.

This is followed by a detailed description of the case. Subsequently, the analysis and findings of the study are discussed. The paper will conclude by highlighting some possible insights to be gleaned from the research.

## Organizational transformation and the management of changing customer relationships

In a complex business environment where change and uncertainty are the only constants, organizations have to discard outdated practices in exchange for highly flexible structures that are adaptable to the ever-changing environmental conditions (Smithson et al., 1994). Specifically, business organizations are turning to IT as the means to identify key issues and sketch competitive strategies to enhance their responsiveness (Nutt and Backoff, 1992). Amid this development, researchers have sought to discover the right formula for organizational transformation in a digital landscape, and in the process, introduced a number of perspectives from which to explore the phenomenon.

Some of the previous work on IT-enabled organizational transformation has focused primarily on defining novel organizational forms that can respond effectively to external challenges. These emerging architectures have been categorized under multiple terms such as ‘adaptive’ (Toffler, 1985), ‘virtual’ (Mackenzie, 1986), ‘hybrid’ (Powell, 1987) and ‘flex-firm’ (Toffler, 1990). However, most of these proposed structures demand coordinative efforts across entire value chains. This in turn spurred an impressive amount of discourses on the potential and utilization of IT to facilitate collaboration in restructuring businesses.

From empirical studies conducted in private organizations (Winter & Taylor, 1996; Sarker & Lee, 1999), many have theorized on the relationship between IT and business process transformation. On closer examination, these theses of IT-enabled business metamorphosis may be grouped under different themes. One of the most familiar perspectives for understanding the role of IT in transforming organizations is the identification of the success factors that are crucial for turning around obsolete business practices. Extensive in nature, these success elements are assumed to be the minimum pre-requisites for organizations seeking e-transformation, and they cover issues ranging from the preferred characteristics of underlying organizational infrastructures in supporting re-invention efforts, to the necessary managerial qualities in supervising these reformations, and the use of issue tensions to derive win–win situations (Davies & Mitchell, 1994; Korpela, 1994; Bowker et al., 1995; Nutt & Backoff, 1997; Markus et al., 2000). Other researchers have focused their attention on exploring the impacts of such changes on internal work processes, employee behaviour and company performance (Klenke, 1994; Mumford, 1994; Robey & Sahay, 1996; Winter & Taylor, 1996).

Even though some of the previous studies (Venkatraman, 1994; Markus & Benjamin, 1997) have proven to be invaluable in furthering business knowledge in e-transformation strategies and their implications, much of the current debates centre on the push for internal operational efficacy, neglecting external expectations that constitute the core motivators behind these change initiatives (Davenport, 2000). Specifically, Nutt et al. (2000) noted that current literature says little about how to uncover problems or to fashion and then integrate ideas for strategic change to cope with key issues by drawing on the knowledge of, and building support with crucial stakeholders.

Moreover, in almost all of the previous IT-enabled organizational transformation studies, any in-depth enquiry into how public organizations manage e-transformation has remained relatively limited. Some have offered possible explanations to the apparent dearth of research activities in the area. For instance, Bozeman & Kingsley (1998) believed that there is a general impression of public agencies as rigid and risk-averse establishments, with little desire for improvements. Even if re-engineering were to be enforced within these organizations, most scholars would be quick to presume that private sector principles could be seamlessly transplanted onto public services (Dillman, 1998).

The shortage of governmental transformation and organization–stakeholder relational studies may be interpreted as the theoretical and empirical gap that needs to be filled before a holistic approach could be made towards strategizing re-inventive efforts within organizations. In fact, this gap is increasingly amplified as the rate of IT diffusion within governmental agencies progresses aggressively (Norris & Kraemer, 1996; Stratford & Stratford, 2000), and public organizations envision privatization as a strategic transformation alternative (Veenswijk & Hakvoort, 2002).

With the rapid adoption of emerging technologies to restructure archaic managerial functions (Moon, 2002) and re-deploy services onto new communication media (Milford, 2000), a paradigmatic shift in basic government operations appear inevitable (Wimmer et al., 2001; Ho, 2002), as public services are being reconfigured to steer away from traditional book-keeping functions (Seavey, 1996; Norris, 1999). Indeed, Lawson (1998) highlighted the significance of e-government, describing it as the provision of public service in a ‘‘one-stop, non-stop’’ manner where ‘‘power is transferred to the people’’. This opinion is shared by Aichholzer & Schmutzer (2000), who went further to describe e-governments as the transformation of the business of governance as well as the foundation of governance itself.

In addition to the above definitions of e-government, a number of related perspectives such as e-Business, Citizen, Knowledge, Process and Tele-cooperation, have been proposed to provide a more holistic picture of this social phenomenon (Lenk & Traunmuller, 2000). A comparison between these different perspectives of e-governments is summarized in Table 1.

From the aforementioned definitions and perspectives of e-government, it can be deduced that this new genre of public administrative reforms resembles the typical market-driven mode of governance where policies are aligned along the commercial principles of competitiveness, efficiency and productivity (Halligan & Turner, 1995; Haque, 1996). Nevertheless, this business-like transformation of public services has led to widespread controversies over the implications on the ‘‘publicness’’ of public organizations (Haque, 2001). The ‘‘publicness’’ of the public sector has been a common concern among political scholars, especially with regard to its fulfilment and representation of public interests (Coursey & Bozeman, 1990). Citing obstacles such as the accumulation of excessive power and the inaccessibility of information on the pretext of official secrecy, Bozeman (2000), Garnham (1990) and Haque (1994) have criticized public agencies for their indifference as well as lack of accountability towards public stakeholders. It is thus not surprising that many theorists have eagerly searched for alternatives to enhance the ‘‘publicness’’ of the civil service (Ventriss, 1989; Thomas, 1999).

With recent moves towards a market-oriented governance model, the quest appears to be over as there is a preconceived notion that public interests are best served by improving service quality and customer satisfaction through overcoming inefficiencies in operational activities (Clements, 1994; Kelegama, 1995). However, such ideas have been under scrutiny for their parochial and over-simplistic assumptions about the parallelism of the fundamental objectives and processes between public– private organizations (Dillman, 1998). As pointed out by Frederickson (1997) and Haque (1996), while the re-engineering of public agencies may have diminished public–private distinctions and introduced business management culture to obsolete bureaucracies in need of revitalization, it has also marginalized public service responsibilities, displacing them with standard business norms. Gregory (1999), for instance, has hypothesized that an overemphasis on business results may encourage public organizations to devote more attention to predetermined productivity targets and compromise their capacity for public responsiveness. In fact, emerging studies have illustrated that contemporary public domain reforms have adopted private sector values, which erode the social obligations of public agencies (Haque, 1998).

Hence, at this juncture of e-governmental evolution, public administration faces a critical challenge of maintaining equilibrium between business process improvements and organizational responsiveness towards diverse and unpredictable customer expectations (Ledingham, 2001). To address this dilemma, Haque (2001) advised that the premise of modern public management should not depart from the alignment of governmental transformation with a strategic focus on establishing dialogic relations – or relations where dialogue between the two parties is an integral feature – to cater to the needs and preferences of differing stakeholders (Dozier et al., 1995; Rainey et al., 1995; Taylor & Kent, 1999). Reflecting a similar view, Kruckeberg & Starck (1998) believe that stakeholder relations, if properly managed, are integral to the convergence of aims between organizations and their partners. This in turn serves as a self-perpetuating regulator in promoting a sense of ownership in the organization among the stakeholders and forging strategic alliances between them.

The study of a systematic and strategic relationship management process within the context of an e-governmental initiative can therefore be perceived as the bridge linking the transformation studies of public and private organizations. In addition, our research seeks to supplement current knowledge on the strategic considerations that organizations undergoing e-transformation are preoccupied with, by examining the external implications of e-transformation on customer relations.

## Research methodology

This study adopts an in-depth case research method for gathering evidence. The qualitative methodology is most appropriate where the research question is exploratory in nature and exists within a broader sociological context, thus necessitates rich descriptions of the social environment (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). Furthermore, such an approach allows the exploration of unforeseen relationships and offers better insights into the inter-dependencies among the factors captured in the study (Benbasat et al., 1987).

The case study was conducted at the IRAS over a period of 6 months, and several techniques of data collection were employed (Benbasat et al., 1987). A series of focused interviews (Merton et al., 1990; Klein and Myers, 1999) were conducted with the Chief Information Officer (CIO), the e-filing system design team, the e-filing system implementation team and the e-filing administrative group, to solicit data on the intra-organizational considerations behind the implementation of the e-filing project. The information gathered was triangulated with the perspectives derived from subsequent interviews with external customers or taxpayers (Orlikowski, 1993). From the 30 h of dialogue, the researchers were able to build up a qualitative in-depth compilation of data points within the study environment (Lacity & Janson, 1994) that focused specifically on developmental issues pertaining to the e-filing service provided by the IRAS, with lesser emphasis on the technicalities of the system (Eisenhardt, 1991).

Given the unique circumstances of this research where everybody is a stakeholder of the organization, the study accepted a more interpretivistic perspective (Walsham, 1993) of the data collected. Hence, the experience and the contextual understanding of the researcher provide additional background information that is valuable to the interpretation of the evidence found (Lacity & Janson, 1994).

## IRAS: a pioneer in e-transformation initiatives

The IRAS was inaugurated in 1992 to administer income and property taxes, as well as the new value-added tax called the Goods and Services Tax (GST). Additional responsibilities included addressing the high staff turnover at the organization, which was almost three times that of other public services. Also, the IRAS had to shoulder the reversal of negative sentiments towards it: reflected in a 1997 government report where the IRAS was given the lowest rating in the entire government sector for public satisfaction. Since then, the tax administration has progressed towards an integrated, computerized system that has yielded improved results. First, the staff size has stayed constant with low turnover. Secondly, tax arrears have been reduced with improved auditing and consistent property valuations. Finally, public satisfaction with the tax service has increased tremendously.

Before the creation of the IRAS, tax administration in Singapore was a process of the classic hierarchical bureaucracy, and was both time-consuming and inefficient. In 1998, however, new direct taxpayer services (Internet filing) were introduced, revolutionizing the entire tax filing system. Even though tax officers still deal with approximately 400,000 calls annually, about

43% of tax inquiries are being handled by the automated electronic filing system. In an IRAS survey in 2001, 94.1% of individual taxpayers, 89.6% of corporate taxpayers and 94.6% of goods and services taxpayers have expressed their satisfaction with the services of the IRAS, saying they found the services convenient, as well as competently and courteously provided

## The e-filing system

With the arrival of the Internet, the IRAS perceived that its phone filing services could be extended into the World Wide Web. Since the launch of the extended system on 16 February 1998 by the Commissioner of Inland Revenue, taxpayers can file their income tax returns either through the Internet or by telephone. Termed e-filing, this new S\$1.9 million electronic filing system can even be accessed by citizens who are stationed overseas. The e-filing campaign began in 1998 and has been repeated yearly during the tax return periods.

The whole e-filing process is paperless. Even receipts need not be submitted, but are kept by taxpayers in the event of a need for verification. When the returns are filed electronically, they are entered into the IRAS information system automatically. The system was initially available only to salaried employees (about 1.4 million people). It has since been expanded to include employers and those with business incomes. Only taxpayers in the high-income category earning more than S\$150,000 a year must submit the details of their income by post for statistical purposes, and even then, they are permitted to e-file the remaining portions of their tax returns. The IRAS estimated that if 20–30% of taxpayers submit their returns through the e-filing system, its investment in the system would be recovered within 5 years.

Since the introduction of e-filing in 1998, the top management of the IRAS has reported an approximate 100% growth in the number of e-filers annually (see Figure 1). The IRAS thus presents a golden opportunity to study the evolution of organization–stakeholder relationship and its implications in an e-government setup, so that lessons may be drawn to guide the strategic management of e-government implementations in the future.

## Evolution of the e-filing system

The IRAS e-filing system evolved from the information system (I/S) architecture of the organization. From its creation in 1992, the IRAS has been on a mission to redefine its tax administration practices. In the past, the tax system was marred by sluggish manual tax processing systems and the pile-up of paper documents. During that time, the IRAS experienced a shortage of staff to process the tax returns, which often resulted in an inefficient and lengthy tax cycle. An estimated number of 300 tax returns were left uncollected every year, which snowballed with each passing cycle. In addition, other bureaucratic procedures proved to be detrimental to customer relations as taxpayers became upset by prolonged delays at the IRAS in locating their personal tax folders.

## Phase I: digitizing taxpayer information

The first significant change to the tax filing process brought about by the re-engineering effort was the implementation of the imaging system in 1992. The step was taken with the sole purpose of cutting down the increasing number of paper files that were needed to store the overwhelming number of tax documents. The imaging system makes use of precision scanners to scan the physical returns and create digitized images of the documents, which are then stored in a centralized database that is accessible to all tax officers. Even though it may be impossible for the scanners to capture accurately all the information on the hand-written returns, and data entry by hired personnel is still required to complete the records, it should be emphasized that the centralized database of scanned images is beneficial from the point of view of both organization and taxpayers. For the IRAS, a database of digitized information translates into substantial cost savings as productivity is greatly increased. In the past, multiple tax officers had to take turns in going through a single physical case file and the resultant delays in tax processing was inevitable and unproductive. These inefficiencies have now been reduced with a central database where multiple and concurrent authorized accesses may be made to any one set of taxpayer information. For taxpayers, the database means that they can now obtain one-stop service to their enquiries. That is possible as all tax officers are now sufficiently equipped with the relevant information to address taxpayers’ enquiries by simply calling up the taxpayer’s digital folder on their terminal screens in a matter of seconds.

Phase II: automating organizational business processes The digitization and centralization of taxpayer data within the organization provided further possibilities towards a system that could efficiently process most of the tax returns without physical intervention. According to the IRAS statistical approximation, 80% of tax returns are considered to be ‘‘normal’’ and do not require additional verification by tax officers. As such, it was considered a waste of manpower resources to go through every individual case physically. The desire for more efficient human resource allocation gave birth to the Inland Revenue Integrated System (IRIS) in 1995. As the name suggests, the IRIS is an information system that integrates all tax-processing functions into a single informational infrastructure.

Within IRIS, there is a set of in-built pre-defined evaluation criteria that is designed to process 80% of the ‘‘normal’’ tax returns, with the remainder being routed to the appropriate tax officer with the essential skill domain, and even then, the routing is fully automated. The Workflow Management System (WMS) is the subsystem in IRIS that is primarily responsible for channelling unique tax cases to the appropriate tax officer, and it follows a huge number of rules to match a case to the appropriate tax officer. Nonetheless, it must be highlighted that there is no fundamental change to the tax-processing measures of the IRAS as the design of IRIS and the development of virtual tax valuation are modelled on physical tax administrative practices.

## Phase III: developing the phone-filing system

The implementation of IRIS has successfully integrated backend tax processes into a singular I/S architecture. The next logical step towards efficiency, as perceived by the IRAS, was the automation of the data input function. As mentioned above, even with the imaging system, physical data entry has still been unavoidable, which was why the IRAS introduced the phone filing system in 1995. The phone filing system was designed to provide an alternative to physical forms, allowing taxpayers to input their data directly into IRIS by following a series of simple phone instructions that guide them in the tax-filing process. However, phone filing did not catch on for two reasons. First, due to security concerns, phone filing was only made available to a limited group of taxpayers – those who had a single source of income. Moreover, despite the simplicity of the phone instructions, most taxpayers found it uncomfortable using a non-visual tax filing system.

## Phase IV: designing the e-filing system

Around the same period, the increased popularity of the Internet caught the attention of the top management of the IRAS. A decision was made to capitalize on the capabilities of the Internet by duplicating and enhancing the phone filing experience onto the WWW. As a result, the Internet e-filing system was launched in 1998 for salaried employees, and its service base was further enlarged in subsequent years to include all individual taxpayers. The Internet filing system adopted a customercentric approach in its design. The design team comprised representatives from all the tax divisions in the organization and the majority of the discussions centred on understanding the taxpayer perspective of the system. In addition, at various phases of the development of the system, taxpayers were invited to take part in focus groups and forums so as to provide external feedback on the functionality of the e-filing system. The activities of the focus groups and forums included the testing of a prototype prior to the launch of the system.

Another feature unique to the Internet filing experience is the utilization of an Electronic-Filing Personal Identification Number (EF PIN) for authentication purposes. The EF PIN is exclusive to the e-filing process because, unlike other authentication PINs on the Internet, the lifespan of each PIN is limited to only one e-filing process. It is mailed to the taxpayer prior to the tax filing period, and once the taxpayer submits his/her electronic returns, the PIN is terminated. The one-time use of the PIN also eliminated the need for taxpayers to remember a number that they would only use once every fiscal year. In recent years, the IRAS has even begun developing a standardized PIN which may be used across other government e-initiatives, thereby providing citizens with integrative access to all e-government services.

Phase V: maintaining and improving the e-filing system Even after the Internet filing system went on-line, the IRAS has maintained an operational E-filing Helpline from 8 am to 10 pm daily, 7 days a week during every tax cycle to ensure the stability and reliability of the system during peak periods. Besides system maintenance, continuous efforts have been made to enhance the e-filing system as well. At the end of every tax cycle, a postmortem is conducted to cross-examine the problems that may occur during the tax filing period. This involves addressing the large number of customer queries received during the tax filing period. The analysis from these investigative measures forms the basis of improvements to be incorporated into the e-filing system for the next financial year. Apart from these reflective actions, the IRAS also set up an independent Taxpayer Feedback Panel in 1999. Consisting of members from a cross-section of taxpayers, the sole purpose of the panel is to review and enhance current IRAS services while serving as a resource pool to generate fresh ideas and deliberate on suggestions to meet the ever-changing requirements of taxpayers.

## Phase VI: extending e-filing services

In a bid to make e-filing effortless for taxpayers, the IRAS established links with a number of government agencies and huge business organizations to transfer relevant information of their employees directly into the IRAS central database every tax cycle. Once the information for a particular taxpayer has been uploaded into the system, all that remains to be done by that taxpayer is to submit a series of zero returns through the e-filing system. This auto-inclusion scheme has to-date been supported by data transfer agreements with a total of 1,197 business organizations or 2% of all Singaporean companies. This small percentage of participating organizations may seem insignificant, but according to the statistics, these companies account for an approximate figure of 550,000 or 46% of all employees in the country.

It is clear from the above account of e-filing development in Singapore that the traditional thinking associated with the conventional management of customer relations no longer applies in the Internet economy. The process of e-transformation can be seen to be an inevitable process initiated by an organization seeking to rise up to the competition in an increasing e-environment. This in turn bears significant implications for the future of organizational e-transformation developments.

## Lessons learned: from outsiders to strategic value networks

From the preceding case description, it is clear that the e-Filing system has introduced significant modifications to the underlying business processes of the IRAS, leading to a re-definition of relationships between the organization and its stakeholders. Therefore, through an understanding of the structural and relational developments embedded within the e-government initiative, this case offers potential lessons that may have a bearing on the future management of e-transformation programmes in the public domain.

## Treating customers as outsiders leads to low customer satisfaction

Typical of ‘primitive’ bureaucratic governmental stereotypes, the IRAS started out as a rigid establishment with undesirable service quality and inefficient business operations (Moon & Bretschneider, 2002). In addition, customer relations were not managed strategically, leading to a tarnished public image for the tax agency. As explained by one of the IRAS interviewees,

We had difficulty clearing all the [tax] returnsy To do all the work, we estimated that we would require 2,000 people, while we had only 1,600. So there was no way we could finish the job. Many taxpayers would have to wait, and even then, it took us one and a half years to clear the lot, so a lot of people got angry. Also, every time at the end of each year, we always had about 300 returns we could not collect. And of course, it kept snowballing.

Compounding the problem, the use of paper files for documentation, as previously mentioned, rendered their retrieval a hassle that raised tempers whenever taxpayers made an unexpected appearance. An informant commented,

The problem with files is the need to search for them; you do not know who has the documents. Customers can come unannounced. So whenever they are here, you will need to retrieve the files. In the past, not everything was online. That was why we had to hunt for the files. We looked everywhere and yet could not locate them. It became very embarrassing and taxpayers got very agitated. ‘Why did you lose my file?’ That was their reaction. Then we had to explain that we didn’t lose their file, [but that] it was with someone else. And then they wouldn’t understand but asked, ‘Why? If it is with someone else, why can’t you find it?

All these procedural clashes strained the already distant relationships between the IRAS and its taxpayer customers, further eroding what little confidence the taxpayers might be having of the IRAS’ tax administrative abilities. Not surprisingly, this observation is resonated in the work of Garnham (1990) and Haque (1994), which posited that the barrage of red-tape that public bureaucracies face is counter-productive to the building of positive relationships with their intended audience.

However, viewing the interviewees’ account of the procedural clashes within a wider context, one might consider them a phase in the organizational transformation life cycle that could serve as an intuitive snapshot of traditional relationship management practices where stakeholders are regarded as no different from passive outsiders. Particularly, the lack of market exposure for the organization, coupled with the unique sanctions and expanded coercive powers that it had, might be considered the reason behind reduced incentives for public accountability, leading to mandatory actions and a dictatorial mode of communication (Rainey et al., 1976; Robertson & Seneviratne, 1995). Indeed, a dictatorial tone of communication has been associated with the nonchalant attitude adopted by the tax agency in dealing with customer queries. Schachter (1994), for instance, noted that in the past, civil administrators expected publics to adapt to their style of functioning instead of the other way round, thereby alienating customers from policy decisions (Kotchegura, 1997).

Consequently, as a by-product of such prolonged apathy towards stakeholder interests, conventional governance models are plagued with a stagnated and complacent business atmosphere, where there is minimal desire within the largely procedural-driven organization for operational improvements (Bozeman & Kingsley, 1998). This in turn reinforces the lethargic relationships between bureaucratic institutions and their deserving consumers that are best characterized by low levels of customer satisfaction with no value creation, either internally for the organizations, or externally for their customers.

## Limited (improved) customer satisfaction can be achieved via internal operation efficiency

Given the downward spiralling situation of the IRAS that was akin to the plight of apathetic and ritualistic bureaucracies described in the literature, the implementation of IRIS should be commemorated as a big step by the organization in normalizing its deteriorating relationship with its customers. Through the assimilation and dissemination of information from a centralized data source, IRIS erased customary delays, which often accompanied taxpayer queries in light of prior paper filing measures. By so doing, the IRAS replaced an obsolete physical taxation system with a responsive onestop taxpayer inquiry service through revolutionary changes to its technical as well as functional architectures. One of the IRIS operators described the system that epitomizes the change,

With IRIS, you can see each transaction, what is happening. There is a history. And because the form itself is a digitized image, you can see what the taxpayer declares. I think the history is useful, you can see who updates what and when. So if you do not find the [physical] file, never mind, you don’t need it anymore.

Incidentally, Lawson (1998), Reschenthaler & Thompson (1996) have also predicted the emergence of one-stop service centres as a natural evolutionary stage for the survival of public management in the Internet era. Indeed, citizens are increasingly demanding consolidated governmental service portals as the means to accomplish multiple transactions in a timely and convenient manner (Washington, 1996). For the IRAS, the induction of IRIS has therefore moved the organization in the direction of a ‘‘client-based’’ organizational model where business processes are seamlessly integrated for the benefit of its consumers (Calista, 1986; Rainey & Rainey, 1986; Ho, 2002), thus yielding increased levels of customer satisfaction among its stakeholders.

However, despite greater emphasis on customer requirements, it can be deduced from the case findings that the momentum for change at this phase of e-transformation is still primarily technologicallydriven with value creation being more focused on internal operations. For instance, IRIS was devised as a technological innovation for streamlining internal functions with supplementary options being incorporated for future improvements through reactive deliberation of customers’ opinions (Norris & Kraemer, 1996). In a sense, communication between the IRAS and its taxpayers remained rather negotiable in nature due to the fact that the tax agency maintained its role as the sole decision maker on initiative adoptions, whereas the taxpayers for whom the services are intended, negotiated for successive enhancements. An IRAS tax officer recalled,

In the past it was like taxpayers were people who owed us money. To be honest, we actually needed [to learn] to see taxpayers as customersy and not simply people bound by law to pay taxes

The need for such a change in mindset is noted in the literature. Rainey (1990), for instance, has also cited bureaucratic resistance and inefficient resource management as the main hurdles to the formation of a customer-centric movement within the public sphere. Yet in the case of the IRAS, the implementation of the e-filing system has served as an opportunity for the organization to rid itself of administrative obstacles and re-invent its relationships with its immediate service recipients.

## Integrative communication yields value for customers and organizational efficiency

With the initiation of the e-filing system, establishing close ties with customers became imperative and essential for the IRAS. Intuitively, the e-filing system represents a fundamental shift in tax administration by showing faith and confidence in taxpayers to ensure the accuracy of their personal income declarations. Hence, as a corollary of such empowerment, responsive communication links had to be constructed to promote greater sensitivity to the needs of the taxpayers (Wimmer & Traunmuller, 2000). In particular, efforts were expended to align the perceptions and concerns of the public agency with those of its stakeholders. As described by a top IRAS management executive,

Our vision is to be the leading tax administration in the world. While taxpayers have no choice about paying taxes, we believe in making that experience as pleasant as possible. In the IRAS, we believe that through excellent taxpayer service, we can bring about higher levels of compliance.

To make good on its promise, cross-functional representatives were recruited into the e-filing project team right from the beginning, to form a wellspring of experiences that could provide insights into the myriad of diverse taxpayer behaviour. As the head of the project team elaborated,

We have cross-functional representatives in the system design team together with the external vendor. We think in line with the customers’ views when we brain-storm about system design. We try to anticipate the customers’ interpretation of the terminology we use and [we consider] the customers’ background.

Moreover, during the process of developing the e-filing system, independent feedback from taxpayers was also solicited through focus groups and consultative forums. In fact, this practice was retained even after the launch of the e-filing system as part of the continuing efforts of the IRAS to secure public confidence in the refurnished tax filing procedures. As rationalized by an interviewee,

We need to go through the whole process and let the taxpayers see it the way we do. If we find that we have placed something there that two persons interpret differently, we will request for input. When we perform testing, we involve taxpayers and then observe the result. Whenever we obtain unexpected results, we ask why they interpret [the test situation] in the manner [that they have].

From the IRAS’ perspective, it was clear that these attempts to reconcile probable perceptive differences would help to offset negative public impressions of government bureaucracies (Oszlak, 1997) and reduce public scepticism surrounding market-led reforms in the civil administration (Haque, 2001). Explicitly, the e-filing system signifies a revamped mode of governance in an emerging generation of information-driven organizations where power distribution patterns are reconfigured through information accessibility (Allen et al., 2001), to grant stakeholders enhanced privileges to exercise strategic influence over corporate decision-making processes (Thomas & Velthouse, 1990). In other words, stakeholder participation is gradually being recognized as a pervasive aspect of competitive business management.

Naturally, with taxpayers duly acknowledged as valued customers (Elgarah & Courtney, 2002) through the e-filing system, the IRAS can no longer neglect the voices of legitimate consumers as the crisscrossing of expectations has explicably translated into mutual dependence between both parties in the achievement of their objectives. Indeed, this correspondence is invaluable in re-engineering business processes within e-governments for the strategic alignment of varying interests to achieve high levels of customer satisfaction and organizational efficiency (Kruckeberg & Starck, 1998). Furthermore, even though communication at the current phase of e-transformation at the IRAS has attained integrative status, it must nonetheless be emphasized that the integrative interaction is still reactive in character. As inferred from the case, the IRAS does not actively seek probable ways of reaching out to external members at this early stage of e-filing development. Consequently, value creation is still concentrated on operational efficiency even though it has been extended to both internal and external stakeholders.

## Two-way dialogic communication leads to sustained customer satisfaction

Nevertheless, following the change in its corporate mentality, the IRAS has placed itself in the position to shape strategic partnerships through communication means such as the Taxpayer Feedback Panel (TFP), which integrates the objectives of the stakeholders into the mission of the organization. In its press statement of 1 September 1999, the IRAS stated,

The Taxpayer Feedback Panel will serve as a more structured and regular communication channel to receive feedback from taxpayers. The Panel can focus on specific areas of concern, extending their feedback to constructive discussions on how to fine-tune and improve existing systems, policies and services.

The collaborative stance taken by the IRAS concurs with the recommendation of Porter (1992) that organizations should establish lasting ownership bonds with crucial stakeholders to offer them a voice in governance, thus increasing their extent of investment and commitment in the partnering relationship. Besides, it has been widely documented that stakeholders, acting independently to expropriate value to themselves, hinder an organization from maximizing its potential (Jensen & Meckling, 1976; Shleifer & Vishny, 1998). The unprecedented move towards forging a long-term alliance between the IRAS and its stakeholders should therefore guarantee that both the government agency and its partners would share a unified vision of the strategic direction for the future development of the e-filing system (Nutt et al., 2000).

With their renewed status as strategic partners, taxpayers can be better described as having a two-way dialogic relationship with the IRAS, where customers are no longer dismissed as mere subjects in the eyes of bureaucrats, but are respected peers in the pursuit of mutually beneficial cooperation. In other words, the theoretical imperative of two-way symmetrical relationships is to provide a procedural means by which a public agency and its stakeholders can communicate interactively with the ultimate objective of maintaining continuous as well as constructive dialogue (Cutlip et al., 1994; Taylor & Kent, 1999). More importantly, this constant exchange in the form of a formalized Community of Practice (CoP) (Wenger & Snyder, 2000) serves as a testament to the conversion of the IRAS to a knowledge-driven organization with the support of IT.

Since it is easier to arrive at a convergence of aims between the IRAS and its stakeholders through knowledge integration arrangements, sustained levels of customer satisfaction are likely to occur with value creation manifesting at both strategic and operational levels for the organization and its customers. The advantages of these knowledge-based partnerships are best summarized by one of the respondents,

We invited taxpayers from all walks of life and we ask them to come in and be members of this panel. So far, we have about 20 members representing different industries and service lines. The chairman is elected from the members themselves with one of our deputy commissioners sitting in as the IRAS representative. Also, this panel has a regular meeting schedule [drawn up] on a quarterly basis. If they gather any feedback, they will come back to us. On the other hand, if we are launching any new services, we will let them know and allow them a preliminary look at the new system. They are like our external but consistent source of feedback.

In spite of improved stakeholder relations, the mandate of e-transformation does not simply stop here as the IRAS seeks to extend the knowledge capabilities of the e-filing system to create value networks within the framework of virtual coalitions. The objective is to integrate the various stakeholder categories to allow impeccable collaboration across the entire value chain of the organization.

## Multi-directional strategic value creation capitulates total customer satisfaction

As an extension of the above discussion, the strategic value network may be perceived as the final but creative phase of customer relationship development in the IRAS etransformation drive. It is the expansion of relational inter-connectivity from a pure two-way reciprocity between the tax agency and each of its respective stakeholders, to form N-way alliances that will generate value for participants belonging to the same network. The auto-inclusion scheme highlighted in this case is an example of such value creating networks in the making. By providing tax information on behalf of its employees, substantial benefits can be derived for all parties participating in the scheme.

On the part of the employers, participation in the autoinclusion scheme translates into greater efficiency. Rather than catering for the requests of individual employees who may approach the company separately for documentation in support of their personal tax filing needs, it is simpler for the company to transmit the tax information directly to the IRAS. For the employees, they are spared the effort and hassle of sifting through and supplying the details of their employment information for their tax returns. For the IRAS, direct access to taxpayer information at its source – that is from the employers who are paying the salaries of the taxpayers – means a reduction to the tremendous costs of data capturing and safeguarding against erroneous tax declarations. Eventually, the IRAS believes, the auto inclusion scheme can be harnessed into a paperless tax filing paradigm where all relevant tax information is automatically channelled into the e-filing system, without any form of physical intervention. As explained by the CIO,

Doing auto inclusion means we can reduce our tremendous data capture efforts because we capture so much of the tax information. That’s why when you do e-filing, it is not as tedious as if you were to file all the different forms; you only have to complete the part that is necessaryy Since we started the auto inclusion scheme, we have been systematically trying to auto include as much tax information as possible. And, hopefully one day, we will reach the stage where taxpayers do not have to do anything anymore (in the filing of their tax returns). That’s our ultimate goal.

Essentially, the auto-inclusion scheme demonstrates how n-way dialogic communication facilitates collective knowledge sharing in stakeholder networks to shape strategic initiatives, which create value that is not only internal and external to the organization, but also multidirectional for all actors who are connected via the relational webbing. Through the satisfaction of mutual expectations, the charting of such stakeholder value maps serves the eventual aim of achieving total customer satisfaction (Goodhue et al., 2002). In effect, it is foreseeable in the near future that these collaborative relationships may become the defining characteristic of organizational transformation in the digital economy.

In sum, the evolution of stakeholder relationships in the IRAS can be elucidated as a progressive response to embryonic customer expectations in the face of technologically induced globalization. The strategy adopted for e-transformation in the IRAS complements its development of organization–stakeholder relations in the sense that the revolution in business routines strengthens the relationship between the organization and its stakeholders, which in turn leads to positive refinements in corporate management and decision-making processes. Furthermore, as organizations acknowledge and establish more intimate ties with their partnering stakeholders, strategic planning can advance from reactive responses to anticipative actions in light of external fluctuations – similar to what the IRAS has achieved through the creation of the Taxpayer Feedback Panel and its Strategic Value Network. ## Conclusion: e-transformation beyond organizational boundary

This research has closely scrutinized the journey of a public agency in implementing an e-government initiative in conjunction with the management of an intimate relationship with its stakeholders within an IT-enabled transformation environment (Venkatraman, 1994; Markus & Benjamin, 1997). Specifically, the study traces the development of the IRAS e-Filling initiative from 1992 to 2003. The inherent limitations of a single case study notwithstanding, this study has offered an analytical account of the experiences and lessons learned in the transformation of the IRAS from a bureaucratic agency (in the early 1990s) to one that possesses anticipative and responsive capabilities (see Figure 2). The data used in this case study are derived from a variety of sources that have assisted in the construction of a retrospective, comprehensive picture of the e-transformation programme (Lacity & Janson, 1994) in a public agency. The study has explored a diversity of issues within the business reformative processes entailed in the adoption of an e-government initiative, with the aim of contributing to the theoretical and managerial knowledge on the subject of e-transformation.

Based on the five phases uncovered, the study makes a novel contribution to e-government and organizational transformation literature. It extends existing knowledge through the proposal of a model that captures the evolution of the relational architecture within the context of an e-governmental transformation process. In fact, the proposed framework offers not only the depiction of the historical evolution of governmental re-invention within an e-business environment, but may also serve as a strategic guide for organizational e-transformation and act as a defining tool for practitioners to assess the alignment of their corporate transformation plans with the stakeholder relational structure. This understanding can be critical and deterministic towards the overall success in any re-inventive efforts. A development model contributes to these re-inventive efforts by characterizing the prevalent parameters to be monitored for each phase of e-transformation.

The findings and the proposed framework in this study may also bear important managerial implications. Indeed, it can be gathered that e-transformation is an essential step in altering the often perceived uncompromising and uncompetitive nature of bureaucratic institutions by revolutionizing the relationships between the organization and its stakeholders. Moreover, the competitive edge to be obtained from any e-transformation strategy is intricately tied to the degree of connectivity between the organization and its stakeholders, which is a derivative of customer satisfaction and the quality of communication between the organization and its customers. This understanding can be valuable to practitioners in determining the scope and extent of competitive benefits that they could derive from an e-transformation campaign, and also in steering the entire organization towards attending to the needs of its stakeholders.

While our single case study has provided empirical validation of the notion that e-government implementations should be accompanied by e-transformation management through collaborative communication with stakeholders, we acknowledge the limitations of our study in providing statistical extrapolation across the board. Nevertheless, as clarified by Yin (1994), the generalizing properties of a case differ from quantitative studies because survey research relies on statistical generalization, whereas case studies rely on analytical generalization. In analytical generalization, the investigator is ‘striving to generalize a particular set of results to some broader theory’ (p 36). Taken in this light, our analysis could provide a vocabulary that researchers and practitioners could employ in the identification of similar components in other organizations, so that future e-transformations can be compared and benchmarked.
