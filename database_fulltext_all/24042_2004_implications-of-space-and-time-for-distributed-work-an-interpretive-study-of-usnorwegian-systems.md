# Implications of space and time for distributed work: an interpretive study of US–Norwegian systems development teams

Suprateek Sarker<sup>1</sup> and Sundeep Sahay<sup>2</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Department of Information Systems, Washington State University, Pullman, WA 99164, USA; <sup>2</sup>School of Informatics, University of Oslo, Oslo, Norway

Correspondence:

Suprateek Sarker, Department of Information Systems, Washington State University, Pullman, WA 99164, USA; Tel.: +1 509 335 5724, Fax: +1 509 335 4275 E-mail: sarkers@cbe.wsu.edu

## Abstract

In this paper, utilizing the often taken-for-granted notions of space and time, we develop an understanding of how collaboration is possible among virtual team-members spread across the globe. We do so by interpretively examining the actual experiences and work practices of virtual team-members located in the US & Norway, engaged in information systems development (ISD) projects. We identify a number of collaboration inhibitors, along with strategies used by team-members to address the challenges posed by the mismatch in time zones and the lack of physical proximity.

European Journal of Information Systems (2004) 13, 3–20. doi:10.1057/palgrave. ejis.3000485

## Introduction

Virtual teams are increasingly being used to accomplish work in organizations, especially in the arena of information systems development (ISD) (Carmel, 1999; Jablin & Sias, 2001; Hollingshead & Contractor, 2002). While popular press continues to extol the virtues of virtual teams, researchers in the field have noted that not much is known regarding how virtual teamwork is carried out in practice (e.g. Briggs et al., 1998; Jessup, 2000; Mannix et al., 2002; Schunn et al., 2002). Limited research exists on how such work arrangements can be made effective (e.g. Furst et al., 1999; Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999; Montoya-Weiss et al., 2001; Armstrong & Cole, 2002), especially in dealing with space/place and time issues in the context of distributed collaboration (Montoya-Weiss et al., 2001; Walsham, 2001; Sarker & Sahay, 2003). In fact, experts on virtual teamwork have repeatedly indicated the importance of these issues by observing that effective virtual teams need to ‘bridge’ (Saunders, 2000; Armstrong & Cole, 2002), ‘overcome’ (Eom & Lee, 1999), ‘routinely cross’ (Kezsbom, 2000) and even ‘transcend’ (Eom & Lee, 1999; Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999) the time and space/place divide among geographically distributed members using Information and Communication Technologies (ICTs). Notably, ‘location transparency,’ signifying an independence of virtual team-members from their locations (i.e. place and time) through the use of ICTs, has become a taken-for-granted concept in the literature on this topic (e.g. Carmel, 1997, 1999).

Woolgar (2002), however, warns us that our tendency ‘to accept uncritically the claimed effects of technology [may result] in theories .. that tend to depend on synoptic, top down.. depictions of technical capacity and effect.’ He adds that the first-generation studies on virtuality and virtual organizations have, in general, adopted a normative stance ‘with little reference to actual circumstances and experiences’ (p 4). Our own review of the IS literature also revealed few critical attempts to examine the implications of time and space for virtual collaboration. Further, consistent with Woolgar’s views, we found that most studies in this area have been based on normative expectations driven by prior theory (e.g. Montoya-Weiss et al., 2001; Sarker et al., 2001) rather than on the actual conduct of work (e.g. Barley and Kunda, 2001).

The focus of our paper is thus on issues of time and space/place, and their role in shaping the subjective experiences and work practices of distributed members of virtual teams. Specifically, in this study, we seek to address the following question:

What are the different challenges that the separation in space/place and time present to virtual teams, and how do virtual teams deal with these challenges?

Through our interpretive study of eight virtual teams engaged in Information Systems Development (ISD), we have identified a number of problems attributable to place and time separation. Problems linked to place appear to arise from limited human connection, lack of agreed upon norms of virtual presence and turn-taking, invisibility of remote team-members’ physical actions, mismatch in practices (e.g. language, communicative styles, working styles, festivals) specific to cultures rooted in places, and incompatibility in ISD-related knowledge and skills. Key problems related to time appear to be arising from mismatches in psychological and social clocks of team-members, complexities in accounting for time zones, negative interpretations of time lapses, and difficulty in comprehending temporally disordered sequences of chat and threaded messages. A number of strategies involving the use of technologies (e.g. the use of different media with varying degrees of social presence, and enabling the creation of a shared ‘placeless’ space), organizational skills (e.g. creation and implementation of norms and improvisations, and appropriate distribution of work), and individual capabilities (e.g. cultural sensitivity and altercentricism, and the ability to adopt a polychronic approach) were discovered to be effective in dealing with the complexities. We believe that our major contribution is the articulation of problems and potential solutions based on an examination of actual experiences and practices of virtual teammembers involved in ISD projects.

The rest of our paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we provide an introduction to the key theoretical concepts of this paper – space/place, and time. Thereafter, we provide a description of the empirical setting for data collection and the research methodology used. This is followed by a theoretically informed rich description of specific inhibiting features of place and time, and strategies used by teams to deal with them. Finally, we summarize key implications, limitations, and future directions of this line of work.

## Space/place, time and virtual teamwork

## Space and place

Space and place are terms that are treated as synonymous in much of the discourse on virtual teams. However, writers from other domains such as philosophy and human geography (e.g. Tuan, 1977; Casey, 1993) have since long argued against equating place with space. More recently, similar arguments have also started appearing in the IS literature (e.g. Sahay, 1997; Schultze & Boland, 2000). Drawing on this tradition of work, in this paper, we emphasize the distinction between place and space in the context of virtual teamwork.

Place and space reflect distinctive meanings and identifications of people to locations – physical or imagined. Spaces serve as containers or receptacles for places whose meanings are shaped by what one does in them (Curry, 1999). Places represent psychologically meaningful domains (Godkin, 1980) grounding identifications through a personal and intimate sharing of experiences (Carter et al., 1993), while spaces are usually experienced through distant and standardized symbols with little locally inherent meaning (Tuan, 1977; Buttimer & Seaman, 1980). A place is like a ‘home’ laden with experience and meaning that helps to provide human beings with the sense of attachment, stability, and security. In contrast, a physical space of residence is more like a ‘house’ with little emotional and existential attachment (Eyles, 1989). While spaces represent arenas in which universalized and homogenized activities occur, places emphasize the need for situated and local action. Space and place thus conjure up various contrary and opposing images such as distance–proximity, universality–locality, standardization–particularity, and freedom– attachment. Despite the apparent opposition in what the place and space imply, it is important to note that the two terms are inter-related, and can sometimes be difficult to separate (Tuan, 1977; Schultze & Orlikowski, 2001).

The distinction between space and place points to the need to examine the notion of co-presence, and to understand how individuals deal with an absence of it (Hine, 2000; Schultze & Boland, 2000; Schultze & Orlikowski, 2001). Potentially ICTs can serve as ‘disembedding mechanisms’ to separate the space (and time) of interactions and tasks from their physical locations, creating the potential of ‘fostering relations between ‘absent’ others’ (Giddens, 1990; Schultze & Orlikowski, 2001), and facilitating the extension of locally specific social relations to different space and time contexts (Walsham, 2001). However, this believed potential of ICTs, which is based on the assumption of work being carried out in space (as in the context of virtual teamwork), is mediated largely by the ability of humans to transcend their own physiological and social needs for co-location and proximity (Boden & Molotch, 1994).

Much of the interactions in virtual teams occur not in physical places, but in electronic spaces (or what Castells (1996) describes as ‘placeless’ spaces), where ICTs facilitate access to information flows, and convey a sense of ‘abstraction,’ ‘openness,’ ‘infinitude,’ and ‘freedom’ (Tuan, 1977; Castells, 1996; Hine, 2000; Schultze & Boland, 2000). These electronic spaces represent new localities ‘disembedded from their cultural, historical, [and] geographic meanings’ (Castells, 1996), wherein new behaviors, language games, and other cultural practices are co-constructed over time (Introna & Tiow, 1999; Hine, 2000). The implicit assumption that ICTs can make the precise physical location of individual participants (i.e. their places) irrelevant to the interactions is reflected in the visions of ICT-enabled ‘location transparency’ (Carmel, 1997) and ‘switching of resources’ (Mowshowitz, 1994). Such visions also imply that the skills and knowledge of these distributed individuals sharing placeless space can be seamlessly integrated and utilized toward the virtual organization’s objectives, bringing forth ‘the hope of making [human] organizations more flexible by freeing it from the constraints of place’ (Schultze & Boland, 2000, p 187).

In our view, virtual ISD teamwork presents an interesting domain in which developers have to constantly straddle the physical and electronic worlds, often conducting work through ICT-mediated interactions with members whom they may not have known before. While such interactions carry strong assumptions of placeless or space-based work, at the same time, human beings have a ‘compulsion for proximity’ (Boden & Molotch, 1994) to ‘increase attention, social impact and familiarity’ (Kiesler & Cummings, 2002), and to enhance the degree of authenticity and realism of one’s experiences (Sahay, 1997). Furthermore, given the relatively short-term nature of virtual teamwork, and the inclusion of members from different parts of the globe, it becomes complex to detach the cultural, historical, and physical location-specific meanings from the interacting individuals, and to transform electronic spaces into meaningful places for team-members (Introna & Tiow, 1999; Schultze & Orlikowski, 2001).

We thus argue that while on-line connections make collaborators ‘visible’ on an electronic space to some extent, it does not automatically enable the seamless bridging of place for virtual team-members, a characteristic critical for effective virtual teamwork (e.g. Carmel, 1999; Eom & Lee, 1999; Lipnack & Stamps, 1999). Virtual team members remain embedded in their familiar surroundings, while electronically interacting with remote others, and need to thus simultaneously negotiate the meanings and norms associated with their physical location as well as those emerging in the on-line forums (Hine, 2000). The focus of our analysis is to understand the tensions that arise as a result, and the strategies that teams and individuals develop to manage them.

## Time

Time is one of the most elusive concepts related to work (Cooper & Rouseau, 2000), and this point has been highlighted in recent IS research (e.g. Walther & Tidwell, 1995; Lee & Liebenau, 2000; Orlikowski & Yates, 2002; Massey et al., 2003). While it is generally accepted that IT affects temporal aspects of contemporary society, the complex relationship between ICTs and time remains poorly understood (Lee & Whitley, 2002). In the literature, this relationship tends to be described primarily with respect to the technical capabilities of ICTs that result in the speeding up of production and coordination processes. Such a perspective marginalizes the broader (and arguably more critical) questions related to the underlying mechanisms that enable this ‘speed up,’ and also the manner in which different social groups deal with the challenges arising from such intensification of work.

Scholars adopting a broader perspective on time see it as a fundamental category in organizational life that helps to define some form of ‘temporal order’ (Dubinskas, 1989), which can potentially undergo reconfiguration through the mediation of ICTs. Time is all-pervasive as it constantly helps to provide the organizing frame of reference for work groups through agendas, meeting schedules, videoconference sessions, and strategizing about ‘our time’ and ‘their time’ and about synchronizing activities. Further, the success or failure of IT projects is heavily dependent on time. Project outcomes are often judged based on whether time deadlines are met, which in turn depends on issues such as effective coordination of work and the ISD team-members appropriating revised temporal routines of work, that are often influenced by ICTs.

While the conception of time as a material commodity and the focus on objective ‘clock time’ remains ingrained in the organizational management literature (Whipp, 1994; Hassard, 1999), increasingly, the subjective aspects of time are being emphasized (e.g. Barley, 1988; Costea, 1996; Hassard, 1999). Subjectivities arise due to a number of reasons such as cultural differences, multiplicity of social norms around ‘speed’ of response in communications, time-zone differences, and varying work patterns. In order to comprehend these subjectivities, one needs to be sensitive to the fact that the relationship between time and virtual teamwork is complex and deeply embedded in individual, organizational, and societal levels.

The assumption of the dominance of clock time is evident in virtual and distributed team projects that are managed under the assumptions of ‘follow the sun,’ implying that the clock times of different countries around the globe can be seamlessly integrated (Carmel, 1999). This assumption is difficult to implement in practice because of a number of reasons. Individuals have multiple temporalities that may not be aligned with each other. For example, the biological/social clocks of the distributed individuals are often not synchronized, and this makes the integration of their times very difficult (Montoya-Weiss et al., 2001). This is particularly the case if the time-zone differences among locations of distributed team members are substantial (Carmel &

Agarwal, 2001). In addition, team members have ‘timeframes’ (Failla & Bagnara, 1992; Butler, 1995) based on their prior experiences that they draw upon in order to interpret future events. Individuals also engage in extemporaneous and improvised behavior in order to deal with the constraints they may experience because of clock time. Such improvised behavior, by its very nature, is difficult, if not impossible, to synchronize. Research also suggests that teams, collocated or virtual, have their own temporal ‘rhythms’ (Maznevski & Chudoba, 2000), and these rhythms influence both how technologies are used by the work groups, and how, through the use of technologies, the temporal orders for work groups are redefined (Barley, 1988).

It is also important to consider the fact that virtual team members typically are drawn from different countries and societies with varying cultural assumptions regarding time. For example, Krishna et al. (2004) describe how, typically, Japanese professionals take longer time to reply to an e-mail as compared to Indian professionals. The consistently delayed response by the Japanese professionals in the study was attributed to their limited competence in the English language, and to their work-related communication culture, which values completeness in their mode of replying. In contrast, Indian engineers appeared to be enacting the norms of communication that they had appropriated during their prior experience of working with North American colleagues, who were seen to reply almost immediately. These different temporal rhythms around the use of e-mail led to communication challenges, particularly how ‘silence’ was interpreted in different locations. Past research also suggests that power asymmetries between the subgroups from different parts of the world usually forces ‘weaker’ groups and societies to synchronize their own temporal rhythms with those of the stronger (e.g. Hongladarom, 2002), and such a practice of synchronizing by routinely inconveniencing the weaker subgroup may lead to resentment and to a dysfunctional collaborative relationship.

The ‘networked nature’ of time (Kavanagh & Araujo, 1995) also contributes to the complexity of managing time in a virtual team context. As distributed team members involve themselves in multiple activities associated with different time zones simultaneously, they are forced to negotiate time with actors in their local and remote/virtual networks. For example, in establishing a schedule for a synchronous on-line chat session, in our study virtual team members from each location attempted to negotiate meeting times (avoiding late nights or early mornings) that would be as convenient as possible for themselves and their own families, while also being flexible enough to minimize the inconvenience of remote team members located in different time zones.

Individuals working in ICT-enabled virtual teams have to deal with yet another form of time-related complexity. While it has been convenient for co-located members of traditional organizations to adopt a linear chronological view of time in the interactions, recent analysis of internet-based communication reveals that ICTs bring forth the possibility of ‘disordered temporal collage of jumbled tenses’ (Hine, 2000). As different events unfold in different time zones and are recorded/reported on the electronic media, the distinctions between past, present, and future tend to get increasingly blurred, resulting in ‘timeless time’ (Castells, 1996). Dealing with jumbled tenses and blurred distinctions between past, present, and future can be quite disorienting for virtual teammembers who are unprepared to deal with this temporal complexity.

In summary, we argue that while ICTs do indeed provide the potential to synchronize work or to ‘follow the sun,’ the social processes associated with virtual teams, including the manner in which ICTs are appropriated in them, significantly influence how temporal complexities are actually negotiated. Thus, one of our key interests is in unearthing the different time-related challenges faced by virtual team-members, and in understanding the creation of suitable structures and work practices that can help deal with these challenges. This focus is in line with Orlikowski and Yates’ (2002) recent call for adopting a practice-based perspective where time is experienced in organizational life through a process of ‘temporal structuring.’

## Empirical approach

## The study setting

In our study, the virtual teams were comprised of students from two universities working collaboratively to analyze a business information systems problem, converting it into a systems design, and then developing a working prototype over a 14-week period in Spring, 2001. In addition to developing/implementing the final system, each team was required to prepare a number of project-related documents. The participants were students enrolled in ISD courses in a Norwegian university (UN) and in an American (i.e. US based) university (UA). The participants in UA were primarily graduating seniors in the MIS undergraduate program, while the UN members were students in the graduate program in Informatics.

A total of eight virtual teams were formed, each consisting of about 8–10 members. Typically, each virtual team consisted of four to five UA members who were randomly matched with four to five UN members from the other university. Thus, each individual had three to four ‘local’ members and four to five ‘remote’ members in his/her team. Some demographic details of the 74 participants are provided in Table 1. All teams were reasonably balanced demographically, and the factors of gender, within-location diversity in national/cultural origin, and age did not appear to have a significant effect on the collaborative process in general, and on time– space issues in particular.

## Methodology

Our methodology can broadly be described as a virtual ethnography (Hine, 2000; Ruhleder, 2000) concerned with the interpretive study of interactions in virtual space. This study was undertaken by the coordinators (also the authors of this paper), observing the electronic interactions between the various actors, posting relevant project-related information, providing technical support, and intervening in situations that could lead to breakdowns. Interestingly, both coordinators had an opportunity to physically observe (and communicate face-to-face with) half of the participants, and symbolically observe and interact with all the participants in the teams in the electronic space, creating an overlap between the real and the virtual (Ruhleder, 2000). While this made the process of interpretations more complex, the overlaps provided opportunities for triangulation of the ‘virtual’ and the ‘real’ understanding, by adapting ‘traditional field methods to these settings, and [using] these same techniques to open up new possibilities for the collection, analysis, and representation of data’ (Ruhleder, 2000, pp 3–4).

In our investigation of the real-virtual research environment, we utilized three key sources of data in this study: first, the communication transactions (messages posted, files shared, and on-line chat transcripts) between team-members, second, reflections of UA and UN members of each team on their experience at the end of the project, and third, our own impressions formed through participant observations, reflexively balancing personal involvement with detachment.

To systematize our analysis of the vast repository of data assembled, we turned to techniques associated with the grounded theory method (Strauss & Corbin, 1990; Locke, 2001; Sarker et al., 2001; Urquhart, 2001). While ethnography and grounded theory are sometimes seen as distinct methodologies, many scholars believe that the ‘tensions’ between the two approaches are ‘reconcilable,’ and some even contend that grounded theory methodology offers flexible avenues to enhance the ‘analytic edge and theoretical sophistication of ethnographic research’ and also ‘streamline fieldwork’ (Charmaz & Mitchell, 2001). In this study, we utilize the ‘gentle’ and ‘openended’ guidelines offered by the grounded theory methodology within the broader framework of ethnography to bring some order to the formulation and representation of the stories about the virtual teams (Charmaz & Mitchell, 2001).

Essentially, our data analysis strategy drew on guidelines from the grounded theory methodology literature (e.g. Glaser & Strauss, 1967), and followed the logic of the two levels of coding, open coding and selective coding, as discussed by Charmaz & Mitchell (2001). Open coding refers to the fracturing of data, and categorizing it through a process of constant comparison (Strauss & Corbin, 1990). Following the recommendations of

Charmaz & Mitchell (2001) regarding the application of coding principles as part of an ethnographic study, we did not pursue a line-by-line coding strategy but instead adopted a holistic approach to coding the reflection documents provided by US and Norwegian members of each team. This was followed by a process of selective coding of communication transactions in each team. Selective coding is more conceptual and abstract and subsumes earlier codes, thereby allowing the researchers not only ‘to synthesize large amounts of data, but also to organize earlier codes into a coherent framework’ (Charmaz & Mitchell, 2001, p 167). It is important to note that the clear focus on time and space/place in our research question and our ‘theoretical sensitivity’ on virtual teamwork helped shape (though not determine) the coding process. The data, after selective coding, were organized around the broad themes of conditions posed by geographical dispersion (place and time distances) leading to problems (sometimes opportunities), strategies used, and an assessment of the efficacy of the strategies in addressing the problems, and this thematic organization is reflected in much of our discussion below. Summary data organized by the team is presented in Tables 2–5. We now present our interpretations arising from the empirical analysis in the following two sections.

Limited human connection: The separation of the UA and UN team members from each other by several thousand miles did not allow physical situatedness, an important pre-condition for meaningful face-to-face human interactions (Giddens, 1984). More importantly, a lack of co-presence made it difficult to establish the human connection associated with bodily presence and social cues (Sahay, 1997).

Strategies employed and their efficacy Sharing ‘placeless space’: Teams tried to compensate for the absence of physical situatedness by creating ‘placeless’ electronic spaces (Castells, 1996) on WebCT. Such electronic spaces do not automatically imply that the space (of place) is transcended, but rather indicates a transformed notion of spatiality, defined by ‘connection’ and not by ‘relative distance’ (Hine, 2000). Despite this sense of ‘connection,’ gaps with respect to understanding continued to persist, at least initially in the project. A key point that emerged is that the pre-condition of virtual presence was fundamental for effective communication, wherein distributed members developed consciousness of each-others’ presence and of their routines of presence on electronic channels.

Instituting norms of communication and virtual presence: During synchronous communication, virtual presence of distributed members was signaled by the chat program. However, it is important to note that the technology could not authenticate identities of those chatting (Hine, 2000). Further, it was not sensitive to breaks in the actual presence (i.e. attention) of individuals:

Chat participant 1: [Chat participant 2’s name,] are you still there? Chat participant 2: Yeah [pause] y sorry, I was talking on the phoney

Chat participant 1: Do you have questions about this

Chat participant 2: that’s OKy

/\* Clearly, participant 2 was not engaging in the discussion of the issue at hand and was caught unawares as he was taking care of some local business \*/

In such situations, proactive team members signaled their temporary absence with warnings such as ‘I need to be away for a few minutes to make a phone call.’ This practice was appropriated as a norm in many teams.

In an asynchronous setting, maintaining virtual presence turned out to be even more difficult, because it was based on others’ expectations regarding when an individual checked a designated channel for messages. To ensure virtual presence, teams applied norms for logging on and responding. The following message indicates such a norm in the form of a ‘protocol’:

..we decided that if a message was posted by one side.. the other side.. would respond [within 24 hours] saying message was received, and was being looked into, or something along those lines. Both sides lived up to the protocols, which seemed to bring the two [sides] together into a single team.. versus two teams trying to work together.

Using visual and synchronous text-based technologies to create ‘social presence’: Consistent with the social presence theory, which posits that ‘text-based communication has a lower social presence that videoconferencing’ (Hollingshead & Contractor, 2002, 224), a large majority of participants argued for the need for videoconferencing. For example, one team stated:

ythrough video conferencing, we could not only hear each other’s voice but also see each other’s faces. It would have probably helped us to build a stronger social relationship among all team members.

However, one team also highlighted the limits of videoconferencing in creating social presence:

The videoconferenceydid not feel like a normal face to face meeting. Because of the lack of eye contact we did not know when they watched us. Therefore, it was difficult to say ‘hello’y In fact, it felt more like watching televisionywe did not know when we had their attention.

Given that the teams did not have access to videoconferencing initially, a number of team members chose to post their static pictures, which seemed to help somewhat in establishing the human connection and put a face to a name. As UN members of a team commented:

It was very difficult to relate to someone you had never seen. The UA team posted a picture of themselves after a couple of weeks, and this actually helped.

In addition, all but two teams made extensive use of synchronous on-line chat sessions on the WebCT in order to enhance social presence, though some of the teams complained about the lack of social cues and ‘body language’ in this mode of communication.

Minimizing dependencies between locations: About half of the teams attempted to deal with the complexities of place by decomposing tasks so as to minimize the dependencies between members in the two locations (Kiesler & Cummings, 2002). There strategies sought to minimize the ‘frustration due to the lack of a dialogue’ and also to enhance the teams’ effectiveness by allowing ‘the two local teams to simultaneously work on different parts of the project.’

Making communication and work processes visible: As mentioned earlier, members of seven out of eight teams were left wondering, at some point in the project, if their remote colleagues were actually engaged in producing the promised deliverables. Two innovative work-practices seemed particularly effective in addressing this issue: (1) posting detailed minutes of meetings held between collocated members of any location to make their work more visible to remote members; and (2) documenting the name of the member who modified any deliverable, along with the date and description of the precise nature of contributions.

## Diversity in ethnic/national cultures

Problems experienced Cultures significantly shape individuals’ behaviors, for example, through ‘the development of normative scripts for particular situations, culturally based selective perception of behaviors of others, and differential attributions for behavior founded in culturally based expectations (Thomas, 2002, p 88). We observed a number of problems arising from the following cultural incompatibilities among team members: mismatch in preferred language and differential competency in the ‘official’ language, misunderstandings arising from dissimilar conversational styles, uncertainty caused by remote members switching to native language, and performance expectations not being adjusted in light of remote members’ festivals and holidays.

Mismatch in preferred language for conversation: Language issues, especially those of ‘fluency,’ ‘stylistic accommodation,’ and communication styles such as the ‘use of praise’ can be important in cross-cultural projects (Thomas, 2002). Historically, language has been bound to the physical location of the inhabitants. While the globalization processes may be favoring the diffusion and adoption of certain languages, the cultural identity of an individual is still inextricably tied to his or her native language. Individuals, even those having fluency in multiple languages, ultimately prefer to converse in their own language (Stohl, 2000). In our case, English was the official language since the American participants could not communicate in Norwegian, while the Norwegian participants had some competency in English although not always a preference for it. Some UN members expressed their dissatisfaction over the use of English as the official language for the project:

We felt constrained by always having to express ourselves in English. Not all of us have that much experience in expressing ourselves in Englishy

[UA] members responded much faster than we did.. we had to concentrate on what was going on in the chat and what we were writing... when the activity was.. hectic, we experienced that we were writing a message about a topic that no longer was discussed.

Some UN members also perceived their UA counterparts as being unsympathetic to their lack of mastery over the language being used for communication and for preparation of deliverables.

Uncertainty caused by remote members switching to their native language: Ironically, many UA members often felt at a serious disadvantage, given their complete reliance on English language alone. During virtual team-meetings, some UN members of at least two teams switched to Norwegian language for local conversations, making the UA members feel excluded:

UA Member 1: ‘How is the system progressingy

UN Member 1: ‘If you have any questions about implementing of prototype, you can ask [..]

Misinterpretation of conversation style The UN and US members also showed quite distinct differences in their styles of communication leading to certain problems for over half of the teams. UA members made observations such as: ‘The Norwegians tended to be more abrupt and forthright,’ and ‘..they [UN members] spoke in a short, abrupt, and blunt manner’ and this seemed to, at least initially, create some uncertainty among UA members. Similarly, UN members of a team described their remote members as ‘nice guys:’

Americans.. [seemed] more positive by nature, while we are more reserved. This became apparent in the way they gave comments to our work, like ‘great work,’ ‘excellent,’ and so ony. This was nice in the beginning, but we would have liked to get some critical response as well.

Performance expectations not adjusted in accordance with festivals and holidays of remote team members: Festivals and holidays form a central aspect of national (and related ethnic) cultures (Al-Abdul-Gader, 1999), and have implications for virtual teamwork. Apart from the significance these special days may have to an individual in a religious or spiritual sense, they also serve as occasions for socially significant practices such as skiing trips and family visits. In our case, UA members had their week long ‘Spring Break’ around the middle of the project. Similarly, the UN members had their ‘Easter Break’ lasting over 10 days during the ‘crunch time’ in the project. Members of one of the teams, in particular, could not relate to why remote colleagues needed to have such long breaks, and perceived them as being irresponsible and lacking commitment. This issue generated sufficient negative feelings to spiral into a near-breakdown situation in the team.

Adopted strategies and their efficacy Demonstrating cultural familiarity and solidarity: At least two of the teams appeared very effective in establishing a social bond among team-members by relating their own biographical details with places and incidents that the remote members could identify with. This approach is illustrated in the following exchange:

UA member: I am a proud Norwegian-American. My grandfather spoke fluent Norwegian, and we fly the Norwegian flag on certain holidays.

UN member: I’ve [visited] the US twice.. My husband.. is going back to SF.. next month, what he’s going to bring back is a lot of..chocolate chip cookies, in Norway you..got.. long beautiful fjords, yes, but no real cookies :)

Some members attempted to use the other’s language in their attempt to bridge the cultural distance, for example, starting a message with ‘Vi ville gjerne ha to welcome you to the project’ [We wholeheartedly welcome youy] and ending it with ‘Vi gleder meg a treffe dem’ [We look forward to meeting you]. Another related tactic used by many teams was to intersperse substantive discussions with conversations on themes that were not linked to the projects such as weather, job prospects, wolves and cougars associated with UN and UA locations respectively, and earthquake occurrence near UA. Such conversations generally facilitated the formation of social solidarity, though one team did report that such ‘small talk’ was not useful in its case.

Being culturally sensitive: Two tactics helped in avoiding breakdowns arising from language use and unfamiliar conversation styles. First, remote members were given the benefit of doubt:

[A].. problem we faced was the tone of communication by [UN] members.. this is something that we just took as a cultural difference and did not take.. offence.

Second, team members consciously attempted to recognize differences in the language games associated with locations, and minimized the use of unfamiliar expressions, providing swift clarifications regarding language use as necessary.

Being sensitive to remote members’ festivals and holidays: Members of several teams tried to gain an understanding of remote members’ festivals, holidays, and cultural practices through additional reading and research, as evident in the following message of a UA member:

Hi Everyoney I’m going to do the cultural research on Norway this weekend, if anyone wants to get together let me know.

Hermeneutic Reading of Symbols: It is clear that cultural issues discussed are relevant to any cross-cultural context. A virtual cross-cultural context demands an even higher level of vigilance, anticipation, and altercentricism in dealing with these cultural differences, because a remote member is not really engulfed in the culture as a visiting (i.e. co-located) collaborator might be. Instead, the virtual team member is merely a recipient of electronic symbols from an alien culture through the computer screen, while being immersed within a different local culture. Thus, a genuine effort is needed to ‘read’ symbols created in remote cultures (e.g. text such as ‘excellent’ that had very different meanings for UA and UN members) through the deliberate use of the hermeneutic circle (e.g. Hirsch, 1967). In about half of the teams, members actually discovered the importance of this form of iterative thinking, as seen in the sample quote below:

The lack of skill /experience in our virtual counterpart seem[ed] on retrospect to be more a product of miscommunication than on their actual lack of skill.

It appears that by hermeneutically reading communicative behaviors of culturally distant UA collaborators, the UN members of this team were able to revisit and revise their initial understanding of the situation, including their evaluation of capabilities of their UA counterparts.

## Technology-related diversity

Problems experienced Our study revealed a number of interesting problems arising due to technology-related differences between team members emplaced in UA and UN respectively. These problems may be attributed to mismatches in: ISD philosophies/approaches, areas of expertise, levels of skills, ISD language, IT infrastructure, and economic drivers of IT use.

Difficulty in collaborating due to mismatch in ISD philosophies and approaches, and areas of expertise: The literature on ISD indicates the existence of many different paradigms and traditions in ISD, related to different ideologies, technical sophistication, and business environment (Hirschheim & Klein, 1989). Virtual team members drawn from different parts of the globe (or even different organizations within the same geographical area) can experience difficulties due to a mismatch of preferred design approaches and values, as well as technology platforms. Specifically, during the early phases of design, UA members were generally focused on using IT from the management perspective to enable efficiency, often through routinization and intensification of work, and sometimes through some downsizing; UN members, in contrast, seemed generally opposed to systems that sacrificed quality of work life, and laid great emphasis on user interactions and aesthetics. For example, UN members of one team felt that UA members were not showing sufficient concern for the user’s perspective in developing specifications:

We.. felt differences in how to approach the application specificationy European software developers place greater importance on interaction with the client than their American counterparts.

In contrast, UA members reported irritation with their UN members’ focus on the aesthetics (screen color and layout) instead of the core functional requirements. Lack of compatibility in the preferred ISD approaches also seemed to create difficulties for the teams:

[UA] part of the team and the [UN] part of the team had different educational experience. For example, the preference for design techniques was different. [UN] team members have a lot of experience in object oriented techniques as UML, and UA members more experienced with structured/data-oriented approaches.

..the [A]mericans did the specification[s] very differently than we would have done.. It was difficult to read the specificationsy that we had never seen before.

Lack of sensitivity to the differences in the ISD traditions/preferences including diagramming conventions led to annoyance and inaccurate assessment of remote members’ skill levels.

Difficulty in collaborating due to differences in skills and training: Almost all the teams in the study expressed frustration over the levels of competency that remote members had over methodologies and tools (i.e. programming languages and DBMS). This mismatch in the level of skills made it difficult for team members to rely upon their remote colleagues initially. In some teams, the lack of mutual respect arising from differences in skills and educational experience was reflected in the way tasks were distributed.

Difficulty in collaborating due to mismatch in ISD language: Complexities in ISD-related language surfaced as the same word sometimes conveyed fundamentally different meanings to the two sides. For example, basic terms such as ‘prototyping’ and ‘implementation’ meant different things to members in the two locations, even though they were all informatics/IS students. For example, implementation implied the coding phase of the project for UN members, while for the UA team, implementation referred to the process by which IS is introduced/accepted in the organization. Similarly, UA members used the term prototype ‘to describe the first release of a working system,’ while, for UN members, it signified ‘a series of interconnected GUI screens without functionality.’ A disaster due to this gap in understanding between the two sides regarding the scope of the project (to build a ‘prototype’) was fortunately averted in a team as this anomaly was discovered well before the final deadline.

Difficulty in collaborating due to mismatch in IT infrastructure: Since virtual teams draw members from different locations around the world, it is very likely that the IT infrastructure and the ‘computer languages’ (operating systems, programming languages, support tools, etc.) available to them would not be identical. Such incompatibility could, in turn, present difficulties in communicating, accessing, and modifying shared documents, coding, and testing code, as experienced in varying degrees by participants. In this project, the two sides had access to and expertise in very different technologies, as evident from the following quotations:

UA members: [An] incorrect assumption that we made was that everyone in the world had a Windows operating system platform. For us, it is hard to imagine that not everyone uses Windows. While we used Windows, [UN] had Unix based platform. This created the problem of determining what program to use to turn in deliverables.

UN Members: We were aware that the state of Washington is Microsoft’s headquarter, and we also heard from the [UA] members that their skills were often based on Microsoft productsy As everyone knows, there is a Unix tradition at [UN].

This difference caused problems in as many as six out of the eight teams in the early phases of the project, especially, when one side of the team could not modify documents created by the other side (resulting in a struggle between .pdf and .doc). Near-breakdown situations also precipitated when the development technologies were being selected, with members of each side (in a number of teams) pushing for their preferred ISD technology (Windows vs Unix/Linux, Oracle vs Access or SQL Server, PHP vs ASP, etc.).

Rejecting the use of a communication channel due to differences in the economic drivers of technology use: The issue of cost to access the communication infrastructure also emerged in one team. Because of the 9-h time difference, the most feasible time for scheduling on-line synchronous chats was early morning at UA and evening at UN. UN members of one team in particular indicated their lack of enthusiasm for such meetings:

Having chat sessions in the evenings meant that you would have to log on to the internet using a dial-up connection, which in turn meant extra costs. This is probably also a reason why we never did have the ’regular’ chat sessions.

Some strategies and their efficacy Taking inventory of levels and nature of ISD expertise: The starting point of dealing with diversity is to uncover it, and then decide how to harness it. Some of the teams attempted to do precisely this, by initiating a round of communication, where each team-member was required to state his or her areas of expertise. This input was later used to assign project tasks.

Improvising compromises: Several instances for improvisation (Walsham, 2001), many towards a compromise solution, were evident in teams. For example, members of one location, after taking inventory of the skills of remote team-members, re-negotiated the scope and nature of the project with the client. The original project definition required the team to build a full-blown system using JAVA and XML, while members in the implementation team seemed to have skills in Visual Basic, ASP, and Access. Through the process of negotiation, team representatives convinced the client organization that a prototype made using the Microsoft technologies would still be valuable for the company for assessing its real information needs. Another interesting instance of improvisation was the creation of hybrid design diagrams by one team that integrated the ideas from the UML Use Case diagramming and the Dataflow diagramming techniques. In contrast, in another team, when UA members showed no interest in engaging in a discussion on UML because it was beyond the scope of their education, and this seemed to infuriate the UN members. Yet another interesting example of improvisation was seen when a team, half (UN members) of which did not have convenient access to MS Word, and the rest (UA members) did not have easy access to Acrobat, decided to use .txt format during the time both sides contributed to the document. Once the document was ready, it was converted to a .pdf format and submitted to coordinators. UN members in one of the teams also reported a compromise involving the limited use of ‘toolkits’ from the object-oriented world, in deference to UA members’ wishes.

Aligning frames of reference: To collaborate across traditions of ISD, and to improvise meaningfully, often it is necessary to have some level of understanding of the frames held by remote members (Carmel, 1999). For example, some UA members attempted to obtain an understanding of the Norwegian culture and Scandinavian tradition of ISD by reading additional literature. UN members requested lectures on structured methods of ISD. In another instance, UN members requested UA members to coach them in Active Server Pages (ASP) technology, since UA members had prior experience with ASP but UN members had never used it.

Transmit multiple carriers of meaning: Given the potential of electronic texts (and diagrams) to be distantiated, autonomized, appropriated, socially constructed, and enacted differently in different contexts (Lee, 1994), it is useful for the communicators (i.e. transmitters) to use multiple modes of representation. This enables the recipients to ‘triangulate’ the different types of symbols received and reconstruct an understanding that is as close to the originally intended meaning as possible. Four out of the eight teams discovered that text or diagrams alone were insufficient in conveying subtle meanings critical to the design, since the use of standardized representation symbols did not necessarily lead to identical interpretation in a different context. As UN members of one team reported that the envisioned system ‘became very clear [to UA members] after we delivered the screen shots’ in addition to the textual narrative and design diagrams.

Celebrating, not denigrating, diversity: It is well-known that team members from all cultures (national or professional) may not be equally well-equipped to handle every social situation, including tasks (Walsham, 2001). Nevertheless, it is important for virtual team participants to focus on the remote-members’ strengths, not their limitations. It is remarkable, that in most cases in this study, while members of ineffective teams continued to harp on the presumed weaknesses of remote members throughout the project and even after the conclusion, the relatively more effective ones found ways to align strengths of both sides towards project goals. For example, in one team, UN members, who had stronger technical backgrounds (owing to their ‘computer science’ education) than their UA counterparts who were business IS students, wrote:

We.. had little experience in web development in general. We were happy that the Americans had so much experience in this areay The Americans also seemed quite efficient in proposing and finding useful applications for computery It may be the [UA students’] business education, which has helped them explore all these opportunities and find the right ones. As our education is more technical, we think that the two parts of the team complemented each other.

Another team that had a smooth collaborative process viewed the differences in expertise between members from the two locations as a ‘boon’ since they ‘wouldn’t have had the same breadth of skills in a single local team.

## Some implications of time

As mentioned earlier, a review of the management literature largely reflects a preoccupation with the objective chronological view of time (Sahay, 1997), which tends to obscure the subjective views of time that exist, and their implications (Sahay, 1997; Hassard, 1999). In this section, we outline the problems arising especially from the virtual team members’ clock time mismatches, and the disordering of messages. Our analysis suggests that objective as well as subjective interpretations of time need to be considered in dealing with many complexities of virtual teamwork.

## Clock time and structuring of teamwork

Problems experienced We now describe four time-related challenges for virtual teamwork observed in the study:

(a) Different physiological cycles and social activity schedules existed in different time zones that were relatively deeply embedded and thus difficult to change. Perceived lack of sympathy (or, at least, empathy) to these different rhythms posed difficulties in developing relationships.

(b) Local clocks were set at different times, and thus the local times needed to be translated. Further, time adjustments were done on different days in Europe and the US. Lack of vigilance in this regard led to unattended synchronous meetings or missed deadlines, and this, in turn, got interpreted as a lack of seriousness or commitment.

(c) Significant time differences among virtual team members made it difficult to work in parallel. Further, members of several teams experienced unproductive waits before the other sides responded to their questions.

(d) Silence or unexpected delays in replying by remote members tended to be interpreted negatively, with attributions of incompetence and a lack of commitment. This issue was serious enough to occasion a breakdown of even functional relationships.

Since time immemorial, clocks have structured people’s lives. Our sense of temporality is shaped by both our physiological urges and through the internalization of shared conventions for structuring activities in the immediate society we are members of (Hassard, 1999). Virtual teamwork tends to challenge this sense of temporality as members have to work (say, attend synchronous meetings) during what is not considered ‘normal work time’ with collaborators who are located in other time zones and bound by alternative conventions:

UN member: ‘..tired’ /\* late evening in Norway \*/ UA Member: ‘Yep, still have class and work’ /\* day just getting started for UA \*/ UN member: ‘my body just want[s] to go to bed’ /\* late evening in Norway F bedtime \*/

Chat sessions were often held when it was late in the evening for the UN members, when they were already exhausted. In addition, they needed to wait patiently for their UA counterparts to ‘wake up,’ which was especially difficult on Sunday mornings for UA members, after their social engagements on the previous evening:

UN member 1: ‘helloy’

UA member: ‘guess nobody from my group is up yet

UN member 2: ‘little early on a sunday:)’

UA member: ‘That and there was a really big party yesterday for seniors’ /\* social events for all members in virtual teams not likely to be synchronized \*/

As a result of poor attendance of UA members and persisting effects of the previous night, the meeting was not productive, even though deadlines were fast approaching. The incompatible social rhythms of the two sides were also often highlighted in the conversations such as:

UA member: I think it weird to hear you talk about getting drunk. Its 10:00 in the morning!!! yyy UN member: but for us it is, 19:00

The different biological and social rhythms, and the perceptions of how sympathetic (or not) the team members were to remote colleagues’ temporal needs, in some cases, led to problems in developing collaborative relationships, and even created bitter feelings:

ywe tried to establish regular chat-session each week. This failed, mostly due to time difference problems. We also felt that [remote] members showed little consideration for which time would fit us.

The UN members of another team reflected on a recurrent pattern of their UA members always being in a hurry to end the chat sessions due to other commitments:

When we had our [on-line chat] meetings, [UA] members had to go after about an hour y we hardly did have the time to talk about the project. So trying to be funny or personal when [remote] members have to go.. within a short time, would probably not make us socially bonded, quite to the contrary.

In addition, clock times were mixed up, causing coordination problems because of different local times as well as the different days on which clocks were changed to reflect daylight savings:

There was a misunderstanding due to time difference. There was one week when [UA] side did not.. show up for two separate chat sessions. We [i.e. UN] wondered why, and in the end one from [UA] pointed out.. we had turned our clocks one hour forward due to Norwegian summer time. So the [UA] side had turned up, only an hour later than us!

Another obvious disadvantage of time differences noted by a number of teams was that concurrent development of the system was not possible, except in the case of one team where members of one location routinely stayed awake during their night to facilitate the parallel development of systems with their remote members.

Finally, the negative social meanings surrounding delays in response from virtual members emerged as a significant issue, as the following quote from a team reveals:

ysilence was interpreted as negative from our point of view. We were repeatedly asking for information about what we were going to develop.. response time was long. Thus we felt very much on our owny

Another team, that seemed to be working steadily up to this point, experienced a near-complete breakdown on account of non-response. In the words of the UN members:

It does not increase our trust when we get no response on postings, the responses come latey just leaving for vacation.. without notifying their remote members in any way about the delay? As one of our local members put it: At this time I thought UA members were just completely stupid. [Lack of response] was perceived as a confirmation of their lack of commitmenty

UN members of yet another team reported that they had been perceived as incompetent and unskilled by their UA counterparts, due to their delayed responses to UA members’ questions. In their own defense, UN members explained that ‘we often wanted to double check that the answer we gave [to UA members] was correct,’ and ‘this made us use a lot of time before we answered.’

Some strategies and their efficacy Just as mature individuals ‘learn to organize temporal experience in accordance with particular social and cultural processes,’ competent teams use ICTs along with ‘social ingenuity’ to deal with delays in response and clock time differences (Hassard, 1999), and in some cases, turn them into an advantage. Below we discuss some of the specific strategies that were observed to be effective in many cases.

Managing time translations: While synchronous ICTs allow individuals in different time zones to communicate in ‘real time,’ such meetings require coordination through careful time translations. The following example shows how teams dealt with different local times:

UA Member 1: Team UN: If you’re available to chat, please give us a time that works well for youy

UA member 2: [Chatting] is a great idea.. the only problem is coordinating a time for all of us to meet. I looked up the time difference and as far as I can tell it appears that [UN] is 9 hours ahead of [UA]y To make it a little easier this is the time conversion:

Team UN Team UA /\* NB ‘\*’ indicates possible chat times \*/

6am 3pm

7am 4pm\*

8am 5pm\*

/\* etc. \*/

UA member 2: We all need to meet to chat.. Can everyone meet Thursday Feb. 22 at 7:30am [UA] time that’s 4:30pm [UN] time.

After teams had managed to get an initial handle over the time differences, and had understood the need to coordinate, they were, in general, able to organize meetings with ease. One team routinized the convention of stating all teamwork-related times in both UA and UN local times. In another team, based on the suggestions of UN members, the UA clock time was used for coordination purposes by both sides throughout the project, to avoid the possibility of any confusion. Such routines seemed to work satisfactorily, until clocks were adjusted, and some of the teams were not alert to the different times for the changeover. One team handled this changeover effectively because of the vigilance of some of its members:

UN member: ..By the way, in Norway we have adjusted our clocks by 1 hour, so what used to be 16.30 is now ..17.30. I am not sure if you have done that in USAy

UA membery In regards to the time change, our time does not adjust until next Sunday, April 2ndy

‘Relocating’ Time: Whenever individuals are faced with multiple, possibly conflicting demands of their time, the only solution is ‘adjusting the specific time locations of activities’ (Hassard, 1999). Videotaping is an example of technology that facilitates time relocation by providing a means to record events in real time, so that they can be experienced more conveniently at a future time (Hassard, 1999). Virtual teams in our study seemed to use two strategies to relocate times: first, they routinely used the asynchronous features of WebCT (e.g. on-line forums and file sharing) such that remote members and local members would not be constrained by clock time and could communicate ‘24 h a day 7 days a week’; and second, the requested the on-line chat transcripts from coordinators for members absent from meetings due to other time commitments. The second motivation is evident from the following message posted by a UN team-member who had to attend to other pressing engagements during scheduled chats:

Since all of us don’t always have the time.. to attend all the chat meetings, I have contacted [ the UA facilitator] and requested the chat transcripts..

‘Reclaiming’ Time: ‘Specialization and segregation’ of work results in the loss of slices of time, which need to be reclaimed for obtaining efficiencies in work (Hassard, 1999). We found that several teams segregated their tasks by allocating specialized responsibilities to the two locations. Clearly, teams that allocated analysis responsibilities to one location and development to another location experienced considerable loss of time with half the team not contributing much for about half of the project life. In sharp contrast, three out of the eight teams kept both sides of the dyad involved throughout the project in both activities and successfully developed an integrative social structure for the entire team. Consequently, they could reclaim most of the time (that other teams lost). In general, such teams saw the 9-hour time difference as a ‘huge advantage’:

After a chat session [in the evening, local time] our remote team had the whole day to deal with problems that arose.. [UA], in the same fashion, received our reactions and work during the day at the beginning of daytime. From this principle, the usual daytime working period was extended to a kind of ‘continuous project development.’

In contrast, one of the (remaining) teams expressed regret for not taking advantage of the time difference owing to the lack of team cohesion and to the high degree of task segregation.

Routinizing interactions and developing social solidarity: Perhaps, the most critical aspect of managing uncertainty arising from temporal distance is through instituting norms relating to teamwork. While in some teams, members naturally developed rhythms of communicating regularly and consistently with remote collaborators, other teams struggled. Articulating and adhering to formal set of norms appeared to be a useful strategy for such teams. For example, one team dealt with its continuing non-response/late response issue by proposing a ‘communication protocol.’ Another team indicated that the project experience would have been much better if they had created ‘an informal hierarchy and a set of norms for conducting virtual teamwork.’

Norms with respect to response times included, for example:

The Team members will check WebCT on a daily basis.

The Team members will acknowledge discussion posts from other members when they are read. If more work is required to respond to the message, Team members should [indicate] that they are working on a response.

Interestingly, members of teams that had developed strong social relationships across locations could more comfortably tolerate silence without the explicit formulation of norms. They attributed delays in response to situational contingencies (e.g. lack of necessary client input and participation) rather than to the lack of ability or motivation of remote members.

## ‘Timeless time’ and the structuring of teamwork

Problems experienced While it has been convenient for co-located members of traditional organizations to adopt a linear chronological view of time in the interactions, participants in Internet-mediated communications have been known to experience a temporal disordering in the receipt of messages (Hine, 2000). This phenomenon was experienced in two ways during the project:

 First, by team members trying to sort through and make sense of messages originating in different local times and addressing concerns that were either past or future for the other side. Thus, an individual traveling (i.e. reading through the messages in the shared space) is likely to experience temporal shifts, both positive and negative.

 Second, during ‘real time’ synchronous chats, wherein questions, comments, and responses were often jumbled up, it became difficult to causally link a response with a question or a comment due to temporal and topical shifts. For example, the frustration expressed by one of the teams regarding chat sessions appears to reflect the inability to deal with the jumbled up sequence of texts:

[I]n face-to-face meetings you just don’t start talking when someone else is talking, but during chat meeting everybody can say something all [the] time. This often caused several things to be discussed simultaneously... This wasn’t an appropriate way of conducting project meetings.

Such disordering of messages can potentially lead to ‘breakdowns’ in collaborative relationships, as confusions are often blamed on the lack of ‘conscientiousness on the part of remote partners’ and not on plausible situational variables such as differential speeds of data transfer from the team-members’ locations (Cramton, 2002).

Some strategies and their efficacy Developing norms of messaging: At least three of the eight teams in our study recognized that they needed to implement ‘agreed upon rules of conduct’ restricting topic shifts and disciplining the sequence of synchronous messaging in order to make their meetings more productive. For example, a team that experienced fairly unproductive chats stated:

If we’d had a rule likey ‘we talk about one topic at a time, don’t bring up a new topic until we’ve finished the former’y we would probably have experienced less confusing chat meetingsy

Another team successfully implemented a novel practice that was proposed when at least three team members were having difficulty in keeping ‘on track’ due to disordered chat messages. The following practice suggested by a team-member and enthusiastically adopted by the entire team proved to be quite effective:

Regarding chatting, it could be good to always start one’s message with the name of the person [whose question] one is answering.

Problems with asynchronous messaging were partially addressed through norms maintaining logical threads of messaging to the extent possible.

Physical relocation: Two of the teams utilized an interesting strategy of ‘time–space regionalization’ (Nandhakumar, 2002) in order to control timeless time complexities. The strategy involved physical relocation, wherein all team members from the same geographical area would be co-present, and would jointly communicate with remote members through the same computer. UA members of one team explained:

The remote team-members always chatted from one computer lab with all the members in close proximity while all of us were in separate [locations]. This caused their dialog to be more organized and ours to be more spread out. We would type something that one of our own had already typed and beat us to the submit button. The last two chats we were all in the lab room together, which benefited us more. We were able to discuss instantly [UN’s] responses and have one person type.

Functioning in a polychronic manner: Drawing on the literature (e.g. Lee, 1999; Stohl, 2000), we define polychronicity as the ability to handle multiple jumbled threads of conversations simultaneously. It is interesting to see that at least four of the eight teams were able to handle problems in the communications (due to delays in typing, delays in message transfer across the Atlantic, and topic shifts) with ease, largely owing to teammembers’ capability of being polychronic, and were thus, comfortable with the disordering of messages and topic shifts. One of the teams that was dealing with the multiple jumbled threads in the latter part of the project with remarkable poise, observed:

The early chats could be quite chaotic ybecause [questions] were posed when we were really discussing something else and we or they forgot to answer themy. After a while we got quite used to having them and they [the chats] were more effective.

It appeared to us that individuals who continued to encounter difficulties seemed unable to act in a polychronic mode, or had a strong a priori and uncompromising preference for monochronicity.

Looking broadly at the three strategies discussed above with respect to timeless time, our view is that the deliberate strategies of utilizing norms of messaging and of physical relocation are not likely to be as effective as being naturally comfortable with the inherently polychronic nature of internet-based messaging.

## Conclusion

The objective of this paper was to tease out, through empirical interrogation, some of the implications that the elusive concepts of time and space/place have for virtual teamwork. We have attempted to identify challenges arising out of time–space/place divide among virtual team members, and the strategies used to deal with the challenges. In this section, we discuss some of the contributions made along with potential limitations and future research possibilities.

## Contributions

Our study suggests that reducing the friction of locational and temporal distances requires attention to both the technical and the social components of virtual teams. While ICTs act as key enablers of distributed work, they by themselves do not guarantee ‘location transparency’ or work ‘following the sun.’ The study reveals that ICTs do enable connectivity among distributed team members for information exchange, and also replace the physical ‘space of place’ with the electronic ‘space of flows’ as the arena for conducting social exchanges (Castells, 1996; Hine, 2000). However, it is worth noting that while electronic space does provide an alternate social context where new behaviors and cultural practices get shaped, the linkages between the body and the electronic interaction are never completely erased, especially in the relatively short time spans of typical virtual team projects. Principles of social organization, most notably systematically developing a shared frame of reference and a sense of mutuality (Sarker & Sahay, 2003) are used to deal with ‘complications’ arising from differences of place-related issues, cultural aspects (especially language), personal biographies and competencies, and ISD-related methodologies and preferences. Given how difficult it is to establish a shared frame and mutuality in communication even among those who are co-located, we wholeheartedly agree with scholars such as Schultze & Orlikowski (2001), who contend that creating and sustaining a coherent connection among distributed (often fragmented) individuals, occupying a shared electronic space and straddling between their own places and their team’s shared spaces, presents a major challenge. Conscious efforts on the part of virtual team members and managers are necessary in line with some of the strategies/tactics described in the paper.

As in the case of locational separation, addressing the complexities associated with temporal separation requires the use of ICTs in conjunction with social devices. When time is viewed as an objective chronological counter, the maxim of ‘following the sun’ in software development through the use of technology seems easily achievable. The more enduring difficulties arise from the mismatches between the physiological/social clocks and the objective real times, and the human experiences and interpretations of time such as duration of silence and dealing with temporally disordered messages. In such cases, strategies based on social ingenuity and sensitivity such as formation of norms, developing social solidarity, and becoming comfortable with polychronicity are ones that virtual collaborators need to rely upon.

## Limitations and future directions

We now discuss some potential limitations of the study. In terms of the nature of data, an obvious limitation is that the problems and strategies are derived from empirical observations of students within the context of university courses, where conditions (e.g. incentives to perform, nature of IT infrastructure) can be quite different from those surrounding virtual teams in organizations. For example, a professor’s power over students is arguably of a different nature than a manager’s power over his or her subordinates, and the incentives or disincentives of grades that students receive are very different from the motivators used to regulate team members’ behaviors in ‘real’ organizations. Also, the level of availability of ICTs such as videoconferencing and the quality of groupware utilized are likely to be higher in the case of real-world projects undertaken by companies. While we acknowledge the differences in these conditions, we would like to point out that the teams in our study were working with real business organizations on problems for which managers wanted solutions. As such, there was an expectation that the systems developed by the teams would be seriously considered for actual implementation. There was thus a fair amount of realism in the projects, and consequently, team members experienced pressure and also a sense of pride, and this helped to create a level of commitment that is not typically associated with student projects. Also, given the longitudinal and intense nature of the projects, most students participating in the project were appropriated by the world of virtual teamwork, wherein they enacted their socially constructed roles of interconnected and interdependent team members involved in ISD, rather than behave as typical students interested in merely passing a course (Sarker & Sahay, 2003).

Another potential limitation stems from the dyadic nature of the virtual teams, implying that team members in this study were distributed between just two locations. While dyadic configurations are used quite routinely in the contemporary business world, particularly in ISD (e.g. Carmel, 1999; Nicholson & Sahay, 2001), many other configurations are conceivable, such as subgroups distributed over multiple (more than two) geographical locations, or members traveling across multiple locations at different stages of the project.
