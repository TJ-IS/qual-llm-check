## Abstract

The work of this paper centres on a study of business organisations of the former German Democratic Republic (GDR), whose continued existence after the reunification of Germany was accomplished by management buyout (MBO), rather than through acquisition by Western firms. The goal was to investigate the circumstances surrounding the sudden and total upgrade of information technology and systems enabled (and necessitated) by the end of Eastern Block isolation from the West. The paper reports on 1992 contacts and site visits with four MBO privatised firms and 1999 follow-up contacts and site visits with the two surviving firms of that group. Analyses are presented, which draw on literature devoted to IT transitions and IS implementation. The notion of technological switchback is introduced to characterise the unique circumstances embodied by the situation under study. It involves a necessary and sudden advance in information technological capabilities, which comes at the expense of a significant and undesirable regression in information system effectiveness. Parallels are drawn to the plights of firms in other transitional economies and firms facing emerging ‘technological imperatives.’ Conclusions are elucidated, regarding effective short- and long-term adjustment to such situations.

## Introduction

The fall of the Berlin Wall in November of 1989 began one of the most drastic and sweeping periods of peaceful change in recent human history. Paramount among the stimulants of drastic change in this context was the sudden availability of Western information technology (IT) in countries that had long been deprived of its benefits. Indeed, the COMECON embargo of Western technology had spawned an IT industry in eastern Europe that experts considered to be 8–12 years behind the West (Schmidt, 1996). While these benefits were intrinsically undeniable, no road maps existed for guiding the sudden upgrade they induced in existing enterprises of the newly established market economies. The research described in this paper was conceived to investigate the circumstances surrounding the introduction of Western IT in privatised firms of the former German Democratic Republic (GDR).

Automated support of industrial and business functions was not a new phenomenon in the eastern states of Germany. The GDR had a large network of computer hardware and software specialists, whose capabilities were only limited by a lack of access to state-of-the-art tools. When reunification occurred, it seemed that Western technology could be easily incorporated in an environment of nontrivial computer literacy and well-defined needs. The prevailing assumption at the time of reunification was that Western technology would be unquestionably welcomed. In fact, a ‘West is best’ mentality appeared to have no exceptions at the time. This was strongly illustrated by the eastern German embrace of newly available Western consumer goods (Buteweg, 1992). Of course, it has long been known to researchers and practitioners that successful implementation of an information system (IS) is never simple, no matter how certain it is that implementation should proceed. Success usually demands mastery of factors quite separate from the technological improvements that may make the effort desirable (Lucas, 1981). This poses an interesting juxtaposition of imperatives and served as an initial motivator for the investigation described here.

In 1991, when this research project was begun, the situation of existing private enterprises in the eastern states of Germany had two sides. Of the more than 10,500 enterprises that existed at the time of the reunification, about 5,000 had been sold to foreign or western German investors or companies (Bolton & Roland, 1992). Some 1,000 or so had been privatised by management buyout (MBO). The latter group of independent enterprises represents the central subject of this research. Its MBO firms were taken to private operation by the workers and managers who already populated their ranks at the time of reunification. Since they were not start-ups, these firms did have high expertise in the production of their goods or services. In operational matters at least, their information needs were well understood. Since institutional support for their East German hardware and software platforms had evaporated quickly, immediate action was necessary. However, in acquiring and implementing Western IT support, these firms were largely on their own. The initial questions posed in the project’s conception were: Is it working? What are the problems being faced? Are there any unique approaches to this universal technology upgrade that deserve attention or bear repeating? Does this unprecedented circumstance reinforce (or perhaps supplement) existing theories in the field of information systems?

The discoveries and insights of the project reported here centre on three phases of investigation, all of whose primary data came from interviews of key informants. A project initiation phase included interviews I conducted in early 1992 with executive sources of background information, expert advice and contact referrals. This phase led to the formulation of the study’s original assumptions and objectives. A pilot study phase included interviews with the IS directors of four MBO privatised firms, conducted during visits to their companies in the late summer of 1992. As a result of these pilot interviews, my perspectives and beliefs were revised considerably.

Expanded member checking of the results (Trauth, 1997) among academic colleagues and audiences enhanced the interpretation and analysis of my discoveries. A follow-up investigation phase in 1999 witnessed my visits to the two surviving firms of the original group. This in turn brought about further revisions in my understanding of the circumstances under study. The view that emerged represents a convergence of progressive interpretations (in the sense of Lee, 1999) and the process of its crystallisation is an important ingredient for the understanding of its character. For this reason, the paper is organised according to the three phases of investigation and its conclusions are elucidated as they unfolded during the project’s iterations of data collection and analysis.

A major result of the project is the apparent contradiction of a universally held belief. Prevalent views at the time of the study’s conception (and apparently, since that time, too) were that the eastern German adjustment to Western technology constituted an adjustment to superior IT capabilities and IS functionality. No published work has indicated that this ‘quantum leap’ was at all hindered by deficiencies in adopted Western products. The firms of this study, however, show this view to be quite wrong. The Western systems adopted with the technology here seemed to force their adopters to take significant steps backward in operational functionality, from that which they had enjoyed with their former systems. The notion of technological switchback has emerged from this discovery and the concept is developed in the later sections of this paper. The metaphor is intended to invoke the image of a steep hill that cannot easily be climbed by direct vertical ascent. Switchback trails enable progress in the vertical dimension, but only if the climber is willing to backtrack in the lateral dimensions, reversing progress already made in their directions. This research has revealed a steep hill of IT advancement, whose scaling appears to have necessitated an undesirable retreat in IS effectiveness.

Discussion sections of the paper point out other IS contexts in which technological switchback circumstances can surface. In this generalisation of the notion’s relevance, the paper presents criteria that distinguish the phenomenon from normal trade-off decisions that often face adopters of new systems. Coping mechanisms are identified, for surviving the initial shock of technological switchback conditions, and strategies for outliving their effects are also derived from the evidence of the cases studied. These expositions elaborate and draw upon research literature devoted to IT transitions and IS implementation. By ‘enfolding’ this literature into the discussion sections (Eisenhardt, 1989), it is intended that the exploratory nature of the research will achieve proper emphasis.

Since the discoveries of this research are based upon a limited scope of evidence, a word is in order about the objectives of their dissemination in this paper. I do not claim that my observations are necessarily representative of a larger population, but rather that they are symptomatic of a significant condition. They are exposed in order to stimulate further study and discussion, since this condition is relevant to other contexts, but has escaped explication in the literature so far. In that light, the interpretations and implications expressed here should be regarded as ‘tendencies rather than predictions’ (Walsham, 1995). As with many exploratory case studies, the conclusions are intended as ‘propositions to be tested, refuted, confirmed or refined by subsequent research’ (Akkermans & van Helden, 2002).

## Project initiation

In the summer of 1990, when economic union between the two Germanys was enacted, numerous contradictions started surfacing, between prevailing expectations of the time and actual experiences. In particular, anticipated revenues from the sell-off of eastern German assets fell far short of the costs of keeping those assets viable (Templemann & Schares, 1991). Many eastern German companies that were thought to be desirable acquisitions turned out to need expensive rescuing (Reier, 1991). As was later observed, East Germany had once been rated as the strongest economy in the communist bloc and the 10th strongest in the industrialised world. However, ‘after the fall of the Berlin Wall, economists were horrified at what they discovered’ (Menke-Glueckert, 1995, p. 40).

The Treuhandanstalt (THA), charged with privatising eastern German assets, seemed to enjoy a dual role as saviour and villain in the eyes of the press. Accused equally of the heartless liquidation of viable firms and of ‘feather-bedding’ firms that were not fit to survive, it became a favourite scapegoat for the ills of the eastern German economy (Anonymous, 1991). One subset of the THA’s privatisation outcomes involved management buyouts. In spite of numerous legal and financial hurdles, these enterprises successfully argued that they could become competitive on their own in the emerging market economy. While the THA was exceedingly proud of these success stories, it was well known that MBOs were exceptions in a situation whose deck was stacked against them (May-Strobl & Paulini, 1990). Many analysts at the time, in fact, attributed their success in achieving privatisation to the lack of demand among potential outside investors for their company assets. It was often claimed that certain management buyouts were probably not appropriate risks and should not have been allowed (Kokalj et al., 1990). Later analyses have strongly reflected these views of the time. Since upper management in East German firms had usually enjoyed their placement for political (not functional) reasons, most MBOs were led by former middle managers. It was not uncommon that they were viewed as suddenly functioning ‘several levels above their previous competency’ (Anonymous, 1996). In fact, the following quote from a German investment banker might represent a fair characterisation of the prevailing opinions of MBO enterprises.

‘I never once met an eastern German manager who was capable of leading a company in this new set of circumstances’ (von der Heyden, 1995, p. 50).

## Preliminary fact-finding interviews

In early 1992, I arranged to meet with an official of the THA. My goal was to discuss the feasibility of an exploratory research project and to obtain contacts with firms from the MBO privatised group. I was pleased to receive a hearty endorsement for my research idea from this discussion partner and a firm commitment for support of its investigations. It was evident from her comments that the THA was indeed proud of its MBO firms and that she believed their cause might be better forwarded with studies like the one that I was proposing. My discussion partner from the Treuhandanstalt agreed to put me in touch with a number of MBO firms, so that I could conduct a pilot study of site visits and semistructured interviews. My intentions for this pilot study were to discover IS issues of significance in the situation described above.

My discussion partner was able to give me numerous facts and insights about the general circumstances of the time, many of which have been summarised above. She could not, however, speak to information system issues in the situations of privatised companies. I therefore arranged for an interview with the head of an eastern German IS consulting firm located in a northeastern suburb of eastern Berlin. As a former department of one of the GDR’s large data processing combines, the company was quite experienced in support of the old technology and painfully familiar with the adjustments being made to Western systems. My interview partner from this firm was quite forthcoming with patient explanations and enthusiastic interaction.

After an initial discussion of my research project, my host gave me a detailed run-down on the history of digital processing in the former GDR. My host and I then discussed the current situation of independent enterprises of the former GDR. Of the many topics on which we touched, several factors stood out. First, my host was very clear on the desirability of Western hardware and software for enabling the competitiveness of newly privatised firms. My host pointed out that many firms, in their haste to re-establish automated support, were tending to go ahead and purchase systems before they fully understood their needs or the systems’ capabilities. As a consequence, many such firms were locked into newly purchased systems and desperately in need of help with them. Applications being used at the time were exclusively operational and transactional ones. My host assured me that most of the purchased systems did include higher level functions and integrated process support. They simply were not used. ‘If only they would come with more than just disks and books,’ he remarked. Advice and consulting in the ways of a market economy would be fitting inclusions.

One point that we discussed at some length was sufficient to give me pause. My host explained that the attitude toward the West on the part of many newly privatised firms was decidedly negative. They perceived a very strong ‘quick-sale’ mentality on the part of many Western firms. While they enjoyed the entertaining flood of advertising that was working its way into their society, they didn’t approve of the ‘buyer beware’ ethics that went with it.

My host very graciously helped me to formulate questions for addressing the many issues of interest here. Translation into appropriate German language jargon was a significant part of his kind help. The refined questionnaire that resulted from our session is described in Appendix A. Its structure was used in both the pilot and follow-up interview phases.

Popular culture in Germany at the time of the reunification was constantly poking fun at the differences being observed between eastern and western Germans. The two societies had been forcibly separated for 45 years and naturally had evolved in different ways. I therefore thought it appropriate to seek the input of a Western IS professional, in my background data collection. I arranged for an interview with a representative of a major western German enterprise software company. This gentleman was operating out of Western Berlin offices, while seeking business primarily in the eastern states. He gave me 2 hours of his time, after which I had a deep understanding of the features and desirability of the product he was selling. During discussion of the questionnaire I was formulating, this gentleman wholeheartedly echoed the stereotypical views of the day, regarding the hopeless situations of privatised firms that have not engaged Western participation, such as he was offering.

## Technological discontinuities

The situation described by my interview partners was reminiscent of the ‘technological discontinuity’ Richard Nolan (1984) had elucidated in a follow-up discussion of his stage hypothesis (Nolan, 1979). He defined this to be the ‘divestiture of an ongoing technology to make way for an improved technology.’ Nolan noted that many breakthroughs in information technology constitute drastic leaps in the character of support for IS functions, rather than gradual advancements in existing capabilities. He later espoused the embrace of such discontinuities for ‘tearing down’ familiar paradigms of doing business, to reach new levels of competitiveness (Eliot, 1996). In the end-user computing arena, Huff et al. (1988) had mentioned a ‘Maginot Line’ of technological limitations, hindering a company’s advancement, but capable of being ‘outflanked’ by advancing technology. It appeared to me that eastern German firms had been up against this line and were now being forced to outflank it.

Another conception of ‘technological discontinuity’ has emerged in recent years in the literature of organisational planning and production management. This phenomenon has been defined as ‘technological breakthroughs that either enhance or destroy the competence of firms in an industry’ (Tushman & Anderson, 1986). Technological discontinuities have been cited as drivers in the recurring view that organisational growth involves both calm and crisis (Greiner, 1972); evolution as well as revolution (Tushman & O’Reilly, 1996). In their exposition on re-engineering, Hammer & Champy (1993) called them ‘disruptive technologies’ and strong enablers of drastic and necessary organisational change. Since the Nolan paper of 1984, however, prominent interpretations of this concept in the literature all refer to manufacturing technology (Boyer, 1999; Hatfield et al., 2001), product technology (Eisenhardt and Tabrizi 1995; Cho et al., 1998; Stoelhorst, 2002), or both (Tushman & Anderson, 1986; Edwards, 2000). The primary focus in the present research is on information technology used in support of all enterprise functions. That body of work does elucidate conditions that are relevant here, however, not the least of which is its depiction of the near-vertical slope that leads to some technological goals.

The information technological discontinuity being studied here was similar to the challenge being faced by many firms today, in their efforts to confront the imperatives of emerging information technologies. The changes these emerging technologies can bring to an enterprise are very often discontinuous ones. As pointed out by Day & Schoemaker (2000), emerging technologies can transform an entire industry. They need to be anticipated and embraced. Companies always face the difficult task of appraising emerging information technologies and overcoming obstacles to their successful deployment (Kendall, 1997). An interesting study by Benamati & Lederer (2001) addressed the state of opportunity and challenge brought to many firms by rapid information technology change. That state was certainly encountered by the eastern German firms of the present research. The information technology change could not have been more rapid. The emergence of Western technology was for them instantaneous. I expected to discover that this change was being welcomed as a liberation of sorts, from the limits and dictates of suboptimal technology and systems.

## Company A

1992 site visit Company A had always been an independent construction operation during its days in the GDR. With almost 300 employees, it was quite small for East German enterprises, although it was equipped to handle jobs of any scope, high rise and underground alike. The reunification had rendered it one of the largest construction firms in the greater Berlin area, since the average size of Western construction firms was 50 employees. Privatisation was accomplished through an organised buy-out by 235 of the firm’s workers and was a source of great pride for its entire community. At the time of the reunification, most East Germans had substantial savings from an economy that had offered few opportunities to spend. The generous exchange rates of the currency union had rendered the life savings of these employees sufficient to found a corporation which itself was able to secure financing for the purchase of the firm. Company A’s IS Director told me that the Treuhandanstalt had tried to discourage the privatisation, citing a lack of confidence in management’s ability to make it work.

Until 1990, information systems at Company A had always been rigorous, integrated, and reliable, in the opinion of the IS Director. The firm had used its own mainframe system of GDR manufacture for operational functions and a service bureau for costing, finance, payroll, and other business applications. The service bureau had provided eight-day turnaround on punched paper and magnetic tape. The IS Director described a situation in which all of the basic functions of the company enjoyed automated support and that support always produced what was expected.

In 1990, it all broke down and Company A purchased personal computer equipment and specialised construction software from a commercial vendor. The same vendor also supplied them with ‘compatible’ business applications. Running under MS-DOS, the software’s functions were not well integrated and lacked many of the capabilities to which Company A’s employees had been accustomed. The IS Director allowed that the new hardware represented a decided improvement in convenience, with its on-line access to data. He lamented, however, the ‘step backward’ the systems seemed to force on the firm. The IS Director gave numerous examples of the comparative shortcomings of his Western systems. ‘With no way of factoring management and planning costs into individual job costs, it makes me wonder how our Western competitors stay afloat,’ he remarked. The same computer specialists and administrative users were still on board, as had been with the company before reunification. The IS Director related their growing sense of frustration, however, with the feeling that they had lost all control over system functionality and performance. The IS Director’s only plans for the future were to continue to pay the expensive rates of the company’s software providers, to get these systems working right. Steady revenues of the firm would absorb the costs.

Commentary Company A appears to have enjoyed more comprehensive functionality, a better level of integration, and more reliable IS services before its transition to Western systems. This was my first indication that the prevailing assumptions of the day could do with some mitigation. Indeed, the IS Director said he would gladly have taken his GDR systems back, if that had been possible, notwithstanding the increased technological capabilities of his Western hardware. The switchback trail Company A was following apparently was not justified by the technological summit at its end. The IS Director described many clever manoeuvres of his company in the process of surviving the reunification and competing in its new environment. He was pleased to have an appreciative audience and was unabashed in the critical views he shared with me about its outcomes. Hence, I did not believe that his IS problems were the product of incapable management or insufficient resources, as popular views might have suggested.

## Company B

1992 site visit Company B performed design and project planning for road, bridge, and other transportationrelated building and upgrading. Formerly a part of the 8,000-employee planning bureau of a construction combine, Company B had privatised in 1990 with 350 employees. The CEO did not express any particular problems with the process of privatisation. He had, however, enlisted the minority participation of a western German engineering firm, in order to ameliorate concerns of the Treuhandanstalt over whether or not the venture could succeed. This Western firm was playing no role in Company B’s operations or management, however. At the time of my visit, Company B was down to 250 employees, and was actively engaged in numerous aspects of its industry, as testified by the extensive and attractive glossy brochures I was given.

In a separate interview session with the IS Director, I received an informative reconstruction of the firm’s IS history. Already in 1968, Company B was using construction-oriented technical applications to support materials planning, transport, in-process job tracking, and site control. In early 1969, the firm acquired its own ‘Cellatron’ computer, with 127-word primary storage capacity and a punched paper tape secondary storage unit. There was no software available at the time, so Company B formed its own 10-person programming department. Programs they developed were utilised for many years and formed the bases of later systems developed by the firm. The IS Director described a steady progression of upgrades in computing capacity and functionality that Company B subscribed on a regular basis. In the 1980s, the combine had access to mainframes at a central service location in Magdeburg, primarily for batch processing of business applications. This was implemented via punched paper tape sent through the mail. In 1987, Company B acquired an 8- bit PC to communicate with the service bureau using diskettes and, in 1988, the firm got its first CAD workstation.

When reunification occurred, Company B broke up its programming department, assuming that future software needs could be satisfied by off-the-shelf purchase. These programmers were all civil engineers by training, and were deliberately dispersed among the various corporate divisions to provide IT support and advice in conjunction with their other duties. In general, the IS Director reported neutral experiences with the constructionoriented software available for purchase. The only difference in the Western systems for support of Company B’s engineering work was in the user interface. Organisational changes were being driven by the IT changes at the firm, however. Formerly, design projects had been highly centralised, with specialists in various aspects of a project submitting contributions to a local central system. Now every five engineers shared a standalone PC system that was equipped to cover most aspects of a design project. At the time of my visit, Company B was grappling with a question evinced by this technology: whether or not to de-emphasise specialisation and allow project engineers to cover multiple aspects of their projects.

The CEO and I had a lengthy discussion of the business applications of Company B’s systems. This was the only area of centralised processing left in the firm. Running on a Siemens box with a Xenix platform, purchased 1 year prior to my visit, the firm’s business systems consisted of fairly standard commercial software packages provided by Siemens. Management had resolved to modify their own data needs to fit the software’s ‘preferences.’ Unfortunately, even after one year of help from software support personnel, it was still not right. The CEO complained of numerous frustrations in that connection and stated that the old batch system of the former service bureau had provided ‘much more comprehensive information’ for management of the firm. One example he cited was the capability the company used to have for tracking time spent by employees in conjunction with individual projects. ‘Now that is a thing of the past,’ he remarked.

In a closing session with the CEO and IS Director together, the IS Director noted the problems they were facing with integration and coordination of stand-alone hardware and software platforms. Having recently opened a branch office in another city, Company B was beginning to suffer under the wide availability of different (and incompatible) software packages for support of project functions. Future plans for IT were also discussed at some length with both of my hosts for the day. In the business applications arena, Company B was resolute in its intention to adapt to the software. Management was currently looking into the establishment of an electronic connection with their bank, since cash flow was a very big concern for them. They did not see themselves as far enough along to consider the widespread storage and electronic communication of graphics for business or for engineering support. In general, however, both gentlemen were quite sanguine about the future of IT support for the firm.

Commentary The initial adjustment of Company B to the technological discontinuity of the reunification was not without its difficulties. The advent of Western systems did witness some loss of functionality in Company B’s business applications, as well as integration shortcomings for its design programs. Apparently, switchback routing was a necessary detour in the firm’s technological climb. Several aspects of the situation stand out in viewing the company’s adjustment to the situation. One telling strength of the company is evidently derived from its industry. An engineering firm might be expected to possess a high level of expertise in the use of information technology. Company B’s engineers certainly did not experience any great shock in the initial upgrade of their design systems. On the business side, the company was resolute in its intention to re-engineer its processes, rather than complicate the software implementation process. In fact, Company B even showed a willingness to consider altering its organisation and workflows where IT was surfacing as an enabler. In addition, the principles of end-user computing have enjoyed application in this company, as a method of dealing with the adjustment. These factors seem to reflect the high level of professionalism, with which I was impressed during my visit to Company B. I believe they indicate some viable approaches to addressing technological switchback conditions.

## Company C

1992 site visit Company C was engaged in the design and installation of heating, ventilation, sanitary, and electrical systems – primarily for high-rise apartment buildings and large public facilities. As one of 20 different companies, each with 1,000 employees, in a GDR construction combine, Company C had privatised with 280 of its original people. Functioning as CEO since 1990, my host had become IS Director of the company in 1973, when it had initiated the use of computers. He was in a good position to give me a comprehensive history of the firm’s IT experience and did so.

The combine had utilised a data processing group of 80 people in Leipzig, which developed systems and provided batch processing on a mainframe system there. Company C captured local data on punched paper tape, and submitted them through the mail to Leipzig. System development was conducted in a very organised and rigorous fashion, whereby requirements and enhancements were agreed upon (‘not without compromise,’ the CEO noted) between all 20 firms in the combine. They were ‘implemented perfectly by the people in Leipzig. The resulting ‘IDVS’ (integrated data processing system) functioned ‘perfectly.’ In 1985, office computers became available and the firm hired a programmer to develop software for these machines, since they only came with standard productivity tools. At the end of the decade, the firm was exchanging diskettes with the Leipzig processing centre, which provided complete corporate summary information to support higher level decision making.

When privatisation took place, IS support for company functions was started again from scratch. The firm acquired an IBM AS/400 machine with a 3GB hard drive and 25 workstations. After 6 months, it was too small and Company C launched the first of two expansions that had been necessitated by the time of my visit. Utilising standard packaged AS/400 software for business applications and specialised commercial programs for operations, the firm experienced numerous frustrations in trying to get its systems functioning adequately. The CEO recounted an example issue, in which it took one full year to get the payroll and cost accounting systems integrated. All development and enhancement activities were extremely difficult, since they usually involved so many different vendors. My host was pessimistic about the hopes for ever achieving the full integration he had enjoyed in the days of the GDR systems.

The CEO of Company C did express positive reactions to the changes in information technology that had taken place after the reunification. He was very glad to have all data processing taking place in-house. Data entry errors could be cleared up immediately, rather than necessitating an entirely new batch submission. My host noted that the firm was still quite dependent on large paper lists and looked forward to getting much of this on a screen, instead. He did not see a big future for the storage and electronic communication of graphical design files. ‘We build quite singular systems and every day there are changes to their specifications. It isn’t worth it for us to try to save such graphics,’ he noted. The CEO expressed an intention to wait a few years before making any further drastic changes related to information technology.

Commentary Company C also appears to have suffered under a loss of critical IS functionality, with the reunification. At the time of my visit, it was continuing to experience problems achieving smooth and reliable system performance. Company C also appears to have discovered that the systems planning and design responsibilities, formerly handled reliably at the combine level, are not fulfilled as reliably by Western vendors. Although in-house control of IS issues has benefited the firm, the inability to solve IS problems with internal people has certainly mitigated some of those benefits. Indeed, switchback effects here seem to be manifested as strongly in design and support areas, as in the realm of system functionality. I was very impressed with the hard-working and dedicated atmosphere I experienced at Company C and sensed a patient intention to persevere through the trials of this situation.

## Company D

1992 site visit When I visited Company D, it was in its 104th year as a centralised slaughterhouse and meatpacking plant. In the time of the GDR, the company had

160 employees, including veterinarians, nutritionists, and administrators, and was one of 10 similar firms in a meat-packing combine. Its CEO recounted that the firm had enjoyed a production level of between 600 and 700 hogs per day. It had deals with West German firms, who exported its products, and numerous fixed delivery obligations to eastern bloc countries. When the reunification occurred, ‘everything fell apart. It was the toughest time ever,’ my host related. All the eastern bloc customers of Company D regarded their fixed obligations as invalid. Eastern German consumers and former partner firms immediately switched to buying Western products. Company D had to start marketing its products for the first time. The firm started selling meat right from the factory, from used refrigerated sales trucks purchased in western Germany, and from rented storefronts as well. With no change in the workforce, Company D dropped back to 25% of its previous production levels. The CEO described to me a series of obstacles to all his attempts at keeping the company viable and independent. The Treuhandanstalt did not believe the firm could survive on its own, but, with Western consulting help and a Western acquaintance as a partner, the CEO secured approval for purchase of the firm.

After privatisation, Company D made numerous investments in equipment and infrastructure. It expanded into sausage production, slicing, and beef processing and reached production levels at 70% of those experienced in the GDR days. Its information systems had witnessed a similar progression to others described above, including monthly batch processing with punched paper tape and a combine-central mainframe service centre. The CEO remarked, ‘It was enough, because it had to be. There was no alternative.’ He continued, ‘ybut what kind of business can wait four weeks to find out how much it is producing?’ Facing personnel costs that were 70% of production costs, my host’s primary motivation for acquiring new information systems was to bring down administrative costs. In conjunction with a major purchase of personal computer equipment, the CEO hired a software development firm to create custom software for the firm. By the time of my visit, these systems were running and he was paying the developers 3,000 DM per quarter for maintenance, support and training. In discussion of his decision to contract for custom development, the CEO cited numerous visits from packaged software vendors and added that none of them knew the meat business. The system had resulted in a reduction of administrative personnel from 36 to four. The displaced managers were all dispersed to other departments of the company.

The CEO’s plans at the time of my visit included negotiations with another software firm to get his entire operation computerised, including the introduction of computer-controlled meat processing and integrated computer scales. His intentions included getting rid of most of the paper passing through his hands.

Commentary Company D’s approach to the technological discontinuity appears to have allowed it to avoid the experience of switchback sacrifices. The foresight and resources to outsource development of custom systems certainly enabled an effective dampening of the shock that reunification brought. This not only cushioned the blow of sudden IT replacement, but also was able to drastically reduce administrative costs in a relatively short time. The CEO’s refusal to trust off-the-shelf systems stands out as an approach to the transition, which appears to have brought success. The gentleman impressed me greatly with his entrepreneurial spirit and keen business acumen and seems to have illustrated one way to ascend the technological hill directly.

## Preliminary findings

## Technological switchbacks

The historical reconstructions in this study indicated that, within the scope of their available technology, the MBO firms had all achieved high levels of information systems functionality and integration before the reunification. After privatisation, however, three of these companies appeared to have a difficult struggle on their hands, just to reach basic levels of IS support and integration. Unfortunately, published research and writing in the area of ‘disruptive’ or ‘discontinuous’ technological change do not shed light on this situation. All appear to assume that the ‘scientific innovations’ can only result in advancement and not regression. In the absence of prior definitions for this project’s observed circumstance, the technological switchback notion is introduced. The condition represents an IS transition brought about by an IT advancement and reflects an undeniable technological improvement, but an undesirable decline in the resulting information system’s effectiveness. The IT advancement is clearly rooted in the capabilities of the tools, yet their deployment results in unsatisfied information needs, which previous tools had satisfied. The term switchback is used to connote a forward progress that only seems possible by travelling in the reverse direction. A long-term goal of moving to higher ground may eventually be achieved, but not without some significant backtracking in at least one lateral dimension. In the present study, the IT advancement is measured by concrete performance specifications. The Western hardware was better. The decline in IS effectiveness is inferred from perceptual data, with specific facts and examples to bolster their credibility.

The inability of Company A’s new systems to factor management and planning costs into individual job costs would be a good example of the decline in IS effectiveness. Company B’s need to track employee time in conjunction with individual projects and Company C’s desire to integrate its payroll and cost accounting functions both exemplify these firms’ experience of this phenomenon. The advanced Western technology did not include software that could do everything the old software did. In 1992, these ‘steps backward’ appeared to be more conspicuous in my interviews than the technological improvements that brought them. Indeed, on many steep slopes, one can easily lose sight of the fact that a switchback is actually taking one up the hill.

It might be argued that the switchback conditions of this discussion are no different from the normal compromises made by companies whenever an off-the-shelf system replaces a customised one. The current popularity of enterprise packages for replacement of legacy systems could illustrate that. These moves to new system platforms often confront users with the absence of familiar customised functions. I would extend this paper’s metaphor to call these situations ‘winding paths,’ but not true switchbacks. Modifications to the software, reengineering of processes, and change management strategies all represent slight turns or twists in the climb. They are there to surmount obstacles, but the overall gradient of the climb is not too steep for a direct ascent. Two key conditions stand out in the case evidence here, to distinguish switchbacks from twists. The degree of exigency is the first conspicuous factor. For a switchback the transition is quite mandatory, not much time exists before it must be started, and broad is the scope of its effects on the company facing it. The second distinguishing characteristic entails the degree of negative surprise involved in the transition. With switchbacks, the deficiencies of the new systems have not been fully anticipated and, moreover, they are perceived quite negatively. Clearly, the firms of this study have shown high degrees of both conditions, whereas the processes of normal software acquisition tend to mitigate some aspects of both conditions.

There are other examples of technological switchbacks. The information technology transfer going on in the People’s Republic of China offers one. Recent years have witnessed the increasing acquisition by Chinese industries of Western MRPII capabilities. Attempts to implement this technology have enabled great improvements in the production planning of Chinese firms and are regarded as mandatory measures for corporate survival in China’s transitional economy (He et al., 1998). It is now becoming apparent, however, that some time-honoured approaches to Chinese business practice are not supported by these Western systems and probably should be (Gamble & Gibson, 1999; Standifird & Marshall, 2000). In this case, the Western systems actually hinder the practice of these culture-related methods of doing business. That represents a step backward, brought on by the IT advancement. In fact, the importance of some practices to Chinese users can even cause their firms to turn off the MRPII systems and return to their old ways (Martinsons & Tseng, 1995).

## Coping mechanisms

Benamati & Lederer (2001) identified five ‘coping mechanisms’ commonly employed by IT managers to deal with rapid information technology change. The four pilot firms of the current research have shown evidence of these coping mechanisms in the handling of their transitions. (see Table 2). Education and training in areas of present and future significance was the most frequently used method, according to Benamati and Lederer’s surveys. IT advancement can move in many different directions. Employee awareness of these directions, through formal classes, conferences, and reading, is one way to avoid being unprepared for important breakthroughs. This does not appear to have been sponsored by the firms of this pilot study. Of course limited warning, limited resources and limited availability of educational opportunities in that connection deserve to have curtailed this approach right from the start. The firms’ survival without it is perhaps consistent with the distinction elaborated by Edwards (2000) between ‘competence destroying’ and ‘competence enhancing’ emerging technologies. The previous IT experiences of eastern German firms involved skills that could be leveraged here. On the other hand, the IS-oriented ‘tacit knowledge’ or ‘human asset specificity’ established in these firms over the years did appear to hinder their adjustments to the noncustom systems of Western vendors. Since prior research has shown this idiosyncratic knowledge to have high strategic value to a firm (Nam et al., 1996; Dibbern & Heinzl, 2002) a more rigorous look at technological switchbacks from a transaction cost theory perspective may be fruitful.

Another approach cited by Benamati and Lederer consists of instituting organisational procedures for proactively evaluating, acquiring, and implementing new IT. As an instance of this, Company B dispersed its IT personnel into various departments, to provide advice and help with the new systems. This is an effective way to promote the diffusion of new IT knowledge within a firm. Reliance on vendor support and engagement of consultant assistance are two more ways of fostering adjustment to changing technologies. Benamati and Lederer noted that smaller organisations are less likely to use consultants as coping mechanisms, as with the cashstrapped firms of this study. The vendor support approach, however, shows an interesting effect with the firms of this study. By outsourcing complete custom development, Company D relied totally on vendor guidance for planning and design of that firm’s adjustment to the IT transition. This seemed to obviate the need for switchback reactions by the company at all. Company B relinquished much of its design autonomy, by vowing to adapt its business processes to ‘the preferences of the [vendor supplied] software.’ While that approach did not take place without switchback effects, the firm did succeed in adjusting with fewer complaints being surfaced in my interviews, than I weathered with Companies A and C.

Endurance constitutes the fifth coping mechanism used by the firms of the Benamati and Lederer study and the second most popular one for these firms. Working around problems with new technologies (rather than fixing them), ignoring such problems, and picking up knowledge about the new IT in a strictly informal way are examples of this endurance approach. This was evident in the actions of Companies A–C. In the absence of the resources to sponsor complete custom development (as with Company D), some tolerance of switchback issues will evidently be vindicated. This agrees with a statement of Day & Schoemaker (2000) that ‘lack of persistence’ is a danger firms face when dealing with emerging technologies.

A cross-sectional view of the pilot study’s firms has instantiated three of the Benamati & Lederer (2001) coping mechanisms and evinces three implications about viable ways to address technological switchbacks. The first conclusion has to do with the vision that must be adopted by firms facing a transition like this. Expect to lose ground in certain dimensions in order to progress in the vertical frame. Roll with the switchback effects, knowing that their dimensions can eventually be recovered with persistence and time. The second conclusion relates to outside help. Seek partners in the transfer of technology and knowledge, from among the sources of those commodities. Those on top of the hill can often do well in guiding the selection of routes for the switchback excursion. The final conclusion concerns the process itself. Be sure that the efforts expended in making the transition are shared at all levels within the firm. Pulling a heavy cart along the path of a switchback is much easier, when its wheels can also turn under their own power.

## Follow-up visits and interviews

## Company A

1999 contacts In 1999, I attempted to arrange follow-up visits to each of the companies that had participated in the pilot study. After receiving no response from Company A to an initial letter, I reached a secretary at Company A’s location, in a follow-up phone call. She told me that the firm was currently in receivership and that the former IS Director was no longer available. The secretary was not anxious to discuss the circumstances and made it clear that no one else there would be either.

After an examination of newspaper archives at the regional public library in Potsdam, I contacted the local editor of a regional newspaper in the town that was home to Company A. She had covered the protracted demise of the firm. Somewhat intrigued by my interest, she agreed to meet with me to discuss Company A. The editor gave me photocopies of a sequence of articles from her own and from major Berlin newspapers. They documented a long and painful process of the firm’s going under and appeared to acknowledge a situation of mismanagement, bordering on fraud. This editor was very clear in expressing the paradox she believed was at the root of the situation. Company A and its entire community had been very proud of the firm’s independence from Westerners. It appeared, however, that the timely solicitation of Western financial consulting expertise could have prevented the company’s failure.

Commentary Unfortunately, Company A’s bankruptcy prevented the construction of a longitudinal profile for information system issues in the firm. It is also impossible to say whether information systems were related to the company’s demise. It is more than likely that information system problems were coincident with, and perhaps even reflective of, the firm’s decline and fall. The most salient reason for this conclusion is in the ‘software bashing’ done by the IS Director in 1992. I did not get the impression that he was confiding in me with his opinions. I sensed that his cynicism was freely shared with and encouraged among his employees and colleagues. It is well known that the absence of top management support can doom an IS project to failure. Active slander by top management must certainly hurt its chances for user commitment and effort. This is consistent with the newspaper accounts of a similar resistance shown by top management to the financial and managerial processes of the market economy.

## Company B

1999 site visit My return visit to Company B was very friendly and consisted of a meeting with the IS Director, who recalled my previous visit, and a new CEO. The new CEO was very curious about the research and was happy that I had prepared a run-down on its goals and previous discoveries. He expressed a keen interest in the project’s ultimate results. The IS Director was able to correct and elaborate on many of my notes from my first visit.

The CEO discussed the progress of the firm during the period since my previous visit. As shown in the extensive corporate marketing materials he gave me, Company B had expanded to include branch offices in four other cities of eastern Germany. He explained that total revenues had climbed steadily until 1995, when the universal fixed fee structure for its various services was discontinued. Until that time, fixed fee schedules had enabled reliable corporate planning. Performance expectations were well defined and the fees were sufficient to guarantee support for innovations and quality measures – which represented real costs, but were not billable. The discontinuation of fixed fees in the industry had caused a difficult adjustment, which included downsizing the number of employees to a 1999 level of 180. Site management had now become an area of concentration for the firm, with 40 employees engaged in it and formerly only one or two. ‘This is a good cost cover for us and is easier to bid and bill, because the customer sees us at the construction site and accepts that we are real costs associated with the project. With planning, they think we just push a button and get the plans out of a computer,’ he explained.

Information systems expenses at Company B were currently budgeted at 2.5% of revenues. They were incurred mostly by upgrades and were well worth it, the CEO noted. Company B was still using a Siemens central server, now running under Windows NT, with an open systems architecture and fairly standard business programs from Siemens. The personnel had individual PCs, sharing several network plotters, all fully networked and with ISDN connections between branch offices for fast exchange of graphics. The firm was about to launch a LAN changeover from Novell to NT and a conversion to Windows 98 for all its PCs. The IS Director mentioned that they would handle the change themselves.

Software issues received considerable attention in my discussion with the CEO and IS Director of Company B. Business applications had stabilised considerably since 1992. Although the basic function had become possible in the firm’s systems, it was still not possible to track employee time to the extent that it had been possible with the firm’s old GDR systems. It was now possible to integrate project costing and revenue functions and the CEO was enthusiastic about the capabilities this provided for analysis. With 11 characters of primary and secondary keys now available for cost tracking, they could analyse projects by client, project leader, job type, and many other characteristics. They were currently in the first phase of a corporate outcomes analysis. The CEO showed me various reports containing decision support graphics and matrices of summary data.

The IS Director explained that the variety of design software on the market was continuing to plague the firm. Transferability of data between design programs and with other software was becoming quite problematic. ‘Our customers get CAD packages free from vendors and then require that we provide them with data in those particular formats. We have recently had to purchase a fourth CAD package just for one customer,’ the IS Director remarked. He also discussed the problems he had cited in 1992, regarding the decentralisation of design activities that the stand-alone software had induced. The IS Director related a progression his opinion had gone through on that issue. Initially against the decentralisation, he had come to believe that it was not a bad way to conduct design projects. Now, with integrated and compatible data transfer as a prerequisite vehicle, the IS Director was beginning to think that unifying projects would be a useful thing. He then said,

There were some old practices from the GDR that went out the window immediately, and for good reason. Now that we have been functioning differently, there are some approaches that actually may not have been so bad after all y but we are considering them freshly, on their own merits.

Both of my hosts discussed the firm’s future plans for information systems at Company B. It was intended that the company would eventually enjoy a completely transparent network covering all its operations, to result in one big virtual environment. They were currently dealing with Internet software and service providers and anticipated that the firm would soon have a web site up and running. Most of their governmental clients were not very far along with internal systems, so EDI applications, while increasing, were not yet a major consideration. Company B was, however, fully involved in Internet banking.

Commentary Company B’s information systems have shown an impressive progression from the sudden transition imposed by the reunification. Internal IS management and control have become quite strong. The firm continues to embrace advances in information technology and to realise the potentials they offer. A notable aspect of Company B’s adjustment to the market economy is the strategic thinking it has adopted. This has manifested itself well in the firm’s usage of information systems and appears to provide important lessons for outliving the long-term effects of technological switchbacks.

## Company C

1999 site visit For my 1999 return visit, the CEO of Company C welcomed me very cordially and was quite bemused at my continued interest in the firm. He modestly shrugged off my promise to share with him any eventual outcomes of the project, however, noting that he had never studied English and would not be able to read them. We began our discussion with an update on the company’s progress since our first meeting in 1992. The firm was considerably leaner, having reduced the number of employees to 195 (from 280 in 1992). I was able to reconstruct a rough organisation chart of Company C from the building directory in the lobby. The CEO verified the corporate divisions I had surmised and was a bit amused that I thought he might have a printed organisation chart to give me. ‘They only cost money for the nice paper and pretty diagrams. But what good are they?’ he chided. The only change in the organisation since my previous visit was the discontinuation of a separate information systems department. Its responsibilities and a half-time computer operator were placed in the accounting department, since that was the heaviest user department anyway. The people who had occupied the MIS department were placed in other departments, to act as advisors and trouble-shooters. According to the CEO, they like this and are good at it, but they now have plenty of other duties, too.

In conjunction with the downsizing, Company C had been expanding its revenues by specialising in the kinds of work it did well and for which it had better chances of getting contracts. This was primarily in rebuilding the heating, ventilation, and sanitary systems for the large Soviet-style ‘Plattenbau’ high-rise buildings. The CEO explained that the 30 or so competitors of Company C were mostly small contractors, with 30–50 employees. They could be much more flexible and usually underbid Company C for the smaller jobs.

Information systems at Company C had stabilised since my 1992 visit, but not without effort. The firm still relied on an IBM AS/400 with 25 workstations. The workstations were mostly all intelligent workstations in 1999 and the system had been upgraded in 1996 and again with a new box in 1998. Each upgrade, as well as other software upgrades were guided by IBM personnel and were very expensive. The CEO mused at the 250 DM per hour charges for such support in comparison with the 50 DM per hour billing rate for his own employees. The expenditures were worth it, however, in the view of the CEO.

Company C was still using standard accounting software. The payroll program, which had earlier taken them 1 year to integrate with their cost accounting system, had been provided by a Hamburg software firm. This firm went broke in 1996, the program crashed, and Company C was forced to adopt IBM’s payroll program. The new program represented a step backward in functionality, but the CEO felt that he learned an important lesson regarding the reliance on small software providers. This has happened with specialised operational programs, too, whose functionality IBM systems do not address. Faced with lack of technical support, Company C finds a new vendor and pays the price in time, money, and lost productivity. The CEO mentioned numerous problems with the business systems. Sending workers home for partial days or weeks, when there is no work for them, requires some very complicated accounting and the IBM system cannot do it. Company C has had to modify many things about its workflows, just to accommodate the software. ‘We just change, and then we don’t talk about it any more,’ the CEO commented.

The company now does much better with graphics than in 1992. Designing mostly one-time unique systems, it is able to exchange design graphics and specifications back and forth with clients. The CEO, however, did not see the Internet as much of a factor in the company’s future. ‘We are a regional company. What do we need with the worldwide web? We know our clients, we know our suppliers.’ he said. For the exchange of financial information, Company C had the capability of passive access to its bank account, to check that transactions have occurred. Since he was not confident that its security was 100% reliable, the CEO was not willing to move to active account access. When I asked my host about his long-term goals for information systems, he described a strategy of going along with prevailing trends and said, ‘We will go where we must; otherwise, we will wait and see.’

Commentary Clearly, a persistent, ‘nose to the grindstone’ approach has helped Company C power its way through the difficulties it has encountered since reunification. In fact, the case seems to present an endorsement for the hard-working approach, even in the absence of an anticipatory, proactive planning style. The company’s conservative, reactive address of its business situation appears to have enabled continued survival in a very competitive industry. This should not, though, belie the existence of a strategic vision, however unspoken it may be. Company C has shown the ability to learn its longterm lessons. Strategic focus in the business it seeks is a corporate illustration of this. Information system issues have also participated in the organisational learning process. Company C has learned to sacrifice specialty functionality, in favour of stable system support from vendors who are viable in the long term. It has shown a willingness to alter its organisational structure and roll with new technology, even though these adaptations are resisted until absolutely necessary. It is tempting to speculate about how much better things might be, if Company C made greater efforts to assume an ideal Western business profile. Unfortunately, MBA-inspired organisation charts and marketing-driven image building do consume time and money. It is far from clear, in the evidence of this case, that such resource allocations would ever have allowed Company C to survive the reunification at all.

## Follow-up phase discoveries

## IS implementation

A cross-sectional analysis of all four pilot study firms was indeed able to characterise their common plight in a credible way. It also led to some conclusions about surviving the initial shock of this plight’s imposition. Initial survival, however, is not equivalent to outliving the technological switchback experienced by these firms. Success in their efforts to overcome this predicament is more easily assessed with a longitudinal view of the firms and their progress therewith. Other researchers of technology transitions have also explicated the limitations of static ‘snapshot’ views in this regard (Boyer, 1999; Kangas, 1999). They echo the assertion made by Iivari (1985), that implementation success is a continuum, not a discrete event. Only two of the study’s firms were available for follow-up research in that regard. Moreover, that was limited to one-day site visits. However, the historical reconstructions and rich insights gained from these companies’ informants have nevertheless provided some interesting grounds for a continuum view of the technological switchback phenomenon.

This technological switchback ‘life cycle’ reflects an apparent paradox that has captured the interest of numerous researchers: information systems of high technological quality, whose effective deployment has nevertheless been fraught with difficulties. Many of these authors were inclined to assume that the technical quality of a system is an absolute attribute, regardless of the organisational context in which the system is to be used. Ein-Dor & Segev (1978) observed the emphasis in this work on physical realisation of a system – an emphasis that was later described as ‘system delivery’ (Coe, 1996). The ‘West is best’ mentality mentioned earlier did appear to render system delivery as the order of the day. Research in systems implementation, however, had already accepted that this simplistic view is insufficient to guide or assess implementation efforts. A paper of Kwon & Zmud (1987) gave a summary of much of this work in implementation success factors, user-designer interactions, social change processes, behavioural motivations, and implementation risk factors. A growing body of intensive research had also elucidated discoveries observed in the conduct of real-world implementation projects. Since then, research in systems implementation has manifested itself in a congeries of studies that focus on factors, process, cognitions, system types, failures, and other specialised considerations (for a survey of this literature, see Marble, 2000). Taken together, these works provide a wide spectrum of issues that have relevance in the situation under study here.

A comprehensive and flexible model for characterising these issues came from Swanson (1988). Called the ‘implementation puzzle,’ this model is a collection of nine factors critical to the success or failure of information system implementation efforts. Swanson called them management commitment, value basis, performance level, user involvement, resource adequacy, mutual understanding, design quality, project management, and situational stability (Swanson, 1988). He asserted that IS implementation success depends on a fit among these factors, noting that ideal relationships among the factors are neither constant nor unique. Swanson’s use of the puzzle characterisation was intended to reflect the fact that different situational characteristics might imply the special relevance of different factor subsets and different relationships among their elements. Iivari & Ervasti (1994) embraced the scope of this model, noting its particular relevance in light of the current trend toward software acquisition, rather than in-house custom development. Swanson’s model has been shown to enable comprehensive analysis of system implementation projects (Marble, 2003). It is used here to frame the discussion that follows.

As shown in Table 3a, Companies B and C provide an immediate corroboration for the important role of three of Swanson’s implementation factors. They have both exhibited a strong experience of top management commitment to their new systems, an explicitly verified recognition of the high value these systems provide, and a consistent practice of upgrade to the best technology available. Throughout the years encompassed by this study, the surviving firms have shown that the objectives of these three priorities are (at least) consistent with the apparent success of their system implementation pursuits. In Swanson’s view, the management commitment and value basis factors represent direct reflections of company policies and strong drivers of employee attitudes toward the implemented systems. Performance level is distinguished, in Swanson’s model, from the quality of system design features and, in this situation, significantly reflects the IT advancement that implemented systems embody.

An interesting relationship is apparent between two further factors of Swanson’s model in the surviving firms of this study. The user involvement factor describes the extent of user participation in the decisions that drive an implementation project. Resource adequacy refers to the quality and quantity of resources devoted to the project. No evidence suggests that users played a significant role in the acquisition decisions made at the time of reunification. Further, abundant evidence indicates that time and money were critically short. While this shows a low rating for both factors in the implementation efforts of the firms (as recorded in Table 3a), it also illustrates a trade off between factors, which Tait & Vessey (1988) discovered. They found that increased user involvement can actually be a detriment to an already difficult process. As stated in their executive summary, ‘if necessary resources are not available and there is no choice but to proceed, a highly dictatorial nonparticipative development process may be the best way to proceed’ (Tait & Vessey, 1988, p. 90). This seems to describe the situation faced by the firms of this study and appears to validate the sacrifices made in one of Swanson’s factors, due to shortage in another.

## Entry clock variables

Information from the follow-up interviews also allows a dynamic look at the implementation process. In fact, it provides distinctions between Companies B and C, in terms of the remaining four implementation factors of Swanson’s framework. Table 3b shows observations that instantiate these factors, for which each of Companies B and C registered a status. In three categories, Companies B and C are distinguished from each other by the different years when each company achieved its standing. This utilises the ‘entry clock’ measure of Hatfield et al. (2001), who demonstrated the validity of similar temporal variables in predicting corporate survival. The remaining Swanson category and an overall effectiveness category are measured with entry clock values assigned according to my own observations. It is the binary distinction between Companies B and C, which they are all intended to represent.

The design quality concept of Swanson’s model refers to the general characteristics of requirements satisfaction exhibited by a system. It is represented here negatively, by the time when each firm began to compromise on its information requirements, in order to foster a smoother adjustment to the new systems. The situational stability factor accounts for the degree to which the implementation-driven disruptions experienced by system stakeholders hinder the implementation process itself. Here, situational stability is measured by the time when each firm allowed its hardware vendor to become its primary business software provider, too. Although this involved settling for more generic software functionality (the sacrifice in design quality mentioned above), it led to a more stable vendor management situation. The project management factor addresses the implementation process itself and its responsiveness to stakeholders. The status of this study’s firms in that respect is measured by the time when I observed a high degree of internal control over the process, shown by their significant independence from the aid of vendors. Mutual understanding between users and implementers represents the degree of interaction that takes place during the implementation process. It is measured here by the time when each firm dispersed its specialists among the organisational subunits, to serve as advisors and trouble-shooters. The final entry of Table 3b utilises the time when I determined a firm had adopted high-level decision support functionality. This is used as a surrogate measure of implementation effectiveness. In his application portfolio benchmark, Nolan (1979) had cited this as an indicator of system maturity. Other researchers have also found the degree of highlevel planning support to reliably indicate information system performance. (See, for instance, Cragg & King, 1992 or Hatfield et al., 2001.)

With the factor distinctions represented in Table 3b and their consistency with my inferred distinction in overall effectiveness, we can extend the inferences drawn earlier from the pilot study discoveries.

Endurance The endurance coping mechanism was embodied here by a firm’s willingness to ‘adjust to the preferences of the software,’ in accepting, rather than fighting some switchback effects. Items 6 and 7 of Table 3b imply that sooner is better for this. Evidently, switchback conditions can last for a long time. Company C eventually gave in to the sacrifice of design quality in favour of situational stability. Company B was resolved to it from the start, however, and seems to be better off because of it.

Vendor support Reliance on vendor guidance showed itself to be an important means of surviving the initial shock of a technological switchback. Item 8’s implication qualifies this in an important way. Apparently, firms should avoid letting early vendor guidance parlay itself into inordinate dependence on vendors for management of the continuing process of implementation.

Internal procedures Case evidence indicates that both companies dispersed their IS specialists among user departments because of the decreased need for their dedicated development and support activities. Nevertheless, it is likely that this deliberate promotion of enduser awareness is associated with implementation success. Table 3b’s item 9 reinforces that pilot study inference and extends its apparent validity beyond the beginning period of a technological switchback.

## Conclusions

A return to the original questions that drove this project’s inquiries will highlight some interesting contributions of the project’s discoveries. Concerning the IT advancements and the IS transitions under study: Are they working? What problems are being faced? Does this circumstance reinforce or supplement existing theories in the field? Are there any lessons that bear repeating? To begin, the outcomes and difficulties reported here are certainly not as had been anticipated. In fact, the first two questions yielded discoveries that have never before surfaced in the literature. The third question has brought a particularised application of system implementation theory, characterised by the technological switchback notion itself. The fourth question has produced some short- and long-term approaches to switchback conditions, which might well be quite useful to firms facing similar situations.

In many respects, the circumstances and effects that I observed could not be separated from the overall context of reunification acclimatisation. Hence, it will take further research to verify that the discoveries and deductions elucidated here qualify for generalisation. The paper’s tabulations of concepts and observations are intended to organise my impressions into credible proposals. The case descriptions offer a chance for those impressions to be tested by the reader’s own interpretations. It is hoped that readers’ interpretations will then lead to the asking (and answering) of many questions about the technological switchback phenomenon.

The switchback conditions revealed in this research are observable to some degree in other situations involving technological transition. Of course, the circumstances surrounding information technology transfer in transitional economies provide a natural analogy to the predicaments faced by the firms of this study. La Rovere (1997) has enumerated several aspects of this analogy and stated that lessons can be learned from the eastern German experience for technology diffusion in developing countries. It is also possible that switchback conditions confront well-established companies, with longstanding histories of state-of-the-art information technology usage. The ‘technological imperative’ of electronic commerce serves as a vivid illustration of this. Many suppliers have been drawn, for example, into electronic tendering relationships with manufacturer consumers of their goods. While competitive forces have dictated that this technological innovation be embraced and relied upon, a discernible degree of regression has resulted, from the conduct of business relations which was formerly possible. Coltman et al. (2002) have presented a sobering review of such scenarios in the outcomes of electronic business pursuit. Premature breakdown of legacy systems might also show a sufficient degree of exigency and unforeseen woe to invoke the switchback designation.

The first step then for addressing a similar phenomenon in a practical setting is to determine if the situation can really be called a technological switchback. The twodimensional framework discussed earlier can be used to evaluate the situation. The circumstantial dimension of the framework, degree of exigency, reflects external stimuli or forces over which the organisation has little control. Its three components offer three simple questions about the IT advancement being faced. Is it imperative? Must it be embraced immediately? Will its effects be broadly felt across the organisation? The firms of this study experienced the strong external impact that a change in the economic system can have in this respect. The information technology changes were mandatory, immediate, and quite comprehensive. Only one consideration might soften that conclusion. Evidence from the study does not indicate that its firms had any more time available than was actually spent on system selection and system introduction efforts. It might be the case, however, that those procedures were conducted rashly and that these firms really could have taken more time for acquisition decisions. This would mean that the exigency factor might have been controlled internally and the switchback conditions might possibly have been avoided. Continuing issues with system functionality still being lamented 10 years later have led me to rule out that possible scenario. (Had there been a solution available in the beginning, it certainly would have been found by now.) Nevertheless, explicit details about the system selection process are missing from this study’s data and should be included in future investigations of technological switchback phenomena.

The behavioural dimension of the technological switchback framework, degree of negative surprise, reflects the expectations and the reactions of information system stakeholders. Switchback conditions witness a pronounced disparity between high expectations preceding the change and negative experiences resulting from it. It is this disparity in perceptions that measures how drastic the IS backtracking is, which is necessary to negotiate the IT transition. If the transition’s required sacrifice of system functionality happens to be expected or planned, this measure would not yield extremely high values. Its functionality detour might then be called a twist in the path, rather than a true switchback. It is fair to say that the firms of this study were taken by surprise when the ‘West is best’ assumption was so drastically contradicted by their acquired systems. Their expectations were high and their experiences were negative.

Suppose that in some practical setting, circumstantial and behavioural factors lead to the inference of technological switchback conditions. The consequent actions espoused in this paper then represent a specialised application of well-accepted principles from system implementation theory.
