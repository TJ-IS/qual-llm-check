# A model of electronic commerce adoption by small voluntary organizations

Nancy MacKay<sup>1</sup>, Michael Parent<sup>1</sup> and Andrew Gemino<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Faculty of Business Administration, Simon Fraser University, Burnaby, BC, Canada

## Abstract

The study discussed in this paper uses case studies of six small voluntary organizations to determine the primary factors that influence electronic commerce (EC) adoption. The findings indicate that perceived benefits, including efficiency, information sharing, marketing and increased donations have a strong influence on EC adoption. The results also indicate that organizational readiness, including ability to attract volunteers and/or in-kind donations, ability to raise funds, and strategic readiness had a positive influence on EC adoption. Consistent with prior research on EC adoption in the commercial sector, strong support was found for the influence of perceived pressure, including pressure from both internal and external stakeholders. The study also concluded that perceived social risks associated with the loss of human contact and the perception of dollars being diverted from client programs to finance the EC investment had a strong influence on EC adoption. Implications for EC adoption and future research on EC adoption in the voluntary sector are discussed. European Journal of Information Systems (2004) 13, 147–159.

## Introduction

Voluntary organizations are increasingly important to the economic and social well-being of most Western economies (Drucker, 1994; Putnam, 1995; Salamon & Anheier, 1996; Hall & Reed, 1998). For example, in the UK, the voluntary sector is comprised of more than 185,000 charities with a total annual income exceeding d26 billion (Charity Commission for England and Wales, 2002). Registered charities generate over 563,000 paid jobs representing 2.2% of the UK labour force, which is more than the post and telecommunications industry (National Council for Voluntary Organisations, 2002). It is estimated that the activity of volunteers contributed the equivalent of d15.4 billion to the sector.

In Canada, the voluntary sector is comprised of 58,000 employers generating wages and salaries amounting to \$22 billion per year. This represents 8.3% of all paid jobs in the country and 6.6% of the nation’s payroll. The size of Canada’s voluntary sector payroll compares with the country’s construction, mining and oil and gas industries. The sector is characterized by small employers with 53.4% having less than five employees, and participates in activities that range from cultural and recreation, through health, education and social services, to residential construction, manufacturing, retail trade, professional services and finance. The sector’s 900,000 employees are heavily composed of women, which represent 75% of all paid employees. As well, 30% of paid employees in this sector hold university degrees (McMullen & Schellenberg, 2002).

Voluntary organizations are experiencing significant shifts within the social, economic, and political spheres in which they operate (Burt & Taylor, 2000). Competition for funding and volunteers is becoming more acute as new organizations enter the marketplace and as changes occur in the nature of funding available and patterns of volunteering (Ryan, 1999). As the respective roles of government, the private sector and the voluntary sector continue to evolve, the relative size of the voluntary sector and its economic significance is expected to increase (VolNet, 1999). Therefore, voluntary organizations are not without economic significance.

Voluntary organizations are keenly aware of the significant impact that Electronic Commerce (EC) is having on the voluntary sector in many developed countries (Pargmegiani & Sachdeva, 2000). According to Saxton & Game (2001), EC has the potential to revolutionize almost every aspect of the work of voluntary organizations. Over the past 5 years, the adoption of EC in voluntary organizations has increased greatly (Pargmegiani & Sachdeva, 2000). The results of a UK study on voluntary organizations already using EC show that the voluntary sector has much to gain from EC (Saxton & Game, 2001). For example, adopting EC can help further their missions by developing new partnerships with like-minded organizations in other parts of the country and the world. They can reach more stakeholders, who directly support or benefit from their work. Over time, organizations can also support their work by reducing their operating costs and generating additional revenues. However, while some voluntary organizations have taken advantage of the benefits of EC, the majority of non-profit organizations have not (VolNet, 1999). Daniel et al. (2002) found that the voluntary sector was among the industry sectors with the lowest levels of EC adoption in the UK. Many voluntary organizations do not perceive a need to integrate these technologies into their ongoing activities because of cost, time and a lack of understanding of the full potential of EC and its strategic importance to their activities. As a result, the impact of EC in the voluntary sector is much lower than its potential (Saxton & Game, 2001).

In this study, EC adoption is defined as the establishment of a company website to share information, maintain relationships and conduct transactions using electronic networks (Zwass, 1996). This paper examines the factors that influence early stages (Nambisan & Wang, 1999) of EC adoption (i.e., website adoption) by small voluntary organizations. Recognizing that small voluntary organizations represent a distinct group, this study extends research in the following areas: (1) EC adoption; (2) IT in small and medium sized enterprises (SMEs); and (3) IT in the voluntary sector. Clearly, many factors influence EC adoption by SMEs (Premkumar & Roberts, 1999; Poon, 2000; Walczuch et al., 2000; Mehrtens et al.,

2001). The majority of these studies have investigated EC adoption in profit-based SMEs. Since small voluntary organizations have unique IT needs compared with profit-based SMEs, there is a need to investigate the factors influencing EC adoption in the voluntary sector.

## Prior research on IT and EC adoption in the voluntary sector

Despite the growing importance of the voluntary sector, research on this sector lags well behind that of the profitbased or commercial sector in virtually all countries and in all fields, including IT (Hall & Banting, 2002). Very few studies have investigated the role of IT in the voluntary sector (Boyle et al., 1993; Morgan, 1995; Madon, 1999). These studies highlight the fact that voluntary organizations have distinctive and unique IT needs compared with firms in the commercial sector. With respect to strategic focus, voluntary organizations are concerned with meeting a social need rather than the pursuit of profit from the sale of goods or the provision of service on a profit-making basis. Thus, much of the work of charities is not about phvsical products or services but about ideas and information (Saxton & Game, 2001). Their use of IT would not entail a commercial benefit, but would be aimed at improving the organization’s ability to share ideas and information to meet a social need. More specifically, voluntary organizations aim to change people’s attitudes, raise money and awareness of issues, cut costs, spread ideas about better ways to do things, provide people with information, and build their image and brand. According to Boyle et al. (1993), voluntary organizations use IT as a tool for cooperation rather than competition. Regarding IT resources, the National Council for Voluntary Organizations in the UK reported that one of the most important shortages of skills in the sector was the strategic use of IT (National Council for Voluntary Organisations, 2002). Non-profit organizations usually have no in-house IT professionals and extremely low budgets. The lack of IT professionals means that non-IT professionals will normally be the implementers. Many of the implementers will be volunteers with no contractual obligation to the organization and their time may be very limited (Morgan, 1995). As a result, the use of IT in this sector is often haphazard, poorly planned, and inadequately related to the needs of the organization (Morgan, 1995). In addition, a strong reliance on external IT support (e.g., volunteers, consultants) is common in this sector.

Since a majority of voluntary organizations are small firms (Charity Commission for England and Wales, 2002), it is not surprising that voluntary organizations struggle with many of the same IT resource challenges faced by SMEs. Igbaria et al. (1997) reported that SMEs face substantial risks and problems with IT: they have a general lack of computer knowledge, have inadequate hardware and software, need to rely on outside resources, experience a lack of financial resources and technical support, and have recruitment difficulties. With respect to EC challenges faced by SMEs, several studies have identified factors influencing EC adoption in SMEs including business size, IT expertise, IT resources, financial resources and external pressure (Premkumar & Roberts, 1999; Poon, 2000; Walczuch et al., 2000). More recently, Mehrtens et al. (2001) identified lack of perceived benefits, external pressure, and lack of organizational readiness including IT resources as factors influencing EC adoption in SMEs. Daniel et al. (2002) developed a stage model of EC adoption by SMEs, including four distinct stages through which SMEs evolve during the adoption of EC.

According to Saxton and Game (2001), there is a lack of research on EC in the voluntary sector. Although small voluntary organizations experience many of the same IT and EC resource challenges faced by profit-based SMEs, they have unique computing needs due to their cooperative strategic focus and strong reliance on voluntary resources (Boyle et al., 1993; Morgan, 1995; Madon, 1999). This implies that the research findings based on EC adoption by SMEs cannot necessarily be generalized to small voluntary organizations. Therefore, there is a need to investigate the implications of the significant differences that exist between voluntary and profit-based SMEs on EC. In addition, a clear need exists for a model that could be used to explain the factors that influence EC adoption in the voluntary sector. The research findings could identify the various challenges that have limited the adoption and potential benefits of EC in the voluntary sector.

The major purpose of this study was to identify factors that influence EC adoption in small voluntary organizations. An examination of prior research on IT in the voluntary sector and EC adoption in SMEs reveals that the Iacovou et al. (1995) small firm Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) adoption model incorporates many of the factors previously demonstrated to be significant predictors of Internet adoption in profit-based SMEs. This model was developed using case studies and identifies three broad factors affecting EDI adoption by SMEs: perceived benefits, organizational readiness, and external pressure. Mehrtens et al. (2001) developed a model of Internet adoption by SMEs that was similar in form to the EDI adoption model. They defined perceived benefits in terms of efficiency benefits, effective information gathering, and a business tool to build the firm’s image; organizational readiness in terms of the level of IT knowledge among non-IT professionals; and external pressure as arising from customers, suppliers, and potential employees for the firm to be an Internet user, and to communicate electronically.

Based on a review of the literature, and in order to place our study in a framework that could usefully be compared with the commercial sector, we based our study on the small firm Internet adoption model (Mehrtens et al., 2001). To our knowledge, there was no such study conducted to examine the applicability of this model in the voluntary sector. The present study sought to extend previous research by adapting the model where necessary to describe the differences in the voluntary sector. Thus, the main objective of the study was to extend the Mehrtens et al. (2001) small firm Internet adoption model by identifying and incorporating the factors influencing website adoption in the voluntary sector.

## Methodology

The goal of this exploratory research was to determine if a previously validated model of Internet adoption by small firms (Mehrtens et al., 2001) also applied to small voluntary organizations. The unit of analysis was the organization. The definition of a small organization in the voluntary sector for the current research was an independent organization with 100 or less employees. Although there are a wide variety of organizations in the voluntary sector, there is no agreed upon international classification system defining the sector. The following parameters described the general type of voluntary organizations considered in this research: (1) organizational (some form of structure); (2) not part of government; (3) profits are reinvested in the organization; (4) self-governing usually through a board of directors; (5) a registered charity and (6) pursues some public good (Salamon & Anheier, 1996). Typical non-profits tend to help the disadvantaged, provide social services, and support socially beneficial programs.

As little research has been done in this area, and as the phenomenon under consideration (voluntary organizations) was relatively unexplored and undefined, the case study approach was chosen (Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 1994). Bonoma (1985) suggests that qualitative research in the form of a case study should be considered when the phenomena under study meet the conditions of little technical knowledge and high complexity. Certainly, this is the case for Internet adoption by small voluntary organizations. As Bonoma (1985, p. 202) suggests, such phenomena ‘yare so complex it is impossible at this stage of theory development to know what to count.’

Mehrtens et al. (2001), in their multiple case study of SME Internet adoption, indicated their goal was to provide a description of the reasons for adoption that might form a basis for a predictive model. Our study is consistent with these aims, save its domain of focus was on small voluntary organizations. Theory is being extended, in this study by determining the Mehrtens et al. (2001) model’s relevance to understanding Internet adoption by small voluntary organizations, and whether or not additional factors are warranted.

The sample frame for this study consisted of small voluntary organizations in Vancouver, Canada. Data were collected from six small voluntary organizations drawn from a list on the Canadian Centre for Philanthropy (2002) website (http://www.ccp.ca/information/links.html). In all cases, participants were promised confidentiality in that results would not allow the identification of their organizations, employees, or volunteers. Of the six small voluntary organizations that agreed to participate, two were international in scope, and four were local. For more detailed descriptions of the organizations, see Appendix A.

Case studies may be used to generate, describe, or test theory (Yin, 1994). In the latter case, Yin (1994) suggests a single-stage approach designed to produce literal replication. Theory is being tested in this study, in that the Mehrtens et al. (2001) model is being tested in the context of small voluntary organizations. This study’s focus on six small voluntary organizations is well within the range of four to 10 cases recommended by Eisenhardt (1989) and Markus (2000). Within-case analyses were conducted, followed by cross-case analyses until such time as theoretical saturation was reached (Glaser & Strauss, 1967; Yin, 1994).

Data were collected primarily through in-depth interviews with key decision-makers in each organization (see Appendix B for the interview guide). A semistructured interview guide was developed based on the preliminary EC adoption model for small voluntary organizations (see Figure 1). Interviews were recorded and then transcribed. Two researchers conducted each interview, took notes, and reconciled their notes afterwards in order to ensure as accurate and as complete a record as possible. To ensure triangulation of findings, archived records (including websites), and public documents from these organizations were reviewed.

The transcripts were examined, and first coded according to the research model presented. The researcher examined the transcripts using a scheme adapted from Trauth & Jessup (2000) whereby the coding scheme was also emergent, and new codes extending or modifying the research model were included on a case-by-case basis as they arose. The final coding scheme including all new codes was then reapplied to all cases (cross-case analysis), and a cross-tabulation of themes as they related to the research model’s main constructs was developed.

## The six small voluntary case organizations

The six small voluntary organizations participating in this research had all created websites and adopted the Internet in their organizations in the past 5 years. The organizations included in the study are representative of voluntary organizations from a variety of sectors including health, environment, education, arts, culture and disability. This is representative of the sectors in which voluntary organizations participate with the distribution of organizations making up the voluntary sector in Canada as follows: religious (26.0%), civic and social (16.8%), social assistance (12.6%), business, professional and labour associations (10.2%), arts, entertainment and recreation (5.9%), health (4.2%), professional, scientific and management services (4.0), education (3.8%), social advocacy (3.4%) and the remaining 13.1% participating in 13 other sectors of the economy (McMullen & Schellenberg, 2002).

The organizations ranged in size from 28 employees and five volunteers (unpaid staff members) to 100 employees and 300 volunteers. Operating budgets ranged from \$250,000 to over \$5 million. While micro-sized establishments (less than five employees) are prevalent in the non-profit sector (53.4%), about half of employees work in establishments with more than 50 employees (McMullen & Schellenberg, 2002).

## A preliminary EC adoption model for small voluntary organizations

By combining empirical findings from various areas in the literature, it was possible to build a preliminary model showing factors influencing EC adoption in small voluntary organizations. The specific form of the model was derived from recommendations by Iacovou et al. (1995), and Mehrtens et al. (2001). In a study of small firm adoption of EDI, Iacovou et al. (1995) suggested that the level of external pressure, perceived benefits, and organizational readiness significantly influence the level of EDI adoption in small firms. Mehrtens et al. (2001) developed a model of Internet adoption by SMEs that was similar in form to the EDI adoption model. Both within-case and cross-case analyses showed that the form of the preliminary EC adoption model for small voluntary organizations could be borrowed from the small firm Internet adoption model (Mehrtens et al., 2001). The key adoption factors were therefore named: perceived benefits, organizational readiness, and perceived pressure (see Figure 1). One factor that was not included in the Mehrtens et al. (2001) model was that of perceived social risk. This new factor emerged from the case study data analysis. Each of these factors is discussed in the following sections.

## Perceived benefits

The main benefits were expressed in terms of: efficiency, information sharing, marketing and revenue generation and/or increased donations.

Efficiency On the cost savings side, all six organizations expected operational efficiencies to flow from their websites in terms of reducing the amount of calls they received (all of which required staff to handle) and in eliminating the need for production and distribution of marketing materials (e.g., pamphlets, brochures, and newsletters). Websites were seen as an important tool for the purpose of saving on administrative overhead and direct marketing costs.

J, from Organization B put it succinctly, ‘Well, because it’s the most inexpensive, most effective tool to reach a national or international audienceyit’s cost-effective. This was repeated by colleague D, who humorously said:

ypeople use our site for research for all kinds of projectsyit’s a very valuable tool for putting out our information. [Before] you had to print documents and then drop them from low flying planes, I mean, it’s hard to do, expensive, and a waste of resources, I mean, printing a brochure or a report, I mean right now, like, we have, we do reports regularly, by scientists and stuff like that, right, now whenever we do a report, we immediately put it up on the website in PDF formaty A counterpart at Organization A agreed: yit’s cheaper in the long run than printing brochures and sending them outy.

Organization E was most enthusiastic about cost savings from using the company website as its clients (i.e., recipients of services) are from other countries. As one manager put it:

We get a lot of phone calls from other organizations who want to know about us, and sometimes they have checked the websiteyit saves a bit of work, because then you don’t have to start from the beginning. The other thing it does, is a lot of people contact us through the mail and through email from different countries, like I get maybe five or six people a month from New Zealand, India, Japan and Korea.

All six case studies keenly felt the pressure to use raised or donated funds wisely. A persistent problem with charities is administrative overhead. Donors typically want to know that donations will nearly all be spent on programs and on clients. Donors do not want to support bloated bureaucracies. This tug-and-pull between wanting a website to increase administrative efficiencies, and the negative aspect of having to spend money on it was best expressed by an informant in organization B, when assessing their website content and layout decisions:

Well, you can look too polished and slick, and have too many fancy gizmos and animation features. We wanted it basic; we wanted it easy to navigate and simple to read. As with any non-profit organization, we have a responsibility to spend money carefully. If we were to produce a brochure, it’s unlikely to be four-colour, high-gloss, you know, the best stock you can get. We’re going to be polished and professional, we’re going to be articulate, and really think about what our audience wants to know, but we’re not going to overspend the money.

Information Sharing The most important benefit of the Internet to these organizations was as a communications tool. All of the organizations mentioned communication with program clients, the media, and potential donors as the main benefit of their websites. Organizations emphasized the importance of their websites as sources of information for the media, and emphasized the importance of the website in providing timely, accurate and complete background information to media representatives.

Colleague J stated:

I see it as an organic tool; we can update it in a heartbeat, and that’s invaluable, particularly when we have new announcements of money, when we have a new program that we want to talk about, when we want to report on the progress of the campaign as well as promoting what’s going on with [our] campaign.

One of the cultural agencies concurred. Organization C liked the immediacy of the website: ‘I’m hoping that communication with members is more instantaneous, because there’s so many things that happen that if you do a newsletter only three times a year, it loses its immediacy, it’s old news almost by the time you put it out, so I think that that communication will assis’t us in keeping and retaining membershipy’.

Interestingly, except for organization A, all participants used the Internet to communicate passively, not actively. The websites consisted mostly of brochureware, and were not interactive. Interested parties had to actively retrieve the information. None of the organizations mentioned using their Internet presence actively, either by providing for interactivity on the websites, or by aggressively marketing their websites to search engines, portals, and other media that might drive people to their websites.

Marketing In all six cases, the company website was seen as a marketing tool allowing these non-profit organizations to attract new clients, to reach and respond to existing clients more effectively, and to identify new potential clients.

As M, from organization A stated: ‘What do we want from the web? I think we really want it to be an easy place for people to get information, we want it to be the place where they can interact with the organizationyI see it as somewhere that actually succeeds in extending the brandy’

Two organizations (A and B) are closely linked to, and named for their founders. Managers at these organizations expressed cautious optimism at the power of the web to leverage the individual founder’s name recognition and equity. D at Organization A was cautious:

You have to manage expectations. I mean, people have known [founder] for 30 years through [popular television program], and they’ve probably seen him speak once or twice, and they feel they are a friend of [founder], so if they have a problem, then you can think, like, oh, [founder] will come in and speak or something and fix it.

M at organization B echoed this sentiment ‘I see it [website] y really enforces who we are and what we’re doing and has [founder’s name] written all over it, you know, in every way it [website] has the same level of integrity that the brand doesy’.

Donations and revenue generation All six organizations expressed interest in collecting donations or program revenue through the company website.

For example, Organization F stated that revenue-generation and traffic were priorities: ‘I think we’re successful so far because we’ve increased the trafficyand I guess I just don’t see it as a cost-saver so much as a way of providing our services so that it generates additional revenues.’ In their case, the website was the ideal medium to reach a majority of clients who could not use more traditional means like face-to-face or the telephone to access this agency’s services.

This raises an important point: while all organizations expressed delight at the uptake, or ‘hits’ on their websites, without fail, none formally tracked success. Not one had established performance measures related to revenue generation or increased donations a priori. Not even in terms of measuring costs savings (e.g., if administrative and marketing collateral cost x prior to developing the website, it should now cost a fraction of x), despite expressing either the desire to do so, or an interest in monitoring performance.

For example, in organization A, ‘I think an important measure of success obviously is you are getting donations through your webyI think the number of visitors to the site is also importantyIt would be wonderful to get that feedbackyWe haven’t specifically yet.’, and organization F ‘If the traffic justifies itythen it’s easy to say, yes, we spent this much money on the websiteyIf the revenues aren’t there, then you’re saying, you’ve spent \$10,000 on this website, and we’ve had eight hits yesterday. Wow! Uh oh!’. The most direct response to the question of whether or not they had put performance measures in came from D: ‘I’m embarrassed to say we didn’t, and we haven’t.’

The above evidence provided strong support for including perceived benefits as a factor in the preliminary model of EC adoption by small voluntary organizations. The Mehrtens et al. (2001) model defines three categories of Perceived Benefits: relative advantage, communication, and the Internet as a business tool. Although all three categories were applicable to small voluntary organizations, some refinement to term definitions was necessary. In addition, a fourth category of revenue generation and/or increased donations was added. Table 3 shows these revised and adapted term definitions.

## Organizational readiness

The Mehrtens et al. (2001) model identifies two aspects of organizational readiness expressed in terms of the IT readiness of the firm: the level of Internet knowledge among non-IT professionals, and the level of computer systems in the organization. If these criteria were to be used, then we would conclude that none of the six organizations in our study were ready for EC adoption. Nonetheless, all six developed cogent websites, and even evolved them one or two generations. All six organizations were quite ready to develop websites when the opportunity to do so (i.e., mostly in terms of funding or volunteers) presented itself. These results are not surprising given that Iacovou et al. (1995) found that unprepared organizations seemed to become adopters of EDI. The results of this study suggest that the organizational readiness factors established in the Mehrtens et al. (2001) study are not as relevant for the voluntary sector.

The six firms helped identify three forms of organizational readiness as highly relevant to EC adoption in small voluntary organizations: (a) ability to attract volunteers and in-kind donations; (b) ability to raise funds; and (c) strategic readiness.

Ability to attract volunteers and in-kind donations All six organizations had little, if no internal IT staff. Those that did have IT personnel found these people either over-worked or under-skilled. For example, organization F’s reply to a question on their technical infrastructure: ‘We don’t [have one]! We don’t have someone to manage or programy [a volunteer] found our web designery’. Organization C has ‘yone person who is problemsolving most of the time because our [IT] is so antiquated’, while D has ‘your office manager is our IT person, but she, by her own admission, doesn’t know enough to build a website, or to manage it or maintain it’. To get around this challenge, the organizations relied heavily on volunteers and external service providers, or in the case of D, an in-kind donation from a local web developer, who donated their staff’s time to the project!

Organization B stated: ‘I’ve been here 5 years and we had a website when I started, and that was sort of in the early days of websites and it was a volunteer in the U.S. somewhere who helped us produce it.’ Organization D put it this way: ‘ymaybe 4 years [ago], one of the board members who was a computer buff decided [we] should have a website, and he created one, very, very basic [with] the very, very minimum amount of information on ity’ and E plainly stated: ‘We don’t have the budget, we have zero dollars for anything like this, so when [name] did the original website, he did it as a volunteeryand we got another volunteer to help us with ity

Ability to raise funds All six cases perceived benefits to developing a website, however, none saw it as a priority for fund allocation. This changed when funds were either received from external agencies or given from donors for the express purpose of developing a website. Most of the organizations in our study ‘fell into’ developing a website as a result of a fortuitous donation and/or timely grant.

Organization C stated:

ywe at the moment have a web page, which is rather rudimentary. Unfortunately, the budget has never been available to really go into a really interesting site and so on and so forth. However, we have now received some grant money which will allow us to go further with it.

Organization E also received specific funding: ‘I guess it started several years ago when [agency] got some funding to get you know, email and web, and included funding for actually the domain, the site and some training, so we kind of rode along with that wave. It was an opportunity, it dropped in our laps, and we said why not?’ as did organization $C ,$ whose manager wrote a letter to an investment bank requesting a specific donation for development of a website, and received a cheque for \$15,000 in the mail a few weeks later!

Organization B received a significant donation from an individual donor:

For the web for the both of our pieces on the web, both our big lumps invested on the web, they were supported by one donor, and this was someone who called me up and I went for lunch with, a fellow from [place] and he said,’ you know, if you build the best website in the world, I’ll support you’ and so he gave us, you know, big chunks of money a few times. He essentially gave us the \$20,000.

Strategic readiness Chan & Huff (1993) define the first stage of IS Strategic Alignment as ‘Awareness’ – a state where an organization recognizes that information systems and business objectives must be connected. Their model progresses through two subsequent stages: Integration and finally Strategic Alignment. The six organizations involved in this study were all aware of the need for a website, and its desirability in terms of tangible business performance objectives. In that sense, they were ready. That they were able to act on the opportunity when it presented itself attests to their nimbleness, a dimension of strategic readiness.

The organizational readiness results in this study contradict the term definitions provided by Mehrtens et al. (2001) in that their focus is primarily on IT readiness in the organization. This research suggests that while the organizations were not ready in that sense, they were nonetheless adaptive enough to take advantage of funding and volunteer opportunities. Therefore, as outlined in Table 3, organizational readiness does indeed play a significant role in a non-profit firm’s decision to adopt EC.

## Perceived pressure

The third and final construct in the Mehrtens et al. (2001) model is defined exclusively in terms of external pressure, that is, expectations on the part of potential employees, customers, and suppliers for a firm to have a website. Mehrtens et al. (2001) state that credibility in the eyes of these parties was an important motivator for Internet adoption.

In the non-profit sector, the notion of ‘customer’ often refers to a heterogeneous group of people or organizations representing the numerous stakeholders that a nonprofit organization serves (Saxton & Game, 2001). Thus, the Mehrtens et al. (2001) term definition of customer pressure does apply if it is extended to include a much broader group of stakeholders with divergent interests. Thus, we prefer the term ‘Stakeholders’ to ‘Customers’ in this context, and have amended Table 3 to reflect this outlook.

## Perceived social risk

One factor that did not emerge from the Mehrtens et al. (2001) study was that of perceived social risk. Voluntary organizations are concerned with meeting a social need rather than the pursuit of profit from the sale of goods or services on a profit-making basis. The case studies provided evidence of perceived social risk as a factor influencing EC adoption. Organization C sensed a risk of losing human contact with stakeholders resulting from EC adoption. Our informant in organization C identified an interesting paradox. Non-profits, he stated, are fundamentally people-based, or human contact organizations that aim to help others in need. However, the web is at its core a pull-driven medium, low in interaction and contact with humans: ‘But there’s one thing I see as a downside, is that we might end up removing the human component of communication with our membersy

Organizations B and F both provided evidence that one perceived social risk of the Internet was that it might draw valuable, and scarce staff and volunteer time away from more mission-critical tasks (i.e., from helping others). As F put it: ‘The only risk I can see is spending a lot of time on the web if the traffic isn’t there, and I’ve seen this happen, where organizations have a huge website, they invest a lot of resources, but there’s no forethought into building the trafficyour [site] isn’t gorgeous, but until we build the traffic, we’re not going to invest a lot of resources making it look beautifuly’. B added the dimension of transparency related to having a website which requires additional resources: ‘One of the risks is because you are now completely transparent, people who don’t like you can find out anything they want about you and they know the names of people and so ony

Therefore, where recent studies have focused mostly on economic and transactional risk in the for-profit sector (Straub et al., 2002), this research firstly shows that perceived social risk is a salient construct in the model for EC adoption by small non-profits, and secondly, that this perceived social risk is expressed in mostly social, not economic terms.

Of course, this finding is preliminary at this point, and subject to confirmation in future studies. It could be argued that ‘social risk’ might be related to the ‘organizational readiness’ factor, inasmuch as both partly concern themselves with the acquisition, allocation, and motivation of human resources. However, this preliminary investigation suggests that the difference between the factors is more subtle.

The foregoing literature review suggests that Organizational Readiness deals primarily with the acquisition of production resources, both human and financial. In other words, this factor deals with the organization’s capability to attract these factors in the first place and use them effectively and efficiently.

On the other hand, Social Risk is the label we have chosen to describe the potentially alienating effect that a web presence might have on a voluntary organization’s stakeholders. To a large extent, the voluntary sector depends on the goodwill of people (as permanent or temporary volunteers, as donors, as publicists) to enact its various missions. Technology might have the effect of ‘distancing’ the organization from these stakeholders, and imposing a barrier between people within and without the voluntary organization. In his 1953 essay The Question Concerning Technology, Martin Heidegger (1953) alerts us to this effect by asserting that technology is a reflection of humanity, that it shows us to us, and in doing so, distances us from one another. The Perceived Social Risk factor that emerges from this study alludes to this alienation on two levels: alienation of stakeholders from the organizations, and perceptions that scarce resources are being squandered by the volunteer organization to develop, design, and deploy a sophisticated web presence. This becomes even more important when, paradoxically, American websites like the Giving Forum (http://www.givingforum.org) devote a significant amount of attention to explicitly assessing charitable organizations on a number of financial performance metrics, including monies collected and disbursed, officers’ salaries, and administrative overhead expenses. Therefore, for these reasons, and subject to future research, we suggest the Perceived Social Risk might be orthogonal to the Organizational Readiness factor.

## Discussion and implications

This study provided a clear understanding of the factors influencing EC adoption in small voluntary organizations. EC adoption was influenced by four major factors: perceived benefits, organizational readiness, perceived pressure, and perceived social risk. Non-profit firms with websites seek a variety of benefits. The most important benefit of EC adoption was as a communication tool. This benefit included enhanced information sharing with stakeholders such as program clients (i.e., recipients of services), members, the media, volunteers, and potential donors. Firms are also influenced by both internal and external pressure. However, pressure from external stakeholders including volunteers, clients, donors, suppliers, media and other agencies played a more significant role in a non-profit’s decision to adopt EC in comparison with internal stakeholders (e.g., employees and board members). Firms need to be ready for EC adoption, by having an ability to raise funds and attract volunteers and/or inkind donations to develop a website or enhance an existing website. In addition, firms need to be aware of the perceived social risks associated with EC adoption, including the loss of human contact with external stakeholders and the inappropriate allocation of funds to administration rather than client programs.

Many prior studies of EC Adoption and small firms have focused on profit-based firms. Due to their strategic focus of ‘meeting a social need’, this study found that small voluntary organizations have unique needs and concerns related to EC adoption. The final model (Figure 1) shows that four very different types of influence exist; all four influences are important and deserve both recognition and further study. The definitions for each of the factors (Table 3) are an important part of this better understanding and provide a basis for further study.

This study found the Mehrtens et al. (2001) Internet adoption model helpful in examining EC adoption in small voluntary organizations. In addition to perceived benefits, organizational readiness, and perceived external pressure, this study identified perceived social risk to be an important additional factor influencing EC adoption in small voluntary organizations. Furthermore, although the three influencing factors have similar titles, the definitions of each factor were very different, particularly for perceived external pressure and organizational readiness. This study identified both external and internal pressure (e.g., from employees and board members) as significant for EC adoption. With respect to organizational readiness, this study found little support for the Mehrtens et al. (2001) definition, which was expressed in terms of IT readiness of the firm (i.e., level of internet knowledge and computer systems). According to this definition, none of the firms in our study were ready for EC adoption. However, three forms of organizational readiness were identified as highly relevant to EC adoption in small voluntary organizations: (1) ability to attract volunteers and inkind donations; (2) ability to raise funds; and (3) strategic readiness. Thus, this research extended the Mehrtens et al. (2001) model by testing and extending it in another country and in an industry-specific context (i.e., the voluntary sector).

The limitations of the study are related to data collection and analysis. The experiences of six firms formed the primary source of data for the model. It is possible that the focus on firms in one city and in one country (Canada) is a source of bias. Much of the evidence was collected using interviews and interpreted by a team of researchers. The data analysis may have been influenced by researcher subjectivity. The use of the Mehrtens et al. (2001) model may have influenced the interpretation of the case study data.

## Implications

The findings have considerable implications for managers of small voluntary organizations. Those that have not adopted EC must identify business benefits. Through website adoption, voluntary organizations may pursue advantages over traditional methods of communication with clients, volunteers, donors, and other stakeholder groups. For example, for those companies that have adopted EC, the findings indicate that website adoption can lead to more effective information sharing, increased reach (extending the brand), reduced costs through streamlined administration and reduced collateral marketing expenses, as well as the potential for increased donations/revenue generation. Many of the case study participants cited a lack of financial resources and internal IT-skill shortages, as barriers to adoption or further enhancement of existing websites. Yet ironically, website adoption may improve the amount of resources available through the benefits outlined above.

The perceived pressure factor provides evidence that both internal and external stakeholders expect small voluntary organizations to adopt EC. With increasing competition for donations and funds, small voluntary organizations may be able to exploit this opportunity to be in a better position to compete.

Furthermore, a small voluntary organization must be ready for EC. This may require having an ability to attract volunteers and/or in-kind donations to develop or enhance a website. For some, raising funds to develop a website has been a successful approach. The strategic readiness factor highlights the importance of having the ability to be able to respond to donations and/or grants received to adopt EC. Finally, small voluntary organizations must be aware of the social risks associated with EC adoption. Managers cited a loss of human contact and the perception of dollars being diverted from existing programs to finance the EC investment as key risks. None of the participants had received feedback from their stakeholders to substantiate these social risks, and managers would be wise to base their EC adoption decisions on actual stakeholder input to properly understand the social risks and to create a website that will meet the specific needs of its stakeholders.

Managers of voluntary organizations should also consider performance metrics to measure the impact of website adoption. All organizations in the study expressed a desire to measure and track success, however none did. The decision to allocate scarce resources to the implementation of a website may be easier if management is able to demonstrate to themselves, as well as to their stakeholders, that the decision to adopt EC is a strategic one, that is supported by specific and measurable perceived benefits.

## Opportunities for future research

Further research can proceed in several directions. In our research, firm size was less than 100 employees. It would be interesting and useful to identify any differences that might be noted if the model was applied in the context of large voluntary organizations. Alternative research approaches could use the model to help improve our understanding of EC adoption in both small and large voluntary organizations. To test the generalizability of the model, a quantitative survey research strategy is required. Once the generalizability of the model has been tested, such a model could be used to indicate the necessary conditions for EC adoption in voluntary organizations. Researchers could develop valid and reliable instruments for the measurement of two relatively new constructs in the EC literature: EC adoption and EC success. These measures must be tested in both large and small voluntary organizations to ensure the appropriateness of these measures to both organizational contexts. In-depth longitudinal case studies of individual organizations could be used to investigate the stages of EC evolution from initial website adoption to more advanced levels of EC sophistication.

The focus of this study was on EC adopters (i.e., organizations with websites) in the small voluntary sector. In order to accelerate EC adoption, further research is needed on gaining a better understanding of EC non-adopters (i.e., organizations without websites) and the factors influencing their intention to adopt EC. Researchers could build a highly predictive model to influence and predict how and when voluntary organizations will adopt EC.

## Conclusions

This study is the first to research factors influencing EC adoption in small voluntary organizations. The results of the study demonstrate significant progress toward explaining the factors influencing EC adoption in the small voluntary sector. The study concluded that four factors significantly effect EC adoption: perceived benefits, organizational readiness, perceived pressure (both internal and external), and perceived social risks. The EC adoption model for small voluntary organizations was similar in form to the Mehrtens et al. (2001) Internet adoption model. However, some significant differences were identified. In particular, definitions of each of the factors were revised to reflect the differences in a voluntary sector context. Additionally, this research suggests that perceived social risks ought to be included in the model, as it is an important factor influencing the decision to adopt EC by small voluntary organizations. ## Appendix B: Semi-structured interview guidelines Each interview adhered to the following sequence:

\- Provide background information on your organization, the decision to develop a website, and the evolution of that website.

\- Describe the role of the website in terms of your organization’s business objectives.
