# Organizational determinants of IT adoption in the pharmaceutical distribution sector

## Abstract

The aim of this paper is to propose new organizational factors that might explain the differences in the extent and the speed of IT adoption. With this in mind, we carried out an analysis of 16 cases in the pharmaceutical distribution sector in Spain. The results indicate that there are certain intangible assets that favour the introduction and development of IT. Among these are a frank and fluid communication between departments and members of the organization, low levels of conflict, the explicit support of top management towards IT adoption and learning and creative skills of IT-staff. In addition to these factors, we found others that we propose as catalysts of IT adoption. Among these we might mention the special relationship between the member-clients and the company in the case of cooperative firms. European Journal of Information Systems (2004) 13, 133–146.

Keywords: IT adoption; intangible resources; success or failure of IT adoption; cooperative firms

## Introduction

In the field of Information Systems, there are several studies analysing the environmental or organizational factors that help firms to adopt IT more intensely (Grover, 1993; Premkumar et $a l . ,$ 1997; Teo & Pian, 2003). Nevertheless, a deeper understanding of the factors encouraging firms to adopt IT is doubtless useful, for practitioners as well as researchers. First, practitioners may benefit from the analysis of the determinants of IT adoption. Thus, managers interested in introducing new technologies may be able to modify some of the factors that facilitate IT adoption. Second, from the perspective of research, a deeper understanding of the causes motivating a more intense adoption of IT is required. A well-based knowledge on factors that promote IT adoption may prove useful to subsequently resolve research issues currently subject to much debate, such as the relationship between IT utilization and firm performance (Carr, 2003; Teo & Pian, 2003).

The objective of this paper is therefore to identify new factors that may explain the differences between firms in their extent and speed of IT adoption. We shall concentrate exclusively on factors originating in the organization itself and of an intangible nature. Thus, we shall not consider in this article factors that may be related to IT adoption but that originate in the environment. The reason for this narrower focus resides in the fact that environmental factors have already been widely studied previously (e.g., Reich & Benbasat, 1990; Gonc¸alves et al., 1999; Montealegre, 1999). Moreover, there have only been relatively few studies (e.g., Mirvis et al., 1991; Premkumar et al., 1997) analysing organizational factors of an intangible nature that might be behind a greater adoption of IT. Thus, new studies are needed pointing out elements from within the organization itself that may favour IT adoption.

To achieve the objective that we have just mentioned, we have structured the article into five parts following on from this introduction. In the following section, we describe the main theoretical background of this research area. Then, we describe the sector we focused on to test the theoretical model (the pharmaceutical distribution sector). In the next section, we describe the methods used in gathering the information and analysing the cases. In the subsequent section, we describe the results we obtained, while in the last section we draw some conclusions from these results.

## Theoretical background and propositions

Researchers have proposed various models explaining the intensity with which firms adopt IT. Many of these models were designed to explain differences in the adoption of EDI technologies. Among these, that of Premkumar et al. (1997) is one of the most complete, identifying three groups of elements that may be associated with the extent of adoption of EDI technology. The first group comprises a series of environmental characteristics. This group includes elements such as the competitive pressure exerted by partners that have already adopted the technology, the dependence of those partners that have not yet adopted the technology on those that have, trust and the transaction climate between business partners, and finally the support that partners that have not yet adopted the technology receive from those that have.

The second group of elements is termed innovation characteristics, and includes factors such as the relative advantage that the new technology provides, its compatibility with existing technologies, the complexity of the new technology and the costs of adoption. The third group, termed organizational characteristics, includes elements such as company size, the support of top management for the adoption of the new technologies, and the existence of product champions leading the process of change required for the new IT to be introduced.

The three groups of variables that we have just mentioned, with some variation, have also been used in other empirical work on the extent of IT adoption (Gonc¸alves et al., 1999; Santos & Pfeffers, 1998; Dasgupta et al., 1999; Teo & Pian, 2003). Thus, Gonc¸alves et al. (1999) focus on environmental factors and consider the structure of the sector, the number of competing firms, the competitive pressure to adopt IT, the degree of concentration or the influence of government regulation. Dasgupta et al. (1999) identified two types of factors: environmental (competition, government policies and market stability) and organizational (involvement of MIS personnel; national/foreign ownership; performance-oriented culture and organizational size). More recently, Teo & Pian (2003) focus on organizational and innovation factors in an analysis about the reasons leading firms to adopt the Internet. In this case, they considered three groups of elements: those related to the technological strategy of the firm; top management support; and the degree of compatibility of the emerging technologies with those already adopted by the firm.

Of the three groups proposed by Premkumar et al. (1997), we shall concentrate exclusively on the third (organizational factors). Traditionally, researchers have included in this group elements such as top management support, the existence of product champions or organizational size (Premkumar et al., 1994; Raymond and Bergeron, 1996). However, this list of organizational elements is not definitive, and there may be other elements that lead to more extensive IT adoption (Premkumar et al., 1997).

With a view to proposing new organizational elements that may be related to IT adoption, we have reviewed the literature on the effect of IT adoption on firm performance. Among these studies, some point out that IT will exert a greater positive influence on firm performance when it acts jointly with a series of intangible assets (Ross et al., 1996; Powell and Dent-Micallef, 1997; Bharadwaj, 2000). Among these intangible assets the authors mention: (1) the existence of a fluid communication between technical staff and the management; (2) the absence of any organizational conflict; (3) flexibility in the procedures and decision-making; (4) the use of inter-departmental working groups; (5) the explicit leadership of the top management in the process of technological renovation; (6) the dedication of efforts in training for the new technologies; (7) an inclination towards change of the organization members and (8) the fit between the technology used and the socio-economic system of the firm. We shall henceforth call this group of factors ITcomplementary intangible assets.

It appears to be proven that the use of IT jointly with IT-complementary intangible assets has positive, synergic effects on profits, profitability or competitive advantage in organizations (Bharadwaj, 2000). But the question arises: does the existence of IT-complementary intangible assets favour (apart from firm performance) the actual adoption of IT by the firm?

Theoretically, we might establish a relationship between IT-complementary intangible assets and the adoption of IT itself. As was already shown in sociotechnical theory (Trist & Bamforth, 1951) technology is intimately related to human behaviour. In organizations in which the conditions of internal communication, organizational consensus and inclination towards change are favourable, management will find a favourable environment in which to risk financial and human resources by adopting and implementing new technologies. The risk perceived by managers in adopting new technology can be reduced when there are favourable cultural circumstances (Mirvis et al., 1991). IT-complementary intangible assets may reduce this perceived risk, since managers would be more confident that once IT was implemented it would have significant effects on the economic and financial results of the firm. The favourable scenario created from these expectations of good performance may accelerate the introduction of IT and therefore its extent of adoption in the organization. In short, those firms where there are favourable levels of IT-complementary intangible assets will introduce IT more quickly, and hence more intensely. This concept is expressed in Proposition 1.

Proposition 1. Those organizations with a better endowment of IT-complementary intangible assets will introduce IT more quickly, and will therefore adopt IT more intensely.

The relationship expressed in Proposition 1 predicts that there will be a direct link between IT adoption and the presence of IT-complementary intangible assets. However, we believe that this relationship is affected by a number of elements from the organization itself or the individuals that make up the firm, and that these elements will determine ultimately both the extent and the speed with which firms adopt IT. In the literature, a number of these factors are mentioned, such as organization size (Ba et al., 2000), or previous experience in using basic IT (Powell & Dent-Micallef, 1997). However, there are other factors that have not, until now, been considered potential catalysts of the adoption of new technologies in firms, but which may be associated with the intensity with which firms adopt IT. We are referring to positive effects that may be found in social economy firms (cooperative firms) in which the roles of memberowner and client coincide.

Cooperative firms are companies whose capital belongs entirely to either clients, suppliers or workers. Clients, suppliers or workers are, therefore, owners and active members of the firm who participate directly in the economic and productive activity of the organization. Cooperatives are also characterised by the fact that each member (client, supplier or worker) has the same power of decision, regardless of the equity capital they provide. Cooperatives play a fundamental role in various sectors, for example in the pharmaceutical distribution sectors of several nations in western Europe, such as France, Spain and the Netherlands. In the case of the pharmaceutical distribution sector, the clients (pharmaceutical retailers) are the owners of the wholesale cooperative firms. These wholesale cooperatives compete, in turn, against other non-cooperative firms.

In the case of cooperative firms, the double condition of member-client or member-supplier may imply an increase in the commitment of the people concerned with their firm. Locke & Schweiger (1979) and Schweiger & Leana (1986) show that there is a positive association between participation and the level of satisfaction and commitment of members. Moreover cooperatives do not attempt to maximize results: rather they are structures designed for serving their members. Benefits are channelled to the members in the form of lower prices and better services rendered, and IT, robotics, communications and computing technologies can play a fundamental role in this (Vargas Sa´nchez, 2001).

Because the cooperative needs to maintain a strong relationship of loyalty with its member-clients, it is to be expected that it seeks to introduce intensely the technological and human resources needed to increase the level of satisfaction of the client-member. Clearly, one of these resources is IT (Porter & Millar, 1985), which means that this type of firm may be more oriented towards more intense levels of IT introduction. This idea is expressed in the following proposition:

## Proposition 2. Cooperative firms introduce IT more quickly than non-cooperative firms, and thus adopt IT more intensely.

Firms (especially cooperatives) according to Proposition 2 need therefore to maintain a special relationship with their member-clients, so that they are more inclined to adopt IT. In addition, the actual introduction of IT, and the potential positive effects of this on the value chain (Parsons, 1983; Cash & Konsynski, 1985), especially in the medium to long term (McAfee, 1999), may enhance the services that the firm provides to the client, improving client satisfaction. Client satisfaction is in turn the step prior to improving client loyalty. In the organizational literature and the marketing literature there are many studies that confirm a direct relationship between client satisfaction and the loyalty that the client has for their firm (e.g., Yi, 1990; Hallowell, 1996).

The effect IT adoption–client satisfaction–client loyalty will be stronger in the case of cooperative firms due to the stronger ties that bind the client-member to their firm. This climate of trust and loyalty between client and firm may in turn lead to the introduction of new technologies in the future. A number of classic studies on the adoption of inter-organizational information systems (Vitale, 1986; Clemons & Row, 1988) in firms such as American Hospital Supply and McKesson Drug Company, found that a good client relationship was directly associated with the adoption of technologies such as EDI (Premkumar et al., 1997). Although other internal and external factors facilitate IT adoption, the firm may eventually decide to introduce IT in function of the climate of trust and loyalty existing in the client-supplier relationship. This climate of trust and loyalty (which is greater in the case of cooperatives) will give managers the confidence required to invest both time and money in adopting new IT. Thus, according to this view a virtuous circle would be created: IT adoption improves client loyalty and client loyalty increases IT adoption in later stages. Owing to the special relationship between the client and the firm in cooperative firms, this virtuous circle will be more intense in cooperative than in non-cooperative companies (Proposition 3).

Proposition 3. Those firms that adopt IT more intensely (especially cooperative firms) achieve increases in the loyalty of their clients, a situation that encourages an increase in the level of IT adoption in later stages.

## The sector

We have chosen the pharmaceutical distribution sector in Spain as our object of analysis. The pharmaceutical distribution sector may be an appropriate sector for analysing the factors that promote or hinder the adoption of IT. First, because it has a wide experience in using information and communication technologies. In Spain, and in much of the rest of the world, the sector was pioneer in introducing information-handling tools in order to deal with client orders. This is because the sector has to handle a huge amount of complex information in short time-spans, and in response to demanding and highly specialized clients. This is shown by the fact that in the year 2000 Spanish pharmaceutical distribution firms managed a portfolio of over 20,000 products (Vargas et al., 2003), achieving minimal error rates.

As far as the geographical area is concerned, the organizations analysed are firms active in the sector in 10 provinces in central and southern Spain. In this area in the year 2000 there was a total of 16 pharmaceutical distribution firms, which had a gross income of some h6 billion. In this same year, the firms supplied to a total of 3,800 pharmacies, and through them to a population of 8,834,000, which represents 22.4% of the total population of Spain. There are no important basic structural differences between the sector in the area studied and in the rest of Spain (in terms of average size, proportion of cooperative firms, relevant legislation, and increase in income in the past 5 years).

## Methodology

The main method we used is the case-study analysis, along with a series of measures of a quantitative nature that classify each of the cases in terms of different variables. We chose the case-study analysis because it is the most appropriate analysis for creating new hypotheses or theoretical frameworks (Eisendhardt, 1989), and because it is also appropriate for providing answers to ‘what?’, ‘how?’ or ‘why?’ questions (questions that arise in this research) (Verschuren & Doorenwaard, 1999). Other reasons for choosing case-study analysis lie in the small number of units of analysis, the adoption of methods of work-intensive data-gathering within the organizations, the non-random criteria for the choice of sample units (all the units of the population were chosen) and the presence of researchers in the home office of the firms. We used four types of data sources (Yin, 1994): the analysis of documentary sources such as company web sites and secondary statistical sources (for data pertaining to the size and age of the organizations), a questionnaire sent to company employees, a questionnaire sent to clients, and personal semistructured interviews with managers and employees.

During the empirical study, we visited each firm for a period of at least one working day. During the visit we looked at the company installations, especially those related with processes involving some type of IT. We held a total of 36 interviews with different organization members: 16 executives, 14 IT Directors (Computing Manager, IS Manager or Data Processing Manager) and six workers from the Operations department. On two occasions, it was not possible to interview the IT director because there was no such department. We interviewed Operations staff either when there was no IT director to interview, or when we chose to add more information from the Operations level (for example when there were notable differences between the opinions held by managers). The interviews took place between December 1999 and October 2000.

The interview was divided into two distinct parts. The first part of the interview consisted of a set of eight open questions concerning the motives inducing the firm to adopt IT and the effect these technologies were having on the structure, processes or performance of the firm. In the second part of the interview, the manager/employee was presented with a questionnaire with questions using a 5- point Likert-type scale, measuring the presence of IT and complementary intangible assets (see Powell & Dent-Micallef, 1997). The questionnaires had different lengths according to whom they were designed for: top management, IT manager or Operations workers. In the latter two cases the number of questions was reduced: eliminating those questions for which the interviewee would be technically unprepared to provide an answer.

The longest questionnaire, given to the upper management, had a total of 45 questions grouped into seven sections, each one with the aim of identifying and evaluating a resource or result in the firm. The first section referred to the impact that IT might have had on productivity, financial results or the competitive position of the organization. Parts 2–5 contained questions evaluating the presence of various IT-complementary intangible assets. Part 6 contained a total of 17 questions referring to as many types of computing, robotic or telecoms tools. The last section (section 7) asked the interviewee for their opinion about the global position of the organization in various aspects which measure competitive success, such as increase in income, improvements in firm performance, etc. In this paper we shall use the data from sections 2–6 of the questionnaire.

A questionnaire was also sent to the clients of the pharmaceutical distribution firms. This questionnaire was used to establish the number of firms with which each client transacted, and therefore their degree of loyalty towards each organization. The survey was sent to a total of 1,060 distribution clients and we obtained a total of 231 valid replies, which represents a response rate of 21.86%. The remaining details of this survey and its technical specifications can be seen in the Appendix A (Table A1).

## Cases analysed

As we mentioned earlier, we studied all the firms active in the pharmaceutical distribution sector. The organizations analysed had, in 1998, a mean payroll of 136 workers and mean sales of h49.99 million. About half the firms (nine) are cooperative firms owned by their own pharmaceutical clients. Many of these firms were created in the 1940s or 1950s with the aim of managing centrally the orders of the pharmacies, in the face of the increasing complexity of transactions and increasing competition from noncooperative firms. From the 1960s onwards most distribution firms became interested in technological advances in the electronic storing and transmission of information, resulting in an incipient technological development which would peak in later decades. We are therefore in the presence of firms that have had an experience of more than 30 years in the use of IT, a period of time long enough for us to obtain relevant results explaining the different extents and speeds of IT adoption.

## Variables used

We have classified the variables into three types. We start by describing the dependent variables, and subsequently we describe the explanatory and the mediating variables that may be associated with the extent and the speed of IT adoption. Third, we include a group of moderating variables that have been used previously to explain differences in the extent of IT adoption in organizations.

Dependent variables Extent of IT adoption: We identified 17 computing, robotics and telecoms technologies used in the pharmaceutical distribution sector. We determined the level of adoption of each of these technologies using 5-point Likert-type scales following the model of Powell & Dent-Micallef (1997). The extent of IT adoption was calculated using the mean value of the answers provided by different interviewees in each organization. The items used are shown in Appendix A (Table A2).

Speed of IT adoption: We measured the speed of IT adoption as the degree to which the firms had adopted the most advanced IT in 2000. This index was constructed as the mean value of the level of use of the following advanced technologies: internal communications systems or endogenous networks; robotic technologies for managing the distribution warehouses; and technologies based on the TCP/IP protocol (Vargas et al., 2003). These technologies include, respectively, the items IT7, IT8, IT9, IT10, IT11, IT12, IT13, IT14, IT15, IT16 and IT17 (Table A2).

Explanatory variables Client loyalty: This variable measures the level of loyalty that clients show towards the supplier firm. It is measured using a formula based on

the following ratio:

$$
N _ {j} = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} C _ {i}}{n},
$$

where $N _ { j }$ is the mean number of firms with whom the clients of the firm j transact; $C _ { i }$ the number of firms with whom client i of the firm j transacts; n the total number, in the sample, of clients of the firm j.

The values of this variable were obtained from the questionnaire sent to the pharmaceutical clients, which we described earlier.

IT-complementary intangible assets: Based on the work of Ross et al. (1996) and Powell & Dent-Micallef (1997), we have used the following variables measuring the presence of IT-complementary intangible assets:

1. Communication: Variable that measures the existence of an open and frank communication between the different departments and members of the organization. It was obtained by calculating the mean of the evaluations of interviewees, who were presented with 5-point Likerttype questions, concerning the following aspects: (1) written and oral communication flow without great difficulty in our organization; (2) the staff of our organization communicate with the rest of the workers, and not just with those of their own department or section; (3) there are frequent consultations between the computing or communications staff and managers concerning technical or management decisions; (4) the computing or communications staff correctly perform the function of negotiator, instructor and advisor; (5) our staff are very open and have complete trust in their colleagues. This variable, and the following variables, have been formed based on the scales used by Ross et al. (1996) and Powell & Dent-Micallef (1997), and it achieves a reliability measure (Cronbach Alpha) of 0.81.

2. Conflict: This is a measure of the conflict or lack of consensus existing in the organization. It was calculated with the mean value of the 5-point Likert-scale variable, in the following items: (1) there is a high level of conflict in our organization; and (2) there is a high level of conflict between the headquarters of the firm and the territorial centres/warehouses. It achieves a reliability measure (Cronbach Alpha) of 0.85.

3. IT-staff skills: Measures the level of creativity as well as the learning skills of IT-staff. It was calculated using the mean values of the 5-point Likert-type scales of the following items: (1) our technical and communications staff are highly creative; and (2) our computing and communications staff are used to continual learning. This variable achieves a reliability measure (Cronbach Alpha) of 0.76.

4. Propensity to change: This variable measures the propensity to change of the members of the organization, including change resulting from the introduction of new IT. It was calculated based on mean values of a semantic differential scale of the following items: (1) our staff accept change: very slowly, slowly, at an intermediate rate, quickly, very quickly and (2) our staff has accepted the use of new IT: extremely unwillingly, unwillingly, with indifference, enthusiastically, very enthusiastically. This variable achieves a reliability measure (Cronbach Alpha) of 0.65.

5. Top management support: This measures the explicit and active support of the top management towards the introduction and development of new information technology. The values of this variable were calculated using the mean value of the 5-point Likert-type scales of the following items: (1) new technology projects are unequivocally supported by the company management; (2) the company management has lead the renovation of IT in the firm; and (3) training in the use of IT is a priority for our firm. This variable achieves a reliability measure (Cronbach Alpha) of 0.86.

6. Working groups: Unidimensional variable that measures the use of inter-departmental working groups to solve key problems. Its values have been obtained from evaluations on semantic differential scales of the following item: we resort to inter-departmental working groups to solve key problems: never, almost never, occasionally, frequently, very frequently.

7. Flexibility: Unidimensional variable that measures the lack of bureaucracy in the organization. Its values come from a Likert-type scale of the following item: we have very little bureaucracy in our firm. Although flexibility and lack of bureaucracy are not synonymous they are directly related. Non-bureaucratic (or organic) organizations are more flexible than bureaucratic organizations, such that an increase in bureaucratisation (or the level of formalisation in the organization) necessarily reduces the flexibility of the organization in the face of environmental change. This effect also occurs in reverse (Mintzberg, 1984).

8. Technology fit: Measures the fit between the technology used and the socio-economic system of the firm (Powell & Dent-Micallef, 1997). It is calculated from the mean value of 5-point Likert-type scales of the following items: (1) the availability of the computing/communications systems, their response times and the management costs in our firm are better than those of our competitors; and (2) our company’s new IT introduction and development costs are lower than those of our competitors. This variable achieves a Cronbach reliability measure of 0.90.

Mediating variable Cooperative character: This is a dichotomous variable measuring whether the firm is a cooperative or not. According to Spanish law, cooperative firms are those firms owned by workers, clients or suppliers, and which are governed by criteria for membership participation in decision-making and in the economic activity of the firm. Cooperatives must register as such in a different registry from the other firms. We have been guided by information from the Cooperative Registry and the General Registry (both dependent on the Ministry of Economy) to distinguish between cooperatives and noncooperatives.

Moderating variables In this last group, a number of organizational variables have been taken into account. These variables have been previously analysed in the literature on the extent of IT adoption. The findings indicate that they are related with higher levels of IT adoption (Grover, 1993; Premkumar et al., 1997; Gonc¸alves et al., 1999). These variables are size, organization age and previous experience in basic IT.

Size: This variable refers to the mean number of workers for the period 1994–1998. Size has been found to be connected to IT adoption in several previous studies (e.g., Lehman, 1985, Premkumar et al., 1997).

Organization age: Defined as the number of years passed from the founding of the firm until 2000. The older the firm, the more likely it is to have had contact with the IT used in the sector, so that the extent of IT adoption may be greater (Leonard-Barton, 1987).

Experience in unidirectional communications: Previous experience in using basic IT may be linked to a higher propensity to adopt new technologies and therefore more extensive IT adoption (Leonard-Barton, 1987). In this research, we have chosen unidirectional communications as the basic technology in order to measure previous experience. Unidirectional communications is a widely used technology in the sector (Bruque et al., 2003) that allow the pharmaceutical distribution firms to receive orders from pharmacies, usually via the basic telephone network and a modem, or in some cases, via an acoustic terminal.

## Analysis

Since the number of cases is high compared to other studies of a similar methodology, we grouped the firms into homogeneous groups according to the state (more or less advanced) in which they were regarding the adoption of IT. To do this we first developed a cluster analysis taking into account hierarchical as well as non-hierarchical procedures of classification (Visauta Vinacua, 1998; Grande Esteban & Abascal Ferna´ndez, 2000). We considered the groupings resulting from the hierarchical agglomerative methods (maximum, minimum, centroid and Ward). We then determined the groupings by analysing the dendograms resulting from the cluster analysis. The variables used to make the cluster analysis included the 17 computing, telecommunication and robotic technologies that are listed in Table A2 (see Appendix A).

After studying each dendogram we identified that there were clearly three groups of organizations according to their technological level. Once we had determined the number of groups, we applied the k-means non-hierarchical algorithm, with the aim of deciding which organizations formed each group. This method has the aim of dividing the population into k-groups, in this case with k ¼ 3 (number obtained from the hierarchical agglomerative methods defined earlier), with a criterion of global optimization, based on the calculation of centres of gravity using squared Euclidean distances. Each of these groups is characterized by the value of its centres with respect to each one of the 17 information technology variables analysed (Table 1). The cluster to which each firm belongs, along with their distances to the centre, are shown in Appendix A (Tables A3 and A4).

To test whether there were significant differences between the groups, we subsequently ran an ANOVA test, taking as dependent variables the extent of IT adoption and the speed of IT adoption. The results confirm that there are significant differences between the three clusters (F ¼ 45.28 and P ¼ 0.000 for extent of IT adoption; and F ¼ 56.024 and P ¼ 0.000 for speed of IT adoption). Similar results are obtained from the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test. Other tests of stability in the cluster analysis (e.g., Maylor, 2001) were not used due to the reduced number of cases in the population (16).

Once we had determined the groups according to their IT level, we assessed each group of firms according to the aforementioned explanatory, mediating and moderating variables. This information is shown in Table 2.

## Results

As a result of the cluster analysis described in the previous section, we obtained three groups with distinct extents of

IT adoption. In Group 1, there are five organizations, with the characteristic that they have a high technological level in the following aspects: (1) communication with clients, especially using bidirectional communications (IT2); (2) own network connecting the working groups of the firm (IT7); and (3) use of e-mail and file transfer (IT8). However, the technological level in terms of the following aspects is less than average: (1) EDI with laboratories (IT5); (2) EDI with laboratories via Internet (IT6); and movement of merchandise by radio-frequency (IT12). That is, it is a group that favours technologies of endogenous communication, and those connecting the firm with its clients. But it is less interested in technologies concerning operations management, or communication with suppliers. The firms of this group barely use these tools. Observing the data in Table 1, the technological level of this group could be described as intermediate.

Group 2 has four firms, and is characterized by scoring higher than the rest of the groups in the following technological aspects: (1) EDI with buying centres (IT3); (2) EDI with laboratories (IT5); (3) EDI with laboratories via Internet (IT6); (4) Barcode reading systems for classifying articles (IT9); (5) Robotic systems of dispensing articles of different turnover (IT11); (6) Own software for pharmacy management (IT13); Own-domain Website (IT14); and (8) Restricted-access Intranet (IT15). In short, these firms are globally highly advanced in terms of technology, which we can confirm when we see that there is practically no technological aspect in which they score poorly compared to the other groups (Table 1). Moreover, in the more advanced aspects, such as EDI-web systems with suppliers, own software for pharmacy management, restricted-access Intranet, etc, the group is even more favourably placed compared to the other groups.

Finally, it is possible to obtain another series of results from this research process, taking into account the effect of the moderating variables. These results concern the effect that the size, organization age, and experience in the use of basic technologies have on the extent and the speed of IT adoption. First, all the data collected indicate that size is associated with the extent and the speed of IT adoption. This effect can be seen in Table 2 itself, where it is clear that it is the firms of groups 1 and 2 that score more highly in extent and speed of IT adoption.

Secondly, all the data we have analysed allow us to infer a positive relationship between previous experience in the use of basic IT and the extent to which firms adopt new technologies. In Table 2 we can see that the firms of Groups 1 and 2 adopted unidirectional communications technologies before those of Group 3. In this aspect the difference between Groups 2 and 3 is the most significant: the firms of this last group adopted this technology almost nine years later, on average, than the firms of Group 2. During the semistructured interviews, the managers also commented that this previous experience in using IT encouraged them to continue introducing this type of technology in later years.

The age factor was also of interest in this study. We can see that the firms of Groups 2 and 3 are of similar age, and generally older than those of Group 3. The firms of these first two groups also enjoy higher levels of technology. However, evidence from both the interviews and the organizational background of each firm was contradictory. For example, the youngest firm in Group 3 has higher level of technology than the other firms in this group. The results of the interviews with managers and other members of the organizations also proved contradictory, so that we were not able to offer a definitive result in this case.

## Conclusions

The results of the research described above lead us to draw a number of conclusions. These conclusions have led to a number of hypotheses that we mentioned earlier and that we present graphically in Figure 1. First, this paper reports novel results in the sense that IT-complementary intangible assets may not only have positive effects on firm performance (Powell & Dent-Micallef, 1997), but they may also influence the extent and the speed at which organizations adopt IT. Beneficial effects are especially significant when the firm shows the following characteristics: a frank and fluid internal communication, an absence of conflict, managers that support IT, and a technical staff that are highly creative and trained.

Second, one of the main conclusions of this study refers to the relationship that links the client (or clientmember) with the firm. In this sense there seems to be a virtuous circle by means of which those firms that more intensely adopt IT to improve client service achieve more satisfied and therefore loyal clients. At the same time, the presence of clients committed to the organization favours the introduction of new IT that may lead to improvements in client satisfaction and therefore in client loyalty. As we have already pointed out, this effect seems to be much stronger in cooperative firms than in noncooperative firms. Thus, it is possible to assert that being a cooperative exerts a positive moderating effect on the virtuous circle client loyalty–IT adoption. The double condition client-member (or client-owner) in cooperatives is positively related to a greater loyalty of the client towards the firm (Bruque et al., 2003). This relationship is reflected in the greater loyalty measured in Groups 1 and 2 compared to Group 3 firms. This improved climate of trust and loyalty leads, in turn, to more extensive IT adoption in the organization.

Finally, our results confirm that the extent and the speed of IT adoption is related to other organizational factors, which we have taken into account as moderating variables. In the first place, size can be related with the financial and human resources that the firm has at its disposal. Thus, larger firms, having more slack resources, will be more inclined to introduce IT, and they will also possibly have better-equipped technical departments (a situation that may mean that it is precisely these firms that adopt technological innovations first). Size may also be a result of the favourable background of the organization (Lieberman & Montgomery, 1988) (a situation that may have meant that the firm invested early in technology) (Barney, 1986).

Experience in the use of basic technologies may also be a predictor of the extent to which firms adopt IT. From our results we can see that those firms that introduced mature technologies earlier tend to adopt subsequent innovations more intensely and perhaps they also do so more quickly. In other words, there may be a kind of ‘positive inertia’ that allows innovatory firms to stay ahead technologically over time, especially when a firm’s adoption of a new technology depends on the firm owning a prior technology. In this case, competitors cannot follow the adoption of the second technology unless they already own the first one. This effect is consistent with findings from various previous studies (e.g., Pennings & Harianto, 1992).

As far as age is concerned, the results are not as clear (Figure 1). It is thought that organization age favours the formalization of business behaviours and the development of internal processes and of the hierarchy (Mintzberg, 1984) (a situation that may favour a more professional technology management and a more effective planning of technological development). However, looking at our results we can say that age can only have positive effects on the development of IT if it is accompanied by a number of other favourable elements (such as the presence of appropriate intangible assets, or sufficient financial resources).
