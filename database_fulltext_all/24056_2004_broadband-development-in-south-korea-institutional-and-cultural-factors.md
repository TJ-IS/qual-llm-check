# Broadband development in South Korea: institutional and cultural factors

Jyoti Choudrie<sup>1</sup> and Heejin Lee<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

This paper is about the broadband development in South Korea (henceforth Korea). Korea is considered to be a world leader in this field. Based on various sources including a study trip to Seoul that took place in July 2002, the reasons for this success are offered in this paper. The purpose of the study trip was to investigate how Korea achieved such a relatively fast penetration rate in a time span of approximately 4 years. Using documentary secondary data such as, written documents published materials, and primary data, such as interviews, the paper explores and presents the factors contributing to this development. The factors covered include government leadership, fierce competition, low prices due to the competition, cultural aspects, and geographic and demographic aspects. Although some strategies and success factors are Korea specific, there are general lessons that can be drawn. Anyone considering the deployment of broadband and other future innovations will benefit from this investigation into the successful strategies undertaken in Korea. European Journal of Information Systems (2004) 13, 103–114.

Keywords: broadband; Korea; government leadership; competition; geographic aspects; demographic aspects

Broadband is a significant evolutionary step since the emergence of the internet, providing end-users with fast ‘always on’ access to new services, applications and content with real lifestyle and productivity benefits (Sawyer et al., 2003). However, the move to broadband requires a massive investment in new networks and infrastructure as well as the development of new content, services, applications and business models to achieve a return on investment.

The economic significance of broadband can be put into context by referring to similar changes in national infrastructures, such as road, rail, electricity and voice telephony. Each of these infrastructures transformed the economic activity for firms and citizens, and enabled new activities to develop, and provided nations with the ability to gain competitive and comparative advantages. While many of these advantages were unforeseen when the original investments were made, they are now part of the essential daily economic lifestyles and activities. It is this assumption about the transformative potential of broadband that has led many governments around the world to set ambitious targets for the deployment of broadband services (National Broadband Task Force, 2001; Office of the e-Envoy, 2001; Computer Science and Telecommunications Board, 2002; Office of Technology Policy, 2002; BAG, 2003). As the most advanced country in terms of broadband penetration, Korea provides an interesting case to seek lessons from.

Although the direction that the deployment of broadband in Korea is of particular interest to many stakeholders, the main beneficiaries of our research are the policy-makers and the providers of the innovation, in this case, the telecommunications industry. Policy-makers are currently investigating how to increase the take-up of broadband within their own country, and information on other countries’ experiences is potentially important. Conversely, the telecommunications industry is interested in how to improve current strategies. Academics can offer an independent view of the diffusion strategies pursued by various countries, which is the purpose of this paper.

This paper presents findings of the research into the diffusion of broadband in Korea. It investigates how the level of penetration was achieved in a relatively fast time span of approximately 4 years. The background to the study is provided in the following section, where we also present the methods used in the study, and the framework through which we selected main players and their actions, and thereby success factors. The current status of broadband in Korea is described in the next section. Then follows a description of the roles of the main players in the process of development. The subsequent section discusses the success factors. The paper concludes by discussing implications for other countries and contributions of the research.

## The mission and data collection

This paper reports on the findings of a research mission trip (Lee & Choudrie, 2002) plus documentary evidence. The authors organised and led an Overseas Mission to Korea to study its broadband development. The International Technology Service of the Department of Trade and Industry in the United Kingdom supported the mission. The mission delegates visited and stayed in Seoul from 21 to 27 July 2002. During this period, nine organisations (Table 1) were visited. These organisations were selected on the basis of their positions in the market and how broadband has affected them. For instance, since Gabelmann (2001) found that the incumbent monopolist is an organisation that influences the way that broadband is diffused, such an organisation was sought for inclusion

A key question of the mission was regarding the factors that led to the successful deployment of broadband in

Korea. As the diffusion of an innovation involves not only institutional factors (King et al., 1994), but also cultural factors (Garfield & Watson, 1998), the behaviour and culture of the Korean people deserve consideration. For this purpose, qualitative research was selected. ‘Qualitative research methods are designed to help researchers understand people and the social and cultural contexts within which they live’ (Myers, 1997). Data collected mainly through interviews were supplemented and verified by triangulation with documents provided by host organisations and published materials, such as government statistics.

The interviews were conducted in the form of meetings between the mission team and participants from each organisation. The objectives and questions of the mission team were delivered in advance to the host organisations. This was to avoid wastage of any time and also to provide the host organisations with an idea of the types of questions that would be posed. In a typical meeting, the host company gave a presentation intended to answer those questions, and a session for questions/answers and discussion followed this. The meetings typically lasted for 2 h. An exception to the norm was the meeting arranged at KT; this meeting lasted for 4 h. The participants from each host company were all considered to be experts in the area under study. They ranged from appropriate rank and file employees to top management, and were directly involved in broadband business and research activities. For example, they were usually involved in the formation of the pursued strategies of their companies. The government officials who were interviewed had handson knowledge on the way in which broadband was deployed and in certain instances were part of the policymaking process. Open-ended questions were used in order to promote in depth discussions into the topics of interest. The number of participants in each meeting varied from two to six. In some of the visited organisations, there was some confidential information that could not be disclosed and this issue was emphasised at the beginning of the interviewing and presentation sessions.

The mission team consisted of two academics (the authors of this paper), five people from industry belonging to the telecommunication companies such as BT and Cable & Wireless, and a representative from a government advisory body on broadband (Broadband Stakeholder Group). The team contained individuals from diverse backgrounds: technologists, academics, business managers and consultants. This approach was adopted to try and maintain an unbiased approach. Our mission team had a variety of backgrounds similar to those of the group being studied (Wax, 1983).

## Broadband in Korea

Korea is noted for its rapid economic development over the last 6 years. In 1997, a financial crisis hit the country. To recover, the government vigorously implemented economic reforms. Additionally, sectors of the economy that would promote recovery of the overall economy were identified. Information Technology (IT) was considered to be a stimulating sector. The Korean government set the basic direction for a ‘knowledge-based society’ with the program ‘Cyber Korea 21’ in 1999 (MIC, 1999), which further accelerated IT development. As a result, the IT industry grew from 8.6% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) in 1997 to 13% in 2000, the highest proportion among the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries (KISDI, 2002). Table 2 provides some basic data on Korea.

Korea has the highest penetration of broadband in the world (MIC, 2002). The number of broadband subscribers in Korea reached 10 million in October 2002 (Figure 1), with about 70% out of 14.3 million homes connected at the speed of over 2 Mbps. While there are different product groups for residential and business, there is little difference in quality and price. Small businesses are subscribing for residential products. Therefore, the announced number of subscribers (supposed to be households) includes subscriptions from small businesses like shops and SOHOs. The latter are estimated at between 5% (from the meeting with MIC officers) and 15% (KT’s estimate given in the meeting). All of this was achieved in less than 4 years following the introduction of the first broadband services in July 1998.

From the interviews, documentary evidence and observations it was found that the widespread use of broadband has changed the way people use the internet. One of the sources used to support this finding is Figure 2 (displaying statistics for the month of July 2001), which shows that the Koreans spent more time surfing the web than users in any other country. It can be seen that the Koreans spent an average of 1340 min on-line per user per month. This was more than twice as long as for Americans (619 min) and approaching four times the average number of minutes spent on-line in the UK (382 min).

From anecdotal evidence, interviews and documentary evidence, it has become evident that broadband internet is widely and deeply embedded in ordinary people’s lives in Korea. Koreans have a clear perception of the benefits that broadband offers. When asked at the PC Bangs, during interviews, visits to the shopping centres and social occasions, Koreans emphasised the benefits and values they gained from broadband. Koreans perceive broadband as a necessity and have taken it for granted since it is there and very much a part of their daily lives (BBC News, 2002). As the number of subscribers to Asynchronous Digital Subscriber Line (ADSL) approaches saturation point, the Korean market is about to migrate to a new faster service – Very High Bit Rate Digital Subscriber Line (VDSL). VDSL is 5–10 times faster than ADSL, providing up to 13 Mbps, and is symmetric. KT and Hanaro recently began to promote VDSL service to infuse fresh momentum in the broadband service market. They are currently targeting the upscale apartment complexes for VDSL services. Owing to the fierce competition, the price is comparable to that of ADSL. The Korean government, which has recently finalised a new plan to upgrade the country’s network to 20 Mbps by 2005, is also keen to migrate to VDSL quickly (Yang, 2002).

Having obtained an understanding of Korea and the current status of broadband, the next section describes the main players and their roles in the deployment of broadband.

## The main players and their roles

From the interviews and other sources, it was found that the rapid growth of the Korean broadband market was the result of a combination of factors and had involved a wide range of players including government, access providers, equipment vendors, content providers and PC bangs.

## Government

Behind the successful uptake of broadband internet lies the Korean government’s strong policy drive. This involves a consistent policy for competition. A long-term plan for broadband internet infrastructure was drawn up back in 1995. More specifically, there were policy measures used to induce broadband network building on the supply side and to promote the internet and broadband on the demand side.

Telecommunication policies for competition The Korean government has consistently pursued telecommunication policies for competition, based on deregulation and market principles. In order to cope with rapidly changing environments in the telecommunications sector, the government implemented structural reforms in July 1990. Competition was allowed on an incremental basis in the basic telecommunications services and the market for value added services was fully opened (KISDI, 2001).

The internet market was also led by the same principle of deregulation and competition. There has been little entry regulation of internet services. The government’s ‘hands-off policy’ allowed any businesses to provide highspeed internet access and a variety of services only through notification.

Korea information infrastructure (KII) and broadband network Since the mid-1990s, the MIC has placed the development of a high-speed telecommunication infrastructure at the centre of its strategy for the ‘knowledge-based economy’. The government set up a comprehensive plan called the Korea information infrastructure (KII) in March 1995. The goal of the KII was to construct an advanced nationwide information infrastructure consisting of communications networks, internet services, application software, computers, and information products and services.

Recognising that the high cost of deploying new local access networks was a barrier to broadband roll-out, the government provided pump priming funding to facilities service providers (FSP) through the provision of loans at preferential rates worth US\$77 m in 1999 (Table 4). A further US\$77 million was provided in 2000 for less densely populated areas, small cities, towns and regional industrial complexes. This funding has been extended to include supplying broadband to rural areas and will continue until 2005 with additional investments amounting to US\$926 million. The focus of support is shifting from metropolitan to rural areas and small towns where operators are reluctant to invest.

Public money was also provided by the government in the form of prepayment for public services. This was paid back by the FSPs in the form of deferred service charges when public institutions used the infrastructure later.

Promoting the internet The Korean government recognised early on that it would not be sufficient to focus only on network deployment and that it would be necessary to match demand side stimulants with supply-side initiatives.

The government deployed a variety of promotion policies designed to boost internet use among the population. These measures included IT literacy and internet literacy programmes targeted at homemakers, the elderly, military personnel, farmers and excluded social sectors such as low-income families, the disabled and even prisoners. The Government set up the ‘Ten Million People Internet Education’ project in June 2000, which aimed to provide internet education to 10 million people via a range of different programmes. This promotional activity contributed towards the nationwide internet boom as 4 million people including one million homemakers were trained for basic internet skills.

Among the programmes for computer and internet literacy, the one for homemakers is an interesting example. The MIC set ‘homemakers’ as its main target (predominantly married ladies not in employment). Government subsidies were granted to private IT/internet training institutes for training homemakers, which allowed the homemakers to undertake internet courses at an affordable price. The programme was a success and created an internet boom among homemakers. The rationale for targeting homemakers was that they controlled the household budget and had a strong influence upon purchasing decisions within their family. Policymakers believed that without the homemakers’ commitment to the internet, its diffusion among households could be retarded. Most importantly, the programme identified the shared feeling among homemakers of ‘being left behind’ or ‘being ignored by their own children’, and so it stimulated a hidden demand for the internet, particularly for its use in their children’s learning (Lee et al., 2003).

The government has also been active in promoting internet use in schools. Discounts were offered for the provision of the internet in primary and secondary schools under a special agreement with KT. This has led to 100% of the schools obtaining broadband connections.

Disseminating a vision for broadband To disseminate broadband, there were a number of measures implemented by the government. The most frequently mentioned one (as stated by individuals the team met) is the government’s leadership with a vision. The government has consistently been pursuing a policy for a competitive high speed internet market. A vision and guidance for all the related parties including the users was provided. A consistent, strong message was passed both to the industry through its own initiating investment, and to the population using a variety of promotions such as IT/ internet literacy programmes. The disseminated message from the government was that broadband internet will become a universal service like an earlier invention, the telephone and become an essential part of life.

## Access providers

The Korean broadband access market is characterised by fierce competition where six companies compete. Among them, we briefly describe the broadband service development and strategy in two companies: KT, the incumbent operator, and Hanaro Telecom, the second largest provider.

Korea Telecom (KT) KT began to pilot ADSL services in 1997. However, the business case to roll-out commercial services was considered unprofitable. This was attributed to high equipment costs and the long pay-back period, which was estimated at 4 years. In order to achieve a break-even point, it was thought that KT needed approximately half a million subscribers who were willing to pay US\$50–60 per month for the service. Comparatively, the business case for ISDN at the time appeared much stronger. Although KT witnessed the successful launches of broadband via cable modem (Thrunet June 1998) and ADSL (Hanaro April 1999), it discarded its plans to roll-out ISDN. Instead it offered commercial ADSL services in June 1999 at its competitors’ price level of US\$30 (40% less than KT’s original price plan). KT’s subscriber numbers exceeded 2 million and doubled to over 4 million by March 2002 due to their existing networks and capacities of service and marketing, which was achieved within 1<sup>1</sup> years of the provision of the commercial ADSL service offering.

KT accredits the speedy success of its DSL deployment partly to the very dense housing patterns found in Korea.

More than 90 per cent of the households are located within 4 km from KT’s local exchanges. Most offices and half of all the households are in apartment complexes and dense commercial areas. With short distances, not only is one of the main disadvantages of ADSL technology avoided (speed of access drops substantially as the distance from the local exchange increases beyond about 4 km), but also economies of scale are achieved when offering an ADSL service. Therefore, the majority of the population in Korea has at least a 2 Mbps broadband access connection, and faces very little quality degradation in the service (Figure 3).

Broadband access is expected to be one of the largest contributors to KT’s growth. Gross sales in KT’s ADSL services amounted to US\$891 million at the end of 2001, comprising 9.6% of overall KT revenue (Table 5). For 2002, forecast revenues from ADSL service are expected to reach US\$1.3 billion and comprise 12.7% of the overall revenue.

Hanaro Telecom Hanaro Telecom is the second largest facilities-based service provider. The company was founded in 1997 and initially licensed to provide voice services (Hanaro has about 800,000 local phone subscribers, which is about a 3.7% share in the current voice market, the rest of which is dominated by KT). Hanaro owns a DSL service infrastructure, which the executives claimed during a presentation, and was further confirmed in their official documents, to be a state-of-the-art network underpinned by an extensive optical fibre network. This infrastructure has a significant presence in Korea’s seven major cities and in a further 73 cities across Korea. Hanaro also leases parts of the Hybrid Fibre Coax (HFC) network owned by Powercomm (a subsidiary of the Korea Electric Power Corporation that owns the vast cable network infrastructure across the country).

In terms of the entrants to the broadband services market, Hanaro was one of the first movers and was instrumental in moving the domestic communication market from the voice phone service to high-speed data services. The company’s broadband subscriber base reached an estimated 2.9 million in October 2002. These subscribers are split 50–50 between their ADSL and Cable infrastructures.

Hanaro’s fast growth from a newly licensed start-up to a second local carrier is attributed to the following factors.

\- Hanaro’s strategy of becoming a facilities-based service provider

\- Aggressive marketing and pricing to pre-empt the market

\- Government competition policy that supported Hanaro’s business plan

Hanaro’s original strategy was focused on a fast network build-out in the high-density areas. Hanaro’s total capital expenditure for this rapid network roll out was approximately \$4 billion.

In summary, service providers such as KT, Hanaro and Thrunet are competing against each other with their own network platforms. This competition between different facilities-based providers is one of the major factors that led to the exponential growth of the Korean market. However, there are some negative signs. Despite the generally buoyant mood in Korea over the socioeconomic benefits that broadband provides, two negative trends are observed: (i) it is increasingly difficult to increase the average revenue per user (ARPU) due to the fierce competition (and flat fee payment), and (ii) capital expenditure requirements impose financial pressures on most broadband access players. As a result, there is a possibility of consolidation in the industry. Furthermore, the privatisation of Powercomm will have a major impact on the structure of the broadband market.

## Content providers

In Korea, the creation of broadband content appears to be a natural progression from the creation of the broadband network services. In this regard, PC Bangs have had a major influence (see the following subsection). The existence of the broadband network has provided a route for connecting the customers and content providers. In turn, this has enabled the content providers to invest in, and provide more content suitable for broadband bandwidth connections.

Among the available contents, the most frequently mentioned as top attractions for broadband users are:

\- Online gaming, in particular, Massively Multi Player On-line Games (MMPOG)

\- Video-on-demand, typically for TV programmes (e.g. to watch an episode of your favourite soap you missed)

For each, we present one representative company’s business operations.

NCsoft NCsoft is the world’s largest independent on-line gaming company, specialising in Massively Multi Player Online Games (MMPOGs) that require high bandwidth for transmitting graphic data. MMPOGs are one of the most engaging forms of on-line gaming. From the interviews, it was learnt that the developers of the game have created a context whereby a ‘virtual world’ is created and hundreds of thousands of players have to co-exist with one another.

NCsoft’s ‘Lineage’ online game commands a 47% market share in Korea. Lineage is highly interactive and replicates various human behaviours – such as politics, economics and other social interaction. During the peak hours, up to 180,000 users play simultaneously. Each player’s actions can permanently shape that world. Lineage is less about dexterity and more about interaction with real human beings in real time. The developers, sellers and anecdotal evidence told us that due to the aforementioned features, Lineage has generated a massive following and has created significant network effects.

To obtain the game, players can either pay a monthly fee of \$23 to play from home or pay per play at a PC Bang for an hourly fee (about US\$0.80 per hour). NCsoft earns a fee from the Internet Service Providers (ISPs) who offer NCsoft’s games to their users. Originally 80% of the revenues accumulated by NCsoft were generated from the PC Bangs. However, as broadband become widely available, the population began to play the game at home. Subsequently, NCsoft has had a reduction of nearly 50% in the numbers of participants playing the game from a PC Bang. Currently, NCsoft charges the PC Bangs \$60 for an IP address.

SBSi SBSi is the interactive and multimedia arm of a private terrestrial broadcaster (SBS). The company develops on-line content from its own programmes. SBSi offers leverage to SBS’s existing programme content. That is, to monetarise the back-catalogue as SBSi has exclusive rights to SBS TV content. Once a programme is produced, the company owns the rights so it is easier to re-sell via another medium, such as, the internet.

Recently, SBSi began to charge for the video on demand service. A natural outcome was that the number of viewers to the SBSi pages dropped, but this imbalance is now being balanced as the number of paying customers are increasing. From the sales figures of the period September 2001 to June 2002, 58.7% are for TV programmes, and 23.1% for movies. SBSi charges a small fee for an individual programme. A charge of about US\$0.40 is imposed upon the viewers who want to view the content. This charge for the content makes possible the impulsive purchase of, for example, an episode of a soap that was missed on a previous day. There are on average 4200 paid users per day. Selling back-catalogue programmes in this way provides SBSi with revenue of about US\$390,000 per month (figures revealed at the time of the visit).

To obtain an SBSi programme, the viewers have to purchase a form of cyber money known as, ‘SBS Money’. These are available in units of $1 0 { , } 0 0 0$ Korean Won or 50,000 Korean Won. Pre-purchasing of this money in bulk offers a convenient payment method and makes possible the viewing of a programme at an individual’s convenience. This amount is then debited when a purchase is made or a programme viewed. ## PC Bangs

In 1998 as public interest in the uses of the internet began to increase, PC Bangs (PC Rooms) appeared. Similar in concept to the internet cafes, these privately run facilities provided members of the public with access to highcapacity PCs and broadband internet connections via leased lines. This concept proved to be massively popular with younger people, and the number of PC Bangs mushroomed to more than 21,000 in 2001.

At the outset, PC Bangs were swiftly identified by the youth, as convenient, fun and fashionable places to play and undertake tasks of importance to them. These tasks include e-mail, chatting, music and video downloads, file sharing, playing on-line games, and discovering and establishing new on-line communities. As the numbers of PC Bangs increased, they acted as a major market multiplier, causing thousands of new users to emerge, who were eager to discover the benefits of high-speed access and to consume broadband content. Crucially, they provided the first commercial market for content developers and particularly the on-line games sector, with major players like NCsoft relying on PC Bangs for 80% of their revenues at some stage.

The development of PC bangs to a large degree partly explains the reason for why young Koreans have embraced broadband. Most Korean’s were initially drawn to PC Bangs to play on-line games. The most popular online game then was Starcraft. Since these games required high-speed access, which most people did not have, they could not participate in the games from home. This demand was satisfied at the PC Bangs. As broadband became available to homes, these players rapidly subscribed to connections offering broadband since they were aware of its potential.

For many Koreans, the PC bang is not simply a place to use the internet and to play on-line games. Anecdotal evidence suggested that PC Bangs are a place for meeting friends and playing together. The continuing, persistent popularity of PC Bangs, despite the availability of broadband in the home proves that the PC bangs is a factor to consider when investigating the use of broadband in Korea. PC Bangs are used not only as a place for Web access, but also as a place for socialising.

## Broadband equipment vendors

In 1999 at the start of the broadband boom in Korea, the average cost of providing broadband access was estimated to be about U\$600 per port. Then the equipment required for the broadband infrastructure, especially ADSL-related ones, was mainly obtained from foreign companies. The Korean broadband equipment vendors were not ready to enter the market.

As with most of the incumbent telecom operators around the globe, internal debates within KT were occurring. The issue was to make a choice between ADSL and ISDN technologies. At this point, a majority of the equipment vendors were not committed to a full-scale development of broadband technologies. In fact, the equipment manufacturers in Korea were looking to KT for a lead. However, since the launch of broadband service by Hanaro Telecom in April 1999, a number of Korean equipment manufacturers had entered the broadband equipment market. These companies included large ones such as Samsung, Hyundai and LG, in addition to start-up companies funded by the venture capital boom in the same period.

Soon after the beginning of the year 2000, KT launched its ADSL-based broadband services. Nearly 7 million customers subscribed to broadband services within the next 18 months. This represented a growth of about 400,000 customers per month. There were difficulties faced by the vendors that included satisfying the demand of increasing numbers of subscribers and in supplying enough equipment to the service providers. As an example, KT was forced into having DSLAM (Digital Subscriber Line Access Multiplexor) equipment (exchange-based equipment to provide ADSL broadband services) from eight different vendors due to insufficient supply during this rapid expansion time.

This unprecedented growth in the adoption of broadband services in Korea offered timely opportunities to the local communication equipment manufacturers. They offered innovative solutions and equipment for the broadband market in Korea. Among them, Samsung has been successful as a key supplier to KT, eventually becoming the current sole supplier for DSLAM. Samsung stated that an important factor to their success was their innovation in supplying key software for the low-cost management of all ADSL-related equipment including the Customer Premises Equipment (CPE). Encouraged by their experience and expertise obtained within the local economy for the last several years, Samsung is now entering the foreign broadband market.

## Success factors

Following the data collection, coding of the interviews was undertaken and this led to the identification of categories. Six key factors that headed the rapid roll-out and take-up of broadband services in Korea were identified. These are: government leadership, facilitiesbased competition, the PC Bang phenomenon, pricing, some aspects of Korean culture, and geography and demographics.

## Government’s vision, strategy and commitment

The Korean government has a vital role in the development and implementation of a detailed and sophisticated strategy for broadband deployment, focusing on both supply and demand side issues.

In 1995, the MIC set out a vision for the transformation of Korea into a knowledge-based economy. This vision occurred alongside the development of the first Korea Information Infrastructure (KII) action plan in 1995. The plan quickly recognised the scale of the required financial investment to boost demand and the need to motivate the operators, including the incumbent. In 1997, a financial crisis hit Korea and had made the government consider options that would stimulate the economy. Solutions to this problem were viewed to be connectivity and informatisation. Consequently, these solutions were considered to be top priorities.

There is much discussion on the role that government has in deploying broadband access. The debate gets fierce, particularly when it comes to the issue of public money spend. While some are of the view that government funds in the form of direct investment are a key factor in the Korean story, others consider that it cannot be the main reason for success.

Although there is a consensus on the government leadership and commitment, there is a debate on this aspect of the government role. One of the directors in a service provider gave the following reasons why the government investment should not be overemphasised. First, some figures from the MIC tend to include KT’s investment as the government one. This practice comes from the legacy where KT is a state-own corporation. Second, the investment was made over an extended period of time (say, since the launch of KII in 1995), and on a wide scope of network infrastructure, not specifically for the broadband network. Third, even if we take the figures such as \$1.5 billion, which is an aggregate amount spent by the government, the amount is not substantial at all for an individual facility service provider, particularly when the amount is split into several companies. For example, Hanaro invested \$4 billion on network infrastructure only.

However, government investment certainly played a role. It acted as a catalyst that induced companies in the private sector to enter the market. This provided the organisations and industry with support and reduced the uncertainty related to such a large investment project.

Government has not only assisted in building a network infrastructure, but the adopted approach has also provided demand side stimulants by promoting the educational benefits in a society gripped by ‘education fever’. This was one of the major drivers of consumer demand for broadband (see the subsection later on). By establishing a clear vision and strategy, the government ensured a high degree of confidence and certainty for private sector companies.

## Facilities-based competition

It is often argued that healthy competition both between infrastructure networks and within each network technology (e.g. ADSL) has a pivotal role in the deployment of broadband Internet (OECD, 2001). In Korea, there was, and is, vigorous infrastructure competition within and between ADSL and cable modem networks. The Korean broadband access market is characterised by strong facilities-based competition in which the three main companies compete. Access is offered in two main ways. Either entrants build their own network in infrastructure competition or entrants use the incumbent’s network and resell capacity on it in service competition (Christodoulou & Vlahos, 2001; Michalis, 2001).

When KT dominated the voice market, new entrants focused on data services for their market entry strategies. Thrunet and Hanaro were initially able to gain market access by leasing network capacity from Powercomm.

Hanaro later added its own DSL networks to the HFC networks leased from Powercomm. They were able to gain rapid subscriber growth by targeting large multidwelling apartment complexes and other high-density areas.

When KT initially began to offer broadband, it achieved it via ISDN. It believed that its firm grip on the telecommunication network would protect its market share. However, Hanaro’s aggressive marketing strategy encouraged KT to consider entering the market as soon as possible and launching its ADSL service.

## Pricing

The Korean government recognised that to be successful, broadband access would need to be priced at affordable levels for the middle-income households. This was estimated to be approximately US\$30 per month. The fierce competition described above pushed prices further down and they have now stabilised at US\$25 per month for broadband Lite (2 Mbps including modem rental) and US\$33 per month for broadband Pro (8 Mbps including modem rental). At these price levels, broadband is seen as affordable and attractive.

## The PC Bang phenomenon

The PC Bang is perhaps a unique Korean phenomenon in terms of its popularity. However, it serves as an example of how external influences led to broadband deployment. According to the Ministry of Culture and Tourism, to which PC Bangs are required to register, there were about 12,000 PC Bangs in November 1999. Owing to the difficulty in specifying the business, there were many unregistered PC Bangs. It is estimated that the real figure was about 20,000 then.

The PC Bang can be described as a business model in which constant access to the Internet is open to the pubic through leased lines (Park & Lee, 2002). PC Bangs are equipped with high-speed leased lines and multimedia computers. They offer high-speed access to the internet at less than one dollar per hour. Internet PC Bangs were imperative in generating a nationwide internet boom. Many Korean users were first exposed to high-speed access to the internet using PC Bangs. The users became so accustomed to using high-speed services that they were not able to return to dial-up methods. This was one important factor that drove the Koreans to becoming broadband subscribers.

## Culture

Rapid internet diffusion in Korea can be attributed in part to some characteristics of the Korean culture. Education is a high priority in Korean society (Hofstede, 1991). Parents are very committed to providing the best education possible for their children, and spending a significant proportion of their disposable income for educational purposes such as extra curricular tutoring.

The Korean government capitalised on the ‘education fever’ as part of their strategy to stimulate demand for broadband services. As well as ensuring the rapid connection of all schools to broadband, the government encouraged the integration of the internet into daily school life. According to the Korea Education & Research Information Service (KERIS), relatively simple initiatives such as encouraging school teachers to post their homework assignments on their own personal web sites and requiring students to submit their assignments by e-mail could create a feeling among parents that the internet is a necessity for their children’s education. Service providers such as Hanaro and KT also focused on educational benefits in their advertising campaigns to encourage parents to invest in broadband connections for their children.

It is also often remarked that Koreans are technologysavvy. They are very open to new technology and tend to be rapid adopters of new and innovative products. The conjectured strongly homogenous Korean culture may account for some consumer herding characteristics and may also account for the rapid development of strong online communities based around the personal interests of users in areas as diverse as on-line games, hobbies, music, sports and even politics.

## Geography and demographics

Dense housing patterns deliver significant economies of scale for broadband network deployment. A favourable characteristic for the cost-effective deployment of the broadband infrastructure is the fact that 80% of Koreans live in densely populated urban areas. Both KT and Hanaro supported the view that these housing patterns were critical to the success of their broadband strategies.

It is particularly significant that 95% of the population live within 4 km of KT’s local exchanges as this made the almost universal deployment of DSL by KT both possible and practicable. It should also be noted that 49% of the population live in large apartment complexes. As a result of building regulations dating back to the 1970s, these apartments all have a single communications room in the basement making it very cost effective for operators to provide broadband access for the whole building. New entrants were quick to target apartment complexes as a means of achieving rapid customer growth.

The very high degree of urbanisation and dense housing patterns reduced the cost of network build (at a time when equipment costs were very high) and allowed the rapid deployment of new broadband infrastructure.

The geographic and demographic features of Korea are certainly a factor that contributed to the fast deployment. But post hoc rationalisation should not allow this to be causal. Canada, the country that has vast lands and low population density, takes the second position in the league table with 19.2 broadband subscribers per 100 inhabitants (OECD, 2002). This shows that while high density in a small land can be a favourable factor, low density in a vast area does not have to be an inhibiting factor.

## Conclusions: implications and further research

The rapid deployment and adoption of broadband services in Korea has occurred due to a number of factors and the determination of multiple players. Some factors are intended, others not. Moreover, many of the factors that have contributed to the Korean success could be classified as specific to the country if one is looking for reasons for comparative failure in another country. These include high population densities, high prioritisation of education and the strong role of government in industrial policy. Clearly, the Korean model cannot be copied wholesale into other countries’ strategies. Nevertheless, there are a number of implications that we can draw from the experience in Korea.

First, by setting out a clear vision and strategy the Korean government ensured a high degree of confidence and certainty for private sector companies investing in broadband. The government did not seek a detailed economic case to justify its policies and investment. Instead, its commitment was based on a belief that the country’s long-term economic development rested on its ability to compete in a global knowledge-based economy. On current performance, this policy appears to be bearing fruit.

Supply and demand forces work together in the adoption and diffusion process of IT innovations. A demand-focused approach is often regarded as more important than a supply one in stimulating technology innovation and application. Users must be prepared to use new technologies and services so that they can benefit from the enhanced capabilities. Therefore, the Korean government pursued a variety of policy measures to create internet demands along with support for network infrastructure. Examples are the ‘Ten Million Internet Education’ programme and provision of broadband connection to all elementary, middle and high schools across the country. This focus on the demand side contributed to the nationwide internet boom in Korea altogether with PC Bangs, on-line games, on-line stock trading, and so on.

The Korean case also illustrates that if the demand for broadband internet access can be matched by cultural expectations, such as the Korean emphasis on education, then diffusion can be rapid. When the MIC set up internet promotion policies, they identified the cultural demand on the internet, particularly among ladies.
