# Strategic aspirations for net-enabled business

Gary Hackbarth<sup>1</sup>, William J. Kettinger<sup>2</sup>

## Abstract

The net-enabled business innovation cycle (NEBIC) model describes a path by which firms employ dynamic capabilities to leverage net-enablement. Some firms strategically aspire to follow this path in a more gradual fashion striving for business process improvements (incremental strategy) while others aspire to exploit rapidly net-enablement to achieve business innovation (leapfrogging strategy) that offers completely new market opportunities. Study results suggest that firms adopt accelerated leapfrogging strategies when faced with more severe external competitive pressures. This combined with strong leadership, a propensity to embrace internal user involvement, IT maturity, and an accommodating firm structure, as indicated by path accelerators, result in higher aspirations for business innovation. Firms shying away from leapfrogging strategies tend to protect existing customers and employees from more radical changes. These firms sometimes lacked the internal capability to enact more aggressive strategies and thus had to acquire the necessary capabilities before aspiring for business innovation. European Journal of Information Systems (2004) 13, 273–285.

Keywords: Net enablement; dynamic capabilities; NEBIC; integration; leapfrogging strategy; incremental strategy; electronic commerce; organizational readiness

## Introduction

In a competitive environment of unabated technological change, firms attempt to leverage the capabilities of digital networks to facilitate netenabled business processes. The resultant Net-Enabled Organization (NEO) uses technology and associated architectures to access customers online, integrate supply-chain partners in real-time, realize efficiencies in internal operations and offer new digital products and services (Wheeler, 2002; Ash & Burn, 2003; Daniel & Wilson, 2003; Zhu et al., 2003).

Firms rushing to install net-enabling technologies in the absence of a welldirected strategy sometimes follow a path to failure. The dot.com failures of the early 2000s epitomize firms that leaped into completely new areas of business counting on overly enthusiastic IT strategies but never finding markets for their poorly conceived business concepts. Such over estimation of the strategic contribution of IT can lead to miscalculations in anticipating business growth, difficulty managing cash, and an inability to forecast correctly changing business environments. Equally poignant are the failures that come to firms that chart a course of incremental improvement that is too slow to match the market dominance of competitors.

While there are failures, success stories also exist. During the economic turbulence of the late 1990s, many brick-and-mortar firms adopted and gradually integrated the Internet into their business processes to evolve incrementally into brick-and-click firms (e.g., Sainbury’s, Harrods, FNAC, and ALDI). Alternatively, companies like TESCO (who moved from being a grocery retailer to a distributor of a broad range of financial, clothing, electronics, and other informational based products) have chosen to innovate rapidly to transform their business and markets via their net-enabled infrastructure. What this suggests is that, based on the promise of netenablement, some firms aspire to an incremental ‘business improvement strategy’ while others desire a more dramatic ‘business innovation strategy’. In essence, while some firms ‘walk’ their incremental/business improvement path to business value, others firms aspire to ‘leap’ to a business innovation. They do this in the hope of dominating a new market as a first mover, to exit a troubled industry, to establish new lines of business, or to reach completely new markets. If successful, the resultant net-enabled firm is better able to execute transactions, rapidly exchange information, and rejuvenate business processes faster and more efficiently (Wigand, 1995).

To better understand the strategic target and associated paths of net-enablement aspirations, we have adapted the work of Wheeler (2002) and his net-enabled business innovation cycle (NEBIC) model. Founded on the theoretical premise of dynamic capabilities (Zott, 2003; Collis, 1994; Teece et al., 1997; Helfat and Peteraf, 2003; Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000), Wheeler (2002) states that the NEBIC is a ‘Holistic Theory’ encompassing a full range of organizational actions from recognizing emerging IT to measuring the customer value created through network enablement. Clearly, firms differ in their dynamic capabilities to integrate, build and reconfigure internal and external competencies as a consequence of net-enablement (Zollo & Winter, 2002; Daniel & Wilson, 2003; Sambamurthy et al., 2003). From a strategic perspective, this difference is in part due to differing strategic aspirations. Aspirations are a kind of process vision that is followed by the design of business processes that leverage innovations that will earn customer value in the future (Davenport, 1993: Davenport & Stoddard, 1994; Kettinger et al., 1997)). This leads us to our research question: What are the distinguishing characteristics of firms with different strategic aspirations for net-enablement?

In the next section, we describe the adaptive transformation net-enablement model whereby firms’ either aspire to implement incrementally an IT strategy to match economic opportunities or leapfrog to a business innovation strategy. The presentation includes a brief discussion of dynamic capability theory, the adaptive transformation net-enablement model, strategic aspirations and path accelerators followed by a discussion of the study methodology, results and implications.

## Aspirations and the NEBIC model

## Dynamic capabilities

Firms innovate and alter processes in response to external environmental forces in concert with their internal capabilities (Johnston & Carrico, 1988). Danneels (2002) found that firms could be divided between those that met competitive challenges drawing on existing capabilities or those that aspire to capabilities they do not currently possess. Often, when pressed to change, firms stretch their existing capabilities to solve problems they may be ill-equipped for in order to maintain their competitive position. They do this by developing, escalating, and retaining dynamic capabilities that hone internal technological, organizational, and managerial processes within the firm such that they can organize to counter the competition effectively (Teece et al., 1997). Firms may also gain capabilities unrelated to their core capabilities through merger or acquisition (Chandler, 1991). Eisenhardt & Martin (2000) argue that these dynamic capabilities are sets of specific and identifiable enabling processes such as product development, strategic decision making, and alliancing that are neither vague nor tautological (Eisenhardt & Martin, 2000). Furthering this concept, Zahra & George (2002a, b) state that a firm’s routines or processes can form unique dynamic capabilities, which allow organizations to make strategic changes that give them the flexibility to operate in dynamic markets. Ultimately, making strategic choices about dynamic capabilities involves long-term commitments to specialized resources (Winter, 2003). For example, firms must decide whether to apply newly developed technical competences to existing lines of business or to go after new markets through a business innovation strategy (Danneels, 2002).

## Net-enabled business innovation cycle

Straub & Watson (2001) call for research in the area of net-enabled strategy to explain how organizations use enabling information to support, enhance, differentiate, and substitute technology for physical processes. To this end, Wheeler (2002) proposed the NEBIC to measure, predict, and understand a firm’s capability to create customer value using digital networks. This holistic theory suggests that firms follow an ongoing cycle of developing and exploiting dynamic capabilities for IT selection, matching IT to current economic opportunities through reengineered business processes, exploiting IT to achieve new business innovation, and by continuously using IT to assess customer value. Given the right set of capabilities, firms will transition along the NEBIC path as they proceed through business improvement and business innovation strategies. As organizations learn from their customers’ responses, this cycle repeats itself. Zahra & George (2002a, b) agreed with Wheeler (2002) and reiterated that the way in which an organization sets up, manages, and integrates net-enabled innovation is the real key to customer value creation. Sambamurthy et al. (2003) further suggests that information technology investments and dynamic capabilities influence firm performance through differences in organizational capabilities, entrepreneurial action, and co-evolutionary adaptation. Finally, Daniel & Wilson (2003) recognized that firms attempting ebusiness transformation make use of two distinct groups of dynamic capabilities that either work to better integrate the existing business or innovate in new opportunity areas. Our work is an extension of these studies by moving into the issue of strategic aspiration for either an ‘integrative’ or ‘innovative’ ebusiness transformation and mapping the path of how this occurs.

The adaptive transformation net-enablement model shown in Figure 1 is an adaptation of the NEBIC. The center of Figure 1 explicitly shows the four stages of netenablement progression described by Wheeler (2002). These four stages represent evolving capabilities of the firm to integrate and transform net-enabling technologies internally to the organization and externally into their marketspace. Organizational learning, shown by the dotted black arrows, evaluates and advocates further innovation of net-enabling technology to internal or external business processes to achieve even greater customer value. The progression of dynamic capabilities development and exploitation moves towards increased customer value, beginning with choosing enabling technologies (ET). Enabling technologies might include commercially available technology that targets specific business applications or market segments or they may develop proprietary solutions. Organizations often first target localized IT capabilities and expertise. In essence, during this first stage, firms tend to experiment with technologies to see what they can do to create islands of digital applications not tightly tied to any clearly articulated business strategy (Kettinger & Hackbarth, 2000; Williams, 2002).

As net-enabling (NE) technologies prove themselves, firms begin to more systematically match IT to specific economic opportunities (EOs) (Wheeler, 2002). The heart of the matching dynamic capability is to more effectively distill net-enabled technologies, current business strategy, and existing environmental conditions into strategic and tactical business options (Wheeler, 2002). This occurs through both vertical and horizontal integration of information within existing firm processes, usually across functional lines of control (Kettinger and Grover, 1995). In this way, organizations tend to first seek internal integration, with a primary focus on the business process improvement. For example, a firm might reengineer its internal processes to support an online ordering and payment system to cut costs and provide customers with more convenience. As capabilities grow, firms begin to focus on reconfiguring products, services, sales channels, and sourcing. ‘Business innovation’ capabilities empower relationships by cross-functional and interorganizational expansion to involve customers, suppliers, and partners in a more interactive association. Firm’s aspiring to these more dramatic changes in the status quo look to a competence in using net-enabling technologies for more radical changes in their business conduct including design of completely new ways of meeting existing customers needs, reaching new markets for existing products, or establishing new lines of products or services.

## Strategic aspirations

Driving a firm’s net-enabling capabilities in our model (see Figure 1) are a firm’s net-enablement aspirations. Aspirations are the focus and desired outcome of a netenablement strategy. They represent a longer-range strategy that drives an operational strategy (Steiner, 1979). As a firm gains, or perceives to possess netenablement capabilities, it is likely to have higher aspirations as to what can be done to leverage information benefits that emerge. Realization of firms’ strategic aspirations may come through direct acquisition, internal development or a sequenced incorporation of capabilities needed to exploit market vulnerabilities that gain market share (Porter, 1980). Similar to the past strategic planning literature that has recognized the notion of intended strategic outcome (e.g. Mintzberg’s (1973) ‘intended strategy’), aspirations are more than just goals and objectives but represent the ambitions, needs, and wants of the firm’s leadership and employees.

As organizations first attempt to articulate their aspirations, they look at NE technologies in an effort to understand them and the implications of their use. Business improvement aspirations seek to leverage netenablement to improve internal business processes primarily around efficacy. Business innovation aspirations seek to leverage net-enabling technology with customers and suppliers to innovate new business opportunities and revenue schemes, often in completely new lines of business. Increased customer value is a consequence of following strategic aspirations based on complementary capabilities that propel a firm rapidly through the NEBIC model.

Aspirations in Figure 1 represent a firm’s strategic desires or ambition to develop and/or exploit a dynamic capability associated with net-enablement. We suggest firms differ in the aggressiveness of their net-enablement aspirations. These differences can be categorized as two alternative aspirations: the ‘incremental strategy’ and the ‘leapfrogging strategy’. Business improvement aspirations are measured incrementally and suggest an incremental strategy that systematically adds NE capabilities to improve internal business processes and leverage economic opportunities. Firms aspire to business improvement. This approach tends to follow an incremental ‘walk’ rather than a more radical ‘leap’.

Business improvement aspirations present a lower risk to firms by using applications that tend to support and automate transactions (Farbey et al., 1995; Daniel & Wilson, 2003). There is time to determine the acceptability of new technologies and lead-time to develop new products, marketing initiatives, and supply-chain configurations (Wheeler, 2002). Davenport (2002) suggests this type of process improvement plays a big role in shortterm cost reduction by fine-tuning key processes and chopping out nonvalue-adding activities. These NE process applications tend to serve tactical objectives, which improve cycle times or cut operating costs (Gebauer & Scharl, 1999; Ash & Burn, 2003). For example, getting customers to agree to use online selfservice to cut labor costs is a process improvement (Scheer & Habermann, 2000). As firms aspiring to process improvement can assess the effectiveness of various ET applications in a measured fashion, a lower risk exit strategy exists for aspirations that do not pan out. In this way, firms can build on their internal capabilities and move toward higher business innovation aspirations in the same paced and structured way over time.

Alternatively, a leapfrogging strategy emerges for firms who have the requisite capability to match economic opportunities (business improvement) but desire to move very quickly past business improvement to achieve business innovation. For example, they may sense the immediacy of market opportunities and seek to move into a new and untested business model made possible via digital networks (Sambamurthy et al., 2003). Managers possessing such business innovation aspirations may believe that NE is a fundamental source of business scope reconfiguration needed to redefine the ‘rules of the game’ (Venkatraman, 1994; Boonstra, 2003). Firms pursing business innovation aspirations are willing to reconfigure products, services, sales channels, and supply chains to gain or regain a competitive advantage rapidly. They may attempt to confront obstacles, such as functional area turf battles head-on, moving rapidly to remove hindrances to their objectives. As an example, firms like Yahoo!, utilize the concept of continuous morphing to describe the comprehensive ongoing transformations they need to regenerate their transient competitive advantage (Rindova & Kotha, 2001).

## Competitive pressures and path accelerators

A firm’s aspirations for net-enablement are influenced by the strategic decision maker’s perceptions of the interplay between the external competitive environment and internal firm dynamics. As firms scan the environment, opportunities may not be presented in an orderly and actionable way, thus if organizations lack suitable NE technologies, or complementary internal or external resource enablers, they may not be able to see all the economic possibilities, react to a hypercompetitive environment, or initiate desired strategic moves. Firms may face such conditions as IT immaturity, managerial indecisiveness, internal political resistance, or reluctance from customers and/or suppliers. Thus, the presence of unique internal and external conditions complementary to the introduction of NE technology will help determine how rapidly a firm can successfully move up the path toward business innovation. Specifically, perceptions of external competitive pressures help determine the urgency and extent of competitive action deemed necessary (Zhu et al., 2003), while perceived internal conditions factors (such as management’s commitment to change) moderate strategic aspirations for NEBIC path movement.

In terms of external competitive pressures, firms scanning the environment must evaluate barriers to entry, customer power, and supplier power affecting entry into their own markets and entry into the new markets (Porter, 1985, 1990). Those ascribing to a strategic marketing approach stress early market share gains to jump far ahead of the competition (Lint & Pennings, 1999). Latecomers may aspire to leapfrogging early movers via technological leapfrogging strategies (Cho et al., 1998). The existence or absence of netenabling technologies interplay with the needed response to these external competitive pressures and influence the aspirations for NEBIC path movement. Clearly, firms may react too slowly to external competitive pressures or innovate too quickly beyond their ability to leverage resources and assimilate net-enabling effectively. This study will examine the following perceived competitive factors: market share, barriers to entry, supplier power, and customer power. This will be discussed further in the Methods and Results sections.

Internal path accelerators are the presence of internal conditions that facilitate the integration of net-enablement technology such as management’s commitment to change and willingness to impact people, the maturity of the IT infrastructure and in-house IT expertise, employees comfort level with new technologies, and the overall cultural capacity for change. The presence of these conditions can facilitate the possibility of a quicker path move to business innovation and hence heighten strategic aspirations (Daniel & Wilson, 2003). Senior managers reason that if their firm possesses the right path accelerator conditions and net-enabling technology, they may aspire to a leapfrogging strategy designed to leverage these capabilities before their competitors.

Past research has identified factors that act to accelerate business transformation. Venkatraman (1994) looked at managerial leadership, IT connectivity, managerial resistance, financial constraints as enablers and inhibitors of evolution levels of transformation. Fink (1998) generalized accelerators into three main groups: (1) organizational culture and suitable IT, (2) sufficient internal resources, and (3) evaluation of the external environment. Sawy (1985) looked at three stakeholder groups affecting transformation: top management, computer services, and variety of users. Other accelerators facilitating path movement include organizational structural flexibility (Teo et al., 2003), employee skills (Caldeira & Ward, 2003; Heine et al., 2003), process management competence (Kettinger & Teng, 1998), organizational factors (Damanpour, 1987), and work environment and politics (Parker et al., 1995). Yetton et al. (1994) suggested a model of strategic change and fit around structure, user involvement, management roles and strategy. Chatterjee et al. (2002) identified three significant meta-structuring actions of management affecting the assimilation of technology like net-enablement: top management championship, strategic investment rationale, and extent of coordination. Bate et al. (2000) integrated many similar factors into a holistic model of transformational change around structure, leadership, and culture. In sum, past literature suggests that a firm’s management leadership, user involvement, structure, and the suitability of IT reflect internal capabilities that influence the aspirations for either an incremental or more radical transformational strategy. These factors will be examined in this study as internal path accelerator factors as will be discussed in the next sections.

## Methods

Forty firms in the Southeastern part of the United States that had recently completed an eBusiness strategy consulting engagement using the same consulting methodology were contacted to participate in the study. Firms were chosen based on their willingness to allow the results of their eBusiness strategy consulting report to be considered input data for the study. Additionally, each firm senior executive (typically the CEO, CFO and other senior personnel) were asked to communicate what eBusiness strategies they aspired to and what technologies and accelerators were necessary to achieve their aspirations. All 40 firms agreed to participate, but of the group, only 28 firms could clearly articulate which strategy (either LeapFrogging or Incremental) they were aspiring to follow. Additionally, an independent assessment of each firms consulting report by teams of graduate students independently judged whether each firm intended to follow an incremental or leapfrogging strategy. Thus, this study was limited to these 28 firms. While the names of firms cannot be divulged because of proprietary agreements, the 28 firms represent companies in high-tech (electronic procurement systems, insurance, trade data, wireless communications, telephone serves provider, etc.) as well as low-tech industries (brick manufacturer, grocery distributor, building products manufacturer, etc.). Figure 2 lists each firm, provides a tracking number, current strategy, aspired changes, strategy type, and intended payoffs flowing from their aspirations.

Executives from each firm were interviewed with open ended as well as a standardized protocol – see Figure 3. In addition to the interviews, the standardized consulting format of each firm’s eBusiness strategy consulting report assisted the researchers in identifying external competitive pressures and internal path accelerators that helped shape their aspirations for one strategy or the other. The consulting methodology analyzed by the researchers focused on collecting construct and variable information related to external competitive pressures and internal path accelerators as shown in Figure 3 and discussed in the previous literature.

As mentioned previously, to further evaluate their qualitative data in some useful format, a standardized interview protocol with quantified questions was also used. The questions shown in Figure 3 generalize a wideranging data collection effort that included firm documentation and discussions with senior management. Each question was then evaluated by business graduate students (teams of 4 or 5) under the supervision of the researchers as part of their course grade in a strategic management information course. The researchers provided both written and oral guidance to the business students to ensure the same relative understanding of the end points (either low or high). Each individual independently and then as a team assessed each variable from either low to high and assigned variables or constructs a relative value based on a holistic assessment of available information gathered from each firm. Ultimately, we wished to develop a 2 - 2 matrix that would allow us to position each firm along a continuum of both Internal Completive Pressures and Internal Path Accelerators.

As shown in Figure 4a, firms are listed in ascending order by the summary score of internal path accelerators for each assessed variable on a 5-point scale. Firms in Figure 4b are listed in ascending order by the summary score of external competitive pressures on a 10-point scale. Summary scores do not determine whether a firm chooses an incremental or leapfrogging strategy, rather the summary scores reflect an independent assessment of a firms’ accelerator capabilities including an evaluation of the competitive pressures felt by each firm.

In order to construct the 2X2 matrix shown in Figure 5, each construct was weighted the same. Competitive external pressures (Y-axis) and internal path accelerators (X-axis) were plotted for each firm from low to high using SPSS version 11.5. Each number in Figure 5 corresponds to the firms listed in figures 4a and b. The resulting matrix is subdivided into four quadrants and is titled for discussion purposes.

As shown in Figure 2, 14 firms pursued business improvement aspirations using an incremental strategy. None of the firms aspiring to an incremental strategy felt they had adequate IT capabilities, 10 of the leapfrogging firms felt their IT capabilities were exceptional. Aspiring business improvement (incremental strategy) firms sought IT applications that broadened the scope of their operations. They wanted applications that were cross-functional and provided interconnectivity between business units. They were interested in cost reduction and enhancing current business practices. For example, the grocery distributor (firm 26) aspired to business improvement. They control a medium amount of the market in their region and feel barriers to entry are high enough due to high marketing costs and low profit margins. Their suppliers vary widely in size with larger suppliers exerting pressure on business practices and smaller suppliers readily switching to other distributors. There is little incentive to change because the small number of independent grocery retailers keeps buyer power up while the decline of independent grocers in the face of increased competition from chains has not become acute. Additionally, the limited number of suppliers allow for some control over independent store strategies in any given region. These factors have led this firm to be conservative in their choice of strategy.

Fourteen firms aspired to business innovation via a leapfrogging strategy as shown in Figure 2. They sought to change the way they do business and establish new revenue streams. Leapfrogging firms sought to identify cross-enterprise involvement with customers and suppliers, desired new revenue streams beyond their traditional business scope of operations, and required drastic improvements in customer service. The developer of electronic procurement systems (Firm 5) looked to become an application services provider industry in the healthcare industry, thereby creating a new revenue stream. The trucking firm (Firm 3) expanded its wireless communications network to provide more connectivity with customers. Additionally, they were investigating the building of automated loading systems to improve efficiencies.

## Potential leapfrogger

Insurance provider (A) (Firm 8) seems positioned to be a potential leapfrogger. ## Incremental innovator

Firms 20 (electronic connectors) and 25 (outdoor recreation) are incremental innovators. They are comparatively low on all the external competitive pressures and view their position as no better or worse than anyone else in the marketplace. These firms see opportunities in lower costs and the ability to offer more choices to customers through net-enabling technology. Additionally, they may recognize that they lack the internal path accelerators permitting a leapfrog strategy. Incremental innovators also often lack net-enablement leadership, which might explain their choice of an incremental net-enabling strategy. For example, senior management at the medical supply business (Firm 21) sent mixed messages about their commitment to an e-business project because of a perceived negative impact on the commissions of sales representatives. Both the staffing services agency (Firm 18) and personal services company (Firm 28) wanted to be more assertive in the market by having all records and communications online but cannot, because clients of both firms and their outsourced workers did not always have the IT capability to conduct transactions via the web with all needed parties. In essence, IT was not ubiquitous enough to support an aspiration for more business innovation.

## Leapfrogging innovator

Leapfrogging innovators are high on both internal path accelerators and external competitive pressures. The transportation design company (Firm 1) feels protected by high barriers to entry and they have few suppliers. Firm 1 has a culture of sharing ideas and adopting new technologies. They constantly searched for talented people with technical skills who will fit their organizations engineering work ethic. Furthermore, there is employee pride in recognizing they have been an industry leader for over 50 years. Net-enabling technologies are part of this firm’s culture. Leapfrogging is a strategy that maintains the barriers to entry and keeps the market share high. The climate control company (Firm 9) is being disintermediated by its supplier. The supplier is aspiring to business improvement by implementing a sophisticated third party ERP computer system in order to sell directly to the customer. In an effort to make the company valuable to the supplier, the climate control company aspires to a business innovation by purchasing and training on the same software, in essence, making them part of the solution rather than a problem to be bypassed.

## Discussion

An incremental strategy is not the only aspirational path to customer value – firms can, and do, aspire to a leapfrog strategy.

Firms aspiring for business innovation tended to exhibit strong managerial leadership, an inclusion of internal user involvement, a propensity for the innovation of IT capabilities, and the recognition to alter the structure of the firm to accommodate their business aspirations. Aspiring business improvement firms tended to have a more cautious management team and at least the perception of less capable IT. Internal users seemed more mired in the status quo rather than exhibiting some of the exuberance of management teams aspiring to greater innovation. There was also less thought given to altering firm structure necessary to achieve business innovation.

The internal user involvement of the firm can be a major determinant in how aggressive or passive a firm can be. In the study sample, the firms with the highest internal user support for change aspired to business innovation via a leapfrog strategy. It also appears that in situations where the outcomes are less than guaranteed, managerial support may not materialize.

Some firms aspired to leapfrogging because they believe they are ready to move up the NEBIC rapidly to transform their businesses to achieve dramatic payoffs. During prosperous times some organizations perceive the opportunity to introduce more innovations (Mustonen-Ollila & Lyytinen, 2003). The trade data firm (Firm 10) knew they needed to gain and attract online customers. Their competition was online and making money through volume transactions. The paradigm of the trading industry was changing and they needed to get onboard and they wanted to leap to a lead position.

In several cases, it was clear, firms had to transform or perish. The climate control (Firm 9), wireless communication (Firm 4), and trade data (Firm 10) clearly fell into this circumstance. Other firms like the trucking (Firm 3) and transportation system design (Firm 1) firms were leaders in the industries and had to continue to innovate to stay ahead of the pack. Firms like the electronic procurement systems company (Firm 5) wanted to gain market share and needed a leapfrog strategy to jump-start their business. It is significant that no firm initiated a leapfrog strategy because of declining customer relationships or customer demands for better service. Rather, aspiring business improvement firms valued their customers and chose not to rock the boat. This would suggest that competition for new customers and the need to protect existing customers drives business strategy over the need to utilize greater netenabling technologies.

There is some preliminary evidence that IT readiness is a necessary condition to support net-enabling strategies as Wheeler (2002) suggests. Three firms in our study electronic conductors (Firm 20), telephone service provider (Firm 22), and gas grill manufacturer (Firm 27) lacked adequate IT preventing their implementation of leapfrog strategies. They recognized the perceived threat but felt enabling technologies fell short of their expectations. In their opinion, ‘NE had not quite gotten there yet’. In essence, these firms were afraid to leap early, not wanting to squander resources.

In proposing our model, we are not suggesting an alternative to the NEBIC but rather we are using the NEBIC as a foundational framework to better understand firm aspirations and movement through the existing NEBIC model. Firms aspire to be bigger and better by invoking their vision of the future. This vision remains a vision unless new innovations and accompanying business processes lead to greater customer value by following the NEBIC. Our view is that internal and external factors conspire to slow or speed development of new innovations but may be mitigated by a firm’s choice of either business innovation or business improvement. It is equally important that firms learn to compete within themselves to acquire the necessary dynamic capabilities to compete successfully or else they expend unnecessary resources overcoming external factors outside their control.

## Limitations and implications for research

While we believe this exploratory research effort has uncovered fertile ground for future inquiry, we recognize that such an initial investigation has limitations. First, the firms involved in this study are from different industries with varying internal accelerator compositions as well as external threats. We selected this diverse industry set to explore our ideas in multiple business contexts, but future research might better control for industry differences. Second, while our accelerator list was based on past research, we do not contend that this is an exhaustive list and future research should explore additional internal and external capabilities that affect aspirations for enabled transformation. In addition, we weighted each construct the same when developing our summary scores. A different weighting may have shifted a firm slightly towards one axis or the other. Finally while we have tried to control for arbitrary interpretation by the nature of our structured qualitative research approach, we recognize that other researchers might have interpreted the case and interview data with slight differences. Future research might move this research from qualitative to theory testing. In addition, the relationship between strategic aspiration and implementation outcomes must be further examined.

## Implications for practice

To implement a net-enabled strategy, a firm must continually evaluate their current business processes and strategies. A firm must know whether they aspire to business improvement or business innovation. In addition to identifying why firms wish to leapfrog, managers need to know if they can. Before embarking on a netenablement strategy, manager’s should ask, ‘What do we have that we can utilize in this task?’ and ‘What is it about this firm that will prevent us from attaining our intended strategy’?

Benefits derived from deployment of enabling technologies increase as a company attains higher levels of interaction between their net-enablement and facilitating accelerators. Along with potentially greater benefits come greater costs in terms of assembling capabilities that must be in place or developed. We found that leadership, user involvement, IT readiness, changes in organizational structure, processes, relationships, and information flows were related to choice of strategy. Any company will probably need to develop capabilities beyond its current levels if it aspires to business innovation. A systematic evaluation of both the internal path accelerators and the external competitive environment of the organization will enable a company to develop a checklist of desired capabilities and identified risks.

Examination of the 28 cases used for this study reemphasizes the need for organizations to possess leadership that is willing to embrace change and permeate this feeling throughout the organization. Intangible characteristics like cultural readiness for change must be present as well. Recognizing deficiencies can alleviate headaches associated with the attainment of lofty goals.
