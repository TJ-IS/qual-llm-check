## Abstract

While much research has examined the use of information technology within process improvement (PI) programs, there has been a paucity of research examining the impact of such programs on information systems (ISs). The purpose of this study is to investigate the impact of PI programs on ISs. The study involves case studies in four organizations that have implemented PI programs. The findings indicate that changes in ISs were not envisaged as being central to the PI programs. However, the implementation of the PI programs had a significant effect on the roles and responsibilities of those people impacted by the program. Information requirements changed as a consequence of these responsibility changes. ISs were found to make a significant contribution to meeting these new information requirements.

## Introduction

n an ever-changing business environment, organizations are under increasing pressure to find new ways of competing (Tapscott and Caston, 1993; Eisenhardt, 2002). In response to the needs of such a changing marketplace, organizations are implementing programs aimed at radically redesigning organizational processes and structures (Hammer, 1990; Davenport and Short, 1990; Talwar, 1993; Earl, 1994). The last two decades have seen a series of IT-enabled initiatives from business process reengineering (BRP) to enterprise resource planning systems (ERP), which have raised the importance of strategic IT investments (Ross and Beath, 2002). These programs contrast with the every-day changes that organizations implement in scope and expected benefits. Kock (2001) uses the term ‘process improvement’ (PI) to refer to ‘improvements in quality or productivity of processes in general, whether they are of radical or incremental nature, or target local or interdepartmental processes’.

Davenport and Short (1990) argue that there is a recursive relationship between information technology (IT) and organization transformation, and that ‘each is the key to thinking about the other’. Enterprises that find themselves in competitive crises as a result of their outdated IT infrastructures tend to invest heavily in transformation programs (Ross and Beath, 2002). In general, IT meets the needs of organizational change by allowing a level of integration between steps within an organization’s value chain that previously seemed unattainable (Porter and Miller, 1985; Barrera, 1997). Such integration focuses on IT as a mechanism for improving organizational efficiency by automating, redesigning or eliminating organizational processes (Chatterjee et al., 2002). Yet most studies report little or no impact of IT on firm performance (Davamanirajan et al., 2002). As well as using IT to redesign organizational processes, some researchers have argued the importance of using IT to redesign information processing systems within organizations. Dampney and Andrews (1991) propose that the integrity of an organization’s information resource will determine the longevity and soundness of the corporation. Ward et al. (1990) propose that the most appropriate structure for an organization’s IS is one that mirrors the structure of the organization. Nevertheless, ISs as an information provision mechanism have not received due attention within research studies on PI. In particular, there is a paucity of research on the effects of PI programs on information requirements and processing within organizations.

This paper examines the effects of PI programs on ISs in four companies in the UK and Ireland. Redesigning roles and responsibilities in a quest for organizational improvement is seen as being central to the PI programs studied. Information and ISs are found to be key enablers of organizational effectiveness in such programs, as they aid the performance of people charged with new roles and responsibilities. However, the importance of ISs was generally not recognized by those designing PI programs. The researchers conclude that there should be more attention given to such IS redesign within PI programs.

## PI and IT

In a demanding and ever-changing business environment, organizations are facing multiple challenges and opportunities to which they must respond (Hayley et al., 1993; Coulson-Thomas, 1994; Porter, 2001). Competition has become more global; distance is no longer an inhibiting factor for firms engaged in trade (Allen and Hauptman, 1987; Alter and Hage, 1993; Levinson, 1994; Eisenhardt, 2002). In addition, the product life cycle is becoming shorter, therefore putting pressure on firms to decrease the time it takes to develop new products and deliver existing products effectively (Rockart and Short, 1989). This change in competition is forcing organization structures to become more flexible and adaptive to market needs (Miles et al., 1992).

IT offers the capability to redefine the boundaries of markets and structural characteristics (Keen, 1991; Scott Morton, 1991; Baskerville and Smithson, 1995; Chatterjee et al., 2002), alter the fundamental rules and basis of competition (Porter and Miller, 1985; Porter, 2001), and provide a new set of competitive weapons (Senn, 1991; Venkatraman, 1991; Porter, 2001). IT is also a key enabler of the PI programs implemented by organizations in response to the dynamic nature of the competitive environment (Hammer, 1990; Davenport, 1993; Martin, 1995; Ross and Beath, 2002). However, pragmatism has replaced the enthusiasm for IT evident during the final decades of the last century. Managers now want concrete evidence of how IT contributes to the success of a business (Serafeimidis and Smithson, 2000). While IT applications may be crucial to redesigned processes, IT can also act as a constraint on business process redesign, with its existing organizational legacy of hardware and software built to serve the processes and functional divisions of the past (Davenport and Short, 1990). ISs and technology are also seen to act as inhibitors of industry transformation (Howard et al., 2001).

Kumar and van Dissel (1996) document the evolution of thought on the relationship between technology and organization structure. They note that the early studies of the influence of technology on organization structure (e.g. Woodward, 1965; Perrow, 1967) typically took a very narrow view of the influence of technology on organizations. This view was based on the premise that technology was inflexible and therefore a given, and that the subject of interest was the impact of technology on the organization (Robey and Sales, 1994). A more mature perspective of the relationship between technology and organizations takes the position that while technology influences organizations, organizations at the same time, influence the design, implementation, and use of the technology to meet their information requirements. The organization–technology relationship, therefore, is better characterised as a mutually adaptive structuration relationship rather than the unidirectional relationship implied by the early researchers (Poole and DeSanctis, 1990).

Evidence suggests that the role of IT has changed from merely automating existing processes to enabling organizational change in response to the needs of a changing business environment (Davenport and Short, 1990; Hammer, 1990; Talwar, 1993; Martin, 1995). A common thread running through change methods is the enabling role of IT, regardless of the orientation of the PI program being implemented (Ross and Beath, 2002). This is evident for both programs of continuous and radical change. In creating a culture of continuous change, the deployment of IT can lead to efficiency gains as workers seek incremental improvements in work processes (Martin, 1995; Hughes and Golden, 2001). As part of a more radical business redesign project, IT can be used to radically reengineer processes, and increase process throughput (Davenport, 1993). The methods for changing an organization must be concerned with culture and the organization of human resources (Avgerou, 2001). However, they must also be concerned with how appropriate systems can be built fast enough to support changing information requirements (Martin, 1995; Hughes and Golden, 2001).

IT is an important raw material in the development of ISs within the organization (Earl, 1989). ISs play a significant role in the performance of organizations (Dampney and Andrews, 1991) by meeting the information requirements needed to support current activities and decision-making structures. PI programs affect all facets of the organization including structure, management, roles and responsibility, and information provision. If ISs mirror such organizational characteristics (as proposed by Ward et al. (1990)), it is reasonable to conclude that PI programs will affect them. However, the degree to which they are affected, and in what form, is unclear. A lot of the research to date in this area has focused on the enabling role of IT in PI programs and has not significantly considered the impact of PI programs on ISs.

## Research method

The objective of this study is to ‘investigate the impact of PI programs on information systems’. The focus of the study is on PI programs as defined by Kock (2001). The PI programmes represented in this study are large planned programmes that were designed to have a substantial effect on an organization. The definition of ISs is in line with that of Earl (1989) as being the demand for, and the provision of, information in organizations encompassing the information the business creates, uses and stores. This is distinguished from IT, which Earl (1989) sees as the raw material, such as the hardware and software, used to build ISs. The research questions used for the study were as follows:

1. What is the contribution of IT and IT personnel to organization change program teams?

2. How have the effects of organization change programs on roles and responsibilities affected the demand for information?

3. How have organization change programs affected the provision of information?

4. How have organization change programs affected systems applications and technology?

5. How have organization change programs affected the role of the ISs function?

Past research has concentrated on the use of IT in driving PI programs. This research genre has not significantly considered the impacts of such programs on organizational ISs. By addressing this gap in the literature, this study aims to explore relationships between ISs and PI programs that have previously been under-researched. The study does not aim to ignore the contribution of IT to PI programs, but rather focuses on the intentional and unintentional effects of such programs on systems. This study is classified as being exploratory due to the scarcity of previous research in the area.

Marshall and Rossman (1989) suggest that the following research strategies are suitable for exploratory research; a case study, a multi-case study, or field study. Deciding on an appropriate strategy is subsequently a question of tradeoffs (Stone, 1978). Yin (1989) proposes that case studies are appropriate when the object is to study contemporary events, and where it is not necessary to control behavioural events or variables. Benbasat (1987) proposes multiple cases as it allows for crossanalysis and the extension of theory.

A multiple case study research design was chosen to facilitate the collection of context data on the effects of PI programs on ISs. The case study approach is considered appropriate as it allows the researchers to probe the relationship between PI programs and IS in more depth than research methods such as surveys and experiments. Four organizations were chosen for study. These were chosen to represent different types of PI rather than any other factor. However, three of the programs studied were in large organizations (Table 1).

Each organization was selected based on their PI experience and their willingness to participate in the study. Having received an overview of the PI project, the researchers, in conjunction with the contact person, identified the key personnel in each organization. The primary data collection methods used for the study were semi-structured interviews and document analysis. An interview guide was used because it is more comprehensive and systematic for data collection than the conversational interview and more flexible than either the standardised open-ended interview or the closed, fixed response interview. The interview guide consisted of 104 questions covering the following topics; the nature of PI programs, the drivers of PI programs, role of IS in planning and implementing PI programs, transformation approaches, PI program details, effects of PI program on information demand and provision, effects of PI program on roles and responsibilities, effects of PI program on systems applications and technologies, and the effect of PI program on IS function.

Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 16 people involved in PI programs in four organizations. Interviewees in each organization included senior management, the IT manager, and other key change team members as shown in Table 2.

The interview data were triangulated with data gathered from PI program documents and IT department documents. The data were analysed using tables and data displays as suggested by Miles and Huberman (1994). This approach facilitated an exploration of the key issues within each organization and an analysis of these issues across companies. The organizations and the PI programs that they undertook are discussed below.

## Case descriptions

This section presents the findings from the four cases and analyses the data collected in order to address the research questions.

## PEPSI-COLA Manufacturing (IRE) Ltd

PEPSI-COLA Manufacturing (IRE) Ltd is a company that has undergone redesign of a key work process. As part of ‘Pathfinder’ — an organizationwide review of the firm from a strategic view point — it became clear that a number of key issues had to be addressed if the company was to remain competitive in its business environment. The order fulfilment process was identified as one of the most critical areas is need of change. PEPSI is involved in the manufacture of soft drinks concentrate, which it supplies to a worldwide market. Its Irish operation is based in Cork. The business operations of PEPSI had grown very complex, to the point of being unable to cope with the volume of complexity from a process control point of view. The volume of production was increasing as were the expectations of customers who wanted a more efficient service. An increasing product range in the marketplace, with shorter shelf-life, only served to compound the problem that resulted in the need to focus on order fulfilment.

PEPSI instigated a change program to redesign the order fulfilment process that had a number of defined steps. ## Guinness Group (Ireland) Ltd

Guinness Group (Ireland) Ltd undertook organizationwide restructuring programs that were designed to keep Guinness at the forefront of the brewing industry. Two themes underlie the changes at Guinness — cost reduction and organizational transformation. Guinness is a world class company involved in the traditional brewing industry and the importing of foreign beer. It has sites in Dublin, Dundalk, Kilkenny and Waterford, as well as several regional sales offices. The company is operating in an increasingly challenging and competitive market both at home and overseas. Management recognized the need to be more customer-focused, cohesive and effective in the way they do business. This study focused on two distinct change programs — Plan 2000 and the single company.

Plan 2000 focused on cost reduction through the identification of core business activities and the contracting out of non-value-adding activities. Its focus was on internal activity and its redefinition, with a view to achieving greater efficiency. The single company change program focused on organizational transformation and is more significant in terms of its impact on culture and attitude. It was a more fundamental type of change Plan 2000 because it attempted to affect a cultural change to remove the cell walls between the different sites and also to become more externally focused than in the past, giving particular attention to the customer. It was aimed at transforming the structure from being territorial to being a strong cohesive unit; from being slow and reactive to proactive and positive; from being excessively complex to achieving a greater degree of simplicity. It was based on the premise that growth in a market of reasonably finite size will be achieved by operating more effectively and being innovative. It is a never-ending journey because the environment is constantly changing. This study focused on both Plan 2000 and the single company change programs that were implemented concurrently in an attempt to gain a richer insight into the change process.

## Bank of Ireland

Bank of Ireland is a leading financial institution and one of the largest companies in Ireland, with branches nationwide and a significant number of subsidiary companies. The study focused on the redesign of the banks purchasing function. The aim of the change program was to ensure that purchasing became more central, cohesive, and clear. It was previously dispersed throughout the organization, but now resides in the new group purchasing department. The new group purchasing department originated as a result of a major cost review within Bank of Ireland. The nature and extent of its role within the Bank is still evolving. Previously, purchasing — which includes stationary, travel, maintenance, equipment, etc. — was mainly an operational procedure, but with considerable emphasis placed on sourcing, supplier management, contract negotiation, reviews and similar matters. Purchasing was done professionally, but without either a group focus or a global view of the purchasing effort and its impact on Bank of Ireland group and its profitability. The objectives of the new group purchasing function were to achieve leverage in buying, to achieve maximum value for money and to contribute to group profitability. The new approach provides focus for more effective groupwide purchasing.

## Dutton Engineering (Woodside) Ltd

Dutton Engineering (Woodside) Ltd is an example of a company that has completed an organizationwide restructuring in response to the demands of a changing business environment. The program involved changes in both management and work processes and resulted in an empowered workforce operating in a multi-skilled team environment.

Dutton is based in the south-east of England and operates in the niche market of stainless-steel fabrications. In the face of increasing global competition, the company was experiencing difficulty in delivering a quality product to the customer on time. In response to the poor economic performance of industry, the British government had introduced quality standards which they felt would solve the competitive problems being experienced by firms. Initially, Dutton sought an improvement in quality and received the BS 5750 Part 2 certification, but soon realized that focusing on quality alone would not solve their problems because it represented too narrow a perspective to achieve lasting organizational improvement. What was needed was a complete change in the way in which the company did business in order to meet customer expectations more effectively. This was achieved by creating a new organizational form that focused on the core business process of delivering value to the customer and moved away from the traditional hierarchical structure.

The change program took 6 years to complete. Turnover doubled from £1m to £2m — even though the working year at Dutton was 4 weeks shorter — and at the same time the workforce has decreased from 40 to 25. Dutton won the coveted Wedgewood trophy in recognition of its contributions in the pursuit of excellence — the smallest company ever to win the prestigious award. The success of the company is reflected by the constant stream of companies who examine and analyse the change that has taken place at Dutton and try to implement similar initiatives in their own organizations.

## Case studies analyses and findings

The literature (e. g. Davenport and Short, 1990) on organization change programs has emphasized the key role of IT. In fact, some contributors (Hammer, 1990) propose that it is this technology that is the basis for such changes. However, within the organizations studied here, the PI programs were driven by factors such as competitive pressures and operational effectiveness. IT played a support role. Each of the four organizations studied had a vision of what the respective PI programs were to achieve. According to the managing director of Guinness, ‘it was important to set a vision or things would drift — but also not to be blind to the real issues that develop along the way’. The vision in PEPSI was to ‘deliver a quality product to the customer’. Interestingly, none of the visions explicitly considered ISs and technology. This is an important finding, as it appears to contradict the role of IT as advocated by various proponents of PI. Nevertheless, as the visions for the PI programs came to be transformed into reality, issues arose that necessitated the consideration of IT solutions in a manner that allowed IS personnel<sup>1</sup> an expanded role in the change process.

Although the four cases represent different types of PI programs and contexts, we were able to analyse the material across a number of factors in order to answer the research questions. We have summarized these factors in Table 3.

## Finding 1: the role of IS personnel on change teams evolved during PI programs

A team with responsibility for leading and directing the PI program was present in the four organizations studied and included representation from the IS function. However, the form of this representation varied as shown in Table 4. In PEPSI and Guinness, the IS functions were seen as key contributors to the PI program and were involved in the change process from conception. One representative from the IS function was assigned to the change team in Bank of Ireland, but others had an IS background. Despite such representation of IS functions on change teams, it is clear that the role of IS personnel was not to be technology driven. According to the Technology Services Manager in Guinness, ‘we wanted to get away from sending in a systems analyst and delivering IT solutions’.

The role of IS personnel on the change teams was not pre-defined in any of the organizations studied, but as the PI programs evolved over time, so too did the role of IS personnel. The role grew due to the greater demand for information, which emerged as the PI programs progressed. Consequently, the potential of IS to provide this information became an issue. In Bank of Ireland, IS personnel came to be viewed as ‘subtle drivers’, as their expertise in applying IT solutions was called upon to deal with many issues that arose during the change process. This concept of IT as a driver was again evident in PEPSI where IS personnel gained influence over the change process due to their knowledge of the technology. According to the process owner, ‘People didn’t have exposure in the area (IT) — they might have a solution to an issue but didn’t know whether there was an IT solution — IT provided the answers.’

The growing acceptance of the role of IS personnel in a PI program was illustrated in Dutton, where IT was initially seen as a small element of a changing production process, but was quickly recognized as a solution to the problem of ‘informating’ the workforce. Similarly, IT was not seen in Guinness to be an important element of the PI programs. However, it is now recognized that many of the changes that took place could not have been implemented without the help of IS personnel, who gained a lot of credibility and acceptance within the company as a result.

## Finding 2: PI programs led to changing roles, responsibilities, and information requirements

The PI programs have had a significant impact on activity and structure within the organizations studied. New organizational structures were created in both Guinness and Dutton, while PI programs in PEPSI and Bank of Ireland radically changed operations. These changes have had a knock-on effect on roles and responsibilities within these organizations, and as a consequence, the nature of information requirements has changed as shown in Table 5. Such information changes appear to be related to creating a more empowered and participative workforce at lower levels of the organization. This empowerment has created a demand for management information at lower levels as a result of removing middle-management structures. Changes in information format and type are directly attributable to this demand change. While the changes in activities, structures, roles and responsibilities, were largely as anticipated, the associated effects on information requirements were generally not expected by the organizations. This is surprising given the intrinsic relationship between organizational processes and ISs.

At PEPSI, functional-based operations were removed in favour of process-oriented design with many work groups merging. The company has reorganized around order fulfilment and roles have become more focused to reflect this move. According to the Financial Controller ‘within the ‘supply-chain’ organization, everyone has been impacted’. Such a change led to an increased demand for information as people sought to get a better understanding of the new working environment. Dutton realized from the outset that roles and responsibilities were going to change significantly as a result of the PI program. PEPSI and Dutton did not anticipate the extent to which changes in roles and responsibility would lead to subsequent changes in information requirements. The team workers at Dutton now need information relating to business issues such as cost accounting and pricing and not just relating to production processes. At a management level within the companies studied, there was a subtle change in information requirements. At PEPSI, as certain decision-making passed to process workers, the information needs of management became more summary in nature and centred on key performance indicators. At Dutton, management responsibility has shifted and its role now is to serve as a resource for the teams. The creation of a new group purchasing function at Bank of Ireland led to changes in roles and responsibility for people working there, but this did not have the same degree of impact on information requirements as it did at PEPSI or Dutton. Responsibility for negotiating centralized purchasing contracts gave staff greater decision-making power than previously was the case. This led to changes in the demand for information.

In the case of Guinness, the changes in roles and responsibility were generally anticipated. However, the consequential impact on information requirements, which occurred mainly at management rather than lower levels, was not foreseen. The move to a single company organization intended to reorient the company to a single integrated customer focused unit. The new structure resulted in changing management roles and because of this the demand level for information increased as they sought a better understanding of the new business environment. At a work process level, increases in responsibility due to changing roles did not lead to greater decision-making power or have a significant impact on information requirements. While supervisory roles were eliminated and workers became responsible for processes, changes in information needs were driven by the production process and not by an increase in the level of decision-making activity. This is still controlled to a great extent by upper management levels. The disproportionate changes in decision-making power uncovered at Guinness may be attributable to its hierarchical organization structure, and despite communicating the desire to move to commonality in decision-making, social attitudes remain hard to change.

All four of the organizations studied experienced an increased demand for information as a result of the PI programs. Information has become more critical to performance. In addition, the nature of the information demanded changed, becoming more specific, relevant and timely. The scope of the information provided has also changed from operational, historic reports to the provision of more management-type information. At PEPSI, there was a greater demand for information as a result of redesigning the order fulfilment process. The information needs and requirements of people to perform their tasks changed. For example, workers needed more detailed information on new performance indicators, such as order status, to make better-informed decisions. As a result, an IS to provide realtime data analysis was introduced. The IT manager admitted that while they did consider the effects of the PI program on transactional data, management information issues were not anticipated. The IT manager explained; ‘we knew that redesigning the order fulfilment process was going to change the way in which people worked and the control that they had over the process. And yes, we also knew information needs were going to change, but we didn’t see the link between changing roles and changing information requirements at the time. It was an oversight really’. A similar situation was evident in Dutton where the empowerment of production operatives and the reduction of administrative staff resulted in the need to redesign ISs in an effort to meet the decision-making needs of workers. In particular these operatives have demanded more timely business-wide information in relation to a wide variety of internal and external indicators. As the Accounts Manager explained; ‘our welders are computer literate, can talk intelligently to the designers and understand costs from an accounting perspective. They deal directly with customers’.

## Finding 3: PI programs have had a significant impact on the provision of information within organizations

PI programs have had a significant impact on the provision of information within the organizations studied as summarized in Table 6. The impact is evidenced by changes in the level of demand for information and the type of information provided. In addition, the sourcing and capture of information has taken on a more important role in an attempt to meet the new demands placed on information. Furthermore, the format in which information is presented and its dissemination throughout the organization has changed significantly as a direct result of the PI program. The degree to which changes in the provision of information were anticipated in advance varied. However, none of the organizations fully realized in advance how PI programs would affect the demand for, and the provision of, information.

## Sourcing and capture of information

Evidence of the capture of information in a processfocused, rather than function-focused, manner to meet the increased demand for information was apparent in all organizations studied. This is because the sourcing and capture of real-time information became critically important to the efficient and effective performance of redesigned processes.

At. PEPSI, the IS department has contributed significantly with the sourcing and capture of relevant information. However, this need was not initially recognized. According to the Financial controller ‘we had no concept to be honestyit was only as the change program progressed that we began to address the issue — we should have realised in hindsight, to a greater extent where we were going’. Systems have since been introduced that track the data moving through the redesigned process to maintain an up-to-date account of order status. These data are then captured and presented through the medium of key performance indicators, which allow delays and bottlenecks to be removed with the minimum amount of disruption to the order fulfilment process as a whole. As the logistics manager explained ‘Tracking and capturing data from the order fulfilment process allows us to work with real-time, online management information rather than historic reports and therefore, response to problems is much faster than was previously possible’.

As the PI program evolved at Dutton, significant resources were spent on the electronic sourcing and capture of relevant information from the new work processes on the floor. According to the MD, ‘the most important single point in the business is where the customer meets the company, and this is handled at the shop-floor level. The teams make decisions based on the information provided’. This information provided is then manipulated using IT to comply with the information requirements of the decisionmakers on the floor. According to the IT manager ‘the data collection from the floor and the resulting provision of real time information to the team members on a variety of indicators such as machine productivity, inventory levels etc. allow more informed decisions to be made regarding orders’.

## The format and dissemination of information

The PI programs studied have had a significant impact on the format in which information is presented and its dissemination. Evidence exists of a move away from textbased presentation of information to a more graphical-type interface. Changes were also identified in the dissemination of information, to the extent that information was being made more readily available through the organization.

PEPSI experienced changes in the format and dissemination of information as a result of the process redesign. The provision of information became much more graphical and detailed in nature, with the use of trend data becoming increasingly popular. According to the financial controller; ‘workers find it much easier to analyse and interpret data presented in such formats, and this has led to a better transfer of understanding’. The Logistics Manager explained; ‘the use of more graphical, EIS-type information provision has enabled us to drill down through the process and identify any trends or potential concerns’.

The situation at Dutton was unique in that changes in the format of information presentation could be directly associated with the changes in the dissemination of information. The move to a multi-skill team environment has resulted in the decision-making process shifting from management to the workers on the floor. Because of this change, information flow within the organization has become more open. In addition, there has been a greater demand by workers for information that is relevant to the business as a whole and not just related to production processes. Furthermore, the format in which the workers want the information displayed has become much more graphical with heavy use of trend analysis.

The dissemination of information at Bank of Ireland must be examined from a different perspective due to the nature of the change involved. Before the group purchasing function was established, purchasing information was isolated in nature and related to specific functions. What now exists is a situation where information relating to centralized supplier contracts based on defined activity portfolios is available throughout the organization. Therefore, the dissemination of information could be described as being more open and accessible. Again there is evidence of an increased use of graphical data. According to the Purchasing Manager ‘the format of purchasing information became more user-friendly through the increased use of trends and graphs. This gave people a clear indication of whether progress was being made’.

At Guinness evidence of information dissemination, relating to the business environment in which Guinness operates, is less clear. An IS was introduced that provided information to managers on a wider range of indicators than previously, but this remained at senior management level and did not filter through the organization. Therefore, the increased exposure and awareness of management to business information did not have an equivalent effect on the dissemination of information. Moderate changes were made to the format in which information was presented to senior managers. According to the Technology Services manager, ‘greater use of spreadsheets and presentations tools have been incorporated into a more user-friendly interface where key ratios and trends are now being displayed in more graphical forms’. However, the changes to the format of information provision at Guinness have been modest in nature compared with the other organizations studied.

Finding 4: Systems applications designed to improve the efficiency of process management

Systems applications have been used in the companies studied to enable the redesign of processes at the production level and to improve the efficiency of process management. This was as expected given the traditional approach to applying IT within PI programs. However, ISs have also made a significant contribution to organization effectiveness by meeting the new information requirements caused by changing roles and responsibilities. This is important, as it exposes a frequently overlooked role of ISs in PI programs: ISs facilitate the effectiveness gains from information-centred organizational redesign.

## Process support technologies

The role of technology in process support is evolving and can be examined at two levels; process automation and process management. The literature (Davenport and Short, 1990; Hammer, 1990) stresses the importance of technology in automating and upgrading production processes and while there is evidence in the organizations studied to support this proposition, such automation is not considered central to the success of the PI program. However, at the process management level, the manual redesign and subsequent application of IT to certain tasks to improve both efficiency and effectiveness was regarded as an important step in achieving process flow.

At PEPSI, the extent of IS support was confined to work processes. However, IS has been applied to both production and managerial processes in Guinness and Bank of Ireland. Such applications have produced efficiency gains by automating and supporting work. As part of successive PI programs, Guinness has looked at available production technologies that could possibly increase efficiency in the brewing process. At Bank of Ireland, the management and tracking of all group purchasing activities is facilitated by database software, developed by IS personnel, and running on a Local Area Network. The PI program implemented at Dutton was centred on bringing the decision-making back to where the work is done through the empowerment of people. Systems applications and technology were very much a secondary concern.

## Information support technologies

The introduction of ISs and supporting technology has contributed significantly to meeting information requirements flowing from new decision-making structures. A summary of the systems and technologies introduced is shown in Table 7. It needs to be stressed, however, that the systems and applications were introduced in response to changing information requirements, and therefore were needs driven, not technology driven. Changes in ISs have resulted from a greater demand for information throughout the organization. Such changes were business driven focusing on improving flexibility and decision-making.

At Guinness, structural de-layering and responsibility changes led to the redesign of ISs to respond to changing information needs. This redesign was primarily to give users greater flexibility in meeting their information requirements. There was a push towards work group systems. According to the Technology Services Manager ‘the onus was on the IS&T department to empower the workforce by giving them the information they needed to make decisions in a team-working environment’. The use of Lotus Notes and other groupware tools has enabled systems to be developed to complement the changing nature of the organiation structure. More recently, Guinness has moved from a COBOL to an Oracle database environment in response to management needs for greater query-type systems with drill-down features. The provision of query and drill-down features enables managers to make betterinformed decisions.

## Finding 5: The ISs function in transformed organizations are more specialized in focus

The redesign of organizations is beginning to demand a change in the ISs function in organizations. However, the nature of such changes differs as shown in Table 8. Changes in organizational structures, roles, and responsibilities have resulted in marked changes in information requirements.

This has manifested itself in the changing nature of ISs used in organizations. Structural de-layering, empowerment and increased decision-making responsibility have led to the need for greater flexibility in the development and use of ISs. Such changes are seen in the expanded role of the user community in the deployment of ISs’ functions, and the increased awareness of organizational issues among IT personnel. Business ownership rests with users. They often drive systems development and demand control over some systems’ capabilities. IS departments are consequently becoming more specialized in the services they provide.

The IS functions in the organizations studied were key ‘enablers’ of the PI programs undertaken and in no way constrained the process of program implementation. They responded positively to new sets of information requirements as a result of changing roles and responsibilities through the provision of more timely, relevant, userfriendly information for decision-making.

At PEPSI, the IT department remains centralized due to a lack of adequate resources. What has happened though is that the IT department has become more specialized in terms of support and development of systems. This has been achieved by assigning IT staff to specific projects, enabling them to build up a body of knowledge in a certain domain. The IT manager maintains that the situation is changing to one where the ownership of systems development now resides at the user level and there is pressure on the IT department to meet their demands. ‘The sponsorship and drive for IT solutions to business problems is coming from people running the process and not from the IT department yIT might throw out a few seeds to get things moving, but it makes for a more effective working environment if the drivers for new software or systems come from the outside.

The skill sets of the IT personnel within Guinness are changing. To a great extent, the traditional roles of systems analysts and programmers no longer exist. Instead, people are gaining skills through continuous training to take positions such as project leaders and managers. The Technology Services Manager explained ‘these new technologies don’t give all the solutions — what they give is a different set of problems. By moving too quickly from one environment to another you lose the advantages already built-up. Careful planning is required at all time’. A new role of business information manager has become important in the more open IT environment that now exists at Guinness. The business information manager is IT-aware, but not a specialist, and reports to the IT department. Such a position exists in each business area and is the interface between IT specialists and users. It has led to the IS&T department becoming more flexible in meeting user demands for information.

## Conclusion

Concerns with organizational effectiveness dominate PI programs that focus on redesigning roles and responsibilities in the quest for organizational improvement. People rather than IT form the basis of competitive innovation. However, this study indicates that the source of such innovation from an IS perspective has less to do with competitive automation than with organizational informating.

ISs issues need to be addressed when PI programmes are being designed in order to ensure that an appropriate ISs infrastructure is put in place to meet new information requirements. The reactive design of ISs, such as found in this study, can lead to situations where more information is provided in addition to that which are already demanded. This could rapidly lead to information overload for some organizational personnel. The study revealed that the amount of information demanded increased and that the format of such information changed. It is also clear that empowered personnel rely more on ISs and have greater expectations of IS personnel.

ISs redesign within PI programs needs to ensure that such programs do not result in a reduction in the effectiveness of an organization’s ISs infrastructure. Systems personnel need a more defined role in designing PI programs. An important aspect of this role will be the redesign of information systems to facilitate decisionmaking resulting from new work patterns. However, it is unrealistic to imagine that ISs will be capable of continuous change without changing the basis on which they are currently developed. Further research in the area is required.
