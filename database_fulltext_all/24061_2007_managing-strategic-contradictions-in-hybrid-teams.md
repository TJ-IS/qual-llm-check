# Managing strategic contradictions in hybrid teams

Karlene C. Cousins<sup>1</sup>, Daniel Robey<sup>2</sup> and Ilze Zigurs<sup>3</sup>

## Abstract

It has become a common practice to organize work teams to include members in multiple geographic locations. In contrast to co-located teams and purely virtual teams, these ‘hybrid’ teams combine face-to-face communication with computer-mediated interaction. In this paper, we report a qualitative study of management practices in three hybrid teams in one organization. We adopt the theoretical approach of strategic contradiction, in which apparently contradictory pairs of elements can be managed by attending to their possible synergies or clarifying their distinctions so as to make balanced trade-offs over time. Our data reveal four sets of paradoxical frames in hybrid teams: remoteness–closeness, cultural uniformity–cultural diversity, rationality–emotionality, and control–empowerment. In referencing these paradoxical frames, teams engaged in three cognitive processes: (a) integrating to produce synergies between opposing tensions, (b) differentiating to clarify distinctions between opposing tensions and to balance trade-offs over time, and (c) polarizing to remove tensions between opposing elements by using one element to reduce the effects of another. Both integrating and differentiating processes were found to be instrumental to sustaining contradictions as interdependent dualities, whereas polarizing processes were found to preserve contradictions as dualisms. Our findings advance the understanding of managing strategic contradictions by showing how managers influence cognitive processes that paradoxically emphasize remoteness and closeness, cultural uniformity and cultural diversity, rationality and emotionality, and control and empowerment.

Keywords: virtual teams; hybrid teams; dualities

## Introduction

Information technologies are frequently regarded as the key enablers of work that is geographically distributed (Townsend et al., 1998; Saunders, 2000). Such work environments are commonly referred to as virtual, whether referring to teams or organizations (Schultze & Orlikowski, 2001; Robey & Jin, 2004). A virtual team is typically conceived as an interdependent group of people working toward a common goal but separated by a number of boundaries, such as those of space, time, culture, or organizational affiliation. Such arrangements require team members to communicate and to coordinate their activities predominantly through information and communication technologies (Bell & Kozlowski, 2002; Dube´ & Pare´, 2004).

Professional and academic discourse has identified many virtues of virtual teams (Qureshi & Zigurs, 2001; Schultze & Orlikowski, 2001; Kock et al., 2006). Virtual teams are composed of human resources in remote locations, thereby removing the need to transport people among locations (Apgar, 1998) and promoting individual autonomy (Davenport & Pearlson, 1998). Using such advantages, virtual teams may assemble human resources from different functions in different locations to serve customers (Lipnack & Stamps, 2000; Hinds & Kiesler, 2002). Although virtual teams offer these and other advantages, empirical studies have identified challenges as well. Several studies of virtual teamwork show that it is difficult for distributed team members with no prior relationships to develop trust. Although trust may develop swiftly in virtual teams, it is fragile and difficult to maintain (Jarvenpaa & Leidner, 1999; Paul & McDaniel, 2004; Zolin et al., 2004). Both positive and negative attributions about team mates are made quickly and based on scant evidence (Cramton, 2001), and cultural diversity exacerbates communication problems (Sarker & Sahay, 2004).

Much empirical research implicitly assumes that virtual team members never meet face to face (Kirkman et al., 2004). Consequently, less is known about hybrid teams, which fall between the extremes of never vs always meeting face to face (Kock et al., 2001; Griffith et al., 2003; Fiol & O’Connor, 2005). Indeed, laboratory experiments with virtual teams often treat face-to-face interaction among subjects as a potential contaminant or confound of the experimental treatment, which is typically the collaborative technology under investigation. For example, a review of research on virtual teams showed that, of 49 empirical studies, all but one of the laboratory experiments relied entirely on communication via technology, whereas about half of the field studies were of hybrid teams (Dube´ & Pare´, 2004). In practice, few virtual teams actually restrict team members from meeting face to face, suggesting a need to investigate the ways in which hybrid teams mix their modes of communication.

Hybrid teams face unique challenges that require managers to address a variety of potential paradoxes. In a paradox, two logically opposed conditions are stated to exist together, thus creating an apparent logical impossibility. Although paradoxes have been acknowledged and used for decades to gain insights into social phenomena, Smith & Tushman (2005) recently argued that they should become more central to the study of organizations. They proposed the concept of strategic contradiction to emphasize the importance of framing paradoxes in top management teams. The present study uses Smith and Tushman’s theoretical lens to investigate the management of paradoxes in hybrid teams. We pose the research question: How can managers and team members manage the paradoxes inherent in hybrid teams in order to enhance their effectiveness?

Based on our investigation of three hybrid teams in a single company, we identify four types of paradoxical frames in hybrid teams: remoteness–closeness, cultural uniformity–cultural diversity, rationality–emotionality, and control–empowerment. The tensions inherent in these paradoxes may seldom be resolved or eliminated completely, so they are likely to remain as tensions that managers of hybrid teams must confront and even use advantageously. We represent these tensions as either dualities, in which opposing tensions are treated as interdependent, or dualisms, in which opposing tensions are treated as independent. Our analysis demonstrates that managers address these paradoxical tensions by using information technologies and face-to-face meetings in ways that reflect different cognitive processes. Two cognitive processes identified by Smith & Tushman (2005), namely integrating and differentiating, are associated with team success, while a third cognitive process, namely polarizing, is not.

## The challenges of hybrid teamwork

In hybrid teams, members may be co-located and rely upon both face-to-face and electronic communication on a daily basis. For example, Zack (1993) studied electronic and face-to-face communication among editors in two daily newspapers, reporting that they used different communication media for different purposes. Hybrid teams may also be geographically distributed and meet on rare occasions. For example, Maznevski & Chudoba (2000) studied three teams in a global manufacturing company, where team members were located in different countries. Most of the teams’ work was conducted via electronic media, yet coordination meetings were scheduled periodically to assess key directions. As Fiol & O’Connor (2005) suggested, geographic distribution and reliance upon communication technologies are tendencies in hybrid teams, but their defining characteristic is the use of both remote and face-to-face communication.

Hybrid teams face unique challenges that are not present in purely virtual or purely face-to-face teams. Fiol & O’Connor (2005) posed a number of propositions linking hybrid team characteristics with member identification, suggesting that occasional face-to-face contact was insufficient to establish team identification that was stronger than subgroup identification. As a result, they viewed identification in hybrid teams as a serious challenge that could adversely affect performance. Additional challenges faced by hybrid teams include developing and transferring organizational knowledge (Griffith et al., 2003), managing conflicts (Hinds & Bailey, 2003), and making decisions (Maznevski & Chudoba, 2000).

Hybrid teams are also susceptible to the formation of faultlines. Faultlines in groups refer to the presence of demographic attributes that potentially subdivide groups (Lau & Murnigham, 1998). For example, groups composed of both male and female members might divide into subgroups based on gender differences. As more demographic characteristics align with each other, faultlines between subgroups may become stronger, presenting additional challenges to the management of group activities. In hybrid teams, faultlines may form based on geographic location and its associated demographic attributes. For example, a hybrid team composed of two co-located subgroups in different countries would introduce cultural and language attributes that subdivide the team. In addition, if the work functions in separate geographical locations differed (e.g., marketing teams in one country and manufacturing in another), faultlines would be further deepened by factors other than demographic differences.

Two general approaches have been taken to address the challenges facing hybrid teams. First, particularly in the information systems literature, information technology is proposed as a bridge that enables team members to operate across time, distance, culture, and other potential divides. Technology may promote communication through features that support more effective dialogue (Tan et al., 2000) and provide temporal coordination mechanisms that allow team members to manage conflicts more effectively (Montoya-Weiss et al., 2001; Saunders et al., 2004). Second, more frequent face-to-face meetings are proposed to establish stronger social relationships among workers who are usually separated from each other (Bell & Kozlowski, 2002). For example, Suchan & Hayak (2001) studied a successful virtual team that was initiated with a planned face-to-face meeting that incorporated team-building exercises. Team success was attributed to the trust established in the initial faceto-face meeting, team-based rewards, and a culture of open communication. Strengthened relational links have also been associated with more effective information exchange and higher task performance (Malhotra et al., 2001; Pauleen & Yoong, 2001; Orlikowski, 2002; Kirkman et al., 2004; Kotlarsky & Oshri, 2005).

Despite the apparent sensibility of these recommendations, certain difficulties remain. First, although information technology is hailed as the chief enabler of virtual teamwork, technology can also be a barrier to effective teamwork. Indeed, reliance on information technology in virtual teams may create conditions that increase conflict (Mortensen & Hinds, 2001; Hinds & Bailey, 2003) and reduce the amount of tacit knowledge generated and shared in organizations (Griffith et al., 2003). Thus, information technologies may be insufficient to bridge temporal, spatial, and cultural differences in distributed teams (Yoshioka et al., 2002). Second, increasing the number of face-to-face meetings in effect reduces the degree of virtuality in a team (Fiol & O’Connor, 2005). Although less virtuality may improve relationships among team members, it may also remove some of the advantages created by higher degrees of virtuality. Thus, teams that meet more frequently incur higher costs of travel across locations and members may experience higher levels of stress (Orlikowski, 2002). In effect, the challenges of hybrid teams are addressed in ways that often make such teams more similar to co-located teams.

The logic behind such recommendations is to remove the source of the problem, that is, by removing the tensions between opposing elements by using one element (e.g., face-to-face meetings) to reduce the effects of another (e.g., distance). The main theoretical premise here is one of dualism, which treats opposing elements as distinct concepts that compensate for each other’s effects (Jackson, 1999). From this perspective, management practices become compromises in which the advantages of geographic distribution are traded off against the advantages of co-location. In effect, the tensions inherent in hybrid teams are eliminated by removing their source: for example, the negative effects of distance are removed by bringing team members physically together more often. The management practices consistent with the logic of dualism thus sacrifice specific advantages in order to avoid disadvantages.

Rather than viewing management practices as removing the tensions in hybrid teams, an alternate approach employs the logic of duality, in which opposing elements are considered inseparable and interdependent. For example, rather than trying to remove the negative effects of spatial separation by bringing people closer together, remoteness and closeness may be regarded as a duality. Thus, effective management practices in hybrid teams would seek to have members be simultaneously remote and close. Such practices may appear to be logically impossible, or paradoxical. Thus, it is important for managers and members of hybrid teams to manage paradoxes so that benefits of hybrid work arrangements are not lost.

Paradoxes have long offered significant theoretical insights into management practices. Cameron (1986) suggested that organizational effectiveness is inherently paradoxical because an organization must possess contradictory attributes to be effective. For example, effective organizations might emphasize their past traditions while simultaneously experimenting with innovations. The tensions induced by these contradictory elements lead organizations away from concentrating on specific practices, even those with proven value, and instead direct managerial attention toward the ways in which contradictory elements can co-exist. To illustrate, Palmer et al. (2001) argued that the effectiveness of a virtual organization engaged in film production was associated with the interdependent co-existence of old and new management practices rather than the substitution of new practices for old ones.

In the following section, we introduce Smith & Tushman’s (2005) theory of strategic contradiction, which focuses directly on the effective management of paradox in top management teams. We adapt and extend their theory in order to analyze the management of paradoxes in hybrid teams. Adopting a cognitive approach, Smith and Tushman use the concept of strategic contradiction to explain ways that managers can recognize and accept the simultaneous existence of contradictory agendas. Smith and Tushman identify two cognitive processes, integrating and differentiating, to describe different ways to accept paradox in strategic decisions. In our view, cognitive processes that accept opposing agendas reflect dualities rather than dualisms. We argue that an approach that combines these theoretical insights can better depict the complexity of the management of hybrid teams. Our theoretical approach is discussed in greater depth in the following section.

## Theoretical approach

Smith & Tushman (2005) used their model of strategic contradiction to gain insights into new product decisions that balanced the need to support existing products with the desire to create new products. Their model introduces the concept of paradoxical cognition, which consists of cognitive frames and cognitive processes that together shape organizational action. Managers who are able to adopt cognitive frames that embrace paradox are able to recognize and accept the simultaneous existence of contradictory forces. These paradoxical frames create a basis for cognitive processes that can address fundamental contradictions, such as those experienced in hybrid teams.

Smith & Tushman (2005) proposed two specific cognitive processes that assist paradoxical cognition: differentiating and integrating. On the one hand, differentiating involves clarifying distinctions between an existing product and an innovation, resulting in balanced trade-offs over time. An existing product and an innovation are each associated with a separate organizational architecture and associated logic, and differentiating involves recognizing and reinforcing the differences in these architectures and logics. The underlying premise is that clearly identifying and articulating opposing agendas allows decision makers to allocate resources to each over time, so that both existing and innovative products can succeed. Integrating, on the other hand, involves the development of conceptual connections between or among different dimensions, so that the level of analysis is shifted from the product level to the organizational level. Integrating may identify synergistic solutions involving resources that opposing activities may share.

Although Smith & Tushman’s (2005) framework focuses on top management teams and the management of product innovation, their model can be extended to understand opposing tensions in hybrid teams. Figure 1 shows Smith and Tushman’s model, as adapted for hybrid teams. The model shows antecedents that lead to paradoxical cognition, which in turn results in team and organizational outcomes. Key constructs include paradoxical frames, cognitive processing, differentiating, integrating, and performance outcomes. Our adaptation of the model for hybrid teams emphasizes geographic dispersion and team reliance on information technology as antecedent conditions.

The cognitive processes of differentiating and integrating both seek to convert a paradoxical frame that consists of logical opposites into a frame in which opposing elements are interdependent and complementary. Rather than focusing on the boundaries between opposing concepts, differentiating and integrating processes guide insights into ways of combining opposing concepts. Social theorists refer to these different ways of thinking about paired concepts as dualities and dualisms. A duality ‘envisages stronger and richer interactions of paired elements than occur in a dualism’ (Jackson, 1999, p. 546). In contrast to a dualism, the elements within a duality are neither separate nor opposed, although they remain conceptually distinct. In a duality, characteristics that depend on each other may be in tension with one another, thus creating potentially paradoxical relationships. Dualities have assumed central importance in theories of human agency and social structure (Giddens, 1984; Reed, 1997), technology and structure (Orlikowski, 1992), socio-technical systems theory (Mumford, 1996), organizational effectiveness (Cameron, 1986), and organizational change (Leanna & Barry, 2000; Feldman & Pentland, 2003). Our purpose in using duality to enhance the model of strategic contradiction is to gain insight into the tensions underlying hybrid teams and the specific ways in which paradoxical frames might manifest themselves. Rather than expecting the problems of working across remote locations to be mitigated by more frequent face-to-face meetings, we can ask how hybrid teams could be both remote and close at the same time, or both culturally diverse and unified. If the tensions inherent in such dualities are examined closely, we might also reveal management practices that sustain dualities and enhance team effectiveness. For example, a team’s management might foster a close-knit spirit in a team that remains distributed in time and space, thus reaping the advantages of being virtual (e.g., inclusion of key participants from remote locations) while avoiding the negative effects of remote work (e.g., lack of trust or communication problems).

Prior research provides modest evidence of the value of studying dualities in work teams. Druskat & Wheeler (2003) observed the paradox faced by leaders of selfmanaged teams. On the one hand, leaders need to exercise control over decisions for which they are responsible, yet on the other hand they must delegate decisions to the team. In their study, the most effective leaders resolved this paradox by exerting both control and empowerment. A more classic example is found in McGrath’s (1984) review of research on group performance, which concluded that effective teams were simultaneously (and synergistically) both task and socially oriented. Although social activities might be viewed as reducing time for task accomplishment, research has shown that social activities actually enhance task performance (McGrath, 1984). In other research, Earley & Mosakowski (2000) investigated culturally diverse teams and found that they often formed a new team culture and identity that allowed them to be both culturally diverse and unified. Also, Salk & Brannen (2000) found that shared norms emerged in a joint venture between Japanese and German partners, at the same time that culturally specific patterns of interaction among venture members were preserved.

Because the notions of paradoxical frames, cognitive processes and dualities are new to research on hybrid teams, it is unclear what specific dualities might prove relevant in hybrid teams. Moreover, it is not clear what roles managers play in creating and sustaining dualities. Clearly, management is in a position to influence team practices through leadership, reward systems, training, and other means. Managers may devise programs for team building, they may orchestrate face-to-face meetings, and they may select and implement relevant technologies. The study described in the following section was devised to understand management practices that create and sustain paradoxical cognition in hybrid teams through the identification of specific dualities and dualisms and their roles in cognitive processing.

## Method

## Research design

The research design was a comparative case study of three hybrid teams in a single organization. Because the teams were nested in one organization, they shared many commonalities related to industry characteristics and corporate policy. Each team was organized in the same manner, placing functional groups in different locations on the same team. Thus, some of the potential confounds present in field studies were removed in the research design (Dube´ & Pare´, 2003). The teams differed in performance, management practices, and the use of technology, allowing for the analysis of these differences across cases. The research design also reflected some of the traditional strengths of qualitative case studies: engagement with the phenomenon in its natural setting, flexibility regarding sampling, openness to discovering aspects of the phenomenon, and sensitivity to complexity and nuance (Mason, 2002). These strengths of the research design were instrumental to our generation of insights regarding paradoxical frames in hybrid teams.

## Research site

The site chosen for this research was the largest division of a U.S. garment manufacturer and importer, here given the pseudonym Town & Country, or T&C. T&C had sales of approximately \$300 million in 1998, when the data for this study were collected. The company competed in an industry that underwent significant competitive changes during the 1990s. Many firms in T&C’s industry failed to survive the challenges of more globally distributed manufacturing and customer demands for more timely order fulfillment. T&C coped with these pressures by seeking closer alliances with both customers and suppliers in order to coordinate inventory, shipping, and manufacturing schedules more tightly. A process reengineering project conducted in 1995 created three teams focused on specific customer groups. We refer to each team using the pseudonyms Trailer, Striver, and Champion, names that represent metaphorically the differences in performance among teams. Each team was cross-functional, including sales and merchandising personnel from one Northern U.S. city (referred to hereafter as the North) and administrative, production, and customer service personnel located in a small town in the Southern U.S. (referred to hereafter as the South). The two locations were separated by approximately 800 miles. Figure 2 shows the configuration of the three teams schematically and identifies the location of managers and team members who participated in this study.

Each team was evaluated on its annual profits, earning a bonus if profit exceeded a specific target figure. Team performance depended on the contributions of team members in both the North and South locations, and the bonus provided financial rewards to each member of the team. Although the bonus compensation practice did not distinguish between the contributions of individual members, individual portions of the bonus were distributed as a percentage of base salary. Thus, more highly paid team members stood to earn higher bonuses than lower paid members. According to one of the managers, bonuses could reach 100% of a person’s base salary. Trailer’s performance was low enough to disqualify it for a bonus, while each of the other two teams earned the bonus. The Trailer team identified the need to grow sales, cut expenses, cut inventory, and to control variance as factors needing improvement, indicating that internal team processes were somewhat responsible for their poor performance.

T&C was selected as the research site because its hybrid teams not only spanned corporate functions but also culturally diverse regions of the United States. North– South cultural differences were related to the cosmopolitan character of the Northern city and the more local character of the Southern town. These differences of location, regional culture, and corporate function formed potential faultlines between the North and South locations.

Similar information and communication technologies were used by each of T&C’s three teams. To communicate between the North and South, all members depended primarily upon electronic mail and telephone and used other media, such as fax and videoconference, to a lesser extent. Videoconferences were used by the Striver team to avoid business travel between North and South when relatively minor issues were involved. The Champion team used fax to ensure the accuracy of customers’ logos placed on the product. However, Champion did not use videoconferences because the equipped conferencing room in the North was several city blocks away.

We judged these minor differences among teams in the technologies at their disposal to be inconsequential because email and telephone were used intensively in all teams. It was common for workers to communicate with their counterparts in the other location 10 times per day using these media.

Each team also convened face-to-face meetings to give team members in both locations the opportunity to meet each other and work together in physical proximity. All teams scheduled periodic meetings (e.g., quarterly) of the entire team and encouraged travel by individuals between the North and South when deemed necessary.

## Data collection

Data were collected using face-to-face, semi-structured interviews with 20 individuals. A team of three researchers, including the second author, conducted the interviews. Three interviewers conducted one interview together; two interviewers conducted 17 of the interviews together; and one interviewer conducted two of the interviews alone. All interviews were tape-recorded with permission from the respondents. The interview guide included six broad topic areas: team performance, use of face-to-face meetings, use of technology, communication, learning, and management. These topics were chosen for their relevance to practical issues facing hybrid teams rather than their relevance to particular theories. Responses to questions followed a natural course, wandering to topics of interest to the respondent. Interviews lasted between 40 and 60 minutes and were conducted at both the South and North locations. A manager and at least one team member from each team were interviewed in each location. Members meeting these criteria were nominated by T&C management, and the scheduling of interviews was coordinated by T&C’s administrative staff. The interviews were primarily conducted in rooms reserved for that purpose. Table 1 summarizes the team membership and geographic location of the interviewees.

One of the interviewers had worked for T&C for 15 years prior to leaving to pursue an academic career. The researcher had been employed in a corporate staff position and was not a member of any of the teams studied. Thus, the research team had the benefit of knowing the company from the inside in a general sense. During the data collection period, the research team also toured the Southern manufacturing facility. In both locations, the researchers observed office layout, dress, and physical appearance of employees. However, prolonged observation of people at work was not feasible. Each of the field researchers wrote notes summarizing their observations and impressions on the same day of the interviews. The researchers also met and discussed their impressions at the end of each day of interviewing. During data analysis, field notes were consulted to provide context for the comments made during interviews.

## Data analysis

The interviews were transcribed and coded in three rounds of coding. First, we applied a priori codes for team performance, use of face-to-face meetings, and use of technology. These categories produced ‘descriptive’ codes based on the interview guide (Miles & Huberman, 1994). This initial coding was conducted by one of the authors and confirmed by the other authors. The analysis consisted of cross-case comparisons. The coded text segments for each team were displayed in a matrix, which allowed comparisons across teams on each of the coded categories. As we compared across teams, differences in the way teams were managed became apparent. These differences were conceptualized as either dualities or dualisms in a second round of ‘interpretive’ coding that moved beyond the descriptive codes (Miles & Huberman, 1994).

The second round of coding identified pairs of concepts that we interpreted as either dualities or dualisms. We examined the seven dualities used by Palmer et al. (2001) in their study in the film-making industry to determine their fit with the context of our study, and we chose five of them. Their routines-creative work duality was not applied because it was specific to the film-making industry and was clearly not relevant in T&C’s context of manufacturing and customer service. The constraint– freedom and trust–control dualities were similar in the context of our study and were therefore merged and renamed control–empowerment. Two of the remaining dualities were also renamed to better reflect the context at T&C. The cost effectiveness–excellence duality was renamed to efficiency–quality, and competition–collaboration was renamed to individual–collectivism. An additional duality, cultural uniformity–cultural diversity, although not listed by Palmer et al. (2001), was deemed to be relevant to T&C. Cultural diversity is a discontinuity discussed by Watson-Manheim et al. (2002), and it appears as a consistent issue in research on virtual teams (Dube´ & Pare´, 2004; Powell et al., 2004). Hence we included it in our coding. Table 2 summarizes the sources and definitions of the six dualities that were used for the second round of coding.

Two of the authors coded the data to reflect both sides of the six dualities: remoteness–closeness, control– empowerment, rationality–emotionality, cultural uniformity–cultural diversity, cost efficiency–quality, and individualism–collectivism. All authors reviewed the initial codes to confirm consistency of the coding process and to further investigate issues mentioned in the interviews. Further investigation was performed by going back to the interviews and field notes to make sure that the respondents’ comments in each coded text segment were interpreted consistently. Ultimately, two of the six chosen pairs of concepts (cost efficiency–quality and individualism–collectivism) were judged not to be useful in explaining differences in management practices across the teams studied.

As we continued our analysis, we consulted additional literature on paradox in an attempt to clarify our understanding of the relationships among dualities, dualisms, management practices, and team performance. Although earlier literature on paradox emphasizes the importance of understanding contradictions (e.g., Poole & Van de Ven, 1989; Lewis, 2000), later work explicitly links paradoxes to performance. In particular, Smith & Tushman’s (2005) analysis demonstrated how a team’s cognitive framing of paradoxes may affect team performance. Drawing from Smith and Tushman’s theory of strategic contradiction, we conducted the third round of coding to identify the cognitive processes underlying the dualities and dualisms identified in round two. Following Lewis’ (2000) advice to use narratives to identify paradoxes, we classified practices as either integrating or differentiating to reflect the treatment of opposing tensions as dualities. In addition, where oppositions were treated as dualisms, we classified such practices as polarizing. In the third round of coding, two of the coauthors separately coded an initial, small set of data and discussed and resolved any differences. The same two coauthors then coded a larger subset of the data and achieved a 72% agreement on coded items. One of the coders then coded the remaining data.

As shown in Table 3, an integrating process involved identifying possible synergies between opposing tensions. For example, a respondent might talk about how the use of teleconferencing could bring team members together while allowing them to remain physically dispersed. The use of teleconferencing thus serves as a synergistic tool allowing the two opposing tensions to coexist. A differentiating process involved clarifying distinctions between opposing tensions so that both elements of a contradiction could receive appropriate attention. Here the objective is to balance opposing tensions so that neither one dominates the other. For example, instead of empowering the entire team, managers might empower a small group as a controlled experiment, with the objective of balancing control and empowerment over time. A polarizing process, on the other hand, manifested an independent relationship between the paired concepts. The objective in a polarizing process is to remove the tensions between opposing elements by using one element to reduce the effects of the other. The intent is for one element to dominate the other, with no intent for opposing elements to co-exist in the near or long term. Consequently, management practices become compromises at specific points in time. For example, a respondent might talk about the use of email to avoid unpleasant interactions with another team member. Thus email is used to preserve distance and detachment between team members rather than bringing them together.

## Results

We discuss the results in order of the four paradoxical frames that were evident in the data: remoteness– closeness, cultural uniformity–cultural diversity, rationality–emotionality, and control–empowerment. Within these frames, we present the results for each team – Champion, Striver, and Trailer – and relate the paradoxical frames to integrating, differentiating, and polarizing processes. Our focus is on management practices involving face-to-face meetings and use of technology.

As discussed in the next sections, the paradoxical frames were manifest as dualities in the Champion and Striver teams and as dualisms in the Trailer team.

## Remoteness–closeness

The most obvious challenge faced by each team was to bridge the geographic distance between the North and South. The actual distance between the North and South locations was substantial (800 miles), requiring an estimated 9 hours of combined air and auto travel. In addressing the paradox of trying to be remote and close simultaneously, the Champion and Striver teams used differentiating and integrating processes, while the Trailer team used polarizing processes.

Champion: Although the Champion team was challenged by the distance between North and South, team members were able to be remote and close simultaneously by using integrating and differentiating processes. One Southern team member described technology’s role in facilitating the level of communication needed for successful teamwork across distance.

Striver: Similarly, the Striver team recognized that although technology enhanced communication within the team, the disadvantages of team members being in different locations were difficult to overcome:

This statement is evidence of a differentiating process, since it clarifies how technology could allow team members to be close, yet preserve remoteness. Although technology use facilitates interactions across distance, communication gaps remain.

Trailer: Members of the Trailer team also used a variety of communication media in an attempt to compensate for distance. However, their attempts to be ‘close’ produced frustrations. For example, a Northern member of the Trailer team expressed his frustration at trying to communicate with email.

A person outside said we have been beating a dead horse with emails. – It’s something I have been working on for a week. After I called her and told her on the phone, she said, okay I can get it. And here I have been trying to email back and forth for a week and nothing has been resolved. [Mark]

Trailer’s Northern manager also confessed his use of email to avoid unpleasant contacts with certain team members:

This guy was a difficult person to deal with. I have a tendency to communicate with him by email. I have no problem picking up the phone and calling anybody else. It is because I don’t want to deal with the issue of him, you know, getting upset. [Alvin]

In effect, these polarizing practices used technology to preserve remoteness rather than to overcome the effects of distance.

## Cultural uniformity–cultural diversity

Related to the physical distance between the North and South locations were the cultural differences between people in the North and South. Cultural differences included pace of life, cordiality, and manners. The cultural uniformity–cultural diversity paradoxical frame was present as a duality in the Champion and Striver teams but existed as a dualism in the Trailer team.

Champion: Champion’s Northern manager explained how the awareness of cultural differences affected the team’s approach to working across the North–South divide. Rather than becoming frustrated about the Southern workers’ more ‘laid back’ approach, the manager adjusted her expectations and approach to contacting people in the South, which reflects an integrating process.

Sometimes I have to realize that the South is on a much more structured schedule. They come in, there is a lunch time, and they leave at a reasonable hour. Our day is much longer. I have a tendency to look for people at lunch hour, because I sit at my desk most of the day. I have to be aware that they are entitled to that time spot. Because sometimes I am looking for people at the wrong times, you know, in the evenings. [Amy]

In this way, Champion’s managers were able to sustain the duality by recognizing and accommodating members’ cultural differences, resulting in synergy between communication styles across cultures.

Striver: Striver not only recognized North–South cultural differences but also adopted management practices to produce greater inter-cultural understanding through integrating processes. In particular, sessions at face-toface team meetings were devoted to cultural issues. As explained by Striver’s Southern manager:

One of the things we have done at our team meetings is to do a lot of role reaction. For example we worked through a role playing exercise of a telephone call. We actually had individuals from the South playing the role of someone from the North and vice versa. It was kind of eye opening. The person playing the role of the North called up and was real abrupt and real aggressive, where the one playing the role of the South was real mushy mouthed. They really exaggerated the differences in the backgrounds.

There’s a lot of that that had to take place, and still needs to take place. ythere’s a melting when you start dealing with people and who they are and what they do versus their background and what you perceive their background to be. [Steve]

Striver’s Northern manager confirmed the team’s improvement in intercultural understanding, attributing much of it to the activities at the face-to-face team meetings. Thus in Striver, the face-to-face meeting was viewed as an integrating process, used to foster understanding about cultural differences while simultaneously serving as a cultural melting pot.

One of Striver’s Northern team members spoke about how she learned to adjust her confrontational style with Southern team members.

I think I was confrontational to a lot of people down South when I first came. Because it is not hard for me to want to go 75 and 90 miles an hour, and I think that I had a hard time coming to grips with the fact that maybe those people really aren’t going to go but 45 miles an hour. And I had no problem just jumping right down their throats. And all of a sudden, it was like, this is not the way to win friends and influence them.

And then I realized that there are times that I just have to take a big deep breath and make the phone call and say, ‘Let’s work our way through it.’ yBut I am sure I probably irritated a lot of people. Hurt their feelings. yBut I learned a big lesson from that. [Annette]

These lessons included appreciation for local conditions and constraints, which extended to technology use. For example, when visiting the Southern location, the Northern team member learned about the South’s ‘summer hours’:

We in the North can’t get summer hours but the people down South only work four and a half days a week. Because it is like every Friday afternoon, you might as well forget it. Don’t even bother calling because you are not going to get anyone. yBut you just have to learn to deal with that. Because that is just the way people’s personality and their regionalization of the country is. [Annette]

By using face-to-face meetings to enhance understanding of the cultural differences between North and South, team members could determine more appropriate ways to use technology. This reflects an integrating process with the effect of unifying the culture of the Striver team around common practices.

Trailer: The evidence from the Trailer team was quite different. Trailer’s Northern manager expressed admiration for Southern hospitality while acknowledging that people in the North were not like those in the South:

I find the people who are located in the South who work for us a lot more cordial than the people in the North. I think that that is just the way you are brought up. They think nothing of going out of their way to take you out to dinner. And when we are in the North, possibly it has to do with the commutes that we have to deal with, we go home and leave them on their own. And that is not right. [Alvin]

Several Northern workers explained the difficulty of working with people in the South because the South moved at a slower pace than the North, and this was evident in technology use. This affected the North’s ability to respond in a timely manner, for example, to requests for information coming from Asia. Instead of sending information to the North after working hours so that it could reach Hong Kong by the next day, the North ‘just lost a day, and we are still sitting on the same issue that we started today’ [Mark]. In sum, Trailer tended to polarize cultural differences.

## Rationality–emotionality

A rationality–emotionality paradoxical frame is evident as a duality when there is no distinct separation between the rational, business-like side of a relationship and the socio-emotional side. That is, emotionality and rationality are interdependent. We found evidence of this paradoxical frame as a duality in both Champion and Striver, but it was manifested as a dualism in Trailer.

Champion: A Southern member of the Champion team described the emotional aspect of teamwork as follows:

We get along pretty well. We have our times when we don’t mind hanging up on each other, slamming the phones down in each other’s ears, but overall we get along really well. I think our whole team gets along with the North really well. Of course, there are a couple of people there that kind of cause a little conflict ever so often. But it is nothing that can’t be resolved. [Hannah]

Another Southern member of Champion described a similar relationship with a remote salesperson:

Well, we have a salesman that I will be seeing tomorrow and I can’t wait to get there. I have a few things to discuss with him. A problem with him is that he travels a lot. And we get a lot of his phone calls that we know nothing about. yI get blind sided a lot when it comes to him. But I deal with it. I am going to say to him, ‘you know, I need a little help here. I don’t think that is fair to me just to drop your work on me and not even let me know it’s going to come.’ So, he and I sort of have a love–hate thing going on. We let each other have it. But it works out. [Debbie]

In both of these examples, telephone conversations served as the medium for interaction between North and South members. Notably, the use of this rudimentary technology supports the integration of emotional behaviors with task performance. This integration was also sustained by face-to-face communication. A Northern Champion worker’s description of her activities with her Southern counterpart during trips to the North illustrates the integration of rational and social concerns:

I actually like the people I work with. You know, if Hannah is up here for the weekend before Monday, I have no problem going out with her on Sunday night. yI actually enjoy that. yAnd that is what makes it a lot of fun. Because we definitely work, and there are serious points to it. But, when we will all have dinner, you know, it is fun. I mean, I come home from these meetings, and friends of mine are like, ‘you really had a good time?’ And I am like, ‘I love going and seeing these people. I mean, it really is a lot of fun.’ [Carolyn]

Thus in Champion, both technology and face-to-face encounters accommodated integrating processes used to advance both emotional and rational concerns. Worth noting here is the contrast with Trailer’s Northern manager, who explained that the members of his team could not entertain Southern visitors because of long commutes. Clearly, that excuse was not on the minds of Champion’s team members, who saw entertainment as interdependent with work activity.

These reports from Champion suggest a role for emotional behavior in contributing to performance. Whether the emotion is expressed as negative (anger), positive (fun), or ambivalent (love–hate), it is not separated from more rational concerns for making decisions, getting information, or working seriously. For these reasons, we consider such statements to be evidence of the emotionality–rationality duality.

Striver: Striver’s management actively sought to introduce emotionality as part of the team’s experience. In one example, the Northern team manager devised a ‘Captain Underpants’ award, named after a fictional hero in a series of contemporary children’s books. A Northern team member who served on the Captain Underpants committee explained how the award worked:

Anyone who has done over and above their call of duty in their job to the customer, to suit the customer’s need, someone will send an email to everyone on the Striver team. And at the end of the year, for the next team meeting, this committee will get together and see who deserves to wear the Captain Underpants cape. y It is pretty cute, you know. So you see these emails every other day or two. ‘Captain Underpants to blah blah blah.’ yIt is nice when you see them. I think that it is really nice that your name gets recognized for doing something. It is a good idea. It makes people feel good. [Regina]

The Northern team manager, Paul, was also known for his clever congratulatory certificates using presentation software. Regina explained that such awards were given for attracting new customers to T&C:

When it is a new customer, a new order, Paul will send like a little PowerPoint picture congratulating me. And everybody would get one. [Regina]

These examples from Striver show how technology was used to create synergy between achieving rational and emotional objectives, reflecting an integrating process.

Trailer: The evidence from the Trailer team is different in terms of the rationality–emotionality frame. Most of Trailer members’ perspectives on the use of email suggest that it was used primarily for rational concerns. For instance, a Southern member justified her choice of email to communicate with Northern team members:

The reason I use email is – if they are giving you direction on how to handle a specific problem, you may need to refer back to that later. It is kind of a cover your bases type of thing. I rarely pick up the phone and call the North unless it is something that we are working on or if it is urgent, or if I have someone who has not replied. [Judy]

Her comments suggest a rational approach to the use of technology, since email was used solely to provide a record of communication. This evidence reflects a polarizing process.

Most of Trailer members’ comments about emotional issues indicated that emotion and business should be separated, suggesting a polarizing process where emotional issues are suppressed so that rational objectives could be advanced. A Southern member of Trailer was asked what it took to make people on her team happy:

I am not sure that my job is all about keeping someone happy. It is making everything run smoothly. yIf I’m going to come out looking out great, then I have done my job. So we have a report that reflects that. And it is distributed every week. And we get a grade. yAnd as long as it hits above 90– 95 percent and up to 100 percent, I am comfortable with that. I want 100 percent. yI think that everybody will be completely frustrated if they saw that, okay, we are rock bottom. [Judy]

The comment reflects a belief that one can only be happy if one has performed well. This contrasts with comments from the other two teams about using emotionality to advance rational objectives. The comment also shows that Judy believes her job should focus on the task instead of emotional issues, that is, she treats rationality and emotionality as distinctly different with rationality being advanced at the expense of emotionality.

## Control–empowerment

The paradoxical frame of control and empowerment was evident as a duality in descriptions of work experiences that consisted of both control and empowerment simultaneously. This language was evident in both the Champion and Striver team members’ statements, which expressed feelings of empowerment while also understanding limits of authority. By contrast, the Trailer team’s comments that related to control and empowerment showed evidence of dualisms.

Champion: Control–empowerment was apparent as an integrating process in a Southern Champion team member’s description of her relationship with Lyle, her manager:

Lyle does most of his informing us on things that he has heard from the North. Like if something really important is going onythen normally he will get us together and let us know what is going on or what changes are being made or what the plans are. I would say that, you know, he is pretty good as far as keeping us informed.

There is a lot of constructive criticism. yA lot of other meetings you go into you feel like you can’t open your mouth and voice your opinion. But all of ours seem to be pretty open so that if you have a complaint or if you have a comment that you want to make, you can. You feel at liberty to do that. [Vanessa]

These comments show two processes occurring simultaneously, with face-to-face meetings serving as a vehicle to facilitate synergy: control through leader direction and empowerment by workers contributing constructive criticism.

Striver: In Striver, empowerment was viewed as an experiment that had only partially succeeded. Striver’s Northern manager described how members of the team had been assigned responsibilities on committees, made their reports, and then received criticism for their ideas:

We have presentations from the different people in the group and it is all-inclusive. Those things never used to exist, particularly in our non-management people. yNow they include more participation. Break-out groups and discussions dealing with topics for getting ideas.

And I was disappointed with it, the output of it, because I didn’t find the recommendations to be specific enough and actionable enough. – It is good to know what we are faced with. What is difficult. What is working. What is not working. So, therefore, a smaller group of the people who work in those businesses have now been charged to continue this and come back with it. [Paul]

Thus, the attempted empowerment of team members produced mixed results, causing team management to restrict empowerment to a smaller group. Team management recognized the need to restrict empowerment in the short term to facilitate learning, so as to progress toward their long term goal of facilitating simultaneous control and empowerment. This is evidence of a differentiating process.

Trailer: In Trailer, control and empowerment were expressed as polarizing processes rather than integrating or differentiating processes. To illustrate, Trailer’s Northern manager explained how team members were involved in the periodic team meetings and how managers were learning to let go of their traditional controls:

The agenda is basically set by myself and about five other people who participate in those meetings. However, we try to get the people who are involved in our business involved in the team meetings to make presentations. And that has worked well. yThere has been a tendency on the part of people who are involved in management to let go, to allow the people involved in operations to go about their business. [Alvin]

From this comment, it is apparent that the Trailer manager understood empowerment as letting go of control, a dualism that treats empowerment as an alternative to control. Trailer’s approach contrasts with the integrative processes of control–empowerment evident in the Champion team.

Despite the intention to empower members of the Trailer team, it was difficult for some managers to ‘let go.’ Trailer’s Southern manager was candidly critical of the controlling attitude that prevailed among Trailer’s management:

What we are doing on one side is we have formed teams and we have said to those teams, ‘We have empowered you to make decisions.’ But unfortunately, we have got too many power management personnel in place. yThey have got their thumb on it, you know. And they are not going to take that thumb off, because the time they take it off they think there will be no need of them. yAnd we still feel too much that management is the only person in the system that can make a sensible, logical decision. [Leroy]

Within Trailer, electronic mail was often the chosen medium for broadcasting criticism of team members and protecting oneself with documented evidence. As such, technology supported the polarizing processes carried out by managers to reinforce control at the expense of empowerment. For instance, the Southern manager described members as being worried about their bossesy

ychewing them out and sending them out these ugly emails. yOur vice president of operations actually sends out severe reprimands and email directly to a person out there on the floor and copies everybody in the world. [Leroy]

Another way that technology supported polarization was the manner in which some members of Trailer preferred email communication over other media because email produced a documentation trail. A Southern team member explained:

With email, there is no disputing that document. It is clear. It is there if you ever need to refer back to it. There is no disputing what directions you have to deal with somethingy It is a kind of cover your bases type thing. [Judy]

The Southern team manager confirmed this practice:

Unfortunately, in my opinion, the problem with email gets right back to management philosophy. And I think email is being used probably 60 percent CYA – cover your assets.<sup>2</sup> [Leroy]

Such comments serve to illustrate the dualism of control vs empowerment in the Trailer team. To empower meant giving up managerial control, something that some managers were prepared to do but others were not. From the team members’ standpoint, empowerment was welcomed but often not realized. The comments also show the use of one form of technology – email – to reinforce the dualism of control–empowerment.

## Discussion

Our focus in this paper is to understand the paradoxes inherent in hybrid teams and how they may be effectively managed. The theoretical foundation for our inquiry stems from Smith & Tushman’s (2005) concept of strategic contradiction. According to Smith and Tushman, strategic contradictions are paradoxical frames confronting teams, and team outcomes are affected by the cognitive processes employed to deal with those contradictions. The process of integrating produces synergies, while the process of differentiating produces balance among opposing agendas over time. We extend Smith and Tushman’s theory to include a third cognitive process, namely polarizing. We use the framework to generate fresh insights into the management of hybrid work teams and show how paradoxical frames can be sustained through the use of team design and technology. In addition to identifying paradoxical frames specific to hybrid teams, we associate integrating and differentiating with positive team outcomes, while associating polarizing with lower team performance.

Figure 3 shows how the original theoretical model is informed by our findings, showing the specific ways in which managers might view the contradictions experienced in hybrid teams. We identified four types of paradoxical frames in our analysis: remoteness–closeness, cultural uniformity–cultural diversity, rationality– emotionality, and control–empowerment. All four appeared as dualities in the Champion and Striver teams and as dualisms in the Trailer team. Champion and Striver created and sustained dualities through integrating and differentiating processes, while Trailer sustained dualisms through polarizing processes. Although it is paradoxical to consider logically opposed elements as coexisting in an inseparable relationship, dualities may be associated with effective team practices. Both Champion and Striver appeared to use integrating and differentiating processes to integrate control with empowerment, rationality with emotionality, cultural uniformity with cultural diversity, and remoteness with closeness. By contrast, Trailer’s practices manifested dualisms in which one aspect of team functioning (e.g., control) was used to reduce the effects of another (e.g., empowerment).

Our analysis identifies two main mechanisms for embracing contradictory elements in hybrid teams: the use of technology and face-to-face meetings. Communication technologies are essential to the functioning of hybrid teams, and each team at T&C used a variety of communication technologies intensively: electronic mail, telephones and voicemail, videoconferencing, and fax. For many workers, remote communication consumed a large portion of each working day. However, teams differed in their use of technology. For example, Trailer used electronic mail to broadcast mistakes and ‘CYA,’ thus polarizing opposing tensions within the team. By contrast, Champion and Striver used the same technology to integrate opposing tensions by, for example, acknowledging exceptional performance and empowering team members. These results are consistent with theories that stress the importance of users’ appropriations or enactments of information technologies (Carlson & Zmud, 1999; Orlikowski, 2000; Poole & DeSanctis, 2004; Boudreau & Robey, 2005), and the results confirm research on unanticipated uses of technologies in virtual teams (Majchrzak et al., 2000). By using technology to support integrating and differentiating processes in teams, managers may leverage paradoxical situations effectively.

Teams at T&C also differed in their use of face-to-face meetings. In each team, face-to-face encounters allowed members to resolve work-related issues and to improve social relationships. However, team managers differed in the way they conducted face-to-face meetings. Champion’s formal meetings were described as forums for problem-solving and constructive feedback. Striver’s management also attempted to empower team members, permitting participation in establishing meeting agendas and proposing solutions to specific problems. Designing face-to-face meetings in these ways supported the integrating and differentiating cognitive processes in Champion and Striver. By contrast, the agendas of formal meetings at Trailer were controlled by management, who assigned work to committees composed of rank-and-file members, who in turn reported back to management. This practice sustained polarization in the Trailer team.

Champion and Striver also used face-to-face meetings to acknowledge both cultural diversity and shared identity within the teams (Earley & Mosakowski, 2000; Fiol & O’Connor, 2005). Practices that sustained this duality included role-playing and focusing on individuals rather than making generalized attributions based on culture. By contrast, the Trailer team continued to represent cultural differences as two opposing poles.

These two team design mechanisms allow dualities to be created and sustained through integrating and differentiating processes. In an integrating process, the use of face-to-face meetings and technology creates synergy across opposing tensions. For instance, email and teleconferencing can simultaneously allow team members to operate at different locations while facilitating communication across distance. Identifying synergies thus allows team members to be both remote and close. Conversely, in a differentiating process, face-to-face meetings and the use of technology do not necessarily serve as synergistic tools. However, these practices may provide a way to assess the different logics that allow opposing tensions to exist. For instance, when team members are aware that misinterpretations can result when using technology to span distance, they are sensitized to the unintended consequences of using technology to be both remote and close. By differentiating the opposing tensions, team members learn to adjust their actions and improve their performance.

We extend the range of cognitive processes in hybrid teams by proposing polarizing as a third process that creates and sustains dualisms rather than dualities. Polarizing processes reflect the resolution of opposing tensions by using one element to reduce the effects of another. For instance, managers in the Trailer team found it difficult to view the paradoxical frame of control/empowerment as co-existent. As a result, face-toface meetings were understood as mechanisms for empowering team members by letting go of control. In this instance, control was sacrificed for the sake of empowerment.

Given that technology and face-to-face meetings can be used in various ways to manage strategic contradictions in hybrid teams, we also need to understand how managers exercise influence over cognitive processes. We discovered two main ways in which managers exercise influence: serving as behavioral models, and using language in ways that accept paradox.

First, managers became behavioral models through their own use of technology. For example, Champion’s management used email to communicate needed information, and Champion’s members modeled this practice by using email constructively rather than defensively. The humorous uses of email by Striver’s manager were also contagious within his team. Where managers use technology defensively or broadcast criticism, as in the Trailer team, team members learn from their model and do likewise. These results are consistent with the findings of a study of managers and remote workers (Staples et al., 1999), which showed that workers were influenced by managers’ own practices in using electronic media to communicate.

Second, managers used language in ways that promoted the acceptance of paradoxes and their framing as dualities. Cognitive processes are directly influenced by leaders’ sensitivity to using language that reflects the tensions between logically opposed concepts (Fiol, 2002). For example, the managers of the Champion team were capable of communicating the need for workers to exercise self control, while also communicating the limits of self control. The ability to maintain a simultaneous emphasis on control and empowerment is consistent with findings of recent research on empowerment in virtual teams (Bell & Kozlowski, 2002; Kirkman et al., 2004).

## Conclusion

As communication systems, coordination tools, and other ‘death-of-distance’ technologies have appeared, virtual and hybrid teams have become popular approaches for leveraging an organization’s human resources. By removing some constraints of physical distance, information technologies present tremendous opportunities for combining human resources from different locations into a single team. However, hybrid teams in particular face formidable challenges, in large part precipitated by the paradoxical assumptions underlying their creation. It is assumed that people who live and work hundreds of miles apart and rarely see each other can function as tightly interdependent members of the same team. Even with the best technical resources and frequent opportunities for face-to-face interactions, many hybrid teams fail to establish a shared identity, which potentially affects their performance (Fiol & O’Connor, 2005). We maintain, however, that management practices that embrace paradox may overcome these challenges.

Our main contribution is the extension of Smith & Tushman’s (2005) theory of strategic contradiction to provide insights into the tensions within hybrid teams. Our findings support the value of using technology and face-to-face encounters to improve performance in hybrid teams by supporting cognitive processes that address paradoxical frames. If technology and face-toface meetings are designed and used in ways that support integrative and differentiating processes rather than polarization, teams may perform more effectively.

In adopting a cognitive approach to studying hybrid teams, we relied exclusively upon interviews with the managers and members of three teams. Consequently, our findings are not based on direct observation or the examination of artifacts and documentation. Despite these limitations, our analysis contributes to the emerging body of literature that recognizes the value of paradox as a cognitive frame for complex social and technical patterns of work. By drawing attention to paradoxes and other contradictions, theories of cognition explain relationships that have been neglected in other theories.

Clearly, the theoretical ideas introduced here can be studied using teams in other contexts, where additional paradoxical frames might be engaged. For example, we did not find evidence in our data of some dualities that were addressed in prior research (Palmer et al., 2001), namely efficiency–quality and individualism–collectivism. The relationship between individual and collective interests is an important aspect of teamwork, but it is not usually conceptualized as a duality. Thus, when managers talk about the need for team members to make personal sacrifices for team success, they are treating individual and collective interests as a dualism. If viewed as a duality, individual and collective interests could be addressed simultaneously, leading to practices whereby individuals succeed as the team succeeds and in which team success results from individual success.

Managing with sensitivity to paradoxical frames poses a particular challenge to managers who have gained experience managing co-located teams in which direct supervision and traditional control measures are applied. Such teams do not pose the same problems as hybrid teams in terms of their geographic distance, and so the approach to management can be more traditional. As hybrid teamwork becomes more common, however, managers should probably suspend their traditional expectations and exhibit practices that may seem paradoxical but which make sense in the context of distributed working environments. On the basis of this study’s results, paradoxical frames involving rationality and emotionality, cultural diversity and uniformity, control and empowerment, and remoteness and closeness may be incorporated into practices for managing the tensions inherent in hybrid teams.
