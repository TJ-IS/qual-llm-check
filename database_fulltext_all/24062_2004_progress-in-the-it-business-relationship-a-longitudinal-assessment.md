## Abstract

This paper investigates perceptions of the IT/business relationship held by 653 IT managers and their staff and 503 of their business counterparts. On the basis of data collected over a 3-year period using a survey instrument, the paper highlights areas of perceived difficulty in the IT/business relationship and seeks evidence of trends in the business/IT relationship. The data reveal significant differences in the perceptions of business managers and those of IT professionals, especially with respect to issues of IT system efficacy and communication efficacy. Both cohorts demonstrate increasing awareness of the importance of IT as a driver of business activity, as well as comparable upward or downward trends in response on issues relating to IT’s alignment with business strategy and communication efficacy, but only the first mentioned issue is identified as a statistically significant trend.

## Introduction

O <sup>ver</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>past</sup> <sup>decade,</sup> <sup>considerable</sup> <sup>journal</sup> <sup>space</sup> <sup>has</sup>been devoted to discussion of challenges arising in the been devoted to discussion of challenges arising in the information technology (IT)/business relationship and suggestions about how to deal with these. This paper aims to further this discussion by providing insights into perceptions of the IT/business relationship over a 3-year period. Specifically, it investigates the questions ‘What perceptions do business managers and IT professionals have of IT performance?’ and ‘What trends, if any, are apparent in the IT/business relationship?’.

The paper commences with an overview of some key papers relating to the IT/business relationship, and draws from this the list of difficulties and issues which informed construction of a questionnaire designed to test perceptions of the IT/business relationship. Following description of the questionnaire construction and administration, the paper examines the data gathered from business and IT respondents over a 3-year period. The aggregated data are useful in highlighting the areas of greatest difficulty apparent in the IT/business relationship, while a comparison of responses on a year-by-year basis provides a picture of change over time, with possible implications for those who seek to improve the IT/business relationship.

## Issues in the IT/business relationship

Early contributions to discussion of the IT/business relationship highlight tensions arising from the time and expense involved in delivering the potential for strategic advantage attributed to IT, as well as difficulties arising from a ‘culture gap’ created by knowledge and communication inadequacies (Grindley, 1992; Ward and Peppard, 1996). Grindley (1992), Boyle (1994), and Earl and Feeny (1994) all noted the failure of many senior managers to appreciate IT’s potential and its structural or cost consequences, while Ewusi-Mensah and Przasnyski (1994) found that the most common reasons for abandonment of IS projects were escalating costs and lengthening completion schedules or lack of appropriate technical infrastructure and expertise.

Despite implicit recognition of communication or relationship problems as emanating from both sides of the IT/ business relationship (e.g., Keen, 1991; Grindley, 1992; Markus and Benjamin, 1996) and occasional explicit recognition of shared responsibility for communication (e.g. Lederer and Mendelow, 1987; Earl and Feeny, 1994; Ross et al., 1996), the early literature sought primarily to highlight the areas of IT deficiency, developing a catalogue of ‘IT problems’ that needed to be addressed. Researchers tended to report perceptions from one side of the relationship – with either IT professionals or business managers reporting on how IT response could be or needed to be improved – and invariably responsibility for better IT positioning, more productive relationships, or more strategic contribution was laid at the feet of IT managers and their staff. Thus, for example, Boyle (1994) argues that it is a CIO’s responsibility to speak the language of the organisation, create a technology platform and standards that will provide long-term flexibility and competitive advantage, and actively participate in strategic planning. More recently, this argument resurfaced in the work of Bashein and Markus (1997), whose findings that credibility arises from perceived expertise and perceived trustworthiness (attributable to similarity and likeability, prolonged interaction, and appropriate and consistent behaviour) led them to suggest the need for IT personnel to use common language, be team players, demonstrate patience and support, and encourage participation.

In the early literature, recurring themes were the role of the senior IT manager or CIO, the importance of perceived cost effectiveness and value adding, the relationship between IT strategy and business strategy, and communication effectiveness or the need for IT people to ‘speak the same language’ (Boyle, 1994) as business people. Encapsulated within each of these themes were a series of question about whether

\- the CIO is influential in business strategy formulation (Lederer and Mendelow, 1987; Feeny and Edwards, 1992; Stephens and Ledbetter, 1992; Boyle, 1994; Earl and Feeny, 1994) or the CIO is a trusted member of the senior management team (Applegate and Elam, 1992; Earl and Feeny, 1994);

\- the IT function adds value to the business (Earl and Feeny, 1994; Mata and Fuerst, 1995), IT understands and acts on business needs and priorities (Mata and Fuerst, 1995), IT delivers a cost-effective service (Ross et al., 1996), or IT delivers what it promises (Earl and Feeny, 1994; Ross et al., 1996);

\- IT investment and/or products and services are aligned with business strategy and objectives (Earl and Feeny, 1994; Ross et al., 1996), IT is a fundamental driver of business activity and future change (Earl and Feeny, 1994) or a source of sustained competitive advantage (Boyle, 1994; Mata and Fuerst, 1995), or IT is involved in the business decision-making process (Applegate and Elam, 1992; Earl and Feeny, 1994); and whether

\- IT communicates effectively with others in the organisation (Earl and Feeny, 1994; Ross et al., 1996) and speaks the language of the organisation (Boyle, 1994).

Although Ross et al. (1996) noted the need for business to take ownership of and responsibility for IT projects and Mata and Fuerst (1995) used a resource-based model to analyse IT’s potential contribution to sustainable competitive advantage, most early research into the IT/business relationship focussed on what individuals (usually IT personnel) could do to deal with gaps in the IT/business relationship (Peppard, 2001). Even in Cross et al.’s (1997) case study of IT transformation through business intervention and top management team oversight, the focus falls on IT deficiencies, and reported outcomes focusing on more effective IT collaboration in project teams and better IT customer relationship interactions added yet more ‘problem issues’ for IT professionals to address. Despite recognition of the need for alignment between IT and business strategy, the emphasis until recently was mostly on how the IT function and its representatives could add value or establish credibility, rather than on how organisations could involve both IT and business managers to better manage differences and potential.

Feeny and Willcocks’ (1998) discussion of the information systems (IS) capabilities required to address structural, cost and relationship issues marks a departure from the previous general focus on individual and IT responsibility by highlighting the need not only for IT leadership but also for business systems thinking, relationship building, architecture planning, technical progress, informed buying, contract facilitation and monitoring and identification of IS suppliers’ value adding potential. They pointed to the need for business development to be integrated with IT capability through input from both business and IT managers, and for technology to be perceived not only as responsive to changing business demands but also as reliable (or readily fixed by troubleshooters) on a day-today basis. Picking up and reinforcing the theme of shared responsibility for change, Peppard and Ward’s (1999) paper on the IT/business relationship introduced a more systemic and organisation-focused discussion of responsibilities and mechanisms for change. Taking a resource-based perspective, Peppard et al. (2000) also noted that value creation from IT investments was an organisation-wide responsibility. In an interesting departure from previous, mostly single-cohort research, Peppard et al. (2000) – like Feeny and Edwards (1992) and Earl and Feeny (1994) before them – arrived at their conclusions by gathering interview data from both IT professionals and business managers.

In summary, the aggregation of research into the IT/ business relationship produces a list of perceived deficiencies or imperatives that, until recently, have been seen as issues to be addressed by the IT function. Only in the last few years have claims of shared IT/business responsibility for a changed relationship and improved IT effectiveness surfaced. This late recognition of shared responsibility is probably not surprising, given that most of the research reported draws on insights from either IT or business managers, with only a few papers seeking the views of both participants in the IT/business relationship. In these few papers, mentioned above, research evidence is drawn from interviews which provide rich data, but militate against direct comparison of IT and business manager responses. Thus, although we now have some indication of the perceptions business managers and IT professionals have of IT performance, there is little data enabling comparison of specific perceptions of performance, and even less data on trends in the IT/business relationship, that is, whether IT performance is perceived by each side of the IT/business relationship to be improving or declining.

## Gathering information about issues in the IT/business relationship

As indicated above, research over the past decade highlights several key themes in the IT/business relationship, namely, the role of the senior IT manager or CIO, issues of perceived cost effectiveness, value adding, and system responsiveness, the relationship between IT strategy and business strategy, as well as communication, customer service and relationship building. Examination of relevant literature enabled the drawing out of a detailed set of issues mentioned in connection with each of these themes:

The role of the senior IT manager or CIO – Is the CIO influential in business strategy formulation? Is the CIO a trusted member of the senior management team?

Issues of perceived cost effectiveness, value adding, and system responsiveness – Does IT understand and act on business needs and priorities? Does the IT function add value to the business? Does IT deliver what it promises? Do IT systems meet the day-to-day needs of the business? Does IT deliver a cost-effective service? Are IT systems reliable and responsive?

Relationship between IT strategy and business strategy – Is IT a fundamental driver of business activity? Is IT investment aligned with business strategy and objectives? Is IT involved in the business decision-making process? Do business managers take responsibility for realisation of business benefits from IT?

Communication, customer service and relationship building – Does IT communicate effectively with others in the organisation? Does IT speak the language of the organisation? Does IT build a collaborative environment? Does IT manage customer relationships and market itself well?

These 16 issues were used as the basis for construction in 1998 of a draft questionnaire designed to investigate IT professionals’ and business managers’ perceptions of IT performance, as well as change over time in these perceptions. To these 16 issues, in the final version of the questionnaire three others were added, in response to informal interviews of IT professionals attending professional development courses at a British university and to pilot testing of the questionnaire involving three academics running those courses. Two of the additional issues reflected expressed concerns about IT as a viable contributor to business development (Does IT constrain the business?) and about base level service activity (Do IT personnel provide prompt, courteous and professional service?). The third – Is the most senior IT person as likely to become CEO as any other board member? – sought to test the strength of acceptance of the CIO as a business leader, proactively addressing an issue subsequently highlighted in articles by Huthessing (1999), Melymuka (1999) and Maciag (2002).

The final version of the questionnaire contained 19 statements and required respondents to indicate their level of agreement with the statement using a seven-point Likert scale on which 1 indicated ‘strongly disagree’ and 7 indicated ‘strongly agree’.

## Method

The questionnaire was distributed to and completed by all of the 148 IT managers attending either of two courses – Organisational Politics and IT Management (OPIT), Transforming the IT Function (TITF) – run by Cranfield University, UK, during the 3 years 1998–2000. Participation in the courses was restricted to those IT professionals working at middle management or senior levels (i.e. those with responsibility for the management of an IT section and staff) and was invariably funded by the participants’ organisations, which were mostly large, private sector organisations with in-house IT functions but also included some government departments with in-house IT functions. Involvement in the courses was mostly voluntary, although some of the IT managers attending had been directed to take part by their organisations. IT managers attending courses in 1998 numbered 53, in 1999 numbered 52 and in 2000 numbered 43.

In addition to the IT managers attending the course, the questionnaire was also given by those managers to their subordinates (numbering 505 in total, 96 in 1998, 198 in 1999 and 211 in 2000) and to business colleagues (numbering 503 in total, 115 in 1998, 197 in 1999 and 191 in 2000). Course participants distributing questionnaires were asked to ensure that the questionnaire was completed not just by staff who they anticipated might respond positively, but also by those who they anticipated might respond negatively or neutrally. All questionnaires were distributed and completed in advance of IT managers’ attendance on the course. Questionnaires were completed by a total of 653 IT people and 503 of their business colleagues, with IT questionnaires completed numbering 149 in 1998, 250 in 1999 and 254 in 2000.

Using SPSS, responses to questionnaires were subjected to principal component analysis with oblique rotation to identify the factors underlying the range of issues associated with the IT/business relationship. Mean scores and standard deviations for each sample group – IT professionals and business managers – as a whole, and for each sample group in each year from the years 1998–2000 were also obtained. Further analysis, drawing on a twotailed t-test with equal variances not assumed, provided information about the significance of differences in response between the two groups (IT and business) in the years in which data were collected. A final phase of analysis compared the data for IT professionals in each of the 3 years (1998–2000) and for business managers in each of the 3 years in order to test for trends in response. This comparison was achieved using analysis of variance (ANOVA), with equal variances assumed.

As can be seen in Table 2, the responses of IT professionals and business managers to questions about the IT/business relationship were significantly different (at the 0.01, but mostly at the 0.001 level) in 11 out of the 19 questionnaire items. Not surprisingly, where significant differences in response existed, these mostly reflected a more positive assessment of IT performance and potential by the IT respondents than by the business respondents. This was particularly the case for the five significantly different items associated with system efficacy, on all of which IT performance was rated more highly by IT professionals than by their business counterparts. It should be noted that communication efficacy also seems to be an issue, as significant differences were found for three items associated with communication efficacy (IT delivers all that it promises, IT actively promotes an environment of collaboration and business takes full responsibility for realisation of business benefits). While no significant difference in response was found for the three items that loaded most highly on the communication efficacy factor (effective communication, skilful customer relationship management and IT and business speaking the same language), it should be noted that the mean scores for each group on these items were lower than the mean scores for all other questionnaire items except that relating to the CIO’s likelihood of becoming CEO.

Overall, the results obtained for the total sample surveyed between 1998 and 2000 suggest that there is a significant difference in the assessment of system efficacy by IT professionals and business managers, and that the culture gap and communication difficulties identified in the literature (e.g. Grindley, 1992; Earl and Feeny, 1994; Peppard and Ward, 1999) remain salient issues for both groups.

## A longitudinal perspective on issues in the IT/business relationship

Table 3 presents mean and standard deviation scores and levels of significance obtained using a two-tailed t-test to examine the extent of difference in response between the business and IT manager groups for each of the 3 years 1998, 1999 and 2000.

Of the 19-questionnaire items, for seven there is a significant and consistent difference in response between the two groups over the 3-year period. Four of these items loaded on the system efficacy factor and, for all four, business managers were consistently more negative about IT performance than were IT professionals. Significant and consistent differences were also found for an additional item which loaded on the alignment with business strategy factor (IT adds value), and for two items which loaded on the communication efficacy, factor (IT delivers all it promises and business takes full responsibility for realisation of business benefits). For a third item associated with communication efficacy, there were significant differences in response for both the second and third years of the 3- year study. These results flesh out those obtained through the examination of the IT and business responses as a whole (see Table 2) and, like the earlier results, add weight to the observations of Grindley (1992), Boyle (1994) and Earl and Feeny (1994) that many business managers fail to appreciate IT’s structural and cost consequences and the observations of these authors as well as Peppard and Ward (1999) in respect of an IT/business culture gap.

## Trends in the IT/business relationship – 1998–2000

An attempt to test for trends in the responses of the groups of business managers and of IT professionals was subsequently undertaken by using analysis of variance (ANOVA) to identify between-group variance within the business and IT samples over the three-year period. Table 4 presents the analysis of variance results for business managers and for IT professionals over the 3-year period.

As can be seen in Table 4, significant differences in between-group responses for IT professionals were evident on seven out of the 19 questionnaire items, but for only one out of the 19 items for the business manager groups. For the IT professionals, significant between-group differences were found for one item relating to the executive influence factor, for one item relating to the alignment with business strategy factor (IT is a fundamental driver of future business activity), for one item relating to the system efficacy factor (existing information systems meet the dayto-day needs of business), and for four items related to the communication efficacy factor.

Table 5 examines whether these differences in response could be considered indicative of trends in the IT/business relationship. It presents significance figures for betweengroup variance on a year-by-year basis and for the 3 years as a whole. It also indicates overall trends in response on the items for which a consistent upward or downward trend could be identified from the means presented in Table 3. It should be noted that differences from 1 year to the next or for the 3 years as a whole are statistically significant on only some of these items.

Overall, the only statistically significant trend identifiable in the responses of both business managers and IT professionals is that both of these groups increasingly emphasise the importance of IT as a fundamental driver of business activity. Although the differences are not statistically significant, the means for both groups on another question related to IT’s alignment with business strategy (IT adds value to the business) also show an upward trend. On two questions related to communication efficacy, there appear to be contradictory trends, although again these trends are not statistically significant for both groups.

On the question of IT collaboration, there is an upward trend for both groups, yet on the question of whether IT and business speak the same language there is a downward trend for both groups. It is speculated that this contradiction may be explained by a continuing failure to address directly IT/business communication difficulties – the ‘culture gap’ – despite the increasing use of collaborative structures within organisations, such as the project teams described by Cross et al. (1997). Where other trends could be identified, they seem to indicate an increasingly negative response on the part of IT professionals. It should be noted, however, that most of the apparent trends identifiable through inspection of the means for this data set are not statistically significant. Further investigation is therefore needed to discover whether such differences in means are simply attributable to chance or actually representative of ongoing upward or downward changes in perception.

## Conclusion

This paper has sought to examine the state of the IT/ business relationship through the lens of relevant literature and subsequent testing of the perceptions of IT staff and their business manager counterparts over a three year period. Significant differences have been found to exist between the perceptions of business managers and those of IT professionals with respect to issues of IT system efficacy and communication efficacy, yet there is agreement between the two groups on the increasing importance of IT as a driver of business activity. Only with respect to this last issue was it possible to identify a statistically significant trend in the responses of both groups, yet on another issue related to IT’s alignment with business strategy and on two issues related to communication efficacy trends are identifiable although not statistically significant for both groups.

The fact that in this study, change has been measured in terms of response means for different groups of business and IT managers in each year of a 3-year period, rather than by repeat testing of two constant samples, necessarily limits the claims that can be made about trends in the IT/ business relationship. It is therefore suggested that future longitudinal studies involving the same cohorts of business managers and IT professionals are needed. Additionally, inferences that the results of this study represent the current situation in any specific IT/business context must be limited by the fact that a survey instrument rather than in-depth interviewing was used.

Nevertheless, this study is one of a few that presents the perceptions of both sides of the IT/business relationship, and it does so by directly comparing responses to the same set of questions. The study’s findings lend support to existing literature identifying IT/business culture gaps, communication difficulties and problems with system efficacy, and analysis reveals significant differences over the 3-year period in the perceptions of IT professionals and business managers in these areas. It is suggested that other studies are now needed to probe the nature of and reasons for differences in IT professionals’ and business managers’ perceptions in respect of the IT/business relationship, and to identify more clearly significant trends in this relationship.
