# Executive leadership and ITs technical details: teaching approach for Steelscreen.com and beyond

Brian Subirana<sup>1</sup>

Center for Coordination Science, MIT Sloan School of Management, One Cambridge Center, Cambridge, MA, USA

Correspondence: B Subirana, Centre for Coordination Science, MIT Sloan School of Management, MIT Room NE20-308, One Cambridge Center, Cambridge, MA 02142, USA.

E-mail: Subirana@mit.edu

## Abstract

In this paper, I contend that executive leaders can and should be prepared to spot timely opportunities and prevent major failures, even if it requires understanding IT technical details. An essential part of executive leadership is exception management – and exceptions occur everywhere, often surfacing first as details, nothing more. Here I present two cases that show technology is no exception. In this paper, I outline how Steelscreen.com’s and RoweCom’s early years can illustrate why understanding IT technical details helps explain IT-related failures and successes. In particular, I outline the differences between the two stories and argue that the show stopper for Steelscreen was at its crux a technology detail – the other shortcomings could have been addressed elsewhere. I conclude with some recommendations for executive leaders and a brief outline of a teaching approach suggestion for an MBA or a Senior Executive IT class based on the ideas presented. ## Introduction: why details matter

O <sup>ne</sup> <sup>of</sup> <sup>the</sup> <sup>key</sup> <sup>objectives</sup> <sup>when</sup> <sup>teaching</sup> <sup>Steelscreen.com</sup>is to help Executive Leaders understand the relevance is to help Executive Leaders understand the relevance of information technology (IT) technical details in their management duties – not only for IT executives but also for the non-IT executive leaders.<sup>2</sup> I am not trying to make the point that IT is relevant. We assume that the reader believes IT is important to a certain degree. Here, I focus on stressing the importance for executive leadership of knowing what are the relevant IT technical details for a given business issue.

One extreme point of view is that Executive Leadership should not be concerned about details. According to this viewpoint, the Austrian emperor, as an executive leader, was not responsible for the 1805 October 20 flaw that forced its army to surrender.<sup>3</sup> Prior to that date, the Austrian and Russian Emperors agreed to join forces against Napoleon on October 20. However, Austrians used the Gregorian calendar while Russians used the Julian calendar, which lagged 10 days behind. As a consequence, Austrian General Mack’s army was surrounded by Napoleon at Ulm well before the arrival of Russian forces and subsequently forced to surrender. This view also endures that NASA’s senior executives were not at fault when a unit-of-measure mix-up was tied to the loss of a \$125 million Mars orbiter; or that senior Andersen executives were not responsible for email irregularities in the Enron scandal and perhaps not even for the poor public relations management thereafter that led to the fall of the organization. Details have long been at the heart of executive failures and this viewpoint does not account executive leadership as responsible for these details.

In contrast, I feel that Executive Leadership, at its core, is about sensing the organization to anticipate (and resolve) the situations described above and, whenever possible, preventing them before they happen. These are not isolated examples; they have happened in the past and will happen again. Management commands the highest pay in most organizations and its responsibilities include awareness of the relevant technical details unsurfaced by their organizations. If the organization alone, without the executive’s leadership help, does everything perfect (i.e. COO, CFO, CMO, CIO are always on target) then there will be no crisis like the one described above and the CEO will not be put on the line because of them.

In the rest of this brief paper, I outline how Steelscreen.com’s and RoweCom’s early years can illustrate why understanding IT technical details helps explain IT-related failures and successes. In the next section, I outline the differences between the two stories and argue that the show stopper for Steelscreen was technology since the other shortcomings could have been addressed elsewhere. In the subsequent section, we conclude with some recommendations and a brief outline of a teaching approach suggestion for an MBA or a Senior Executive IT class based on the ideas presented.

## Technology details are at the guts of the role of executive leadership in IT: Steelscreen.com and RoweCom, two complementary cases to illustrate it

The analysis that I suggest is centered on two business situations: a failure (Steelscreen.com) and a success (RoweCom). In both, technical details are important. Steelscreen attempted to introduce B2B transaction services to the steel industry, while RoweCom did the same in the scholarly communications industry. There are several factors that can help spot a financially successful B2B company. Table 1 lists some of them. The table also summarizes how these two companies scored alongside these dimensions, RoweCom scoring high in all dimensions while Steelscreen scoring at the bottom in all of them. We now briefly review each company before going back to such tables.

Steelscreen.com, alongside at least 90 other companies, offered steel transaction B2B services to manufacturers, distributors and end users. As events unfolded in 2000 and 2001, no significant orders materialized and the whole industry collapsed. In summary, these 90 companies competed in an industry producing low revenues (if any), by offering a product that nobody could nor wanted to use. The product was seen as threatening by big players despite the fact that the technology never really provided superior value. Big players may have been coerced by regulation to participate, but the technology would have never worked.

In contrast, Richard Rowe founded RoweCom Inc in 1994. Before there was RoweCom, there was Faxon (est. 1881). Faxon was an industry intermediary whose primary value was to help libraries to develop and manage information acquisition contracts with publishers of journals, magazines and other sources. For over one hundred years, Faxon was a family run business with lucrative profits. Perhaps the best way to understand the way in which Faxon was organized is to think about how one might set up a library subscription service in the mid-19th century, an age before phones, computers, motorized land transportation and electronic currency exchange. Until the early 1990s, sales reps relied solely on expensive personal visits. The company used cumbersome manual and paper-intensive processes to deliver its services (albeit with significant error rates – on the order of 1% through the early 1990s). Service transactions were leisurely (6 weeks in length through the early 1990s). Although Faxon readily integrated IT applications to augment its service solutions as they became available, it never radically changed the essence of its key processes.

Traditionally, Faxon had established its pricing as a commission of 5% of each publication’s yearly subscription rate. Yearly subscription prices varied from \$0 to over \$1000 per publication. Not surprisingly, Faxon’s revenue varied widely for activities that incurred essentially the same internal costs. However, since most contracts were bulk contracts, the average revenue was significantly higher than average costs for the service. Contracts with expensive journals were offsetting the money-losing activity of those with low-cost journals. In fact, Faxon – along with two other key intermediary players – dominated over 80% of the market, with steady profits of upwards of \$10 million in the early-1990s.

Until the early 1990s, Richard Rowe was CEO and coowner of the company. At this point, the co-owners of the company, who were then also married, decided to split up. This resulted quasi-simultaneously in the departure of then-CEO Richard Rowe and, shortly thereafter, the sale of Faxon to Dawson, a long-time competitor in Europe. Approximately a year later, pursuing a business plan originally written by his son, Tim Rowe, then in business school at MIT, he hired essential members of Faxon’s IT team that Faxon had earlier dismissed. Off on his own with a horde of important contacts, much of Faxon’s IT knowledge and years of industry experience and credibility, Richard Rowe decided to enter cyberspace and bid \$2 m of his own personal cash. He and his newly assembled team put together an easy-to-use web-based automated journal subscription service. They soon connected it directly with BankOne’s back office systems to automate payments. With the service up and running, they turned to another fundamental challenge: how to attract more customers and thus generate profits. In coming up with a solution for this problem, Rowe pulled another rabbit out of his hat, and in the process became one of the pioneers of B2B marketplace communities.

Rowe went to the Library of the Massachusetts General Hospital, where he presented management with an astounding offer. RoweCom would handle about 10 journal subscriptions each for a \$5 flat fee. The offer was accepted partly because it saved them 5% (or more than \$10,000) in fees to their existing supplier. A year later, when RoweCom had fine tuned the system and assisted library staff in becoming familiar with it, Richard easily convinced them to increase gradually the number of subscriptions they turned over to RoweCom. Before long, Richard Rowe had acquired a handful of similar accounts through other contacts, and found himself in a thriving business once more. Eventually he would acquire Faxon. Let us review why Steelscreen scored low in all dimensions above and see why we contend that technical details were the one factor that could not have been overcome. Let us start by looking at why the other factors were not show stoppers. The first three rows in Table 2 refer to aspects that could have been solved by bringing a senior management’s team to Steelscreen that had the credibility to sign up the heavyweights in a concentrated steel industry. Furthermore, regulation could have been passed to force broad industry participation.

The importance of technical details stems from the fact that customer requirements make it very difficult to improve the existing process with technology. Management thought that a B2B portal would offer transparency and more liquidity to the market benefiting both customers and suppliers. For this to be possible, customers must be able to use the portal to specify what steel they want to buy. This means specifying over 12 steel characteristics, including material type, product, standard organization, standard, alloy, temper/condition, tolerance, certificate, surface protection, decoration/pattern, length, width, thickness and coil/bundle weight plus special needs. This in turn results in over 1 trillion options. Of these, many can be used for a given application such as construction. Traditional practices have evolved ordering into a deceivingly simple process. Take for example a construction SME that buys steel for a given site. The site manager will typically make a short call to a distributor asking for corrugated steel. The distributor then picks which steel in its warehouse matches the specifications given and sends it to the distributor. The task for the B2B site is then to the speed up a 5-min phone call – quite a difficult challenge. Indeed, the proliferation of steel standards and the lack of a unifying framework, make specifying steel through a web application very difficult. What no one has yet figured out is how to specify not a particular type of steel but all the steel combinations that may be suitable for a given application.

Then there is the related problem of specifying what product a customer wants. That is as complicated as defining the space of possible options. A rod for the construction industry can be of many different types and it is quite cumbersome to specify the space of all possibilities. The proliferation of standardization efforts makes it very hard to specify ‘‘or’’ requests. For example, it is difficult to specify something like ‘‘send me any rod that will work on my construction site’’. Furthermore, facing major complexities in content acquisition and acceptance, Steelscreen responded by implementing the ‘‘RFQ’’ transaction process in addition to its Metal-XML initiative. RFQ (Request for Quotation) dictates a set of rigid communication steps for use in the transaction process. Initially intended to standardize and shorten the length of a transaction, the unfamiliar RFQ process ended up creating higher transaction costs than expected. RFQ, like email, is asynchronous. You can respond when you want but latency increases with delay. In short, the cumbersome asynchronous RFQ process could take weeks and did not match the efficiency of the incumbent industry synchronous approach such as a three-minute call to order steel for a construction site. It simply lengthened transaction latency in new ways, creating more problems than it solved (from minutes to weeks in contrast to Rowecom’s financial proposal that brought customer costs down from tens of thousands to a few dollars).

Even broad participation would not have increased value to the industry because of these technical details. Indeed, standardizing content, while possible in RoweCom’s case,<sup>4</sup> is difficult (if not impossible) in the steel industry. Specifying steel to match customer requirements is a very complicated endeavor as mentioned above. The mismatch between user requirements and the portal that Steelscreens IT department built is what ultimately resulted in no orders materializing. None of the other 90 portals succeeded either. Despite the tremendous technical progress in the preceding decades, nobody in the industry could resolve the technical details simply because the technology was not ready for what customers required. In other words, knowing technical detail is at the crux of understanding why this was essentially a revenueless industry.

A common student reaction when teaching the Steelscreen.com case is that CIOs should be the only ones responsible for IT and the ones to blame in this particular situation. Often extreme proponents of this view suggest firing CIOs who do not spot such mistakes. In fact, as mentioned earlier there is little the CEO should do if everything runs smoothly in its management team. This would be an ideal world where the executive team has a number of geniuses ensuring strategy lays out sustainable strategies, finance reports and manages finances appropriately, operations propels products through a flawless supply chain and systems run round the clock. Indeed, if you have a genius as a CIO, perhaps you can ignore technology for a while. It is quite easy to be a manager if your team is always right. However, as technology becomes more pervasive, every genius will eventually make a mistake. Should not the role of the CEO be to spot such a mistake, like the deadly one Steelscreen made? And should not the CEO also help the organization prevent the subsequent consequences? One of our key objectives with this approach is to help CEOs, and executive leadership in general, elucidate their own role in IT – a role I contend cannot ignore IT details. For the sake of argument, let us assume that the feasibility of Steelscreen’s business model hinged on whether steel could be specified or not – is this enough to justify that executive leadership should understand the information technology details and its relevance given that nobody else did? We believe so.

The mistakes of Steelscreen were not an isolated incident in its industry. In early 2001, we identified roughly 90 players who entered the B2B steel market hoping to capture some piece of the industry’s transaction activity. Amazingly, most of these players made the same false ‘‘collaboration’’ assumptions as Steelscreen. A whole industry was created that virtually had no revenue ever. I estimate that over 5000 people were involved and several hundred million dollars were ultimately wasted. This brings me back to the central point that I wanted to stress with this case: the role of the CEO in business issues requires a technical understanding.

One can also make the case that part of RoweCom’s success was due to the fact that Mr. Rowe understood the many technology details involved in the business. Of course, technology alone is not enough and it could all have happened out of luck or because RoweCom’s CTO alerted Mr. Rowe of all the opportunities while preventing all mistakes. In any case, our interviews with Mr. Rowe clearly suggest that he was aware of the relevant details. How he got to them is a different aspect of why he was so successful, one that also provides valuable insights into what executive leadership can do to prevent situations such as Steelscreen’s to occur. In other words, when companies seize opportunities in a timely fashion and prevent mistakes, some may argue that their respective teams did the right thing and that executive leadership is not to be praised. Thus, details are most relevant when failures can be tracked down to what appear, after-the-fact, to be simple-tounderstand mistakes. However, I feel success stories are also useful because they illustrate how executives have dealt with situations where information technology was essential.

## Conclusion: A business to technology drill-down approach for teaching information technology to executives

I contend that executive leaders can and should be prepared to spot timely opportunities and prevent major failures, even if it requires understanding IT technical details. Consequently, I feel there are four key questions all executives should be prepared to answer: How can I spot costly mistakes before it is too late? What will I do to resolve the situations created? How will I help my organization prevent these situations in the future? What will be my personal agenda to keep on top of Information Technology?

I contend that IT Executive Training’s key objective should be to address these four questions. Particularly, my approach does so by looking at a series of two-part modules where a business situation is combined with the relevant technology. In the example outlined above, the business situation has to do with deploying an industry-wide solution while the second part in the module is centered on data integration and standardization technologies. Steelscreen provides a worst-case scenario, while RoweCom demonstrates a best one. As mentioned above, I have found that presenting two such contrasting examples often enhances the learning. An essential piece of this approach is to illustrate how one can drill down into relevant details starting from a business situation. In Steelscreen’s case, a systematic analysis of the business drivers helps spot integration as a key technical barrier that deserves subsequent detail analysis.

Table 3 presents a proposal of how such class may be structured around a series of 10 two-part modules each on a given row with its corresponding details in the columns (including business situation and technical details). The proposal divides the class into five parts based on the key decisions that executives need to make: organizational design, positioning and IT (aka strategy), operational effectiveness, implementation (encompassing internal implementation and market deployment of IT applications) and the future of IT (akin to the personal IT agenda of the executives).

Executive leadership is at its guts, about exception management – and exceptions occur everywhere, often surfacing first as details, nothing more. Technology is no exception. The future will be led by those who can best prevent their organizations from major IT failures while, at the same time, reap the unprecedented benefits IT can deliver. As innovation enables organizations based on ever more complex systems, executive leadership, to keep their ships on track should pay due attention to IT technical detail.
