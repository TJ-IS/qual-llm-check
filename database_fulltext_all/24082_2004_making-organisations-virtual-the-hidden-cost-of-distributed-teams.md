# Making organisations virtual: the hidden cost of distributed teams

Karin Breu, Christopher J Hemingway

Information Systems Research Centre, Cranfield School of Management, Cranfield, Bedfordshire, UK

Correspondence:

K Breu, Information Systems Research Centre, Cranfield School of Management, Cranfield, Bedfordshire, MK43 0AL, UK. E-mail: k.breu@cranfield.ac.u

## Abstract

This paper reports an exploratory case study that used boundary theory to investigate the impact of organisational virtualisation on work units. Specifically, the research analysed the transition in a public sector organisation from permanent, co-located teams to temporary virtual teams and its impact on team boundary properties and activities. The study contributes to existing knowledge of virtual teams and virtualisation processes in several ways. The findings suggest that, contrary to existing thinking, rather than removing organisational and institutional boundaries, virtualisation increases the number and complexity of such boundaries at the work unit level. The findings also indicate that the chronic lack of knowledge sharing on virtual teams is, in addition to difficulties of establishing interpersonal trust, due to a lack of trust in the technology as an appropriate medium for sensitive knowledge sharing. The implications of the study for practice are that efficiency gains from organisational virtualisation can easily be offset by the need to invest additional resources and team members’ time into cultivating a greater number of transient relationships at a distance. Organisations also need to invest in opportunities for face-to-face interaction where sensitive knowledge sharing is a critical resource for a virtual team.

## Introduction

What role does ICT (Information and Communication Technology) play in supporting knowledge sharing within and across work units as they become virtual? These interrelated questions are important because of the increasing use of virtual teams in organisational practice.

The wide adoption of virtual working is promoted by a range of stakeholders in the expectation of economic, social and environmental benefits. For instance, by moving the work to the worker, instead of the worker to work (Apgar, 1998), organisations report reduced absenteeism, lower staff turnover, and millions of pounds of cost savings in office accommodation (Bentley, 2003). The nearly 2600 respondents to our survey in the Sunday Times on The Future of Work stated a preference for flexible work arrangements for the reason that workplace choice enables employees to balance career and home life responsibilities (Cranfield School of Management, 2001). Governments are also promoting virtual working as a means of reducing the burden of commuting on the traffic infrastructure and the environment (Department of Trade and Industry, 2003).

While much has been written on virtual organisations, this literature focuses on organisational design (e.g., Chesbrough and Teece, 1996; DeSanctis and Monge, 1999; Cramton, 2001; Griffith et al., 2003). Organisational virtualisation, in contrast, addresses the transition from the traditional bricks-and-mortar to a virtual organisation (Boudreau et al., 1998; Dutton, 1999). Understanding the transition is vital because over 50% of virtualisation initiatives fail to deliver their objectives (Biggs, 2000). We contribute to the understanding of the process of virtualisation by analysing its actual effects through a detailed case study. Through the perspective of boundary theory, we identified how organisational virtualisation changed work unit boundaries, their properties, and the activities occurring at those boundaries. The case study at

Inspectorate (a pseudonym), a public sector organisation in Europe, was an exceptional opportunity to investigate the effects of virtualisation because the organisation was making a transition from approximately 400 permanent and co-located project teams to a resource pool of 2000 inspectors that were to be dynamically assigned to temporary virtual teams. Through previous research at Inspectorate, we were aware of ongoing initiatives to increase operational efficiency through structural and process change, and a greater use of ICT to support staff coordination, work scheduling, and service delivery. Efficiency-enhancing measures were used by Inspectorate to address the competitive pressures resulting from a gradual introduction of market forces into its hitherto monopolistic market. We selected the Inspectorate as the case study site as we expected work unit boundaries to change as a result of organisational-level virtualisation.

The paper begins with a review of social scientific boundary theory, discussing boundary perspectives, properties and activities, and the theory of organisational virtualisation used to analyse the case. We then introduce the research questions and method, which employed a case study approach using multiple methods of data collection. The empirical findings from the case study are presented, structured around the theoretical framework. The paper concludes with the implications of this exploratory study for theory and practice.

## Literature review

Organisations in the public and private sector alike face ongoing pressures to become more flexible and responsive to change, and are looking increasingly to virtual forms of organisation to reduce organisational slack, facilitate crossfunctional learning (Handy, 1995), focus on core competencies and lower cost (Dutton, 1999). Building blocks of the virtual organisation are work units such as virtual teams and virtual communities, which make extensive use of ICT. For traditional organisations, adopting virtual arrangements is a gradual process. When bricks-and-mortar organisations introduce virtual organisation, they undergo a transition period, referred to as virtualisation, where traditional structures coexist and sometimes conflict with virtual structures (Schultze and Orlikowski, 2001).

Virtual working research has investigated work units, such as on-line communities (Williams and Cothrel, 2000), informal communities-of-practice (Brown, 1998), and virtual cross-functional and global teams (Jarvenpaa and Leidner, 1999), and identified various implications of virtualisation as, for instance, for team processes (Lipnack and Stamps, 1999), leadership (Pauleen and Yoong, 2001), trust (Handy, 1995), communication (DeSanctis and Monge, 1999), innovation (Chesbrough and Teece, 1996), R&D (Gassmann and Zedtwitz, 2003), and risk mitigation (Grabowski and Roberts, 1999). An important gap, however, exists in current understanding of the transition that traditional bricks-and-mortar organisations undergo when beginning to adopt virtual elements of organisation. Specifically, research has not yet explored the impact that organisational-level virtualisation initiatives exert on work unit boundaries, their properties, and the activities occurring at those boundaries.

The notion of boundaries presents a fertile conceptual tool because it captures a fundamental process in organisations, that of relationality (Lamont and Molnar, 2002). Boundary theory is particularly appropriate to analysing changes at the work unit level, which often displace existing boundaries and redefine their meanings, yet its application to research on virtual organisation and ICT-based working arrangements is still uncommon (Hinds and Kiesler, 1995; Yan and Louis, 1999). The analysis of the virtualisation process through boundary theory presents a significant opportunity for Information Systems (IS) research that seeks to further our understanding of the challenges that traditional organisations can expect when introducing virtual working arrangements.

Concepts such as economy, industry, supply chain, organisation, department and work unit all imply the existence of boundaries. Some boundaries are more discernible than others, especially when social boundaries coincide with physical boundaries. For instance, the boundaries of a firm that houses all its assets, resources and employees within a single building, appear concrete and immutable. Certain types of boundaries, although abstract constructions, have legitimacy and social significance, as in the case of the legal status of an organisation. Regardless of type, a boundary determines what counts as ‘within’ and ‘beyond’.

Organisation theorists have developed four perspectives for defining boundaries, which, they argue, are applicable to all units of analysis (Yan and Louis, 1999). Figure 1 maps the four boundary perspectives against two dimensions: (a) the purpose of the boundary as either differentiating and protecting a unit, or as a site of interaction and exchange across units; and (b) the location of the boundary as either between units, or between a unit and its wider environment.

The demarcation perspective regards the boundary as a means for differentiating and protecting a unit from other units (Scott, 1992). The perimeter perspective describes the boundary as differentiating and closing off a unit from its entire environment (Orton and Weick, 1990). In contrast, the interface perspective conceptualises the boundary as an interface between interacting units, such as work units, departments or organisations (Brown, 1983). The frontier perspective views the boundary as the site of interaction between a unit and the environment (Ancona, 1990). Demarcation, perimeter, interface and frontier are four metaphors for conceptualising boundaries in organisations. These perspectives provide a language for describing multiple purposes that boundaries can serve and, hence, the diverse sets of properties and activities they represent.

## Properties of work unit boundaries

A work unit refers to any ‘suborganisational entity composed of multiple individuals performing certain organisational tasks’ (Yan and Louis, 1999: 26). Examples of work units include project and management teams, departments and committees. The relationships between work units have been characterised by three properties: interface, permeability and boundedness. They are illustrated in Figure 2.

The interface depicts the boundary as the site of interaction among social actors from multiple work units (Brown, 1983). An interface between work units may arise due to a number of factors, such as interdependency between units to accomplish a shared task, the need to share resources between work units that are part of the same department, loyalty among work unit members arising from common personal backgrounds, a shared work history or simply their physical proximity. In a tightly organised interface, interaction across boundaries with non-members is highly controlled. A strategic alliance, for example, may involve collaboration between research teams from two independent organisations. In this case, there may be strict rules defining the nature and extent of interaction. In contrast, a loosely organised interface provides members of a work unit with an environment in which they can freely interact with non-members.

While the notion of interface concerns the interaction among social actors, permeability addresses the extent to which boundaries allow information and resource flows between work units and into the environment. When these flows occur easily, a boundary is said to have high permeability (Brown, 1983). Where information and resource flows are constricted, a boundary has low permeability. Permeability of boundaries varies between types of information and resources. For example, historical information may be allowed to flow more freely across work unit boundaries than more sensitive information like future strategy. Generally, inward information and resource flows tend to be less constrained than outward flows (e.g. Schultze and Boland, 2000).

Boundedness refers to the strength and visibility of social boundaries (Yan and Louis, 1999). A tightly bounded work unit has a readily identifiable membership and the members have strong associations with each other and with the work unit. Work units can be tightly bounded, regardless of their formal (e.g. a project team) or informal status (e.g. a community of practice). Tightly bounded work units may resist external intervention if pressure for adaptation and change is perceived as a threat (Schneider, 1991). For a loosely bounded work unit, membership is less clear and the strength of association between members is weak. Where members of a work unit lack commonality and, consequently, do not collaborate effectively, a work unit may become so underbounded that it ceases to exist (Alderfer, 1980). To prevent disintegration of an underbounded work unit, increased effort to maintain boundaries is required.

## Work unit boundary activities

The properties of work unit boundaries are preserved or changed by boundary activities. Some of this activity may prevent ‘intrusion’ from the outside, while other activity may seek input from the environment. Boundary activity may also take place simply to reinforce the presence of the boundary in the minds of work unit members. Three types of boundary activity are identified in the literature: spanning, buffering, and bringing-up (Yan and Louis, 1999).

The interface and frontiers perspectives (see Figure 1) conceptualise a work unit boundary as the location of boundary spanning interactions between members and non-members. Members of work units can assume ‘ambassador’ roles when they interact across boundaries. Examples of boundary spanning activities concern ambassadors reaching into the wider organisation to acquire information, resources and support (Ancona and Caldwell, 1992). Boundary spanning can be competitive or collaborative, depending upon the compatibility of work unit goals. For formal work units, spanning may be facilitated by formal structures and channels, or through informal structures where work unit members use their personal networks to obtain and exchange resources (Krackhardt and Hanson, 1993).

The demarcation and perimeter perspectives (see Figure 1) imply activities that close off the system from the environment. These are referred to as boundary buffering. Members of work units can assume a sentry role when they differentiate and protect boundaries against the external environment. In a work unit, sentries engage in buffering activities and, thereby, influence the permeability of its boundary (Ancona and Caldwell, 1992). Examples of buffering activities are quality control on incoming resources and information as well as the censoring and directing of outgoing flows.

Whereas buffering and spanning involve activity across the work unit boundary, bringing-up activities are solely internally focused. The literature discusses two types of bringing-up activities: focalisation and identity creation.

Focalisation includes the shaping of a collective vision among the members of a work unit, rituals for recognising and rewarding progress, and the socio-cultural appropriation of ICTs (Boland and Tenkasi, 1995). Allowing work unit members themselves to form a shared sense of purpose and, thus, to take ownership of its vision, is regarded as a crucial focalisation activity (Smith and Comer, 1994). Identity creation concerns the evolution of an internal working climate that provides members with a stronger sense of security, mutual respect and identification with the work unit than with the external environment. Work units with a pronounced sense of identity among their members are seen as especially conducive to learning (Louis, 1996).

## Organisational virtualisation

The emergence of virtual organisation is inconceivable without the capabilities of modern day networked ICTs (Lucas and Baroudi, 1994; Handy, 1995). Creating the virtual organisation requires, according to Dutton (1999), three interdependent activities: networking, restructuring, and building a learning-organisation culture.

Networking relates to the use of ICTs to improve an organisation’s efficiency of, and ability for, gathering and sharing information across geographical and functional divides, and enables greater horizontal and vertical connections among employees and corporate resources (Robey et al., 2000). Electronic networks like Intranets (internal to employees), extranets (accessible to customers and/or business partners) and the Internet (public space) push classic organisational boundaries into the virtual space (Schultze and Orlikowski, 2001). ICT also brings new opportunities for organising a workforce, as in virtual teams and on-line communities, that can cut across traditional boundaries with ease (Mankin et al., 1996). A workforce networked through ICT can communicate, collaborate on joint work, integrate information, make decisions, and implement actions on a global scale (Ives and Jarvenpaa, 1991).

Restructuring typically concerns downsizing, outsourcing and re-engineering business processes (Dutton, 1999). Networking opportunities are seized to enable organisational forms that are more complex, yet offer greater operational and cost flexibility (Miles and Snow, 1992) and bring greater efficiency into inter-organisational collaboration processes (Fulk and DeSanctis, 1995). Electronic and networked information and communication flows also introduce new opportunities for removing layers from the organisational hierarchy (Chesbrough and Teece, 1996) and decentralising the allocation of responsibilities and resources (Lucas and Baroudi, 1994).

Finally, virtual organisation necessitates the development of a learning-organisation culture in order to bridge the geographic divides between dispersed organisational members (Yap and Bjorn-Andersen, 2002) and bridge the gap between formal and informal social structures (Ancona and Caldwell, 1992). The frequent creation and disbandment of work units, typical of virtual organisation, increases the need for employee wide knowledge sharing and learning (Schultze and Orlikowski, 2001). Virtual work units may require changes to reward systems that are based on team, rather than individual performance, as well as membership selection processes that take into account the motivational and technological demands on members working in isolation (Lurey and Raisinghani, 2001).

## Research framework, questions and method

From our review of boundary and virtualisation theory, we elicited a research framework (summarised in Table 1) to guide the empirical investigation of organisational virtualisation and its impact upon work unit boundary properties and activities.

The study addressed the following research questions, which were framed in the light of the identified gap in current understanding: What are the implications for work units, such as project teams and work groups, when bricksand-mortar organisations introduce virtualisation initiatives? What role does ICT play in supporting knowledge sharing within and across work units as they become virtual?

The study employed a case study methodology (Eisenhardt, 1989), using semi-structured data collection instruments that were designed in light of the research framework. Data were collected during summer 2001, using multiple methods of data collection to allow triangulation of results: seven individual interviews, one focus group interview, 20 h of non-participant observation, and retrieval of documentary evidence. The interview sessions, ranging from 60 min for individual interviews to 3 h for focus group interviews, were recorded. Verbatim transcripts were produced and, prior to data analysis, were administered to interviewees for validation. Notes were taken at around 20 h of non-participant observation of team meetings, capturing team members’ reflections on the work units in change. Documentary evidence of the structural changes in Inspectorate’s internal organisation and inspection service delivery process complemented the primary data. Categories emerging from the data were mapped to boundary constructs that are reported in the boundary and virtualisation theory. Their potential for explaining the impact of organisational-level virtualisation processes upon the work unit level was subsequently assessed.

## Case study site and sample

The empirical research was undertaken in Inspectorate, an independent national agency in a European Union member state responsible for conducting inspections of public sector organisations under the direction of a national governmental body, referred to as Regulator (a pseudonym). Inspectorate assumes an essential responsibility in maintaining citizens’ confidence in the effective use of public funds for public service delivery.

Police and fire services, prisons, social housing, hospitals and health care, schools and higher/further education, and environmental institutions are examples of public sector organisations that are subject to inspections. Inspections involve the examination of public sector organisations’ regulatory compliance and quality of service provided to the citizens; identification of laxity in following proper procedures; assisting public sector organisations in improving management and decision-making; identification of lessons to be learnt; dissemination of good practice among public sector institutions; and making the results of inspections available to the public. Inspectorate’s traditional role of a control function, expecting inspectors to uncover and penalise inadequate performance, has expanded to include a consultative function, requiring inspectors to be able to say how to improve poor performance, achieve regulatory compliance, and enhance service quality.

Inspectorate is obliged to undertake its work in a professional, objective and cost-effective manner. It employs a workforce of around 2000 qualified professionals with university education. Inspectorate delivers its work through inspection teams. Originally, inspection teams were permanent, had stable membership, and spent most of their time at ‘client’ sites. Inspections must strictly comply with statutory requirements, professional standards (e.g. competence, objective behaviour, due diligence), and deadlines set by Regulator.

While traditionally, Inspectorate owned a monopolistic market in the classic style of a public sector organisation, in recent years, it became increasingly exposed to competitive market conditions. Rather than being assigned inspection projects by Regulator, Inspectorate now competes against private sector organisations. To improve its cost competitiveness, Inspectorate sought efficiency gains from organisational virtualisation, in the course of which the permanent team structure was replaced with temporary virtual teams, which existed, on average, for 3–4 months.

The type of work unit studied in this research was that of intra-organisational temporary virtual inspection teams. For the purpose of this research, we defined temporary virtual teams as geographically dispersed groups of people with a common purpose who carry out interdependent tasks across locations and time, using technology-supported communication substantially more than face-to-face communication, and which arise and disband on demand (based on Maznevski and Chudoba, 2000; Cramton, 2001). A total of seven individual interviews were conducted, including a manager of a local office of Inspectorate, one inspection manager, two senior inspectors, and three inspectors. One focus group interview was undertaken with members from the same team, including an inspection manager, a senior inspector, two inspectors and a trainee inspector.

## Case analysis and findings

## Virtualisation initiatives at Inspectorate

Since the introduction of competition, Inspectorate attributed the declining rate of success in winning inspection contracts against private firms to an inability to compete on cost and, therefore, decided to seek operational efficiency improvements by introducing virtual team structures. We analysed this initiative by applying the theoretical framework of organisational virtualisation, consisting of networking, restructuring, and developing a learningorganisation culture.

## Undependable infrastructure inhibits networking

Inspectors traditionally carried technical documents and client information in paper form, as they had no electronic access to corporate resources from client sites. When Inspectors required information they did not have to hand, they returned to the local office to download and print the documentation from the intranet.

Inspectorate’s networking initiatives introduced remote intranet and database access to ease information retrieval, and provided remote e-mail access and mobile phones to facilitate communications. Usage of these facilities from client sites, however, proved mostly impracticable. Inspectors reported that remote access was, much of the time, infeasible as the entire inspection team had available only one telephone landline for all of their communications — receiving incoming calls, participating in audio conferences, and accessing e-mail and the intranet. Interviewees commented:

They are forgetting that we are sitting in client offices with one crappy telephone line and that we’re not necessarily going to be able to log on, get our e-mails. (Senior Inspector 2)

It’s missing the point. If you’ve only got one telephone line, you can’t stay logged-in all day because you really need the phone to do other things. (Inspector 1)

To remedy the situation, Inspectorate sought to obtain permission from clients to install ISDN lines on their premises for use by their inspection teams. Where permission was granted, inspectors still encountered difficulties with using the lines, due to unreliabilities of the ISDN modem and the absence of technical support for solving problems. An inspector explained:

We’ve actually had an ISDN line fitted into two or three of our offices at client sites in November but we’ve yet to get it to actually work. They’ve given us the damned ISDN line but it’s missing the card or something. I’m not technical. (Inspector 3)

Inspectors further encountered cultural barriers to using Inspectorate’s resources remotely. Clients, who tended to have little experience themselves of working virtually and using remote electronic resources, were suspicious of inspectors, whose services they paid for, doing actual work when being on-line, and accused them of wasting their time. An inspector explained:

The other thing is that because of the offices we’ve got are actually in client’s premises, you can’t just do what you want. The problem is what the client thinks. They are paying a lot of money. If all they see you do is seeing you on e-mail, instead of doing the work, it’s not a good PR job for [Inspectorate]. (Inspector 5)

The poor electronic access to the corporate intranet from client sites was detrimental for inspectors who depended on technical documentation for their day-to-day work. For instance, essential documentation was previously held in the so-called ‘Blue Book’, which was no longer available in printed form.

The Blue Book used to be very useful. It was like an encyclopaedia. Specialists made sure that whenever new procedures came out, it would be updated. If you needed to know what on earth they meant by [technical term], the Blue Book would tell you. We need that basic tool but it doesn’t exist any more. (Senior Inspector 3)

Inspectors’ experiences with the ICT support were highly dissatisfying, as one Inspection Manager explained:

What really got everybody upset was the equipment not working at remote locations. And that was, like, three or four people just exploded. You know, it’s true, it’s true, everything I say. It’s a common problem. (Inspection Manager 1)

Overall, the findings revealed that inappropriate ICT infrastructures at client sites prevented inspectors from making effective use of Inspectorate’s information resources.

## Restructuring increases number of work relationships

Under the traditional organisation structure (see Figure 3a), inspections were conducted by permanent teams, consisting of an Inspection Manager and between six and 10 inspectors of varying seniority, ranging from trainee to senior inspector. Each inspection team owned a set of clients and, thereby, a fixed number of inspection projects, which they had to complete over the course of a year. Teams had great discretion in determining the amount of time they dedicated to an inspection project. There was wide consensus among inspectors that the generous allocation of inspectors’ time built significant slack into the inspection process and, thus, resulted in an uncompetitive cost model. When asked how much time they spent on a project, an interviewee replied, ‘as long as it takes to get the job done’ (Senior Inspector 6).

By inspecting the same set of clients every year, the team gained intimate knowledge of the client organisations’ people, cultures, and structures; their operational strengths and weaknesses; and performance developments over the years. The permanent team structure was naturally very supportive of informal, local knowledge sharing. Each team member was dedicated to a specific area of expertise and ensured that amendments in national and European government policy and laws were interpreted and integrated into the inspection practices of the team. The advantage of the permanent team model consisted in its knowledge management capabilities and service quality, yet, at the expense of cost effectiveness.

Inspectorate’s restructuring initiative virtualised the organisation structure by replacing the permanent inspection teams with temporary, virtual inspection teams. Whilst the upper levels of the organisation hierarchy remained intact, the permanent teams were broken up and the entire inspection workforce was aggregated into a single resource pool, thereby introducing a temporary team structure (see Figure 3b). Inspection Managers, who were part of permanent inspection teams in the past, were now placed into separate functions and given the new responsibility for winning inspection contracts and resourcing their delivery by buying inspectors’ time from the resource pool. The new arrangements meant that inspectors would now work for multiple Inspection Managers and, given the temporary team structure, work with a continuously changing set of peers.

## Focus on individual performance inhibits emergence of learning-organisation culture

The third component of effective organisational virtualisation according to the literature concerns the development of a learning-organisation culture. The data revealed no evidence of an effort within Inspectorate’s virtualisation initiative for developing a culture of organisationwide learning and knowledge sharing. Organisational learning had also not been an explicit concern in the past, where the organisation relied on informal and local learning within the co-located, permanent teams.

Incentive systems based on team, as opposed to individual performance are recommended in the literature as important factors for encouraging a learning-organisation culture. At Inspectorate, however, we found evidence of two new initiatives that sought to encourage focus on individual performance instead. First, the traditional system of awarding pay on length-of-service was replaced with an individual productivity measure, introducing ‘billable hours’ as a metric for assessing inspector performance on the basis of the number of hours they had charged to clients. Second, a new project resource management system was created whereby a fixed number of man hours was mandated for project completion.

Impact of virtualisation on inspection team boundary activities The case study explored the extent to which the organisation-level virtualisation processes impacted upon inspection team boundary activities in terms of the spanning, buffering and bringing-up efforts that they required of team members. The data analysis revealed that, to be able to operate effectively, virtualisation required team members to expend greater effort on building external relationships, sharing knowledge with peers, and building internal team cohesion and trust. Their ability to achieve these requirements, however, was found to vary.

Virtual team members increased boundary spanning activities because they now needed to access client and technical knowledge that used to reside in the co-located teams

Spanning activities concern the interactions between members of a work unit and the external environment. The analysis revealed that the demand for boundary spanning activities significantly increased when the previously permanent teams were replaced with the temporary virtual teams (see Figure 4).

In an inspection process, two principal types of knowledge are applied and created that our interviewees referred to as ‘technical knowledge’ and ‘client knowledge’. Technical knowledge includes inspection procedures, policies and guidelines. It changes frequently in line with government policy and the law, requiring inspectors to continually refresh their technical knowledge. Client knowledge includes an understanding of the organisation’s culture, historic development, governance, performance history, and operational strengths and weaknesses.

Figure 4 Virtualisation impact on team boundary spanning.

Under the permanent team structure, inspectors could easily and directly access the owners of technical knowledge by liasing with the Local Office Manager and the respective experts on their team. As inspection teams serviced a fixed client group they could also naturally build intimate client knowledge over time. Under the virtual team structure inspectors needed to engage in new spanning activities if they wanted to be able to access the owners of technical and client knowledge. Inspectors could no longer rely on their team members for acquiring and sharing technical knowledge due to the temporary team arrangements; they now depended on access to a personal network of peers. The direct sourcing of technical knowledge from Local Office Managers was also impaired, compelling inspectors to seek knowledge from the temporary Inspection Managers. As Inspection Managers were now no longer part of the permanent inspection team, they were less available for responding to inspectors’ requests. An interviewee explained:

It’s very much a feeling on my part that Inspection Managers have now their own network but the people in the field don’t get to know from them what’s going on with clients any more. (Senior Inspector 2)

Inspectors agreed that client knowledge was vital for a successful client engagement and their ability to make recommendations that could help clients improve performance. The temporary virtual team model, however, had largely removed opportunities for building up intimate client knowledge. An interviewee explained:

When you go to the same client year on year, you get a good idea of what the business of the client is, what its particular issues are, who the key players are, what the key risks are and how they might be addressed. It’s like — you are in the picture. The problem now is we’ve got these stupid arrangements and, as a result, the client knowledge just goes. Which it shouldn’t. (Inspector 6)

While client knowledge was accumulated within the boundaries of the permanent teams, it was now dispersed throughout the pool of inspectors. Every time a newly composed team prepared an inspection, they had to try to retrieve historical client knowledge from the pool of inspectors. An interviewee explained this change:

When I went to [client name] – I’d never been there before – because the Inspection Manager wasn’t there, I didn’t get the information on the place. I didn’t know who did what. I was always having to ask a colleague, who is now in a different team, to find out who did what, what was going on. (Inspector 4)

To summarise, in the accounts of inspectors, virtualisation introduced the requirement for more conscious spanning with a set of peers that was much wider, constantly changing, and less easily accessible.

## Virtual team managers increased buffering activities to ensure virtual team members met aggressive project deadlines and budgets

Buffering activities control and regulate resource and information flows between a work unit and its external environment. The data revealed that virtual inspection teams were less able to buffer team boundaries against external resource and information demands than the permanent teams of the past (see Figure 5). Owing to the permanent nature of the previous teams and their client relationships, there were hardly any demands for knowledge access across teams. Inspection Managers had to engage rarely in buffering activities, as they were permanent members of their inspection teams, and, thus, had first-hand knowledge of the client engagements and complete control over inspectors’ time.

With the introduction of the virtual teams, the work unit’s capacity for buffering their boundaries was greatly diminished. Teams were now no longer the owners of a particular client relationship and were no longer able to accumulate knowledge about a fixed set of clients. The continuous assembly and disassembly of teams dissipated client knowledge throughout the inspection pool.

Under the virtual team model, Inspection Managers faced the new challenge of scheduling inspection projects and resources such that, ideally, teams would be composed of inspectors who had previously worked with a given client. In practice, however, this proved frequently infeasible when inspectors with the client knowledge required for a new project were still tied up in an on-going engagement. An interviewee illustrated this change:

Unfortunately, you’re never going to get around that because of the nature work is now. So, if you like, teams used to go around in one piece. It would be nice to think that you, when you go back to a client, you go back in the same team year on year. It’s no longer like that. Sometimes we achieve it but, most of the time, we don’t. (Inspector 2)

Inspection Managers now had control over inspectors’ time only as long as they were part of a temporary team. With the number of man hours allocated to projects now fixed, Inspection Managers were keen to ensure that the time available was dedicated to the inspection, leading them to buffer a team’s time resources against external demands. This resulted in a dilemma for the team members who, on the one hand, were pressured to dedicate their time exclusively to a particular inspection, whilst, on the other, were increasingly approached by peers working on other teams with requests for information on clients they had previously worked with. As peer-to-peer knowledge sharing had not been institutionalised under the virtualisation model, inspectors were unable to evidence effective use of time when responding to peer requests. Interviewees explained:

![](/api/attachments/KKQT6N2C/fulltext/images/4475550e234545ffb8c7515b30cca093017d42177b56e799d64d47dbb48afb42.jpg)  
Figure 5 Virtualisation impact on team boundary buffering.

Most people are accessible one way or the other. But your time must be dedicated to your client. (Inspector 5) People don’t talk to each other as much as they should any more. They’re too busy. (Senior Inspector 1)

Peer-to-peer knowledge sharing at client sites was also not easily achieved, as inspectors typically shared an office with client staff, which did not afford the privacy to discuss other clients’ concerns with peers. An interviewee explained:

It’s lack of privacy. Clients aren’t very happy when you start discussing other clients. Their perception is that you’re there to concentrate on their inspection. But you might get a phone call asking you about an issue affecting somebody else, which you have to answer. But clients do get a bit touchy. (Inspector 2)

To summarise, in the accounts of inspectors, the virtualisation process inhibited team members’ ability to access the knowledge of peers on other teams.

## Bringing-up activities decreased under a transient team structure

Bringing-up activities concern the internal social relationships and processes within a work unit. The data revealed that permanent team arrangements are far more conducive to bringing-up activities than virtual team arrangements (see Figure 6). Under the permanent team model, bringingup activities within the teams occurred naturally. With teams’ membership and client groups being largely stable, work units evolved a strong team culture, with a shared vision, sense of identity and trust among their members, and working practices that reflected a team’s history and context.

![](/api/attachments/KKQT6N2C/fulltext/images/7e61f2df3a7d139edbbccdc320ca312beb42383c222b3a9e592c1f00e0c184b5.jpg)  
Figure 6 Virtualisation impact on team boundary bringing-up.

The virtual team arrangements offered fewer opportunities for building relationships of trust between members, resulting in client knowledge, especially of a confidential nature, being less widely shared. An interviewee explained:

The sharing of client knowledge is very dependent on how close the relationship is between you and your peers. Unfortunately, you lose the relationship when you move from project to project. (Inspector 4)

Lack of trust between peers was not the only barrier to sensitive knowledge sharing that the virtualisation arrangements introduced. The ICT was experienced as unreliable, fostering reservations about the security of e-mail as well as the opportunities it might present for breaching confidentiality.

It’s often confidential, so it can’t be freely shared. I’m certainly not going to put it in an e-mail or electronic memo. (Inspection Manager 1)

The temporary virtual team arrangements also led to Inspectors feeling isolated and disconnected from the organisation. An Inspection Manager explained:

Isolation is the problem now. It’s not about being left alone, its about feeling lonely. The social side of that is, really, we see the difference, we have to be very careful how we use new ways of working. (Inspection Manager 2)

Initiatives by team members to arrange informal gettogethers rarely succeeded because of the time and cost of travel. A Senior Inspector stated:

We try and organise social events — but, as people are in different places, it’s really difficult to get together. It’s very expensive as well. (Senior Inspector 1)

To summarise, in the accounts of inspectors, bringing-up activities did not naturally arise in the virtual teams, and were made more difficult to achieve by the teams’ temporary existence. The lack of opportunity to build relationships of trust, combined with the lack of trust in technology as an appropriate medium for communicating confidential information inhibited the sharing of sensitive knowledge.

Impact of virtualisation on inspection team boundary properties Virtualisation affected the properties of team boundaries by: creating new interfaces, thereby increasing the complexity and number of relationships within the inspection process; decreasing boundary permeability significantly, thereby constricting information flows between participants in the inspection process; and loosening the previously tightly bounded team boundaries, thereby introducing an extensive, yet unfulfilled, need for communication and social interaction.

## New interfaces increase complexity and number of work relationships

The interface depicts the boundary as the site of interaction among social actors from multiple work units. Virtualisation had a significant impact upon the team interface by increasing the complexity of social relationships involved in the inspection process (see Figure 7). Under the permanent team model, inspectors and Inspection Managers were part of the same team and, given their direct, long-term relationships with a stable set of clients, there existed an interface between inspection team and client.

The temporary virtual team arrangements introduced new interfaces that did not exist under the old model. First, with the Inspection Managers being removed from their teams, inspectors and Inspection Managers had now separate interfaces to the client. Second, new interfaces emerged between the inspectors and Inspection Manager, and the relationship between the inspectors on a given virtual team and the pool of inspectors.

As a result of the increased number of interfaces, building and maintaining social relationships became a major part of inspectors’ and Inspection Managers’ work. The inspection process now depended on the effective management of relationships across four interfaces, in contrast to the single, stable interface under the permanent team structure of the past. In addition, the four interfaces were now temporary and, thus, had to be created anew with every inspection assignment. Finally, the increased mobility of inspectors, both geographically and organisationally, meant that social interaction depended far more on the use of ICT than in the past.

## Decreased boundary permeability restricts information exchange

Permeability concerns the extent to which boundaries allow information and resource flows between work units and their external environment. Virtualisation decreased boundary permeability (see Figure 8).

Under the permanent team model, boundaries between inspection teams and clients were highly permeable, allowing information to flow easily. Inspection Managers, who were now responsible for managing groups of temporary inspection teams, were rarely at client sites and no longer participated in inspections. Virtualisation thus created boundaries with low permeability between Inspection Managers and both clients and inspection teams. Virtualisation also decreased the permeability of the boundary between inspection teams and clients by introducing a temporary client relationship. Inspection teams’ boundary to the pool of inspectors had low permeability, due to Inspection Managers’ buffering activities aiming to safeguard inspectors’ time for their assignments. As a result of virtualisation, boundary permeability decreased significantly and, given the increased number of interfaces, information flows now had to cross a larger number of boundaries.

Decreased team boundedness weakens work relationships Boundedness refers to the strength and visibility of social boundaries demarcating a work unit. Virtualisation had a significant impact upon the work units by loosening the previously tightly knit boundaries (see Figure 9).

The permanent team model created tightly bounded work units where members had strong associations with each other, evolving a strong team culture, sense of identity and trust. This naturally fostered informal knowledge sharing among team members. Virtualisation decreased informal knowledge sharing, in particular the exchange of client knowledge, in the absence of resources for team members to develop relationships of trust.

Virtualisation harms client relationship and reduces service quality Inspectorate’s emphasis on efficiency gains compromised the rigour of the inspection process, which was now contingent upon allocated time, rather than professional judgement. An interviewee related:

When you’re at an inspection, you have a fixed time on the job. You just need to get on with it. (Inspector 5)

Inspectors were critical of the temporary virtual team model and were uncomfortable with its impact upon the client’s perception of Inspectorate’s service quality. A Senior Inspector explained:

![](/api/attachments/KKQT6N2C/fulltext/images/d13ec5d4d4fa5b3d70ceabbabb53cdbea4a5e41db09165d6cc8657e2421b1ee8.jpg)  
Figure 9 Virtualisation impact on team boundary boundedness.

It’s not what I see as the difference but what I am told is the difference by our clients. Basically, the view is that now we aren’t perceived to do our work to the same degree of depth or rigour. (Senior Inspector 3)

With discontinuous inspection team membership, Inspectorate could no longer ensure that teams were composed of inspectors that had a suitable mix of expertise and client knowledge. Inspectors began to use their personal networks to acquire information, since knowledge sharing processes had not been formally implemented. This bore the risk of inspectors drawing on outdated or incomplete information. A Senior Inspector related:

When people are stuck, they go away themselves and try to get more information on what to do on a particular point. There’s a danger that you can miss quite a chunk of expertise in our organisation, which might affect the big picture. (Senior Inspector 4)

In the absence of client knowledge management processes, inspectors increasingly serviced client sites unaware of their inspection history, current concerns, or sensitive issues. This created great resentment among inspectors who were keen to be perceived as professional, competent, and well-informed. An interviewee recounted:

You lose credibility very quickly if you don’t have a damned clue what you’re talking about. (Senior Inspector 3)

In the experience of interviewees who had worked both under the permanent and virtual team structure, the new conditions of work compromised inspection quality, inspectors’ sense of professional integrity and, ultimately, Inspectorate’s reputation with its clients.

## Conclusion

This study applied boundary theory to investigate the impact of organisational virtualisation on work units in a public sector organisation that replaced permanent with temporary virtual teams. Organisational virtualisation, conceptualised in terms of networking, restructuring, and learning-organisation culture (Dutton, 1999), was found to exert profound influences on team boundary properties and activities. The findings from this exploratory empirical study suggest that (a) virtualisation increases the number and complexity of team boundaries and (b) impairs the sharing of sensitive knowledge between peers.

The literature praises virtual teams for the unique advantage of removing temporal, geographical, and organisational boundaries, thereby offering distinctive opportunities over co-located work structures (Boudreau et al., 1998; Lipnack and Stamps, 1999). Some hold that modern ICTs enable team members from a range of locations to collaborate as if they were in a single place (O’Hara-Devereux and Johansen, 1994). Others stress the potential of virtual teams to deliver unique strategic flexibility by allowing organisations to build the best teams for a given purpose regardless of team members’ locations (Townsend et al., 1998).

Our study confirms the proposition that virtualisation removes boundaries of space and time, yet, calls into question the view that virtualisation removes organisational boundaries. Our findings suggest that, at the level of work units, virtualisation increases the number and complexity of organisational boundaries. As teams become virtual and temporary, members must build and sustain a greater number of relationships with peers on their own team and on other teams. Building and maintaining relationships at a distance also becomes more complex as it requires a high degree of facilitation and coordination and, thus, commitment of effort and time.

The requirement to expend significantly more effort on spanning, buffering, and bringing up activities, in addition to team members’ task performance, presents a critical challenge that can readily undermine virtual team effectiveness. Building and maintaining internal and external relationships and managing knowledge flows across boundaries have been identified as key challenges to virtual teams in previous research (Gammack and Poon, 2001). Our study adds that it is not only more difficult to perform boundary activities effectively at a distance but more of these activities are required of virtual team members.

Given the exploratory nature of the research, the implications for practice are tentative. When considering the option of virtualising conventional teams, managers are likely to face a trade-off: the cost and time saving of removing slack through virtual work arrangements can easily be eroded by (a) the additional time that members need to spend on boundary activities in order to be effective or (b) poorer team performance when team members underinvest time in boundary activities.

Owing to the transitory nature of virtual teams, knowledge capture and transfer from individual members to the team and to the organisation is essential to ensure knowledge is preserved for future reference and use (Griffith et al., 2003). Geographic dispersion makes it more problematic to manage team knowledge well (Majchrzak et al., 2000). Despite the criticality of knowledge sharing to performance, virtual teams frequently fail to manage knowledge effectively (Caldwell and Koch, 2000; Malhotra et al., 2001). The chronic lack of knowledge sharing within and between virtual teams has been explained by the difficulty of developing inter-personal trust at a distance (Handy, 1995; Cramton, 2001). Knowledge sharing is seen to presuppose good working relationships and trust. While this view was supported by this study, our evidence adds that the limited sharing of sensitive knowledge, in particular, is also due to a lack of trust in the technology.

Our findings suggest that users distrust electronic media for communicating content of a personal, sensitive and confidential nature, especially when they experience ICT as unreliable. E-mail, for instance, perhaps the most widely used medium for virtual team communication, is especially vulnerable to confidentiality breaches. E-mail messages can, as is well known, easily be monitored by the organisation without the awareness of sender or recipient. Senders can blind-copy communications to third parties, while recipients of e-mail communications can forward messages to others. Although ICT is the lifeline of virtual teams (Maznevski and Chudoba, 2000), it does not provide the levels of control that would encourage team members to use it for sharing sensitive knowledge with trusted peers, particularly when its unintended disclosure to other parties could have personal repercussions.

The implications for practice of this finding are that technology cannot substitute for face-to-face knowledge sharing where the knowledge being shared is confidential or sensitive in nature, regardless of how securely the ICT is designed. If organisations wish to mitigate the risk of losing this knowledge, they need to resource opportunities for face-to-face knowledge sharing by, for instance, supporting in-person team meetings or fostering wider knowledge sharing environments like communities-of-practice.
