## Abstract

We investigate the concept of Smart Business Networks by using Beer’s Viable System Model (VSM) to analyse how such a network mitigates the affect of emergent, and therefore unforecastable, demands upon the networked businesses. We examine the requirements for network smartness, highlight some significant properties of one Smart Network and use our case analysis and concepts from systems theory to suggest some general properties such as natural stability and distributed capability. We have found that smartness is distributed and shared behavioural process standards can act to stabilise complex systems. This is important because it mitigates emergent behaviour within increasingly complex business networks. The contributions of our article apply both to academics and business practitioners and are in its illustration and investigation of one Smart Business Network including how the smart capability functions at a strategic, business process and technical level; and the use of Beer’s VSM to analyse an electricity market.

## Introduction

B <sup>eer</sup> <sup>(1979)</sup> <sup>and</sup> <sup>Checkland</sup> <sup>(1981)</sup> <sup>have</sup> <sup>written</sup>extensively on how organisations and networks exist extensively on how organisations and networks exist within complex environments, which generate emergent demands upon them (Anderson, 1999: 218). In order to remain viable, these networks have to be able to fulfil these demands but as Haeckel and Nolan (1996) pointed out: emergence cannot be forecasted and so a sense and respond capability is required. The object of the study is to understand how unforecastable, unplanned for and potentially devastating exogenous shocks to businesses can be mitigated by the benefits of being part of a network with what we have labelled ‘smart’ properties. The contributions of our article apply both to academics and business practitioners and are in its investigation of the concept of Smart Business Networks. In one such network, an electricity market, we illustrate how the smart capability functions at a strategic, business process and technical levels using Beer’s viable system model (VSM) to analyse it.

## Research method

Our case study of the UK electricity market is an illustration of a complex system that generates highly volatile emergent demands for electricity supply businesses. These businesses use a Smart Network to help meet these demands, which are a legal obligation set by the industry regulator. We suggest that an embedded ‘smartness’ allows the accommodation of extreme flux in user demand. These environmental demands are of the nature of communications and control, which are cybernetic problems. Therefore, we analyse this case using Beer’s VSM, which was designed to analyse highly complex and recursive systems made up of medium term viable sub-systems such as businesses (Beer, 1979).

We then use the VSM as a framework for delineating this complex system along lines of near decomposability (Simon, 1969: 99). We use Simon’s concept of near decomposability, operationalised as Beer’s VSM, to ‘modularise’ a complex business network and investigate the interaction between its ‘modules’. Of course, near decomposability is not the same as complete decomposability so the subsystems exhibit module-like properties (Parnas et al., 1985). Their external connections are loosely coupled (Weick, 1976) relative to their internal connections. Both types of connections contain direct and indirect feedback loops but their ‘intensity of interaction’ (Simon, 1969: 90) is greater for internal than for external connections. This justifies us thinking of them as subsystems and of the overall network as having a heterogeneous structure rather than being a single homogeneous object. Beer describes a system as ‘a group of elements dynamically related in time according to some coherent pattern’. The inter-relations are the business processes that are shared between elemental businesses, for example when a vendor business’ service or product is composed into that of a customer’s metaprocess. For example, when a customer business uses a process enacted by a subcontractor either physically of via the World Wide Web as a Web Service. A business network is a system because the businesses in the network are interrelated via business processes and share dependences such as resources (e.g. goods and information) and metaresources such as trust and power.

## The requirement for network viability

Central to the requirement for organisational and network viability is the concept of the unforecastability of emergence. A system has ‘elements, relations between elements, behaviour and purpose’ (Warboys et al., 1999: 14). There are many other definitions of a system in the literature and most include constructs such as system element and relationships between elements although sometimes process relationships are described as dependencies such as in the coordination literature (Malone and Crowston, 1994), certainly though a business network is a system because the businesses in the network are inter-related via business processes and share dependences such as resources (e.g. goods and information), and meta-resources such as trust and power. Furthermore, a business network is a sociotechnical system because its elements are themselves made up of humans and machines enacting business processes (Mumford, 2000). It is also a complex system and therefore displays emergent properties (Anderson, 1999).

Emergence is a result of the different levels or hierarchies that exist within complex systems. Simon (1969, p. 90), in his watchmaker example, argues that hierarchies can hasten the formation of complex systems by acting as stable intermediate subassemblies. They can be a succession of less ambitious stages to a more complex assembly. However, emergent properties do not exist below the hierarchical level that they emerge upon. It is impossible to observe the creation of emergent properties from hierarchical levels below the level at which they emerge upon thus they are not forecastable (Checkland, 1981). For example, the property of flavour does not exist below the molecular level. In our case, below, the demands for electricity from individual electricity users only displays certain properties when aggregated into total demand upon a single electricity supplier, that is, not just volume of electricity but also number of users and type of user, properties that do not exist on the level of a single user. This means that some exogenous shocks to suppliers’ businesses that are created by environmental complexity, such as customers changing supplier, are impossible to prepare for and their affect on the business may be disastrous. This also is true for a network of businesses whose environment generates emergent demands upon it. In some ways, the challenge for a network of businesses is greater because of the increased complexity of many businesses interacting through shared dependencies and the greater number of connections to the collective environment. Our interest in network viability comes from a desire to help businesses survive longer in environments that are becoming more complex as businesses become more interdependent and these interdependencies are communicated faster by ICT.

## Analysis of the UK electricity market using VSM

## The UK electricity market

The electricity market in England and Wales is a connected set of electricity producers, suppliers and consumers joined by two networks. The first network carries electricity, the raison d’eˆtre of the system, and the second network carries data between the various diverse organisations and private individuals that this sector’s supply chain consists of. The second network is used to control the first network.

In March 2001, the UK government introduced New Electricity Trading Arrangements (NETA) in England and Wales (National Audit Office (NAO), 2003). These arrangements were designed to use market forces to reduce electricity prices by making the electricity market operate more like other commodity markets. Prior to this the arrangements for trading electricity, known as the Pool, were thought to be uncompetitive and open to manipulation. In October 1997 the Minister for Science, Energy and Industry asked the Office of Electricity Regulation (OFFER), the regulator at that time, to review the way that electricity was traded in England and Wales and to suggest a number of improvements in areas that included reducing the price charged to the user and improving choice, quality and security of supply for electricity users (NAO, 2003).

OFFER’s review of the Pool pointed to problems of overregulation that limited the effect of normal market forces such as competition in price setting, together with supplyside and demand-side price influencing. In short the Pool, one of the first examples of a wholesale electricity market in the world, was limited by the complexity of its bidding and price setting mechanism, the inflexibility of its regulations and openness to manipulation of its payment rules (NAO, 2003). The main proposal from the review was that the trading of electricity should become much more marketbased. OFFER recommended that the electricity market should operate more like other commodity markets subject to special requirements for physically balancing supply and demand in order to maintain the security and quality of electricity supply.

These special requirements come from the fact that electricity is a commodity that is very difficult and costly to store; and while its supply cannot be varied instantaneously its demand does vary instantaneously. Balancing rather than buffering stores is used to stop frequent small power cuts caused by high user demand volatility. The Government demands that users should be shielded from shortterm price volatility.

These recommendations led to the current trading arrangements known as NETA. With NETA the value chain for the industry is divided up into the wholesale marketplace and the supply marketplace. Here we focus on the supply market, which consists of generators who are suppliers, pure suppliers and users (see Figure 1). Deregulation is such that Credit Suisse First Boston, a bank, is allowed to trade on the future prices of electricity in the electricity wholesale marketplace and a grocery firm, Sainsbury, can supply electricity to a domestic user for their home.

Deregulation has stimulated a large influx of new electricity suppliers. This increase in suppliers has been in parallel to the increasing number of electricity users changing and rechanging their suppliers. The UK’s National Audit Office found that from May 1999 to June 2000, ‘6.5 million customers – one in four – had saved money by changing supplier, and customers were changing at the rate of 400,000 a month’ (NAO, 2004). This has led to a huge increase in user churn, that is, disconnecting existing customers and connecting new ones, which although reportedly decreasing slightly is still large (Brignall, 2004). Furthermore, the regulator, now called The Office of Gas and Electricity Markets (Ofgem), specifies a whole series of service level agreements that electricity suppliers must meet during this change process. These mandatory requirements are stated in the form of a highly complicated set of business processes represented in dataflow diagrams. The dataflow diagrams themselves are updated and maintained for the regulator by the Master Registration Agreement

## VSM analysis

VSM is a general architecture for viable systems and we now use it to analyse how this network is able to deal with large and unforecastable volumes of user registrations and deregistrations by examining the registration and deregistration process from the viewpoints of the subsystems of the four major parties: the user of the electricity; the old electricity supplier; the new electricity supplier; and the regulator, Ofgem.

Beer (1979) defines the five subsystems of a viable system and how they interact to control an organisation (see Figure 2). The decomposition of a system into discrete but interdependent subsystems with different roles is a key part of the VSM model. Beer (1979) defined the five subsystems of a viable system as follows:

\- Subsystem 1 consists of operational processes – the ‘plants’

\- Subsystem 2 oversees System 1’s processes

\- Subsystem 3 plans operational strategy inside the organisation

\- Subsystem 4 is concerned with outside the system and the future

\- Subsystem 5 sets policy for the system

These five subsystems interact to produce all the VSM’s behaviours and control the production of these behaviours. We can use the VSM to decompose the market into subsystem roles. For example, all the subsystem 5’s of all the businesses in the network set the ‘collective goals’ of the network. We can also use the VSM to decompose the regulator, the electricity suppliers and the users. For example, Subsystem 1, the plant, produces electricity in the suppliers and uses the electricity in the users. The separation of controlling subsystem and controlled subsystem is another property of the VSM.

Beer’s VSM can be viewed as a description of how these five subsystems interact to model the behaviour of each separate organisation in a network or how the network as a whole behaves. In this case, we apply the model to both: the connection and disconnection of individual electricity suppliers and users at the organisational level and the regulation of many suppliers by one regulator at the network level.

The foundations of the VSM are multidisciplinary and include operations research, engineering, business management and production management. It is an abstraction of the salient structural and behavioural properties of a system, such as a business or other organisation, and a mainstay of cybernetics. Here we categorise these properties as: viability, adaptivity, hierarchy and self-reference based partly upon Herring and Kaplan’s (2001: 3) list. We analyse the UK electricity market on two levels: the level of the business player, by focusing upon the users and suppliers, and the level of the whole networked market, by focusing upon the regulator and its business process rules.

The concept of viability means that a system has a capability to maintain itself, that is, its identity. For a business or a Smart Network we would say that viability is a capability to continue to strive to realise its goals. Environmental pressures and requirements, such as market downturns, may make this impossible to sustain and the business or network may cease to exist in the same form. A business may cease trading and the staff will go elsewhere, or a network may disband. Alternatively a networked set of elements (businesses or people) may disband because the goals that are their sole reason for ‘networking’ are realised. An example of this is a project with a clearly defined set of deliverables and a timetable. This is important for the development of the concept of Smart Business Networks because it means that the smartness of the network must support goal resolution. Although this may only enable the viability of member businesses rather than the viability of the network (e.g. in an multi-company project). In other words whilst network smartness must support the goals of some network members, else these members will cease to interact, it need not support long-term network viability.

In order for a system to continue to be viable in the face of environmental requirements, it has to have the capability of adaption. It has to be able to change itself in response to environmental requirements. In this case the environmental requirements are the awareness of a user of a better electricity deal, which in turn initiates the set of steps described below that result in the user changing its supplier. Thus the network of suppliers and users is reconfigured. It could be argued that if our focal network consisted of all users and suppliers then the change requirements would be internal rather than environmental so the whole change is an internal reconfiguration in response to some actual environmental requirement such as a cheaper fuel deal for the new electricity supplier.

However, new market entrants and increased competition are external threats to viability that are met with adaptions such as sectoral change in business practices and multiple company failure, that is, adaption across more than one element in a network system of businesses.

Threats to system viability, the only initiators of system loss of identity through dissolution or merger, come from exogenous pressures originating within the system’s environment. These external stimuli are environmental requirements for adaption that unless met will cause system failure. A business or business network must be able to meet each type of external stimuli or environmental requirement or problem with the appropriate solution. A solution that is common to two types of problem means that there is in fact only one type of problem; from the point of view of the problem solver. An example of this would be when some electricity users want to pay monthly and some want to pay quarterly but the billing systems of the electricity supplier are only able to send out bills quarterly. This is an example of Ashby’s Law of Requisite Variety (Beer, 1979: 89). Solution variety has to be at least as great as problem variety for a system to remain viable. In order for the systems consisting of a supplier and some monthly-users to remain viable each supplier has to adapt its component systems to increase its variety of payment options. Failure to do so will lead to system dissolution when users change to another supplier.

On a network level, one example of this was the UK government’s introduction of NETA in 2001. The new trading agreements meant that many more business were able to enter the electricity supply market and with them they brought new capabilities and resources. This, together with the prior artificially high retail electricity prices normalising under more natural market conditions caused a significant drop in the wholesale price of electricity. Which in turn led to financial problems for the electricity generator British Energy and the supplier/generator TXU Energy. British Energy had to ask the UK government for financial assistance and TXU Energy sold its European subsidiary to Powergen. In order to remain viable TXU Energy adapted to lower prices by disconnecting its European subsidiary component, which then merged with Powergen. The whole network adapted to a low price configuration.

Herring and Kaplan (2001: 3) classify adaptation capabilities into three types: homeostatic (maintaining control variables within parameters), morphostatic (changing the control algorithms that govern homeostasis) and morphogenetic (‘the ability to acquire new components and discard others’). A user maintains profitability by incorporating an electricity price increase into the price of its products using homeostatic adaptability. A supplier displays morphostatic adaption capability when it changes the method it uses to calculate profitability. The TXU Energy demerger is an example of morphogenetic capability used to maintain a threat to viability from negative profitability. On a network level, company failure is another example. All users, suppliers and the regulator display examples of all three types of adaptation capability to remain viable.

As we have pointed out above the near decomposability of complex systems observed by Simon (1969: 99) allows us to model complex systems such as an electricity production, regulation and use system by decomposing it into various roles to which we allocate to specific subsystems. It should be noted that we are also referring here to adjacent hierarchical levels, that is, the network level where the subsystems are ‘use’, ‘supply’ and ‘regulation’ and one level down from this. On this lower level – the level of each user, supplier and the regulator – each is divided into subsystems that are the five roles described above. Our thesis is not that all these organisations have departments that are explicitly charged with these roles but that if they are to remain viable then these are roles that must be successfully performed. In the terms of our case, on the level of the network, the roles are as follows:

\- Subsystem 1 (operations) – supplying electricity (supplier), using electricity such as the operation of a hairdryer or electric furnace (user), regulating the supply and use of electricity (regulator).

\- Subsystem 2 (oversight) – supervising day-to-day electricity supply (supplier), supervising day-to-day electricity use such setting the heat levels of a hair dryer or an electric furnace (user), regulating the supply and use of electricity such as availability and price levels (regulator).

\- Subsystem 3 (operations strategy) – supplying electricity efficiently and effectively (supplier), using electricity efficiently and effectively (user), regulating the supply and use of electricity such as checking user satisfaction (regulator).

\- Subsystem 4 (outside and future) – dealing with environmental and future requirements that affect the supply of electricity such as oil prices (supplier), dealing with environmental and future requirements that affect the use of electricity such as electricity prices (user), dealing with environmental and future requirements that affect, or are affected by, the supply and use of electricity such as global warming (regulator).

\- Subsystem 5 (policy) – whether and how to supply electricity, diversify or change sector (supplier), whether and how to use electricity (user), how to regulate the supply and use of electricity such as for supplier satisfaction, user satisfaction, the satisfaction both or the satisfaction of another party (regulator).

Herring and Kaplan (2001: 4) have used the VSM to generate a viable system architecture (VSA) for the interfaces that link a viable system, that is itself a production subsystem (i.e. Subsystem 1) within a greater system, to the rest of the system and its environment. For example, a sales office of an electricity supplier is a viable system whose product is new customers. The VSA is developed as a software architecture but because it is based upon a general model for viable systems is applicable to our case. Here the production system is represented by the contents of any of the circles labelled S1 in Figure 2. Another example could be a single electricity supply business operating as part of the UK electricity network. It could also be a single factory using electricity or, on a network level, the collective use of electricity. The value of this description of inter-subsystem and environmental interfaces is that it allows an examination of the interface structure and the interfacing protocol.

The interface structure is itself composed of an operational and a control hierarchy. Each interface between subsystems consists of a communications channel, such as a production manager (Subsystem 3) using an e-mail channel to inform all factory supervisors (Subsystem 2) of the next week’s production schedule. This is the operational level.

The control level of the interface is separate to the communications channel and acts to maintain homeostasis for its own viability. If this is not possible morphostatic, and ultimately morphogenetic, adaptation then occur. In other words an interface is used whilst it serves its purpose and if it fails to do so then its use is changed; and failing that another interface is used, i.e. the component is swapped out. An example of this from our case is the regulation of a single electricity supplier. If a supplier does not follow the strict business process guidelines set out by the regulator in the MRASCo dataflow diagrams (MRASCo, 2003) then the regulator takes action. For example, we will now use the VSM in our analysis to show how the parties interact using different subsystems during a change of supplier process. The simple steps occur in this order:

1. Subsystem 4 of user becomes aware of a supplier offering a better deal;

2. Subsystem 3 s of user and new supplier agree the deal parameters and how to connect;

3. Subsystem 3 s of user and old supplier agree how to disconnect;

4. Subsystem 1 s of user and old supplier disconnect;

5. Subsystem 1 s of user and new supplier connect.

Changing supplier involves transferring user information from the old supplier to the new supplier. The regulator row of Table 1 shows how the regulator’s Subsystem 1 checks the effectiveness of this transfer. If the old supplier takes too long then the regulator’s Subsystem 1 communicates with the old supplier’s Subsystem 4 to demand an improvement under threat of action to hinder that supplier’s policy attainment. In other words, the regulator threatens punitive action and the old supplier’s Subsystem 4 asks its Subsystem 1 to hurry and its Subsystem 3 to plan how to stop the delay reoccurring.

The Subsystem 4 boxes of the user and suppliers in Table 1 are shaded to emphasise that the regulator only interfaces with Subsystem 4. However, if the interface that the regulator uses to communicate its threat is not capable of transmitting, or the supplier is not capable of receiving, then the interface is thus not capable of realising its goals as an interface. In our example, this may be because the person that transmits the threat is not trained to use email or the person that receives it has left the supplier company. The control system of the interface acts to correct this by, for example, directing the transmitting person to use the telephone or, respectively, acquiring a new reception person. Here the control system for the interface is embodied in the responsibility of the regulator staff member in charge of communication with badly performing electricity suppliers.

The description of inter-subsystem and environmental interfaces in the VSA also allows an examination of interfacing protocols, which is the description of the process for connecting and disconnecting interfaces (Herring and Kaplan, 2001: 6). But here we confine our analysis to a simple change of electricity supplier at the business level rather than at the interface level because this is all that is required to illustrate some of the smart properties of this network.

The final property of a viable system is self-reference. Self-reference is the property of a system, which makes the existence, design and behaviour of one part of a viable system naturally complete, fit with and be a consequence of the existence, design and behaviour of the other parts of the system. In our electricity market case an electricity supplier is a component of the viable system that is the electricity market. The very nature of the component causes it to strive to increase custom as its design allows it to do; and its behaviours, based upon the design and driven by its nature, actually cause this to happen. On a lower level (i.e. below the network level and below the organisational level) this driving nature, or policy subsystem, is fuelled by the personal and group motivations of humans that staff the policy role of the supplier’s business. The same can be seen for the other subsystems of the VSM when we view them as roles that act to maintain system viability with respect to other subsystems via homeostatic feedback mechanisms.

## Discussion

Our analysis shows the very high structural flexibility requirements placed upon the electricity suppliers during changes in supplier and the regulation of this process. A customer is able to choose to change to any other electricity supplier in the market; therefore each supplier potentially has to communicate data to any other. Furthermore, this complicated process occurs every time a user changes supplier so there is a very high volume of such interactions between all three parties. Each interaction involves the preparation, transfer and processing of large amounts of user data thus generating very high volume flexibility requirements. The suppliers have to change from transmitting zero data to a given competitor, to transmitting a high volume of data whenever a customer changes supplier.

As we list in Table 2 the actual benefits of deregulation were that electricity prices fell (for the users and the regulator, i.e. the government) and new suppliers could enter the market (for the new suppliers). The current suppliers did not directly benefit. This was due to two reasons. Firstly, deregulation led to more market-like conditions that allowed the entry of more electricity suppliers and an associated increase in competition, which drove down prices. Secondly, as well as service levels the industry regulator specifies in great detail the business processes that the suppliers must enact. In other words the regulator describes how the suppliers should behave to each other and to the users.

One example of processes that are enacted by all parties is the description of the business processes that are required to produce a user disconnection and a connection, that is, a change in supplier. These business processes are completely modelled in dataflow diagrams that are easily available and whose evolution is strongly controlled by the regulator (MRASCo, 2003). The model contains the subprocesses: disconnect, connect, check disconnection and check connection. This business process model is a standard for the interactions of all parties. It limits variety at any given stage in the process to a small set of welldefined stimuli and response options, that is, problem types and associated solution types. Also, the publication and availability of the MRASCo dataflow diagrams ensures that all parties are familiar with these standard business processes. Thus type flexibility requirements are fixed to partly compensate for variable structural flexibility and apparent volume flexibility. Furthermore, the aggregate volume volatility of connections and disconnections from the point of view of any one electricity supplier is not great. This, together with the standardisation on type flexibility requirements that are described above, means that volume flexibility requirements are also not great, that is, relatively steady volumes of supplier changes together with a fixed set of supplier change processes (limited type flexibility requirements) mean that the suppliers only have to deal with extreme structural flexibility requirements.

Extreme structural flexibility requirements come from the ability of any user to choose any supplier. This is mitigated by the Data Transfer Service (DTS) operated by Electralink (2004), which was formed after deregulation by the fourteen Public Electricity Suppliers of England, Scotland and Wales. The DTS is in itself a network that enables business-to-business data communications between any electricity suppliers connected to it, that is, there are two networks here: the electricity supply chain and the DTS. Thus the structural costs of supplier-to-supplier communications switching are lowered and structural flexibility is greatly increased. This simple Flexibility Model, incorporating the structural, type and volume flexibility constructs was developed as part of the engineering and physical science research council (EPSRC) funded Flexible Business Integration project.

Another product our VSM analysis, combined with Simon’s insights into hierarchy, is that whilst the same number subsystems of the user and suppliers interact, the regulator’s operations subsystem (Subsystem 1) interacts only with the meta-systems of the suppliers. This mixing of roles (i.e. element’s subsystems) has the effect of enabling the network to exist on three hierarchical levels. If we divide the roles of a VSM into two: production (Subsystem 1) and the meta-process acting on production called evolution (all other subsystems) we can say that a single supplier, or the regulator, exists on two hierarchical levels. These are the production level where business processes directly further the goals of the subsystem (e.g. electricity supply or use) and the evolution level where meta-processes act to change processes on the production level (Warboys et al., 1999: 26).

## Some Smart Business Network properties

The main advantage that Smart Business Networks have over non-smart networks is some form of distributed capability that arises quite simply from the distribution of ‘smartness’ and its existence as a whole network capability rather than within specific network elements or specific relationships between elements. It acts both on a network level and a single business level. Smartness is operationalised as a capability and the distinction is that if the network is smart then all elements within the network have access to this capability. Network smartness can be decomposed into several properties.

Firstly, the ‘smart’ in smart networks is distributed across the whole network. If it were not then it would be smartness associated with one or more businesses rather than with the whole network. One example of this concerns a network of actors. An actor can react faster if it is located functionally local to an emergent demand upon the network. The BBC, Reuters and other news networks exhibit this property by distributing foreign correspondents all over the world. By being located close to an unforecastable demand upon their network, such as a newsworthy event, foreign correspondents can report on the event faster. This is because they may hear about it faster and because they can get to the scene of the event faster. We use the concept of functionally locality to emphasize that it is the functioning of the actor that needs to be local to the emergent demand rather than the actor’s actual self. For example, security cameras and alarms linked to police stations via modems remove some of the requirement for security staff to physically patrol all the areas that they guard.

Secondly, network smartness includes some capability. An emergent demand that requires a response exhibits variety, that is, it is not of constant type. Depending upon the news worthiness of the event the response can vary from an email to a central database or even project managing a full crew of reporter, sound, camera, drive and other specialists. Therefore a response capability must consist of at least two components: a decision-making capability and response production capability. The decisionmaking capability is a choice between possible response production options and the latter is a set of business processes to execute, for example, the choice a foreign correspondent has of if or how to follow the story.

Thirdly, network smartness is a property of the distributed coordination of capability. A single boundedly rational actor would find it impossible to successfully orchestrate a complex business network in detail (Newell, 1989: 400). Therefore, not all orchestration, direction or supervision could be confined to some central role. There must be some local decision-making role within each business that acts in concert with the same role in the other network elements. Not acting in concert would mean that it is not a whole network property. We justify the existence of this property by pointing out that as well as acting singly in response to local requirements, like in our first point, networked actors also act in parallel in response to signals from other network members. The use of network membership as a communications channel is not surprising given the existence of telecommunication networks and the mutual servicing of network members that guarantees network longevity.

Ashby’s Law of Requisite Variety states that the variety of the possible response options to a problem must be at least as great as the variety of possible problems (Ashby, 1958).

Network smartness also acts by aiding the decision-making capability by making decisions easier. In our case study it does so by limiting the number of problem (i.e. requirement) types placed upon the businesses in the network by other businesses and by customers. All business must conform to the standard behaviours set specified by the regulator. Thus the number of response options is less and so the choice is easier to make.

Another way of saying this uses Williamson’s concept of markets and hierarchies as different ways of structuring networked economic organisations (Williamson, 1991). Networked actors that are not hierarchically controlled, that is, centrally controlled, are given local processing power. This increases the variety of possible responses but it can also increase the variety of possible requirements, for example, an increase in supplier search costs. By using local processing, but at the same time constraining the behaviour of actors in the network, the capability to respond is increased but the number of types of requirements to respond to is not. This is not a true coordination mechanism, as written about by Malone and Crowston (1994), but a variety limiter. The dependencies of the networked electricity business are not managed. This smart property exists in the design of an architecture for business-to-business interaction that does not allow the electricity business and the electricity users to make the interactions more complex that they already are.

The smart architecture of the UK electricity network is operationalised partly as a regulatory standard. The regulatory standard, or business process model, is an example of embedded intelligence within a network because it distributes capability to enact a common set of business processes across many organisations. This capability is functionally local because it is directly available to local decision-makers; humans or model-driven business process management systems (BMPS). Intelligence is embedded into this electricity market via an intelligently designed standard for behaviour. A standard is a model projected onto the B2B interaction partners (like an ERP vanilla process model is forced onto one organisation). One can either abstract a model from a subject process or project a standard onto a subject. Both can be used to drive BMPS, or human, process enactment and be examples of network intelligence.

Another smart property of the UK electricity market is provided by the DTS. Infrastructural communications protocols are also standardised in the form of the DTS thus limiting the costs of structural flexibility by reducing the number of possible response production requirements to one standard DTS protocol. Thus the decision-making capability of each user only has to deal with choosing a supplier, not how to connect to it. Both the standard for business processes (the data flow diagrams) and the standard for interconnectivity (the DTS) take advantage of Simon’s concept of near decomposability by modelling only the salient properties of the complex system of users, suppliers and regulator. Thus, by having only to deal with an abstraction, the full complexity of business operation is reduced for all parties.

The constructs distributed capability and functional locality have a hierarchical application similar to their application across the network span of businesses (Simon,

1969: 89). A system where the production level of the regulator interacts with the evolution levels of the electricity suppliers exists across three hierarchical levels, rather than just two, because the production level of the regulator and the evolution levels of the suppliers overlap. Thus the network covers more hierarchical levels than if the regulator and suppliers were connected production level to product level and evolution level to evolution level. The benefit of this is that it enables network smartness, that is, the properties of the Smart Network, to act upon more hierarchical levels, which is of particular relevance in dealing with emergent phenomena.

We can also start to develop metrics that describe network smartness by analysing from the perspectives of all the network stakeholders. On an organisational level ‘smartness’ denotes a positive alignment with the values or goals of the network elements. It increases the organisations’ capability to sense and respond (Haeckel and Nolan, 1996). In this case it is to make unforecastable demand fluctuations that are still satisfied (users) and to be capable of responding to them (suppliers). Elemental goals may be measured, for example, in terms of electricity costs, speed and smoothness of supplier switching, more supply competition, a greater variation in services and more satisfied users and voters; but all members of the network need to perceive their membership as profitable or they would seek to leave. Prospective members would not value membership and so not join. Then member churn, the continuous joining and leaving of members, would reduce member numbers to a nonviable population and the network would fail. For example, in our case if the falling members were the electricity suppliers then the regulator would act to guarantee the electricity supply before network failure. The regulator would change the business process rules of the network so as to make it more attractive to a falling supplier membership, e.g. by increasing the minimum period for using particular electricity supplier. Indeed the original suppliers did not initially profit from the introduction of NETA because of increase price competition and new customer acquisition costs of d40–250 (Brignall, 2004). But those that were able to continue to operate profitably remained in the market, which helped to fulfil their premiere goal, that is, commercial viability. For the regulator, acting upon the level of the whole network, it was not a question of any particular pre-deregulation supplier continuing to exist in this market but whether there were enough suppliers (new or old) to service the users to the specified standards. Opening up the market to new entrants removed a synthetic constraint upon the available variety of business solutions and thus improved the overall efficiency of the market.

On a network level ‘smartness’ denotes a positive alignment with the values or goals of the network itself. This could be embodied in the form of the regulator since the regulator represents the UK government, who caused the creation of the network in its current form. In describing the regulator of a network as ‘the network itself’ we should point out that the regulator is actually an element of the system that functions on a hierarchical level above that of the other elements. The system is an entity with completely different emergent properties to its elements. Properties that cannot even be experienced at the elemental level. Thus the goals of the network itself, which are used to calibrate metrics of smartness on the network level, are a gestalt of elemental goals. This leads us to suggest another property of smart networks: that state of affairs that is sometimes called a ‘win-win’ situation, or a Nash Equilibrium. In a Nash Equilibrium network elements perceive that current operational conditions within the network are both supportive of their goals and of the goals of other network elements, that is, other elements will not seek to change these conditions (Chakravorti, 2003: 8). Thus one metric could be a measure of the alignment of elemental goals that lead to stability in the period needed for goal attainment, which is long term for this networks but short term for a network of contractors on a construction project.

Modelling a Smart Network as a viable system may be highly appropriate because viability is a product of a system’s innate homeostasis. The interactions between subsystems are modelled using a negative feedback control paradigm that by its very nature is anti-oscillatory and so strives to maintain a set point (Herring and Kaplan, 2001: 4). This set point can exist at any hierarchical level and so maintaining it can encompass automatic homeostatic, morphostatic and morphogenetic adaption on the part of the viable system. This has the effect of making the system naturally stable and less prone to dissolution because of exogenous stimuli. An example of this can be found in the design of aircraft before recent fly-by-wire technology. Aircraft designed before the availability of high-speed onboard computer systems that actively stabilised them in the face of external perturbations were designed to be naturally stable. The motivation for this is the limited speed at which a human pilot can correct against environmental perturbations such as chaotic gusts of wind. In this way old aircraft are also smart.

Other risks to network viability are also properties of subsystems, that is, system ‘roles’ rather than specific elements such as businesses. Businesses may enter and leave and the market still functions but if most suppliers start to fail then the operations subsystem is at risk. These risks are systemic risks and take the form of possibilities for large-scale interdependency breakage between elemental businesses across the whole network that in turn degrades subsystem performance. This is because subsystems are the sum of the behaviours of many businesses. A system, such as our network, is a synergy of properties communicated via interdependencies in the form of business processes. For example, new environmental laws may increase production costs and make nuclear generated electricity commercially unviable, that is, the interdependency between production cost and sales price would be affected. Other systemic risks come from the concept of open systems. Open systems require some form of input and output to remain viable. The human body needs food, water and air. The electricity market needs new customers and users to replace ones that leave. The risk to the network is that there are not enough new users or new suppliers because of, for example, competition from new power sources.

Finally, there are strategic and business process implications for new electricity suppliers entering this market that come from viewing the whole UK electricity market as a smart network. They could view the market as a control system and examine its salient flows, in this case churning customers, and act to attract and keep the most profitable customers using a customer loyalty-based strategy. The extremely proscriptive rules of behaviour for suppliers are in effect a business process model that could be used to empower a BPMS and so to automate large parts of their business. This is because it is a capability to behave in a standard manner that is distributed among network members. Essentially, the properties of smart networks that act to stabilise, simplify and coordinate for all members can be described in an architecture and then used by a new member to design their strategic, operational and technical business models.

## Conclusion

Smart Business Networks are viable systems themselves and can be decomposed into subsystems that either together or singularly display smartness. These subsystems, such as process networks or telecommunications infrastructures are themselves networks that must fully overlay the main Smart Network. They display smart properties that contribute to the smartness of the greater Smart Network. This is aligned with the properties of ‘distributedness’ and functional locality. If smartness is not distributed or functionally local, then it is the business that is smart and not the network. This applies across the network span of businesses, in order to affect all members, and up the hierarchy of all its levels, for dealing with emergence.

One general property of smartness in Smart Business Networks could be that they abstract salient characteristics from a complex system to generate a smart architecture, or model, that reduces the perceived complexity for network orchestrators and business managers. Another general property of network smartness that reduces complexity could be the innate stability of Smart Networks that incorporate negative feedback loops within their process architecture.

Future research in this market is focusing on how business process management systems can be incorporated into the modelling of the industry. The current approach of using dataflow diagrams is static and is not architecturally designed in such a way as to facilitate its own evolution, that is, it could be redesigned hierarchically to allow more single action changes using the concept of loose coupling.
