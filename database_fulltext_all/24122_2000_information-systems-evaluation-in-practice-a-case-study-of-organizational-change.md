# Information systems evaluation in practice: a case study of organizational change

VASILIS SERAFEIMIDIS

KPMG Consulting, London, UK and Department of Computing, University of Surrey, Guildford, Surrey GU2 5XH, UK

STEVE SMITHSON

Department of Information Systems, London School of Economics, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, UK

Information systems (IS) evaluation is a dif cult problem in both theory and practice. While theoreticians are moving away from a positivistic approach towards a more interpretive approach, organizations are adopting a more entrepreneurial approach. We examine such a move through a case study of a large utility company in the UK which was relatively unsuccessful in its attempts at improving the evaluation of its IS. This paper uses an interpretive approach to explain the lack of success in introducing the new entrepreneurial evaluation procedures. However, we argue that the failure was not so much due to a lack of understanding of evaluation but more due to a failure to appreciate and support the necessary organizational changes.

## Introduction

The importance of evaluating information systems (IS) has long been recognized. Although ISs are integral to the operation and success of most organizations, organizations have limited resources and these resources should be invested in anticipation of the highest future gains. The initial enthusiasm for information technology (IT) (during the 1970s and 1980s) has long since been overtaken by a sense of pragmatism. Management now seek more concrete evidence of the IS contribution to the success of the business and the ‘real’ value of their often extensive IT investments.

The importance of IS evaluation is only matched by the dif culties attached to this ‘thorny problem (Smithson and Hirschheim, 1998). This is perhaps best encapsulated in the ‘productivity paradox’ (Brynjolfsson, 1993; Landauer, 1995) where, in some cases, the introduction of an IS apparently increases productivity and, in other cases, it appears to reduce productivity. This is not usually a problem of measurement detail but rather a conceptual failing. IS should be seen as complex sociotechnical entities (e.g. Boland and Hirschheim, 1987; Orlikowski, 1992; Walsham, 1993), inseparable from the organizational context within which they are situated and interact, as well as being products of history and human agency. IS are understood better when they are approached as a product of neither the technical nor the social aspects alone, but as their interaction (Rockart and Scott Morton, 1984; King and Kraemer, 1986; Symons,

1990). House (1983) noted that these problems are not unique to the IS  eld but occur in many other disciplines (e.g. education and social research) which deal with complex social situations.

Like much IS research, the study of IS evaluation used to be dominated by a positivistic and scienti c paradigm. The traditional (formal–rational or function alist) conception sees evaluation as an external judge ment of an IS which is treated as if it existed in isolation from its human and organizational components and effects. It also places excessive emphasis on the technological and accounting aspects at the expense of the organizational and social aspects. In doing so it neglects the organizational context and process of IS develop ment and its content, elements which are critical to the successful application of IT in support of a business. In general, more attention has been focused over the years on prescribing how to carry out evaluations (with project-driven and cost-focused accounting frame works) rather than analysing and understanding their role, interactions, effects and organizational impacts (Hirschheim and Smithson, 1988).

Formal–rational approaches have been challenged for their internal validity and their external generaliz ability in other areas of social research (e.g. Legge, 1984). They contribute to one piece of the picture but are not rich enough to describe the complex impacts within organizations. Thus, they cannot encompass the uncertainties, risks and context dependencies con cerning the value of IS investments to a busines which is undergoing often considerable organizational change. Evaluation is a socially embedded process in which formal procedures entwine with the informal assessments by which actors make sense of their situation.

Many authors (e.g. Hirschheim and Smithson, 1988; Iivari, 1988; Walsham, 1993; Smithson and Hirschheim, 1998) have argued that IS evaluation would be improved by using an ‘interpretive’ epistemology. This stance offers a framework for analysis which assists in the understanding and assessment of the meanings assigned by individuals to evaluation phenomena. Interpretive evaluation aims to involve a wide variety of stakeholder groups and to focus on a discourse for learning and understanding. Such designs are driven mainly by the determination of the content according to the organizational context and they are organized around the concerns, issues and actions of the stakeholders. The suitability of interpretive epistemology to the study of IS evaluation was supported further by Symons (1993, p. 74) who argued that

Interpretive methodologies of evaluation actively analyse the experience of organisational reality, focusing on stakeholder interests and perspectives. . . . They increase the effectiveness of organisational activity by ensuring a match between elements of organisational change and the dynamics of organisational culture and politics.

However, in practice few organizations have been attracted to the interpretivist paradigm as expounded by academics. Nevertheless, many organizations have progressed from elementary cost–bene t analysis, akin to book-keeping, towards a more entrepreneurial approach which seeks to deliver  rm bene ts while considering intangible aspects and elements of risk and uncertainty. This can be seen in the increasing concern with ‘pay-off’ (Mahmood and Szewczak, 1999), the ‘new economics of electronic communities’ (Willcocks and Lester, 1998), business value (Cronk and Fitzgerald, 1999) and bene ts management (Ward et al., 1996). In fact, the acknowledgement of the subjectivity, indeterminism and context dependency of evaluation distances the entrepreneurial approach from the positivistic paradigm and aligns it much more closely with interpretivism.

In this paper we examine the case of a utility company in the UK. This company attempted to break away from the traditional approaches to evaluation and adopt tools, techniques and concepts which have a more entrepreneurial  avour. In order to capture the richness and depth required, we take an interpretivist approach in analysing the extent of their success and the problems that they faced. The case study is described in the next section and the  ndings are discussed in the third section in the light of the IS evaluation literature. This initial analysis is inconclusive and so we re-examine the case in the fourth section, treating the introduction of the new evaluation procedures as an example of organizational change. This provides additional insight and lessons which could be useful to other organizations which are considering shedding the straitjacket of traditiona evaluation.

## The case of a UK utility company

This section describes the organizational initiatives related to IS evaluation enacted by a UK, public-sector utility company (UC) competing in a recently privatized market. The developments took place during the period from 1988 to 1996. The interviews were conducted between late 1993 and 1996 while the earlier initiatives were reconstructed from documentation and the memories of the interviewees. The information was obtained from interviews with ten managers (interviewed one to three times each) from various parts of the company and from extensive documentation that was made available. The managers interviewed ranged from upper to lower level middle management and included policy makers as well as operational managers.

## Background history

The UC experienced a number of radical organizational changes during the 1980s and 1990s, while it market in the UK also underwent major change. The company lost its monopoly in 1981 and very soon a competitor entered the UK market. The remaining statutory monopolies were abolished in 1984 and the UC became a public limited company with 49.8% of its shares retained by the government. The aim was to introduce high levels of competition into the market and, as a result, the UC needed to improve its competitiveness, ef ciency and responsiveness to its customers and to become committed to continuous improvement. These objectives were attempted through massive investments in new technologies and a more commercially oriented management structure and culture. In 1989–90 the company spent £210 million of capital expenditure on IT. In 1996, the UC employed 130 000 people, owned assets of £22 billion and had 80% of the UK market. The same year its turnover was £14.4 billion and its pre-tax pro t reached £3 billion representing increases of 4 and 13.4%, respectively. At the same time, the UC faced another round of major challenges: increased globalization of the market, more active regulation in the UK and lowe switching costs for its customers. In early 1996 a new chief executive of cer took over to lead the company through this highly competitive market.

## Improving the IT investment evaluation processes

## Managing IT investments as a business process

During the 1980s, the UC’s policy shifted towards a focus on its customers and the quality of its services and products. The central role of managing information as a resource was identi ed for the  rst time and, consequently, the value and contribution of ISs were challenged from a business perspective. Between 1988 and 1990, the organization conducted a study known as the Strategic Systems Plan (SSP). This produced a business model of the current operations, analysed the top-level objectives and established a business model for future operations. Regarding IT investment management, a radical and innovative move towards changing many of the well-established procedures was required. This focused on translating business needs into IT-based solutions by ‘managing the information resources’ and ‘planning the business’.

In 1989, a study was undertaken in order to examine the methods and frameworks available for IT bene ts analysis and investment appraisal, including information economics, SESAME and return on management. The result, known as the bene ts evaluation of systems and technology (BEST) method, sought to identify and evaluate both the tangible and intangible bene ts and link them to the key business processes of the SSP. BEST also enabled the managers to compare and rank different IT project proposals. However, the  exibility that BEST provided to business managers in formulating rigorous business cases was viewed with scepticism by the  nance managers who had to approve the allocation of funds.

Following this, a more robust and rigorous methodology was pursued, aimed at reducing the risk surrounding subjective quanti cation and gaining the support of senior management. Information economics (Parker et al., 1988) was used to develop the information accounting framework (INFACC). Like BEST, the INFACC aimed at assisting managers in de ning the areas of potential bene ts and linking them to existing processes from the SSP process model. Return on investment was the main method used to assess monetary values. Software tools were employed to facilitate the assessment and estimation of IT investment values.

These initiatives moved the company in the direction of treating IT as a business asset and promoted the notion of business/systems coordination. The BEST process was the  rst step in formulating a rigorous way of thinking about business processes and the contribution of IT, as well as their evaluation against business objectives. The INFACC contributed towards the operational support of the principles within BEST.

## The introduction of a bene ts tracking culture

‘Breakout’ was a major corporate transformation programme (1993–1995) which covered all the business areas of the company. It aimed to assist the UC in becoming a  rst-class organization, in worldwide terms, within a 5 year period. The programme consisted of 76 projects which dealt with business objectives, the revenue gap, business process improvements, the realization of operational bene ts and better alignment to customer needs. The programme was sponsored by the managing director and involved 180 managers and employees full-time and another 1200 part-time.

As part of Breakout, a review of key processes (e.g. the supply chain) was carried out and this identi ed numerous problems in the investment approval and planning process.

(1) The process was not holistic or portfolio driven.

(2) The standards for business case formulations varied among the divisions and cross-comparisons were not always possible.

(3) Too many decisions were taken early on and decision making was not balanced through the life cycle of a project.

(4) The investment decisions were narrowly focused on  nancial issues.

(5) The was a lack of bene ts tracking and moni toring mechanisms.

Breakout was presented as an enabling programme which tried to prove the value of its outputs through itself. Therefore, most of the new ideas and tools were applied and tested  rst within Breakout itself. For example, the balanced scoreboard technique (Kaplan and Norton, 1992) involved classifying Breakout’s objectives and principles under the shareholder  nancial, customer/market, organizational learning and process-related objectives. Another innovation (‘executives accountable’) meant that managers were made responsible for the delivery of key objectives; they participated in the initial meetings and signed up for the objectives identi ed.

This approach worked well for Breakout itself but did not become established as normal practice within the rest of the UC. This shortcoming was addressed by a follow-up initiative called ‘Breakin’, which attempted to use these new approaches to secure operational improvements. Breakin was managed at a ‘local’ operational level rather than company wide. The expected delivery period for Breakout was 36 months, such that it was estimated that £1.78 billion in bene ts would accrue before the end of 1996 (and £1.37 billion in recurring bene ts). By the end of 1995, £340 million worth of bene ts had already been achieved through various initiatives such as market-focused workshops and global customer service. A number of organizational changes were introduced across the UC as part of Breakout. These included procedural changes (e.g. a ‘portfolio management’ approach) and changes in the company’s structure, promoting a move towards a more functional architecture by introducing new authorization committees at different levels (e.g. an investment committee and senior executive teams).

## IT investment evaluation environments

At the same time as the above initiatives attempted to change the approach to the management and evaluation of IS, a number of software-based evaluation environments were developed to assist the ‘evaluators’ in carrying out their new tasks. The Investment Expert System Toolkit (InVEST) incorporated knowledge bases of techniques, cases and examples and corporate information. It was designed to assist a project sponsor in classifying the project (using McFarlan’s (1984) strategic grid) and identifying and estimating the costs, bene ts and risks. In 1992, the initial prototype was greeted with enthusiasm and support but the project was subsequently shelved and the project manager moved to another area.

The IT Investment Appraisal (ITIA) tool supported ‘evaluators’ in compiling business cases. It aimed at standardizing business cases, enhancing and enriching the appraisal process and increasing the transparency of IT investments. Questionnaires were provided to address the intangible bene ts and risk analysis. Although ITIA was released concurrently with the Breakout programme, it received limited acceptance and its contribution was not recognized. A number of similar (and fairly sophisticated) tools were developed locally within speci c programmes, for example the Supply Chain Programme Automation Project. However, they did not manage to spread beyond the host programme and soon disappeared from view. The group operations and  nance division (GOF), which was responsible for the corporate capital authorization process, developed a spreadsheet system called Rigorous Appraisal and Processing of Investment Data (RAPID). Due to its central position in budget approval, the GOF division successfully promoted RAPID as a standard requirement for business cases across the organization. All the managers we interviewed had used or at least were aware of RAPID and its role.

In summary, over the previous 15 years, the UC had been forced to make frequent radical changes in order to meet the changing expectations and demands of customers and other stakeholders. The UC’s transformation from a sleepy public utility to a large competitive corporation  ghting for business in a highly dynamic market meant that it had to rethink its entire orientation, mission, culture and processes. As part of this, the UC recognized the key role of IS. IS were no longer an unwelcome costly organizational overhead; they became a signi cant element in the UC’s business strategy. In order to get the most out of this new organizational resource, a new approach to IS management was needed. As part of this, the UC realized the limitations of the traditional formal– rational approaches to evaluation. Consequently, the UC put considerable efforts and resources into improving its evaluation methods and processes, such that IT was managed from a business perspective and not just from a technical or accounting perspective. A series of methods and tools was developed which implied major changes in the evaluation culture. However, most of these attempts failed to achieve widespread acceptance and were discontinued.

## Possible explanations

This section discusses the UC’s lack of success in making its IS evaluation practices richer and more relevant to its needs. Various potential explanations from recent literature are considered to see whethe they can explain the UC’s relative failure. In order to capture the social and organizational aspects of the case, we use the framework of context, content and process. This framework was originally proposed by Pettigrew (1985) and has been used by other author in IS evaluation (Symons, 1990, 1991; Farbey et al., 1993; Walsham, 1993).

## The evaluation context

The contextual aspects focus on the pressures within the environment which have an impact on the evaluation. These include pressures from both the interna organizational environment and the wider external environment. It is typically these pressures which determine ‘why’ the evaluation is carried out as well as ‘who’ should carry out the evaluation. The various reasons for carrying out an evaluation imply that evaluation can perform various roles within an organization. Furthermore, it is recognized that, in many industries, the context is highly speci c and subject to constant change and uncertainty. Therefore, contingent approaches are often proposed. An important aspect of the environment concerns the political dimension, i.e. the relationships between the various stakeholders.

## Multiple roles of evaluation

A review of the literature suggests numerous organizational roles for IS evaluation including the following.

(1) Establishing by quantitative and/or qualitative means the worth of IT to the organization and its growth (Farbey et al., 1993; Willcocks, 1994).

(2) Ranking alternatives (Hawgood and Land, 1988; Clemons, 1991)

(3) Forming a central part of an incremental planning and control (diagnosis) process (Blackler and Brown, 1985; Hawgood and Land, 1988)

(4) Acting as an input to business and IS strategy formulation (Peters, 1994; Baker, 1995).

(5) Acting as a feedback function (Angell and Smithson, 1991; Baker, 1995) which assists organizational learning (Hirschheim and Smith son, 1987; Earl, 1989; Galliers, 1991; Farbey et al., 1993; Walsham, 1993; Argyris and Schon, 1996).

(6) Acting as a mechanism for gaining commitment and, in highly politically in uenced environments, for legitimization (Gregory and Jackson, 1992; Powell, 1992; Farbey et al., 1995).

(7) Providing a deeper understanding of the interaction between the technology and the underlying organizational processes, culture and politics (Hirschheim and Smithson, 1988; Symons, 1993).

These roles tend to be more holistic, stretching beyond the ‘simple’ technical and accounting issues. The lack of understanding of these different roles or some form of role con ict could be an explanation for the UC’s failure.

However, it is easy to see from the case study that the UC began to appreciate that evaluation was more than a narrow accounting exercise and, in fact, the organization realized that evaluation could have a number of rather different roles. The UC started with the need to establish a coherent approach to achieve the maximum contribution of IS to the business processes. That was the  rst step in moving from a technical perception towards considering the business dimensions of IT. This led to a clear description of organizational processes (the SSP project) and the development of supporting frameworks (i.e. BEST and INFACC) promoting the traditional control nature of evaluation.

However, in the early attempts of BEST and INFACC, the managers learnt much about the organization and its business dimensions. Later, Breakout’s programme management team realized that the organization’s success was tied directly to its ability to innovate, improve and learn. However, in order for learning to  ourish, information feedback loops and dissemination channels are necessary at every management level. The participants identi ed a number of lessons as part of the Breakout project, including the need for cross-divisional collaboration, and extended participation and assignment of ownership and resources. Some of the evaluation tools developed attempted to facilitate investment appraisal learning (e.g. InVEST) but without much success.

An expert system is a good tool for learning. Particularly in the area of IT investments evaluation, understanding and learning are important factors. However, many times technology leads to a deskilling of decision makers which of course is not accepted in a positive way (InVEST’s project manager).

On the other hand, although numerous evaluation initiatives took place, there is no evidence that the experiences and lessons were disseminated among managers on a company-wide basis. The main explanations for this were the decentralization of the initiatives and their limited applicability. However, without doubt, the managers who were involved in these attempts became more aware of the various issues (e.g. the strategic bene ts and risks) related to IT invest ment evaluation. It seemed that cultural changes began to be achieved but without always reaching the levels of implementation.

## Contingency model

The argument for a contingency approach starts from the recognition that today’s ISs have a wide range of impacts which vary according to the circumstances and the context. In addition, managers have to deal with a spectrum of different projects. Uncertainty characterizes most organizational contexts and this invariably impacts on the role of an IS and the role of evaluation, the ways that it is carried out, the use of its outcomes and its participants. This implies that IT projects (as well as their contexts) have certain char acteristics which in uence the choice of a suitable evaluation approach. Furthermore, evaluation requires an ‘emergent’ consideration which should allow it to be developed incrementally and dynamically.

Various studies (e.g. Ward, 1990; Willcocks, 1992) have argued for the use of contingency models in order to assist in understanding the role and impacts of an IS in a particular case. These can be used to select an appropriate evaluation method (e.g. Farbey et al., 1992; Willcocks and Lester, 1994). There is no shortage of proposals within the literature for classifying ISs and from this determining a relevant approach to evaluation. These are normally based on the business role or ‘type’ of IS to be evaluated (e.g.

mandatory, infrastructure and automation). This approach can be seen in the work of Hochstrasser (1990), Silk (1990), Farbey et al. (1992, 1993, 1994), Peters (1994), Ward (1994) and Willcocks and Lester (1994). A rather different classi cation approach is based on the characteristics of the group of people participating in the evaluation (Gregory and Jackson, 1992).

Although the managers within the UC were unaware of these particular models, the underlying principles were incorporated within some of the tools developed by the UC. Both InVEST and ITIA included the classi cation of projects under investigation. The tool developers realized that the bene ts and risks varied according to the type of project and this was an important initial parameter in the evaluation and formulation of business cases.

## Stakeholders in the evaluation judgement

The outcome of an evaluation exercise is likely to have an impact on the various stakeholder groups within the organization. Particular departments, functions or management levels may gain or lose from any consequent reallocation of resources or their status may change due to the allocation of credit or blame resulting from an evaluation. This political aspect of evaluation can be very important in particular cases. Furthermore, because of the inherently subjective nature of evaluation (e.g. Legge, 1984; Walsham, 1993), the af liation of the ‘evaluators’ may have a de nite impact on the methods, criteria and outcomes of the evaluation. The identi cation of stakeholders is often seen in the literature (e.g. Guba and Lincoln, 1981, 1989; Symons, 1990) where, it is argued, a deeper understanding of the perspectives of individuals and groups can reveal the human and political aspects. Farbey et al. (1993) developed an ‘evaluation stakeholder’s map’ for identifying stakeholders. In their bene ts management process model, Ward et al. (1996) investigated the role of the stakeholders who would act as levers or bene ciaries (i.e. the ‘bene t owners’).

The importance of stakeholder involvement in the management of IT investments and the delivery of bene ts was clearly recognized in the case of the UC. The managers who were involved in the IS evaluation originated from various parts of the organization and possessed a variety of backgrounds, skills and interests. It seemed to us that their involvement was as much a case of political representation as one of obtaining access to their particular knowledge and skills. However, this particular ‘evaluation party’ was still dominated by managers from the  nance function, demonstrating their ‘political clout’ within the organization. Their in uence and power in the evaluation process was demonstrated by the widespread use of RAPID and its  nancial appraisal principles. Although IT was a critical factor in the overall success of the UC, the managers from the tech nical and systems development divisions were mostly fairly marginal in the evaluation exercises. As a result of this, they maintained a parallel evaluation stream to consider the technical aspects of the systems.

The quality of studies at the front-end of the life cycle, such as feasibility studies, can be achieved by considering the overall context where the proposed project is going to operate as well as its interlinks with the existing ones. Unfortunately the people who deal with the technology side of the projects are never senior and therefore the integration of the technical values with the business ones is not always achievable (group systems engineering manager).

Bene ts tracking is applied only for  nancial bene- ts and not for technical performance elements in order to judge at a high level the performance of the company’s programmes. In general there is no well-established information feedback loop. What exists is not systematic and its credibility depends on individuals (group systems engineering manager).

Within the UC we saw the aggregation of evaluation responsibilities and practices at various levels within the organization. Generic guidelines were proposed at a senior level and were interpreted by programme of cers who adjusted them to the needs of their programme and handed them down to their project managers. The project managers were the ‘real evaluators’ who actually applied the guidelines and techniques at the bottom line. The organizational ‘distance between the managers formulating the evaluation strategies and the project managers contributed to the limited dissemination of the ideas. The identi cation of this problem and an attempt to provide a solution can be seen in the Breakin initiative which tried to bridge the gap. The lack of bottom-up feedback channels and the general paucity of communication due to the size of the the UC in addition to the frequent lack of central control and coordination of evaluation contributed to the poor reception of both InVEST and ITIA.

## The evaluation content

The content of an evaluation refers to the ‘what’ of evaluation (e.g. the object of the evaluation and the criteria and their measurement). As noted above, there has been a shift away from the traditiona formal–rational (functional) approach which assumes a well-de ned, direct and short term content and

straightforward measures. The nature of IS today requires a broader consideration, one which takes into account factors such as the intangible bene ts and risks. The evaluation goals need to be considered in conjunction with the role that the evaluation process plays and the people involved. The literature includes the use of alternative measures and the extension of existing measures. The former include intermediate outputs based on time, quality, cost and  exibility (Bakos and Kemerer, 1992), organizational learning and commitment (Scott, 1994) and instruments to deal with the intangible bene ts and risks (Coleman and Jamieson, 1994). To improve existing measures, Kaplan and Norton (1992) put forward the balanced scoreboard, a high-level framework which can encompass traditional detailed measures while Hares and Royle’s (1994) framework sought to complement existing  nancial appraisal methodologies.

The UC exhibits an evaluation culture where the major driving force in justifying IT is the ability to demonstrate  rm potential bene ts, preferably  nancially quanti able, in respect of organizational objectives. BEST and INFACC offered the business units an opportunity of ranking the IT investment proposals by their worth to the business strategy and key business processes, using intangible bene ts alongside tangible ones. Unfortunately, the inherent subjectivity was viewed with suspicion by the accountants and senior managers and, consequently, the success of these approaches was limited. Although some of the UC’s support environments (e.g. ITIA) attempted to change the culture they did not manage to win the con dence needed to become company standards. The only exception was the balanced scoreboard technique promoted by Breakout.

During Breakin, we had dif culties in agreeing with directors on hard measures. Many of the initiatives and proposals do not contribute directly in monetary terms. We recommend people to use at least real measures such as productivity and surveys of customer satisfaction (a manager of group business management).

The above  ndings are not surprising. Currie (1995) found that 85.5% of managers believe that qualitative bene ts are as important as the  nancial ones but only 53% attempt to quantify them because of their vague nature. Financial measures are commonly imposed by senior managers as control mechanisms to IT-related decisions (Grindley, 1991; Willcocks and Lester, 1993). In the UC, this was encouraged by the  nance managers whose in uence on the evaluation of IT investments remained very high.

Senior managers want something to measure the soft IT bene ts. However, they still go through the traditional ( nancial) way because it works. I believe that any attempt towards this direction should not disregard the traditional way but it has to build upon it (a programme of cer).

## The process of IS evaluation

The process of evaluation concerns how the evaluation is carried out (the methods and techniques used to mediate it) and the way it plays itself out over time. The process is strongly in uenced by the context within which it is performed and, as such, it can be viewed as the institutionalized behaviour of the evaluation participants (i.e. the expressed way in which they perform their roles). In this respect, process mediates between the evaluation content and context. The content/process relationship in uences the outcomes of the evaluation exercise and, consequently, its perceived success and impacts (Farbey et al., 1993; Walsham, 1993).

Traditionally IS evaluation was very much subservient to IS development, in that evaluation took place at particular stages within the systems develop ment life cycle. Thus, some form of ex ante evaluation was an inherent part of the feasibility study, various progress evaluations appeared within the project man agement of every development and,  nally, some form of post-implementation evaluation took place shortly after the introduction of the system. This one-dimensional and linear approach focuses on the use of resources and the achievement of predetermined (technical) objectives, adopting a short-term view and assuming a stable context. More recent thinking suggests the use of continuous evaluation in order to man age the bene ts after implementation (Schäfer, 1988; Ward et al., 1996; Willcocks, 1996). Further process support can be seen in the meta-methodologies associated with the contingency approach (see the section on evaluation context) and the methods associated with the interpretive paradigm. The latter can involve experimental (e.g. simulation, prototyping, game playing and structural modelling activity) and exploratory (e.g. multiobjective multicriteria, art criticism, accreditation and adversarial methods) techniques which can expose the subjective informal views of stakeholders and hidden political agendas.

For many years, the UC’s evaluation practices had followed the traditional systems life cycle. In parallel, an investment authorization process was operated which was driven by the amount of the proposed expenditure, a variable which de ned the authority levels and process followed. Any additional considerations concerning the management of the risks and bene ts of IT projects were just peripheral. The UC realized the weaknesses of the traditional approaches. Initially,

InVEST was built upon an evaluation decision model but the Breakout project in particular focused signi - cant attention on ‘bene ts management’ for the  rst time. This notion removes the assumption that the predicted bene ts would somehow automatically be realized. This could be seen in the UC both in terms of new concepts (e.g. key measures) and implementation (e.g. suitable organizational structures and procedures).

No business case would be approved if it cannot demonstrate how it will track the bene ts claimed and cannot show how, where and when managers will report these results. However, there are some problems around bene ts tracking. If you look at the project management for example, and the various stages around it, the most challengeable is the implementation one. Many people disappear before that stage. The critical point here is who owns the bene ts delivery (and has the responsibility), the de nition of the line and success (programme manager in Breakout).

Major problems were faced in trying to convince the  nance managers to perceive IS bene ts management in a complementary way to the traditional monetary conceptions. This was a serious obstacle on the way to persuading other departments to change their approaches because the  nance function had considerable power and responsibilities.

The major obstacle to introducing the bene ts tracking philosophy and making it stick was the company’s culture and the negative feelings of people to something new. We experienced similar reactions with the introduction of activity-based costing as a standard costing methodology. \_ One thing that we have learnt is that in all major changes you need a champion at the right seniority level and also good ‘marketing’ of what you produce (programme manager in Breakout).

## The evaluation support tools

The majority of the evaluation environments developed in the UC were ‘local’ initiatives which provided a computer-based tool of varying sophistication (e.g. an expert system or spreadsheet-based model). All of them considered similar issues (e.g. intangible bene- ts and risks) and all experienced similar dif culties in gaining acceptance. All the tools aimed at supporting the ‘evaluators’ in dealing with the management of IT investments. Many developers expected that a toolkit would support and promote a culture based on intangible bene ts and risk management. However, their lack of organizational seniority could guarantee neither constant support, nor the necessary management of the cultural changes. The only ‘successful’ attempt, RAPID, was directed centrally by the GOF division and this focused on the traditional  nancial elements. RAPID was established as a company-wide standard requirement for the formulation of business cases, not because of its superiority but mainly because of the political position of the GOF division.

## Further analysis

The UC’s privatization had been successful and the company as a whole was earning large pro ts. In many parts of the company there was a feeling that a new adventure had started very well. In the  eld of IS evaluation, new programmes were introduced by management, supported by a range of fairly sophisticated software tools. Considering the discussion in the previous section, it can be seen that in changing thei evaluation strategy and practice, the UC had, either intentionally or accidentally, managed to address most of the evaluation issues documented in the literature. There was evidence of an understanding of the context, content and process of the evaluation and worthy attempts were made to make signi cant and potentially bene cial changes in the evaluation procedures to re ect the new understanding. Progress was uneven and some of these issues received more attention than others, for example bene ts management prospered more than organizational learning. However, the  nal outcome was not an outstanding success; most of the proposals were either not taken up widely across the corporation or else were quickly superseded by yet more proposals. The failure was not one of ideas; it was rather one of implementation.

Thus, rather than focusing on the detail of the attempted changes to the evaluation procedures themselves, it becomes more insightful to consider such changes from the perspective of organizational change. In other words, rather than examining the changes from the perspective of IS evaluation, we will consider them as changes to the organizational paradigm, the roles of the stakeholders and to the organization as a whole. There is an extensive body of IS literature concerning IS and organizational change (e.g. Keen, 1981; Markus, 1983; Hirschheim and Newman, 1988; Nelson, 1990; Joshi, 1991) but the ideas have not penetrated the literature on IS evaluation.

## A paradigm change

The  rst point to consider regarding the UC’s proposa for a new evaluation practice is the sheer depth of the change. The initiatives at the UC aimed to harmonize the IT-related decision processes with business decision making (see SSP, BEST and INFACC), involving a paradigm change for the managers and staff

involved. The switch from traditional bureaucratic accounting techniques to a bene ts management and risk analysis culture represented a signi cant change in the underlying concepts and values of evaluation. This was not just the introduction of some new techniques, it was a paradigm shift from a formal–rational conception to an entrepreneurial approach.

The UC made some super cial progress with exploring new techniques but the change of paradigm to one that would succour the new techniques was not made successfully. Hence, when the organization attempted the adoption of advanced concepts, it experienced dif culties with their integration with the existing culture, based on the previous paradigm. Typically, the  nal outcome was a somewhat unstable mixture of the old approach but with a coating of new techniques. In other words, the existing evaluation methods were changed to the extent needed, in the perceptions of the stakeholders involved, but without any changes to their underlying value systems.

This could also be seen as a variant of the ‘crisis of use’ of evaluation (Legge, 1984). The major dif - culties faced in the introduction of new ideas were associated with the institutionalization of the proposed innovations in the given context, a context that was changing constantly. The dif culties in changing the evaluation paradigm have been highlighted by Hirschheim and Smithson (1988). Organizations need to understand the paradigm of the evaluation in use and the one needed for any new situation. This should become the basis for treatment in terms of the impacts on the organizational roles and stakeholders and its organizational integration.

## Changes in the roles of stakeholders

We emphasized above the signi cance of the social actors within IS evaluation. People are not only different in terms of their cognitive style, cognitive  lters and underlying value systems, perhaps, more importantly, people are not just individuals; they are social actors, with various group af liations, who are acting out a variety of organizational roles during the evaluation process.

A wide range of organizational roles is associated with IS evaluation (Serafeimidis, 1997) including the evaluators themselves, those who design the strategy and tools for evaluation (the ‘strategists for evaluation’) and the audience for the evaluation (e.g. users and management). Such roles have formal and informal aspects determining the power of stakeholders and their range of actions in terms of tasks and activities. In addition, each role is related to the information and knowledge that it uses, produces and disseminates.

In the UC, most of the initiatives directly or indirectly in uenced the roles of the evaluation stakeholders. The reluctance of the UC’s stakeholders to use the new  exible approaches could be explained partly by their limited expertise and experience. The wide variety of methods required knowledge and understanding in order to choose and apply them effectively. Most of the UC’s initiatives were also characterized by limited participation from the stakeholders affected. It is likely this led to a poor cognitive representation by individuals and this may have contributed further to the lack of acceptance.

However, more importantly, any attempt to change these roles (including the skills and knowledge associ ated with a role) is likely to place strains on the groups concerned. Thus, the accountants may have perceived that their traditional role was being usurped, while the system developers and business managers were unlikely to welcome the increased responsibility without some form of recognition or reward. These stakeholder groups had long-established relationships with each other and the changes to the evaluation procedures threatened to disrupt a delicate political balance. This was particularly awkward as the role changes were not recognized explicitly and there was much uncertainty and vagueness.

The promotion of new evaluation approaches was largely a responsibility of the stakeholders performing the role of the ‘strategist for evaluation’. Their lack of success might have been due to confusion concerning their responsibilities and the unclear de nition of their tasks and authorities, which was exacerbated by the inherent dif culties of such ventures. The development of evaluation roles had not been taken seriously during the conception and implementation of the evaluation approaches. This is signi cant as the vague de nition of tasks or limited consideration of potential con icts with existing organizational roles is a recipe for failure. Therefore, one might argue that the source of many of the problems experienced in the case study was related to various aspects of the evaluation roles (i.e. positions, tasks, authorities and behaviour).

## An organizational change

There is a complex relationship between IS evaluation and organizational change. Firstly, any individual evaluation should consider the organizational changes resulting from the introduction of the IS. Secondly, the evaluation itself may cause organizational change as the resulting recommendations may imply further changes in the allocation of resources, the structure o the organization, the roles and tasks of various stakeholder groups and, consequently, the overall balance of power within the organization. However, the case of the UC concerns a higher level: the change in evaluation procedures and this clearly needs to be considered as a potentially signi cant organizational change.

In the UC, many changes were initiated as attempts to introduce innovative ways of thinking throughout the organization. Whether top-down or bottom-up, they lacked senior management support and broad organizational vision. As in other cases (e.g. Coleman and Jamieson, 1994) these initiatives were introduced with the hope of stimulating cultural change and were not developed to support structural or process changes. Additional implementation dif culties were related to the complex bureaucratic decision-making processes and the large number of stakeholders involved. With all the committees and groups involved it was sometimes not clear who should make a particular decision and where the accountability lay.

From these initiatives, useful lessons were learned which could have contributed to organizational learning. However, the experience acquired was never widely diffused, perhaps due to the size of the organization and the lack of formal dissemination channels. Nevertheless, in the case of Breakout, managers recognized the need for cultural and organizational changes. ‘Bene ts breakfasts’ were organized to coach participants through different ideas and issues. The programme team allocated an ‘executive accountable for all the Breakout projects (for the  rst time in the history of the UC) who was responsible for delivering the bene ts.

The idea of ‘executives accountable’ was necessary because many projects by that time had no business sponsor or champion. This is an idea widely accepted within our company and supported intensively by the  nance director. However, the accountability is very dif cult to trace (programme manager in Breakout).

The above problems originated in the lack of integration of the new approaches with the existing organizational institutions, including the systems development life cycle, IS management practices and  nancial and accounting procedures and rules. Furthermore, the lack of appropriate cultural and structural foundations (e.g. organizational learning and maturity) helps to explain the limited adoption of the evaluation approaches and the perceived gap. With the current formal and highly bureaucratic structures and culture well entrenched, new options were unlikely to attract support easily. Such major changes are sources of satisfaction and dissatisfaction and become the object of political con ict. This could be seen by the actions of the UC’s  nance department in maintaining their dominance of the evaluation process.

Perhaps the key reason for the relative failure of the evaluation initiatives concerns the sheer size, diversity and lumbering bureaucracy of the UC. Although the proponents of change tried both top-down and bottom-up approaches to making the change there was insuf cient momentum for change. There was a feeling that the existing accounting-based organizational practices worked well enough. In other words, there was a lack of need felt: ‘if it ain’t broke don’t  x it’.

In more theoretical terms, this can be explained by Giddens’s (1979) structuration theory which considers the relationship between structure and action. In the UC, large numbers of staff in the majority of the sites across the organization continued with their existing practices (there was no change in their actions). This constantly reinforced the existing structures (of value and beliefs) which in turn supported the existing practices. In such a situation, changes in structures and actions are extremely dif cult to implement and require considerably more planning, commitment and pressure than the UC’s senior management were willing or able to invest. Had the existing practices resulted in unexpected deleterious consequences, then change would have been easier to introduce. As it was, the organization was prospering and most managers and staff saw no reason to change.

## Conclusion

We have argued that IS evaluation is an important but complex organizational process. The traditiona approach to IS evaluation, based on narrow technica and accounting terms, has limited relevance to the role of IS in today’s organizations. Organizations are moving towards more entrepreneurial approaches, embodying some of the thinking of the interpretevist stream of IS research.

The study of a particular organization demonstrated some of the practical aspects and implications of these newer approaches to evaluation within a particular context. The company instigated a series of initiative relating to how IS were perceived, managed and evaluated. The company made various attempts to improve their IS evaluation and develop evaluation support tools but few achieved widespread acceptance within the company, even though they seemed to make business sense and re ected current thinking within the literature. These initiatives addressed, albeit partially, problems recognized by the company in the context, content and process of IS evaluation within the company.

A consideration of the introduction of these new approaches to evaluation as an example of organizational change explained the lack of success. The senior

management of the organization had failed to realize that these changes involved a change of paradigm within the company and did not provide suf cient support to effect this change. Nor did they take suf - cient care in explicitly reformulating the roles of the people involved to take account of the proposed changes. Instead, existing procedures continued to be reinforced by repeated action, the lack of ‘felt need’ for change and the primacy of a  nance department which was wedded to the traditional approaches. These problems were exacerbated by the size and diversity of the organization. Thus, most of the changes fell by the wayside and were not widely adopted.

Interestingly, these issues are barely mentioned in the literature on IS evaluation; they clearly call for more theory building and further empirical studies. Although these conclusions are based on a single case study, there is no reason to believe that they are not generalizable to similar, large, bureaucratic organizations which are seeking to use ISs to improve their effectiveness and commercial position in a more competitive market. Further research could con rm these views. Nevertheless, we feel that organizations who are grappling with the ‘thorny problem’ of IS evaluation and who are considering introducing new evaluation procedures would be advised to treat such initiatives as signi cant organizational changes. Bearing this in mind and acting accordingly, senior managers are more likely to be successful in achieving the changes desired.
