# Modelling the price, performance and contract characteristics of IT outsourcing

SIMON DOMBERGER AND PATRICK FERNANDEZ

Australian Graduate School of Management, The University of New South Wales, Sydney 2052, Australia

DENZIL G FIEBIG

Department of Econometrics, University of Sydney, Sydney, Australia

The rapidly increasing use of outsourcing for IT services, both in the public and private sectors, has attracted much interest from researchers and practitioners alike. While early studies of IT outsourcing were largely qualitative in nature, more recent studies have attempted to analyse the outcomes achieved in quantitative terms. This paper is consistent with the latter, but goes further by modelling the price, performance and contract characteristics that are relevant to IT outsourcing. A two-equation recursive regression model was used to analyse 48 contracts for IT support and maintenance. The results did not reveal any quantitatively signi cant price–performance trade-off, but did suggest that  rst-term contracts (i.e. the  rst ever contract awarded by a client for the provision of a particular IT service) were more expensive than repeat contracts. Although competitive tendering did not result in lower prices than directly negotiated contracts, it was associated with comparatively better performance. Well-de ned expectations of an organization’s IT requirements were also likely to lead to improved performance when the service was outsourced.

## Introduction

The boundaries of organizations, both in the public and private sectors, are shifting, one of the main reasons for this being the increased use of outsourcing as a method of service delivery. Outsourcing involves contracting with an external provider for the provision of a service which may previously have been provided using in-house staff. It is widely recognized that there is a strong case for the outsourcing of low-skill services, such as cleaning, catering and building maintenance. They are ancillary to the organization’s primary purpose and main line of business; it is therefore not critical that they be provided using the organization’s internal resources. In addition, outsourcing offers a real opportunity of reducing the cost of providing these ancillary services.

Although information technology (IT) is integral to the operations of most organizations and requires a much higher level of skill, it happens to be one of the most outsourced services. The ever-increasing expenditure on IT services and the often exceptionally high levels of service expected by users have been responsible for much of the spotlight on IT outsourcing as an alternative method of service delivery. Even incremental improvements achieved through IT outsourcing can have a signi cant impact on operating costs, service performance, strategic advantage and shareholder value (Bryce and Useem, 1998). While many managers who choose to outsource their IT services would normally favour using their internal resources to achieve these improvements, their decision to buy in the service is in uenced by a number of constraints with the in house option. The problems encountered include a shortage of skilled IT staff within most organizations, an inability to cover a rapidly expanding  eld adequately and the lack of  exibility which can result from overinvestment in a particular technology. It is clear from this that the reasons for outsourcing IT services and ancillary services, such as those mentioned earlier, are distinctly different.

Large-scale IT outsourcing gained considerable publicity after Kodak surprised the market by outsourcing its entire IT operations in 1989. This is believed to have given others the con dence to do likewise and, consequently, there has been a gradual diffusion of the strategy into the boardrooms of a wide range of organizations (Loh and Venkatraman, 1992). However, the practice of outsourcing speci c components of the function to specialists from each area of IT preceded this landmark decision – software devel opment, data processing activities and support and maintenance have been contracted out as far back as the 1970s (Michell and Fitzgerald, 1997). The case for selective outsourcing of individual IT functions, as opposed to turning over all facets of an organization’s IT operations to a single provider, has been strongly advocated by a number of authors who believe i is a less risky strategy (Earl, 1996; Lacity, et al., 1996). Certain IT functions, such as applications development, are more appropriately delivered through ‘insourcing’ – buying in resources from an external supplier to work under in-house management (Willcocks and Currie, 1997). This form of contracting reduces the risk of providing a service which cannot be adequately speci ed in advance and which relies closely on the direction taken and signi cant input of the organization’s in-house management.

Contracting for service provision has been at the forefront of reforms to the public sector in several countries, including the UK, Australia and New Zealand. Although much of the early attention was directed at low-skill services, in more recent years the public sector has added signi cantly to the growth in IT outsourcing. In the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries it accounts for between 16 and 50 per cent of the total government spending on IT (Globerman and Vining, 1996). In comparison, recent estimates for the private sector suggest that IT outsourcing averages 10% of the total IT investment for companies (Weill and Broadbent, 1998). In the UK, the central government remains the largest IT outsourcing customer, accounting for nearly one-quarter of the value of contracts awarded (Currie and Willcocks, 1998). A similar study found that, of the public sector organizations which had not yet outsourced their IT services, three-quarters were considering doing so in the near future (Currie, 1996).

In most organizations, particularly those in the public sector, outsourcing a service currently provided in-house presents a number of challenges, not the least of which is the future role of the existing staff. The fear of losing staff with a close working knowledge of the organization and its speci c internal requirements may discourage outsourcing. In IT contracts a large number of in-house staff are re-employed by the outsourcing contractor because their intimate understanding of the systems is highly valued. An outsourcing contractor is also likely to have access to additional IT staff for use on speci c assignments in these organizations. Given the shortage of IT professionals it may be harder for an internal IT unit/group to secure such staff and manage them. Clearly, as we see the development of a  ourishing market for the many and varied IT services required, it will be even less compelling for these activities to be retained within organizations (Domberger, 1998).

Typically, the outsourcing process involves some form of bidding, as in competitive tendering and contracting (CTC) or direct negotiation between the transacting parties. CTC seeks out the most ef cient service provider from the market and is particularly suited when cost reduction is a key objective of outsourcing (Domberger and Rimmer, 1994). On the other hand, direct negotiation offers the opportunity to match a purchaser’s requirements closely with the service provider’s capability and to emphasize quality and contract performance. In both cases, a contract formalizes the agreement by making reference to a detailed set of speci cations, which includes performance standards, the terms of the transaction and the agreed price. The factors which in uence price and performance and the relationship each has with the other are therefore highly relevant to outsourcing. A study of cleaning contracts modelled both variables and provided evidence that the downward effect of competition on prices was not achieved at the expense of service quality (Domberger et al., 1995). This paper seeks to apply a similar approach by analysing the price and performance of outsourcing contracts for IT services.

The next section of the paper explains the framework for the study, how it is in uenced by simila research in the  eld and the extensions made in our work. The third section provides summary statistics of the survey data on contracts for the outsourcing of IT services which were examined in the study. The fourth section discusses the structure of the regression model used to estimate the determinants of price and performance. The estimated model is then evaluated, followed by separate discussions on the empirical  ndings on price and performance. Finally, the last section discusses the implications of our results.

## Framework of the study

Finlay and King (1999) proposed a research framework for studying IT outsourcing of the speci c systems which assist with the interpretation of information within organizations. However, there are practical dif- culties with this proposal as outsourcing contracts are more likely to focus on speci c IT functions – data centre management, applications development, maintenance and support, systems integration and project management.

We deliberately sought to examine the selective outsourcing of an IT function – IT maintenance and support – which is among the most frequently outsourced services (Apte et al., 1997). The narrower focus means there is greater consistency in the contracts studied, compared to the total outsourcing contracts which differ signi cantly in their component services. However, some of the lessons learned from our study could apply equally to a range of other IT services.

Given the high level of interest in IT outsourcing which now exists within the public sector, we have concentrated on contracts awarded by government agencies. The agencies which rely almost completely on consolidated government revenue for their funding belong to what is commonly referred to as the budget sector and have been categorized as such in our study. The more commercially oriented and self-funded government trading enterprises (GTEs) have been identi ed separately, as have a small number of private sector companies which were included for comparison purposes. The exercise allowed us to examine closely a number of issues that are speci c to the public sector and as to whether they might have affected the outcomes achieved.

Early research on IT outsourcing was mostly qualitative in nature, primarily because of the limited data available. The case study approach in particular has often been used to study and compare a relatively small number of IT outsourcing deals. Lacity and Hirschheim (1993) investigated the factors underlying the more successful IT transactions in 14 case studies of private sector IT contracts in the US. With the growth in IT outsourcing, larger samples of contracts have become possible and, therefore, greater use of empirical analysis has also become more prevalent. For example, a study of the effect of the service quality of the provider and the ability of organizations to build successful relationships used data from 188 companies (Grover et al., 1996). Another study looked at the expected cost savings in 40 companies, with the view of identifying best practice (Lacity and Willcocks, 1998).

Our study is also empirically based and provides a logical extension to earlier research studies. The data were obtained by surveying all government agencies in the State of New South Wales, Australia and a select number of private organizations from the same state.

Recently, Greenstein (1995) explored the factors in uencing a government agency’s choice of vendor in mainframe computer procurement in the US. Speci cally, the study focused on the conditions which induced an agency to opt for competitive tendering procedures when purchasing mainframe computers. It concluded that the extent of vendor competition and the value of the equipment purchased were in uential in determining an agency’s procedural choice.

Our study differs from Greenstein’s (1995) in that it focuses on contracts for hardware and software maintenance services as opposed to the procurement of computer hardware. With the former, contract design and implementation are likely to be highly in uential in determining the price and perceived quality of the services provided. Contract design covers key elements in the speci cation: the length of the contract, the nature of the service (hardware or software maintenance) and the type of equipment to be main tained. As part of the implementation, we asked whether a competitive process was used to select the provider and whether the choice was justi ed in terms of the outcomes achieved. For example, did contrac tors selected through a competitive tendering process provide superior outcomes to clients in terms of price and quality achieved? Other implementation issue related to whether the in-house staff were used to support the activities of the contractor and whether the client had a formal IT management plan.

Further, our analysis attempted to capture both the expected (ex ante) and realized (ex post) dimension of performance. They were measured by eight industry-speci c service attributes which cover the twin dimensions of technical and functional quality (Behara et al., 1995), which are described in detail in the nex section. Client organizations were asked to identify the expected and realized performance of their contractor separately. The empirical analysis was then conducted in terms of both realized performance and the gap between realized and expected performance.

## Survey data

The data for this project were collected through a questionnaire-based survey of 98 clients of hardware and software maintenance contracts. The contracts were awarded by public sector organizations in the State of New South Wales, Australia, but also included a small number of contracts awarded by private sector companies for the purpose of comparison. ## Contract price

The contract price was based on the chargeable hour of the contract over 12 months. Although the mean contract price may be high compared to other services, the survey  gures also include the cost of various supplies and related activities undertaken by the contractor in order to deliver the IT maintenance services. Thus, the contract price represents a surrogate of the scale, scope and complexity of the outsourced IT service, all combined into one measure.

## Contract performance

Clients typically have an expectation of service quality prior to awarding a contract. We refer to it in this study as ‘desired performance’. A necessary part of contract management involves an assessment of the realized performance of the contract. The clients responding to the study were asked to rate the desired performance and realized performance of the contracts for each of the following eight service attributes.

(2) Out-of-hours availability.

(3) Response in emergencies.

(4) Provision at expected cost.

(5) Delivery to expected quality.

(6) Accuracy of advice.

(7) Correctness of error  xes.

(8) Minimization of system downtime.

The original scale for the desired and realized performance ratings was from 1 to 4. A rating of 1 corresponded to not important for the former and unsatisfactory for the latter, while 4 corresponded to very important and excellent, respectively. Rating which were not reported were  lled with zeros to preserve the continuity of the scale on the assumption that they were considered irrelevant or very low in terms of desired or realized performance.

The eight service attributes listed were taken to represent measures of quality. For the purposes of analysis and estimation, a single quality/performance variable (q) was sought. Here there were a number of choices. One possibility was to construct principal components. The  rst principal component, which explains approximately 50% of the variation in the attributes, is essentially a simple average of the realized ratings. It turned out that the responses were all positively and highly correlated. Thus, the simple average of the ratings attached to the eight attributes represents a simple and readily interpretable choice for q, and is designated by $Q _ { \mathrm { R } }$

A second possibility was to consider the realized performance relative to the base, as represented by the desired performance ratings. Constructing a new set of ‘attributes’ by subtracting the desired from the realized rating for each attribute and contract results in data that has as its  rst principal component a variable which is essentially the simple average. Once again this accounted for approximately 50% of the variation in the data. Thus, a second possible proxy for q is $Q _ { \mathrm { D } , \ l }$ representing the average of the eight realized minus desired attributes. The negative mean for this variable indicates a slight tendency to under-perform relative to the desired levels of service quality. Rather than choose between these alternative proxies, the results for both $Q _ { \mathrm { R } }$ (‘realized’ model) and $Q _ { \mathrm { D } }$ (‘differences’ model) were reported.

## Contract characteristics

## Services outsourced

The majority of the contracts related to either hardware (45%) or software (36%) maintenance services but not both. Only 19% of the contracts provided an integrated approach to hardware and software maintenance. The majority of the contracts (91%) provided a hotline/helpline service for rectifying simple problems, assisting with diagnostics and logging of maintenance jobs.

## Clients

The largest grouping of clients belonged to the budget sector (65%), which is funded by the government’s consolidated revenue. The GTE sector, which represents agencies of government which operate on a more commercial footing and trade their services, made up 29% of the sample. Private sector organizations made up the smallest grouping of all (6%). A large proportion of the organizations surveyed (88%) had a formal plan for their IT infrastructure.

## Contractors

All successful contractors belonged to the private sector and no in-house team was selected to provide IT maintenance services in our sample. Slightly more than half (54%) of the contracts awarded were to contractors who had already received prior pre-quali cation. Thus, contractors with a history of poor performance and those who failed to meet  nancial and operational prerequisites would have been excluded from offering their services to the client organizations.

## Method of outsourcing

Of the contracts in the sample, 38% were awarded through competitive tendering and 52% through direct negotiation. The remaining contracts were commonuse state government contracts which provided government agencies with the opportunity of purchasing services from speci ed contractors at rates which remained  xed across all of the government. The rates would have been tendered, but contractors selected to be on the panel receive no guarantees of work from the government – the decision to use a contractor ultimately rests with individual agencies.

## First-term and repeat contracts

‘First-term’ contracts (i.e. new contracts prior to which service provision was either by in-house staff or not required at all) accounted for 38% of the sample. In a game theoretic framework, such a transaction would represent a  rst-period game between the client and contractor. Contracts that went beyond this  rst-period game, referred to here as repeat contracts, made up 62% of the sample. The current contractor was also the previous service provider in 52% of the sample and in only 10% of cases was the contractor different. It suggests that clients and contractors were  ve times more likely to continue their relationship beyond a single term than they were to end it.

Table 2 attempts to highlight some of the differences between competitively tendered contracts and negotiated contracts by reporting the relative frequency of the various characteristics in each grouping. Competitive tendering was used more than twice as frequently as direct negotiation in  rst-term contracts. This is not surprising, as competitive tendering offers the client an effective way of testing the market’s capability, particularly when there is little prior experience of contracting a service. Government directives also tend to favour competitive tendering over direct negotiation, because there is greater openness with the former. In contrast, direct negotiation was almost twice as common as competitive tendering in repeat contracts which continued to use the previous incumbent contractor. There could be a number of reasons for this observation, including satisfactory performance of the incumbent contractor, avoiding the cost of tendering, and risk aversion to changing contractor and the uncertainty that goes with it.

## Pricing arrangements

For 28% of the sample, the contract price remained  xed through the life of the agreement. A number of contracts (21%) were substantially  xed, but permitted limited adjustments for  uctuations in service level and the volume of maintenance activity. The remaining 51% of contracts were completely variable in price and determined by an agreed schedule of rates for a range of different services.

## Quantitative modelling

The methodology followed in this paper involved the application of multiple regression techniques to model the contract characteristics which are likely to in uence price and performance in IT outsourcing.

## Speci cation of the model

The framework of the analysis is a two-equation recursive model of price and performance determination, similar to that of Domberger et al. (1995). The price (p) of the contract is thought to depend on various contract characteristics (X) which contain information on ex ante quality and is established at the time of the contract award. The ex post service quality $( q ) ,$ referred to here as realized performance, is determined by the price as well as the contract characteristics $( Z )$ . The direction of causation is from (p) to $( q )$ , not vice versa, because the latter is only observed after the contract has been let. While X and Z are likely to have many variables in common, there is no a priori reason for them to be identical. In fact, identi cation problems can arise when they are the same. The resulting twoequation model takes the form:

$$
p = X \beta + \varepsilon\tag{1}
$$

and

$$
q = Z \gamma + p \delta + u\tag{2}
$$

where « and u are disturbances. Equations 1 and 2 represent a triangular system. When the disturbances in the two equations are independent it reduces to a recursive system and simpli es the estimation in that separate Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regressions produce consistent estimates. In the current context, independent disturbances would effectively rule out the presence of ‘cowboy’ contractors – those bidding low prices and later compromising on service performance.

Being highly skewed, even after the elimination of unrealistic values, the distribution of contract prices suggested using a logarithmic transformation of price in Equation 1. This was subsequently con rmed by the diagnostic testing procedures, which clearly favoured the log of price as the dependent variable.

The choice of OLS for the quality equations was justi ed because weak exogeneity of lnP was indicated by the Lagrange Multiplier (LM) statistics reported. This can be taken as evidence that the contractors in this sample did not submit unrealistically low bids which could create pressures to cover quality after the contract had been let. ## Interpreting the estimated in uences on contract price

Many of the contract characteristics used to explain the level of contract prices were dummy variables, representing the presence or absence of a particular property. The combination of a logarithmic-dependent variable and dummy explanatory variables requires some care in the interpretation of the associated coef- cients. Suppose the coef cient for SWARE representing software contracts is $b _ { j } ,$ and that the omitted category was combined software/hardware contracts. Then the percentage change in price attributable to moving from a combined software/hardware contract to a software contract alone is $[ ( { \bf e } ^ { b _ { j } } - 1 ) \times 1 0 0 ]$ , where e is the base of natural logarithms. Using the estimator suggested by Kennedy (1981), consistent estimates of these impacts were calculated and are provided in Table 4.

Table 4 reports both the quantitative (%) impact of the explanatory variable on price, and whether it is statistically signi cant. The largest upward in uence on price stems from the system availability (AVAIL):

contracts with adjustable charge rates based on system availability were signi cantly more expensive (188%) than those without such a form of pricing. This re ects the high price premium which is associated with providing the necessary maintenance and support to ensure the system downtime is kept to a minimum.

Three other positive and signi cant in uences on price were in evidence. First-term contracts show an upward price adjustment of 131%, while repeat contracts awarded to the incumbent contractors increased by a more modest 59%. These positive price differentials are measured relative to the control group of repeat contracts which resulted in a change in contractor. The  nding suggests that  rst-time contracts are priced to re ect the higher risk and that, as contractors gain experience with a client’s IT environment, they are able to pass on reductions in prices. The effect of risk on the contract price is also manifest in the estimated coef cient on LENTH – the length of the contract measured in years. The implied elasticity of the contract price with respect to duration is +0.25, estimated at a mean contract length of 1.76 years. Thus, contract prices rise by 25% with every additional year.

The other positive and signi cant impact on price was associated with contracts let by budget sector agencies – by a factor of 157%. This may re ect more stringent requirements by such agencies (government departments) relative to private sector clients and, thus, greater servicing costs. Alternatively, it may re ect a degree of discriminatory pricing, implying a greater willingness and ability to pay.

The one negative and signi cant in uence on price was associated with software maintenance contracts, which were 85% cheaper relative to combined software and hardware maintenance contracts. Software products often stand alone and are customized to the speci c requirements of the client organization. It is therefore not surprising that specialists in this area were more ef cient than generalists offering to cover both software and hardware maintenance services. However, it is interesting to note that hardware maintenance contracts were not signi cantly different in price than combined hardware/software contracts although the estimated coef cient was negative, as expected.

One striking feature of the estimated price model deserves comment: there was no signi cant difference between competitively tendered, directly negotiated and common use contract prices, although the coef - cients on both competitively tendered and directly negotiated contract prices were negative. Two factors could explain these results.

First, the control group constituted common use contracts set up for use by the New South Wales public sector. These contracts were let through an earlier, collective competitive tendering process. It is not fanciful to suppose that the resulting pricing structure would be indistinguishable from that generated by individually tendered contracts. Secondly, if purchasers were well-informed of the prices available through ‘common use’ contracts then, these would naturally become reference points for direct negotiations. Thus, both competitive tendering and direct negotiation could result in prices that were statistically indistinguishable from one another.

Lastly, there was no signi cant price difference between  xed price contracts, those which allowed price adjustments for volume and service levels and contracts based on a schedule of rates. It suggests that the speci cations used were comprehensive and suf ciently described all those factors that went into the pricing of the service at the time of awarding the contract.

## The in uences on contract performance

In general, there were few signi cant in uences, upward or downward, on contract performance. Among the positive and signi cant in uences were contract length and the implementation of an IT plan. The former was statistically signi cant in the mode with $Q _ { \mathrm { R } }$ as the dependent variable, while the latter was signi cant in the differences model.

The positive in uence of contract length on performance is not surprising. As was mentioned earlier in the context of price effects, it takes time for the IT services vendor to gain experience in servicing the client. Hence, other things being equal, contracts of longer duration stand a better chance of being highly rated by the client. Indeed, this result suggests a potential confusion between cause and effect. Is contract performance enhanced when contract duration is above the average length (1.8 years) or are contracts extended by the client when performance is judged to exceed expectations? Without delving into the small print of individual contracts in our sample it is impossible to distinguish between the two effects.

The positive impact of an IT plan on performance is also consistent with a priori expectations. Most contracts in the sample had such a plan and its effect is to formalize the client’s performance expectations. Interestingly, the effect was both large and statistically signi cant in the differences model.

We now come to the interesting issue concerning the in uence of contract price on performance. It has a bearing on the vexed question of a potential tradeoff between price and quality: ceteris paribus, are lower prices associated with poorer service performance? Our model answered this question in the af rmative. However, the quantitative impact of price on performance is very small indeed. The coef cient on price was positive in both performance equations and statistically signi cant in the realized model. The elasticity of realized performance with respect to price evaluated at the mean of the dependent variable turned out to be +0.048. In other words, every one percentage point increase in price raised performance by less than 0.05%. This is hardly suf cient to suggest that price differences translate into substantial variations in performance.

As for the negative effects, contracts let by budget sector agencies were rated lower than the rest, an effect that was statistically signi cant in the differences model. This is all the more interesting when we recall that budget sector agencies typically paid higher prices for the maintenance contracts. One possible explanation is that this group of clients sets a desired level of performance that is unrealistically high and that contractors  nd dif cult to achieve. The higher than average prices associated with this group of clients could be a direct result of this ‘expectations gap’ – a premium which re ects the contractors’ anticipated dif culties in meeting client’s expectations.

The negative coef cient on directly negotiated contracts was more puzzling, for two reasons. First, it was negative and statistically signi cant in the realized but not in the differences model. Second, it is not obvious why a signi cant negative effect should appear in contracts that were directly negotiated, compared to those that were competitively tendered or common use contracts. One possible explanation lies in the fact that the majority of directly negotiated contracts were to be found among budget sector agencies (70%). As we already noted, these agencies’ assessments of contractual performance were uniformly inferior to those of the other client groups in the sample.

## Concluding remarks

In this paper we modelled the price and performance ratings of outsourcing contracts for IT maintenance and support. Our objective was to examine the effect of contract characteristics on both price and quality, as well as their potential interactions, in the outsourcing of knowledge-intensive IT services. The concluding comments may be collected under three headings – the effect of competition, the in uence of risk in contract pricing and the interaction between price and performance.

The clients preferred competitive tendering to direct negotiation with  rst-term contracts. However, in subsequent rounds of contracting, direct negotiation with the incumbent contractor was preferred to competitive tendering. It would appear that clients looked towards a continuation of the contractual rela tionship as bene cial and worth pursuing, unless poor contract performance or other administrative reasons required periodic market testing.

The extent of competition did not appear to affect contract prices – competitively tendered contracts were no cheaper than negotiated ones. This need not mean competition does not have an in uence on price. Rather, it means that its effect could not be detected in this context because of the already highly competitive environment in which contracts were awarded. Moreover, the availability of price information in the market-place through common-use state government contracts meant that opportunities for price discrimination by vendors were severely limited.

Second, risk is an underlying factor in the pricing of IT outsourcing contracts. For example, repeat contracts were priced lower than  rst-term contracts, possibly due to the higher risk and uncertainty often associated with newly awarded contracts. Understandably, this risk would reduce with the greater information and experience gained from having contracted the service for more than one term. In addition, contract pricing which was based on system availability carried a price premium to re ect the revenue risk to the contractor in agreeing to achieve high system uptimes during the life of a contract. However, performance-based contracts were also dif - cult to structure and administer and only used in 20% of the contracts studied.

Third, while a positive and statistically signi cant relationship was observed between price and performance, its magnitude was so small as to deprive it of economic content. In general, this implies that desired performance is predetermined by the speci cation and is not much affected by price variations. Quality of service as assessed by the client is affected to a greater degree by the nature of the client and its managemen style than by economic factors such as competition. The type of client makes a difference, as we have seen, because it determines the management of the relationship and the perceived gap between realized and expected outcomes. Thus, budget sector agencies rated supplier performance lower than the rest and those organizations which had an IT management plan found performance signi cantly superior.

It might also be noted that the dif culty in identifying price–performance trade-offs is evidenced by large and persistent cost overruns in public sector contracts in the UK. Admittedly, these contracts involved applications development (the UK Passport Of ce, Bene ts of ce and others). However, they illustrate the dif culty of establishing ab initio a reliable relationship between price and performance in complex IT contracts.

Finally, we remark on the modelling exercise itself. Most previous empirical studies, including those cited in this paper, are based on case studies. This allows useful lessons and generalizations to be drawn. However, our modelling attempts to take the analysis a step further by giving precision to the relationships and assessing the statistical signi cance of in uential variables. This is but a  rst step in the direction of optimal contract selection and design for would-be clients of IT services.

## Appendix: Bounded in uence estimation

In order to provide some indication of the robustness of the OLS results reported in Table 3, a sensitivity analysis was undertaken whereby alternative estimates were generated using a simple version of bounded in uence (BIF) estimation.

Consider a multiple regression model given by

$$
y _ {i} = x _ {i} \beta + \varepsilon_ {i}
$$

where $x _ { i }$ is the i th row of the $n \times k$ matrix of exogenous variables. The BIF estimator can be written as the solution to a weighted least-squares problem, namely by solving

$$
\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} w _ {i} x _ {i} ^ {\prime} (y _ {i} - x _ {i} \beta) = 0
$$

A simple one-step variant of this estimator chooses weights according to

$$
w _ {i} = \left\{ \begin{array}{c c} 1 & \text { if } | \mathrm{DFFITS} _ {i} | \leqslant 0. 3 4 \\ \frac {0 . 3 4}{\mathrm{DFFITS} _ {i}} & \text { otherwise } \end{array} \right.
$$

where DFFIT $\mathbf { \dot { S } } _ { i }$ is the diagnostic which measures the change in  t caused by the deletion of the ith observation (see, for example, Belsley et al. (1980)). The essential idea behind this procedure is to downweight the most in uential data points in order the restrict the in uence exerted by small subsets of the data (see Welsch (1980) for further details and another application).

On the basis of the OLS results, some observations were in uential according to the DFFITS criteria. For the price equation two observations had DFFITS values ${ > } 2$ in magnitude and there were a further six observations in the range of 1–2. A total of eight obser vations were made where the  t of the regression could be altered by more than one standard deviation uni simply by deleting a single observation. The two quality equations did not seem quite as affected. Both equa tions had only two DFFITS values that were greater in magnitude than unity. Applying the one-step BIF estimator considerably reduced the sensitivity of the results to single observation deletions. In particular there were no observations in any of the equations where the DFFITS values exceeded unity.
