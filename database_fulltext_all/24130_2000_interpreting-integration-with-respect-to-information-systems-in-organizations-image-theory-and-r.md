# Interpreting integration with respect to information systems in organizations – image, theory and reality

TERESA WARING

Newcastle Business School, University of Northumbria, Newcastle upon Tyne NE1 8ST, UK

DAVID WAINWRIGHT

Durham University Business School, Mill Hill Lane, Durham City DH1 3LB, UK

The integration of information systems (IS) is a phrase which is commonly used in both research and practice within the IS  eld and across many organizational contexts. This paper uses a historical review to explore how the image of integrated IS has been constructed, evolved over three decades and how this has resulted in the widespread acceptance of idealized goals and bene ts. A literature search for an underlying theoretical basis for the concept of systems integration is made, highlighting a diversity of opinion. Two case studies are used to examine the meaning of integration relative to the images and ideals, theory and reality. The study concludes that there is an urgent need for better conceptual and theoretical models of integration to enable more effective discourse of the concept. These models must also encompass and include more diverse non-technical factors for the adoption of more complex forms of IS.

## Introduction

Many diverse opinions exist within communities researching and practising forms of integration of information systems (IS). It is a concept which may be construed to mean ‘all things to all people’ and yet, perhaps, it is too easily accepted at face value without an adequate comprehension of its meaning and of the serious nature of the impact of IS integration concerning individuals, functions, the design of work processes, the process of technology adoption, strategy formulation, organizational design and behaviour.

In the era of the ‘integrated enterprise’, increasingly complicated by a growing emphasis on integrated supply chains, many concerns start to surface about the lack of research into the nature of integration. Much research concentrates individually on the large number of complex factors associated with IS implementation which is comprised of human, organizational, technical and social dimensions as opposed to the holistic web of interactions between the components themselves.

Yet Mason et al. (1997) argued that some processes by which information technology (IT) is introduced into an organization would bene t from other types of analyses, among which historical analysis ‘offers perspectives on phenomena that are unavailable by any other methodological means’ (p. 307). Historical research can ‘broaden our understanding’ (p. 307) of the issues and ‘the forces which shape its use’.

In this paper we intend to adopt a historical approach which considers the meaning of integration which has emerged over time, focusing on images, theoretical de nitions, ideals and realities, guided by three main concerns. Firstly, ‘a technology-enabled integrated enterprise’ is a goal which is driven by many senior executives, IT managers, IT service providers, consultants and academics. The second concern is that the mechanisms (comprising technology, planning and implementation know-how) for achieving this goal are assumed to be readily available, understood and easily undertaken. The third concern is that the pace of technological innovation and ‘push’ has outstripped the business or organizational ‘pull’.

The research outlined in this paper involves a histor ical analysis of the concept of integration which has developed and in fact metamorphosed over time with the rapid deployment of increasingly powerful IT. Mason et al. (1997) argued that historical research can compliment nomothetic research by providing the context within which events take place:

Historical research offers perspectives on phenomena that are unavailable by any other methodological means. They re ect the cultural circumstances and ideological assumptions that underlie phenomena and the role played by key decision makers together with long-term economic, social, and political forces in creating them.

(p. 307).

Two cases are presented which demonstrate the real problems which can manifest themselves when trying to introduce an integrated system as opposed to the idealized bene ts realization their vendors advocate. The cases come from both the UK public and private sectors and are used to illustrate the real complexity of managing large-scale integrated information systems taking into account various dimensions of the integration concept.

## Building the image of integration as a goal – an historical perspective

The technological ‘push’ for the adoption of complete enterprise-wide integrated systems solutions has been fuelled by the development and adoption of comprehensive business packages going back to the early days of materials requirements planning (MRP) in the 1960s up to the enterprise resource planning (ERP) packages of the 1990s. In order to understand this growing momentum it is helpful to review some of the major ‘step changes’ along the way. These milestones may be typi ed by the evolution of complex integrated software and technological solutions such as MRP and manufacturing resource planning (MRP II, with a goal of ef cient resource usage) through to computer integrated manufacturing (CIM, with a goal of synergistic bene ts resulting in increased  exibility and a reduction in process and lead times), hospital information support systems (HISS, with a goal of better patient care through seamless information) and ERP (streamlined business processes for competitive advantage).

## The early days – MRP

Integration as a term within the IS literature has appeared with much frequency but with very little explanation. Early developments in integrated IS and IT began in the US. In 1972 the American Production and Inventory Control Society (APICS) viewed MRP as essential technology and began an ‘MRP crusade to spread its use as quickly as possible throughout the US. In the early 1980s it was superseded by closed loop MRP II and the evangelical message continued: ‘MRPII is not a new theory on industrial behaviour; it is all fact. MRPII is what is happening today in a large number of companies’ (Wight, 1981: p. xvii).

These systems had some initial success and began to be adopted by an increasingly large number of companies (Voss, 1986). Academic research found dif culty in keeping pace with this rate of change. However, as part of a major research project on MRP, Voss (1986) surveyed journal articles from 1972 to 1983 to identify those dealing with MRP implementation. Emerging from his study were some extremely interesting patterns. For example, within the  rst few years surveyed there was very little concern with implementation and this issue did not surface until approximately 10 year after the initial diffusion of MRP.

The nature of the concerns showed an evolutionary pattern; early implementation articles were fairly mechanistic and later came articles on people and organizational problems and links to business objec tives. Only at a much later stage, 15 years after thei initial diffusion, did rigorous academic articles appear and only then did the writers begin to admit to the possibility of signi cant problems and implementation failures.

## CIM

Further pressures for change began to manifest themselves in manufacturing during the late 1970s with the onset of global markets, worldwide competition and reduced foreign labour costs. Other factors included the lowering of economic trade barriers and new process and product innovations. If MRP/MRP II was a product of the 1970s then CIM typi ed the integration goal of the 1980s.

Many UK manufacturing industries were going into rapid decline due to high costs, lack of innovation, poor quality and lack of investment. CIM was seen as the saviour for a sector unable to compete in a global market.

During the early CIM initiatives in the mid-1980s, technical solutions were the major objective with organizational issues not being a worry until major problems came to light and started to be reported a decade later. Gunasekaran (1997) stated that:

. . . There are many reports that deal with the technological and operational aspects of CIM, but the issues of integration and adaptability as key objec tives in the design and implementation of CIM are not paid due attention . . . where the integration and adaptability issues are generally affected by factor such as business strategy, manufacturing strategy, availability of knowledge workers, software professionals, complexity of material  ow, information  ow pattern and decision making processes, product and process complexities, supplier purchasing activities, behavioural issues etc . . . furthermore, there is no systematic framework available for improving the integration and adaptability of CIM taking into account different managerial, technological and operational issues.

## UK public sector projects – NHS HISS

The UK public sector has not been untouched by the momentum towards, and also the problems of, IS integration. IS and IT was adopted in response to new government legislation and initiatives with few IS implementations more controversial than in the UK NHS. The NHS Information Management and Technology (IM & T) Strategy launched in December 1992 set a target of the year 2000 for all large acute hospitals to have ‘integrated systems’. Up until this point various UK Conservative Government policy makers had attempted to tackle what they saw as the intractable problem of the NHS in their own way. They were keen to push forward reforms but they lacked information on where hospitals spent their money (Cross, 1993).

The Resource Management Initiative (RMI) was the real start of the move towards integration but this was seen as only a partial solution. Central to the RMI process was the creation of a database which would serve hospital management and clinicians in the planning and audit of their work and provide budget holders with costing and control of activities. The approach was experimental in nature and a number of pilot sites were established whose main aims were to link existing departmental systems individually to the RM database (Packwood et al., 1991).

Even before the RMI had been completely evaluated and the dif culties identi ed, the NHS Management Executive (1992) published its IM & T strategy with a vision of creating a better NHS where staff use information for continuous service improvement and information is shared, handled and communicated securely across the service. The strategic vision for large acute hospitals in the UK as outlined by the NHS Management Executive was for them to develop fully integrated, patient-based IS and these were referred to as HISS.

The HISS programme formally began in December 1988 ‘with the aim of testing the feasibility of specifying, procuring and implementing systems that would cover every operational and information need of acute general hospitals’ (Cross, 1993: p. 39). Three pilot sites were chosen for the  rst phase of the programme. Despite the experimental nature of the programme and even before the  rst wave went live the ‘second wave was under way followed by a ‘third wave’ totalling over £106 million investment in integrated systems by 1995 (NAO, 1996).

The analogy to the much earlier MRP implementations is readily apparent. At the start of the HISS initiative the Information Management Group (IMG) of the NHS were very bullish about HISS and the total integrated solution ‘big bang’ approach. By

1991 of cial documentation was using the word ‘incremental’ to describe the favoured route to HISS. Rigorous academic research into HISS was not encouraged at this time and only selective studie were allowed (e.g. Bayswater Institute, 1994). Consequently, it has been dif cult for hospitals to understand what the problems have been with regards to HISS in any detail. Their only guide has been the increasing number of manuals and documents coming from the IMG in response to dif culties encountered on various projects.

It is dif cult to believe that within the NHS the negative publicity of the HISS experience has not dampened the enthusiasm for integrated systems. To the contrary, the UK NHS Management Executive published yet another IM & T strategy which promised total integration of all health information through the electronic patient record (EPR) and the electronic health record (EHR). Information for Health, the strategy in 1998 until 2005 (NHS Management Executive, 1998), insists that the EPR was essential. It was more likely to be ‘legible, accurate, safe, secure and available when required’ (p. 24). There was also a belief that it would ‘better integrate’ the most recent information about a patient’s care, e.g. from different ‘departmental’ clinical systems in a hospital. It would also aid research and audit. However, what it actually aims to achieve is very vague.

Even more adventurous than the EPR is the EHR which is used to describe the concept of a longitudinal record of patient’s health and health care (cradle to grave). The intention is to combine both the information about patient contacts with primary health care as well as subsets of information associated with outcomes of periodic care held in the EPRs. These are yet to be de ned and will need the cooperation of health care professionals and must also be developed within the context of the EPR.

The strategy states that achieving integrated care is a ‘key national policy objective’ which will allow professionals from a number of different organizations to provide this integrated care service for their individual patients. However, it does point out that ‘If integrated care is to be achieved then agreement on relevant terms, classi cations, groups etc. will be vital to allow accurate communication’. When considering the latest IM & T strategy (NHS Management Executive, 1998) it is inevitable to experience a sense of déjà vu and it must be concluded that there appears to have been little if any learning arising from the private manufacturing sector experience with integrated systems and a minimal dissemination of the issues surrounding HISS. If this had been the case then maybe the proposed roll-out of the EPR and EHR may not have been as ambitious.

## ERP

ERP systems are a natural evolution from the MRP suites of software developed in the 1970s and 1980s. ERP is a term which encompasses business applications such as purchasing, inventory, manufacturing  nance and personnel operations to name but a few. They are now mostly developed on client/server systems and designed to map on to a corporate information structure to provide business models which integrate business functions and processes across heterogeneous networks of multinational corporations. The adoption of mainly ERP client/server-based packaged solutions was rapid throughout the 1990s with a market estimated at \$17.5 billion in 1998, up from \$12 billion in 1997 and forecasted to reach \$20 billion in 1999 (Financial Times, 1999). The market for ERP solutions has become very sophisticated and can be split into four tiers related to company turnover and size. The large enterprises (\$2 billion plus) are dominated by Systems Applications and Production Data Processing (SAP), with the other tiers (\$250 million–\$2 billion, \$26 million–\$250 million and < \$25 million) being dominated by some of the larger ERP vendors such as Baan, Oracle, Peoplesoft, JD Edwards and JBA to name but a few of the now estimated 200 vendors in the market-place (August, 1999).

SAP R/3 is the latest in integration technology based on a client/server packaged application and the vendor SAP is the largest supplier of business application software in the world with sales of \$2.95 billion in 1998 (Financial Times, 1999). It may be viewed as the next generation of packaged integrated component software leading a market estimated at \$66 billion by 2003 (August, 1999). However, the new strategic emphasis is ‘integration’ –  rstly in terms of the internal functions of the enterprise and, secondly, throughout the ‘extended’ enterprise with Web-based linkages to suppliers and customers – ‘integrated supply chain and electronic commerce’. This has given rise to a new market for enterprise application integrators (EAI) whereby the disadvantages of the adoption of a single vendor ERP solution can be mitigated by integrating different ‘best of breed component’ software through Web-enabled technologies.

However, although many organizations have adopted this approach, it is still not clear to what extent the implementations have been a success. Most companies are concerned about the return on investment (ROI) from such a signi cant outlay of capital and many do not have the sophistication to measure this. In fact one report stated that: ‘far from being a new issue, trying to get one piece of software to talk to another is an age-old problem . . . the reality of ERP implementation has left thousands of IT departments struggling to link ERP, legacy, front of ce and supply chain applications together’ (August, 1999: p. 28). The same report (based on industry surveys) cited survey evidence that 64% of IT decision makers said integration accounts for more than 25% of ERP implementation costs with 20% claiming it was more than half and took longer than anticipated.

Many vendors are focusing on this problem by looking for more effective methods for implementation as well as developing more sophisticated business models and tools. In 1999 SAP introduced a process for business benchmarking allied to solution maps (August, 1999). Davenport (1998) critically evaluated the rapid growth of ERP systems by reference to the ‘growing number of horror stories’ about failed or out of control projects. The same author cited major corporations such as Foxmeyer Drug, Mobil Europe, Dell Computers and Dow Chemicals which have encountered major  nancial dif culties due to poor ERP selection and implementation. This is where such systems push organizations towards full integration and the adoption of generic business processes where thi con icts with the actual segregated and logical needs of the business. Davenport (1998) concluded that many chief executives continue to view integrated enterprise systems purely as a technical challenge while ignoring the complex business and organizational issues which manifest themselves during implementation.

Once again this is an area for urgent research but rigorous academic work is conspicuous by its absence although research highlighting critical success factor for ERP implementation is beginning to emerge (Holland et al., 1999a). The majority of publications mainly consist of reports from consultants working alongside ERP implementers – as evidenced by the plethora of White Papers available on the Internet (www.SAP.com) (Cameron et al., 1996) which provide a very positive and technical view of ERP implemen tation. No doubt, as in the case of MRP, CIM and HISS, it will be many years before the organizational issues are described along with detailed studies of the consequent implementation failures.

## The concept of the integrated enterprise – shifting paradigms

As the millennium approached many of the IT practitioner journals continued to promote the bene ts of integration as a generic concept whilst focusing on inte grating ITs as an enabler for enhanced competitive success (e.g. Computer Weekly and Information Week). This has received ‘popular’ backing in the technology and strategic IT management (Scott Morton, 1991), IT imperatives (Rockart, 1996), IT-enabled busines transformation (Venkatraman, 1991), IT and business process redesign (Davenport and Short, 1990) and, latterly, electronic commerce  elds (Currie, 1998).

Best selling texts create future visions for IT-enabled enterprises (Tapscott and Caston, 1993; Gates, 1995). This new era of IT push is encapsulated within the book Paradigm Shift (Tapscott and Caston, 1993), which not only has a chapter entitled ‘The Integrated Organization’, but which also contains visionary commentaries on the new reinvented business environment illustrated by the following extract:

. . . the new enterprise must also be integrated – with an overall strategy and architecture for the business, work organization, information, and technology . . . It is information technology, more than any other factor, that enables the new integrated organization . . . the isolated technology applications of an earlier time are no longer adequate.

(pp. 62–63).

Despite these claims, on more detailed inspection and analysis there is no serious empirical or qualitative evidence. Neither is there any indication of a methodologically rigorous research approach or critical re ection of the case studies (Tapscott and Caston, 1993) which are presented as proof of the authors thesis for the new ‘paradigm shift’ with its implicit goal of the ‘integrated enterprise’ leading to competitive and organizational success. Yet it is readily accepted as a new ‘mantra’ by the IT industry and IT practitioners whilst being treated ‘uncritically’ by many researchers and academics.

It is apparent from this brief potted history of major integrated systems initiatives that the complexity of what is being undertaken has not been fully appreciated by the implementers. There also appears to be very little transfer of learning about IT implementation within sectors and even less across them. The ‘wheel is reinvented’ every time new initiatives emerge and the historical context is completely ignored. This image of highly sophisticated, successful and competitive integrated organizations is supported by work purporting to demonstrate the ‘ideals’, goals and achievable bene ts arising out of systems integration.

## Image – the bene ts of integration

Understanding a concept such as CIM or HISS requires developing mental pictures, which appear tangible and realistic. The reality is that they are both extremely complex and need to be articulated in ways which can be understood. Unfortunately by simplifying the concept much detail is removed and it is perceived as a super cial abstraction. An example of this can be seen in the following extract:

Imagine a factory that machines parts, fabricates metals and assembles items with very few people operating the machines. Imagine an environment where parts are manufactured without the tradi tional long set-up times and long runs involving huge supplies of components. In this factory, parts are automatically counted, inspected and moved.

(Willis and Sullivan, 1984: p. 28).

This image was portrayed in a practitioner journal when the UK Department of Trade and Industry Advanced Manufacturing Technology initiative was just getting under way. Having painted a mental picture the authors continued by outlining the bene- ts of a successful implementation of CIM. In the 1990s and again driven by UK Government initiatives, the NHS also developed the HISS concept and was quick to claim bene ts for the implementation of integrated systems (NHS Information Management Group Management Executive, 1992). These anticipated and idealized bene ts for both CIM and HISS can be seen in Table 1.

Over time, this list for CIM was expanded to include terms such as competitive advantage (Kearney, 1989), ‘added value to customers through lower prices’ and ‘greater service responsiveness’ (Haas, 1987: p. 80).

Even the implementers of SAP R/3 have been able to come up with some repackaged bene ts which make implementation seem compelling: tight integration into a disciplined whole, a single view of the business sharing the same database, process orientation forcing attention on the end result, and rich functionality embodying best practice – there is little R/3 cannot do (Cameron et al., 1996).

The complexity of integration as a concept is problematic in itself without the pressures of obtaining these idealized goals and bene ts. These pressures contribute to a propagation of the ‘image’ of integrated IS and are in fact reinforced by expanding narratives of the idealized bene ts gained from such synergy.

In order to untangle reality from ideals in terms of the achievement of these stated goals and lists of bene- ts, it is important to examine previous de nitions of integration from different disciplines and adapted to different contexts. It may then be possible to gain a greater understanding as to why this is a complex and poorly understood area, why the images and ideals are so readily accepted and why the reality encapsulated after attempted systems integration may not match up to these aspirations.

## De ning integration – uncovering the theory

Complexity begins to manifest itself when the various de nitions and terminology related to the concept of integration are considered. The problem is that there is no one accepted de nition of integration and it is a word which has many different meanings to many different people (Zachman, 1987). A comprehensive review of the literature in the areas of IS, IT, operations and production management, technology, organizations and systems was undertaken to identify a range of possible de nitions and models of integration. These were then aggregated into four distinct areas comprising technical, systems, organizational and strategic domains. Each of these domains are described in turn to illustrate the possible views of the nature of integration where all views might be considered relevant to IS integration within any modern organizational context.

## Technical de nitions

Dictionaries of computing terms de ne ‘integration’ as ‘the ability of computer hardware or software systems to work with previously incompatible systems’ and early authors (Buckelew, 1985) subscribed to this view, referring speci cally to the dif culty of ‘physically’ integrating systems.

For technically able IT managers this de nition conjures up images of computers, networks, protocols, client/server architectures, hubs, routing, bandwidth, connectivity, etc. Their focus is on delivering the ‘right kit’ on time and within budget. Unlike 10 years ago the physical installation of integrated hardware and software is a much simpler process, usually facilitated by specialist communications and networking companies and services; IT managers can now be very proactive in building an IT network infrastructure.

Writers have also attempted to distinguish between the ‘integrated IS’ as a product as opposed to the actual process of ‘integrating IS’ which links separately developed subsystems and makes them appear as integrated (Buckelew, 1985). This gave rise to a de - nition of an integrated IS as:

one providing an end user, at any location, with the capability of assessing information stored at any level of the information processing system and enabling [one] . . . to interchange and share information with any other end user at any location within a single interconnected network and providing the potential for eventual customer access to data.

(Mudie and Schafer, 1985: p. 310).

Even though this de nition refers to the ‘end user and ‘customer’ it is still very much technically focused and, rather than reducing the complexity of the meaning of integration, it adds to the confusion. This concept of integration can be problematic as its meaning is purely technical and tends to ignore the organizational dif culties which arise when functiona boundaries are crossed (Lawrence and Lorsch, 1967; Kimberley and Miles, 1980; Boaden and Dale 1986; Tran eld and Smith, 1987). In order to incorporate a more complete set of factors affecting systems integration across technical and organizational boundaries many authors have attempted to take a more theoretical and systemic (holistic) view of integration.

## Systems de nitions

Systems integration was de ned by Shunk and Filley (1986) as ‘the optimisation over time, of all components comprising an organisational system that generates a measurable output. These include all  xed and tangible assets, people, money, information, technology and energy’(p. 66). This wide view of integration focusing on an organization or enterprise was adopted by many researchers and authors investigating the complex integration requirements for CIM systems.

The late 1980s produced a large output of work (O’Sullivan, 1990) investigating this phenomenon whilst searching for better theories of explaining the complex nature of coupling systems. This was typi ed by Mize (1987) who considered CIM integration in the context of a generic system where a system is described as a set of interrelated elements or subsystems. Two of these interrelationships are interfacing and integration.

(1) Interfacing is de ned as interacting or communicating with another element.

(2) Integrating is organizing various traits, relations, attitudes, behaviours, etc., into one harmonious personality.

Mize (1987) stated that many systems designers only go as far as interfacing and believe that the bene ts of integration will follow. Continuing on this theme Judson (1988) observed that duplicated information and decisions are constantly being re-created, reproduced, transmitted and stored in IS as interfaced systems try to behave in an integrated fashion.

Das (1992) extended Mize’s (1987) de nition to include four types of generic systems interrelationships see Figure 1.

Das (1992) argued that an interfaced system is one in which the elements have one- or two-way communication with other elements but make decisions for their own bene t. An integrated system is one in which the elements have two-way communication with other elements and make decisions for the collective bene t of the system. Das (1992) therefore proposed that interfaced systems have a parasitic relationship while integrated systems have a symbiotic relationship. A universal system is a group of elements with no individual decision-making capability and a central controller. Das (1992) believed that in searching for integration, some CIM designers have fallen into the universal trap and built systems with a single database and no distributed decision making.

Below (1987) stated that few de nitions of integration actually exist. He therefore de ned his own hierarchy of integration as a means of understanding and designing CIM systems. Firstly, he de ned inte gration: ‘integration is a result or an effect of correct commonality’ (Below 1987: p. 17). This de nition is deemed to be applicable to all levels of every organi zation resulting in coherency, ef ciency and increased productivity. Below (1987) differentiated integration from interfacing by focusing on their secondary characteristics:

integrated systems cannot be taken apart without destroying it . . . whereas an interfaced system consists of parts which are replaceable and which are clearly individual . . . integration and interfacing solutions are equally valid solutions to manufacturing and business problems . . . the correct approach is a function of our desired goals, the requirements of the system and the effects which the secondary characteristics have upon the other aspects of the organisation.

From these de nitions comes an argument that integration is mostly about data and communications whereby seven layers of integration can be tentatively identi ed as: physical, data, schedule, functions, attitudes, principles and purpose (see Figure 2). These latter layers necessitate closer examination of the organizational aspects and de nitions of integration.

## Organizational de nitions

Even within the CIM  eld, which has been widely researched, the term integration is, however, probably the most dif cult to de ne and yet has been widely used, e.g. the Department of Trade and Industry’s initiative of the late 1980s – ‘Towards Integration’. The term, when used in the context of CIM, is generally assumed to mean technical integration, i.e. the linking of different types of technology and systems together to comprise the ‘factory of the future’. Voss (1989) argued that this is a very narrow de nition which does not highlight the needs for other integration within organizations. Voss (1989) explored the de nition of integration and proposes  ve dimensions: (1) strategy integration, (2) material  ow integration, (3) technical integration, (4) information integration, (5) organizational integration.

This expansion of the de nition is intended to illustrate that integration is a highly complex process with a number of facets. In theory CIM addresses the middle three dimensions directly with strategy integration being a prerequisite for successful implementation. Organizational integration may or may not be a consequence of CIM (Boaden, 1991).

Although not disagreeing with Voss (1989), earlier work by Boaden and Dale (1986) emphasized the information integration dimension when exploring the key to a successful implementation of CIM. They believed that the ‘simplify, integrate, automate’ approach proposed by a number of authors (Ingersoll Engineers, 1985) ensures that technology and, therefore, technica integration is only applied where appropriate.

The term integration continued to lack de nition and be widely misused even into the 1990s as the following from the Department of Trade and Industry illustrates:

‘integration . . . is about how the whole business is conceived and put together, to achieve the business mission and objectives in a harmonious way. It is integration of the organisation that will achieve  exibility, low inertia, and speed . . . integrated information supports the integrated organization by providing a shared source of accurate, timely data as the basis for communication and decisions.

(Boaden 1991: 68).

This description leaves the reader with many unanswered questions and does not give any guidance on how integration might be achieved. However, O’Sullivan (1992) was more explicit about what integration entails and how it might be achieved. He proposed that integration comprises social as well as technical elements. Social integration involves the integration of people, their ideas, and decision-making processes (see Figure 3). Technical integration is more concerned with the integration of technical subsystems including equipment, techniques and procedures.

O’ Sullivan (1992) placed much more emphasis on the organizational issues of integration. Management integration involves the integration of managers, their decisions and their individual functional strategies in order to adhere to the business strategy and this can be seen to resemble Voss (1989) ‘strategy integration’. System designer integration recognizes that systems are designed by many functional groups which need to be integrated into a project group in order to facilitate the development process. User integration can be loosely aligned with Voss (1989) ‘organization integration’. Here integration involves the integration of users within their technical as well as social environment. O’Sullivan (1992) believed the primary issues here are ensuring that new systems support or augment the role of users to make their tasks more enriching and productive.

## Strategic de nitions

A number of authors believe that ‘integration’ is a strategic issue and, consequently, any de nition should have a strategic component.

Das (1992) has also attempted a classi cation of types of integration which contains a strategic element: (1) resource-oriented integration and (2) activity oriented integration.

Resource-oriented integration is essentially concerned with physical entities and includes computer and network integration, equipment integration, facilities integration and materials integration. Activity oriented integration is more concerned with the processes which occur in a business and includes process integration, information integration, decision tool integration, control integration and product integration. This classi cation differs from O’Sullivan’s (1990) by being less explicit about the organizational issues.

Platts (1995), on the other hand, was critical of approaches in the literature which attempt to break down ‘integration’ into its different types – what he scathingly referred to as ‘a sort of disintegration of integration’ (p. 18). He believed that it has its place among academics to aid understanding but is not at all useful in helping the practising manager implement an integrated manufacturing system. Platts (1995) proposed a strategic framework which encompasses two basic aspects of integration.

(1) External integration: matching manufacturing objectives and policies to the needs of the market and the competitive needs of the company, taking account of any environmental constraints.

(2) Internal integration: developing a set of manufacturing practices which are consistent and mutually supportive and which support the manufacturing objectives.

Like Voss (1989), Platts (1995) believed that manufacturing integration should be viewed from a strategic perspective and several companies have bene ted by using his framework. It is not apparent from this work whether the social integration dimension is explicit though it appears to be touched upon under ‘human resource’ issues.

The systems, organizational and strategic de nitions of integration appear to have emerged in response to the problems associated with implementing a purely technical solution. These de nitions are intended to address issues such as communications between people at both an organizational and individual level, the alignment of business goals, greater communication effectiveness and the sharing of information for a common purpose.

The dif culty for practitioners involved in an integrated systems implementation is which de nition conveys the best understanding of what is involved. Very often the technical de nition is easiest to grasp and does not appear to be too dif cult. Management want simple and quick solutions within budget. Organizational integration is a much more complex process as every organization is different and will not have a generic set of problems. However, this aspect of the integration process has not deterred many organizations from attempting to implement an integrated system, as they believe the bene ts will be worth it in the end.

The complex task of integrating a fairly typical IS application within a common organizational context can only be appreciated by considering the detail of its implementation. In the next section two case studies are presented which highlight many of the problematical issues arising from attempting to rationalize systems to enhance communications effectiveness and align technical and operational systems with new changes in strategic direction.

## Case studies

The following cases were based on primary research carried out in two organizations in the UK public and private sectors. The  rst involves a 6 month action research project at a hospital, North Trust. Data were collected by semi-structured interviews, participant observation, focus groups and diary entries with documentary evidence from the project. This was part of a much larger project but the case presented here illustrates the real problems as opposed to the idealized bene ts from a proposed integration of a payroll and personnel system within a multisite operation.

The second case was part of a larger project examining the implementation of ERP systems within the UK private manufacturing sector (Solunga, 1999). The data was collected by a series of semi-structured interviews supplemented by documentary evidence both from the company Auto-SupplyCo and the ERP vendor ERPCo.

## North Trust

North Trust is situated in the north of England and serves a mainly inner-city population. The hospital itself was  rst built in the 1930s and has grown to meet demand with an excess of 500 acute beds. It is very similar to many other trusts having experienced dif culties in implementing the NHS purchaser and provider quasi-market of the early 1990s and in keeping up with the increasing demands for information by successive cost-conscious governments. A particularly problematic area was that of payroll and personnel. The trust was unable to provide regular and reliable statistics on sickness, absence and staff turnover – essential under the new UK Labour Government. There were also internal management and control problems precipitating a decision to imple ment an integrated payroll and personnel system.

In April 1997 it was an open secret that a merger was being planned between North Trust and Community Trust which was based about 3 miles away. This trust had their own separate personnel department, but their payroll was run by North Trust. This complicated the scenario further.

Prior to the project, there were many perceived problems with the present systems within both the North Trust payroll and personnel departments. Payroll was run by a facilities management (FM) company whose service was becoming less and less reliable over time. Staff in North Trust were expected to process data in a particular way and then send it via a BT link to the FM company. The quality of the information received from the FM company was poor, reports were completely in exible and costs escalated.

The North Trust personnel department were also experiencing dif culties in relation to the quality of their data and the inability of the ‘old’ system to produce reports relating to absenteeism, training and the movement of medical personnel. Management throughout the hospital had no con dence in the system and the credibility of the personnel department was low. Both the payroll and personnel departments had different databases of staff using different staff numbers. There was no link between them.

Management at board level had decided that it could improve the situation by purchasing new systems or an integrated payroll/personnel system. It was essential that the executive managers had the information they needed in relation to staf ng resources. In North Trust the payroll was run by the  nance department (they also ran Community Trust’s payroll) and had a ‘closed’ gatekeeping culture whereas personnel had open access, where doors were ajar and staff were very ‘laid back’.

Although staff from both departments vaguely knew each other they did not work closely together. The Community Trust management block was located 3 miles away in what had been an old house in large landscaped grounds. Their personnel department was organized on a more formal basis than in North Trust but was still fairly open. The staff normally had very little contact with North Trust during the course of their day to day business and may have met them at personnel events held in the area.

The initial decision to procure a new integrated payroll/personnel system was taken by the North Trust executive project board who set up a project team. The project team consisted of three staff from personnel and three from  nance within North Trust and was headed by Pete who originated from NHS supplies and had experience of procurement. Apart from Pete none of the team had ever been involved in a procurement and Pete had never run a project before. Pete’s main expertise was in drawing up speci cations on behalf of other people. The IT department was signi cant by their absence and a deliberate omission by the project team. In the early stages of the project the team met once a week to build up a rapport. The  nance staff only felt the meetings were useful if business was being attended to – ‘they had pressing work to do’. The personnel staff, on the other hand, described these early meetings as team building which was open and honest.

Eventually they went through a short listing process for the new integrated system – documents were divided up within the team according to the expertise of the individuals and they came up with a short list. They all agreed they were looking for an integrated system – however, if there was not one which was good enough, then they would purchase two individual ones; payroll was seen as vital.

Companies were invited to come to North Trust and give a demonstration of their systems. The decision based on the outcome of the demonstrations and the tender documents was to go with a product called Integrated PP. It was not the best system from the personnel perspective but they felt as an integrated system it was the best – ‘there had to be compromise (senior personnel of cer) – and payroll liked it. The project team also visited other NHS sites to see Integrated PP systems working.

The team found their visits to the demonstration sites very interesting and it gave them insight into their internal problems. For example, one site had bought the system and had only managed to get it to work as well as the old one after 2 years. In this instance it was felt that the Integrated PP system was not the problem itself but the way it had been implemented. A hospital in the South West had implemented payroll before personnel – with the dif culty of personnel trying to catch up – this was mainly due to political problems.

With this new insight the team began to realize that they had not really addressed some of the possible business and organizational problems which might occur once they started to embark on the installation of the new system. Fortunately, they had not signed any contracts as the project took a new turn. On returning from the last Integrated PP site visit they were informed that the integrated payroll/personnel system must incorporate Community Trust as the merger had been formally accepted. The implication for the project was that they might have to go through the tender process again.

## The North Trust and Community Trust merger

The  rst meeting of the newly constituted project team was described as ‘tense’ (senior personnel of cer). Staff were eyeing one another suspiciously; two personne departments in one Trust. Were there going to be job losses? Who would be doing what when the new system arrived? The documents for the procurement would need to be amended to re ect Community Trust requirements too. What were their requirements? Preliminary discussions indicated that some aspects of training and recording of training appeared to be done differently at Community Trust. The senior personnel of cer believed that they had come with the attitude ‘just because there is going to be a merger North Trust will not get it all their own way’.

Community Trust had also been looking for a new personnel system and had wanted to  nish the procurement process but the decision was taken out of their hands; the IT manager at Community Trust had dominated proceedings there. Pete from North Trust did not want any IT presence at the project team meetings, particularly ‘anoraks’. This impasse needed to be addressed if the procurement of the integrated system was to continue. It was essential that staff started communicating with one another and that important organizational issues were addressed. Although North Trust had gone through a tender and selection process they had never actually considered how the new system might operate once installed. There were also aspects of the business that the Integrated PP system did not actually deliver; how were they going to progress that?

It was at this point that one of the authors was invited into the trust in order to try and facilitate the discussions which needed to take place regarding the implementation. Pete wanted everyone in the team involved in the procurement; it was their system, not his or the IT department’s. They needed to understand what integration meant to them and its implications for their respective departments. Many unforeseen problems now surfaced including the intransigence of the software vendors over new requirements: ‘It is up to you how the system runs and who does what . . . that is internal operations’. The vendors washed their hands of the whole thing. The integration aspect of the system gradually became more manifest and the accounting departmental staff were not happy. ‘The atmosphere in the room could have been cut with a knife’.

There were many issues relating to the new system which had yet to be discussed; how the system would be administered, how the data would get into the new system, who would be in charge overall, data quality, etc. Finally, over a period of 4 weeks, the facilitator assisted the team (primarily driving discussions through the use of process modelling tools) in outlining in detail how staff from all the participating departments would be reorganized and how the processing would take place. The discussions became very heated as staff began to realize the implications of integration. For example, data entry would begin when new staff were employed, a job currently done by personnel. Could personnel staff be trusted with such a task as payroll relied heavily on accurate data? The deputy director of  nance was not at all in favour of this arrangement and tried to dominate the proceedings.

Further meetings had to be arranged to discuss other political issues which had arisen from the diagramming exercise including the need for new sickness and absence reporting from within the hospital and the need for time sheets. One issue then became apparent as the project proceeded. Should the payroll and personnel departments be merged?

At the  nal 2–day meeting with the vendors of Integrated PP prior to signing the procurement contract, the process modelling diagrams were presented and the project team talked through with the vendors how they would like the system to operate. A number of the organizational issues which had been raised during the modelling, had yet to be successfully addressed. However, all were aware of what they were and negotiations continued. In order to take the project team’s work into consideration the models of the ‘to-be’ system were incorporated into the procurement. The contract was signed in July 1997 with instal lation and training commencing in September 1997. Personnel went live in January 1998 and payroll in April 1998. After this, dif culties remained to be ironed out but the staff continued to work on it.

## Auto-SupplyCo

Auto-SupplyCo are a small to medium-sized company with a £20 million turnover supplying parts and components to the automotive manufacturing industry. The production facility is based in the UK, employs 450 people, produces more than 400 products and is a  rst-tier supplier to a large foreign car manufacturer, VehicleCo. Besides its main customer they supply other British car manufacturers where they are designated as a second-tier supplier. Auto-SupplyCo are contractually bound to supply products to the large foreign manufacturer on a synchronous basis. From the customers’ point of view this means delivering the right product directly to the production line when required in the correct sequence. VehicleCo operates a just in time (JIT) system whereby a signal is sent to the parts supplier via Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) to indicate their purchase order/requirement for a synchronous supply. Consequently, from a supplier’s point of view it is imperative to have comparable EDI systems with all of their customers in the business.

Auto-SupplyCo’s legacy systems comprised a Novel network of PCs based on bespoke database software written in-house. This was used to run its key oper ations including the functions of stock control, master production schedule, MRP, purchasing, goods received and sales orders. Its main function at the time was nothing more than recording movements in and out of stock and maintaining stock balances. With time, the organization moved on to EDI as a method of transacting business with its customers. As the busi ness developed the limitations of the database software became increasingly obvious. It could not generate real-time information as it was unable to reconcile Auto-SupplyCo’s orders with current and planned schedules. This situation resulted in production plan ning being performed manually.

In the  nance department, on the other hand, the business was run using another type of system, an accounts suite of software. It was a proprietary system and not millennium compliant. The two information systems were not compatible and there was no way of linking them ef ciently and effectively. Since there was no transfer of information between the two systems there was duplication of data input, a situation which did not lend itself to ef cient use of resources.

Auto-SupplyCo realized that they were struggling to keep pace with market changes and that a cause of the problem was the limitations and constraints imposed by the current IS. The distinct lack of internal integration of information from their perspective meant that the management of internal linkages between business functions was weak. In this type of dynamic industry, which is information intensive, the management of available information is of key strategic significance. Therefore, Auto-SupplyCo realized that being unable to compete due to the limitations of its IS would have dire consequences for its business.

## The new system

As a result of these issues authorization was given to search for a replacement for the existing systems in August 1997. A project team comprising an information technologist,  nance controller, production controller (logistics and scheduling), production manager, two senior managers and a  nance director was set up. The  nance director led the team.

Auto-SupplyCo found itself in the position of having to make a decision with regard to its IS strategy. Should it choose a package solution or opt again for bespoke software? The company had limited IT resources in terms of skilled IT staff and, therefore, contracted a management consultancy for advice. Having investigated all the options the decision was made to pursue a packaged application/solution strategy. This then required the company to produce a detailed speci cation of its functional requirements. Once again they turned to the management consultancy  rm to undertake this. A comprehensive list of requirements was prepared and the system had to  t with Auto-SupplyCo’s business process model as they did not want to reengineer the business as well as undertaking a major IS implementation.

After a thorough selection process facilitated by the management consultancy, including software demonstrations on-site, one supplier, was selected, ERPCo. They offered the most attractive cost with local service support (however, none of the short-listed vendors, including ERPCo, could match the company’s required business model).

ERPCo offered a completely ‘integrated information services infrastructure for organizations trading in national and global marketplaces’ (ERPCo website). Auto-SupplyCo elected to procure the  nancial and manufacturing solution modules as they were relevant to the needs of the business. In addition, bespoke solutions were found to look after integration with other areas such as EDI and quality assurance (ERPCo’s modules in these areas did not meet the company’ requirements and business model).

Prior to implementation changes took place in the project team as they lost the production manager and some people gained promotion. Consequently, the team recruited new representatives from each department and nominated ‘super-users’ to train other members of their departments. Each of the departmental representatives was required to prepare a document stating how thei section worked. They were then required to work along side consultants from ERPCo and the management consultants within the implementation project team. From the outset of the project it was made clear that the project was not IT but business led. In addition, it was decided that a very tight  t between the current business practice and the software would be required as opposed to the business  tting the software. At no time were plan put in place for organizational or structural changes.

ERPCo initially used an Integration De nition (IDEF)-based automated tool for business process capture and analysis. The management consultants working alongside the project team performed the modelling with Auto-SupplyCo personnel checking and approving the models.

Unfortunately, an area which had been overlooked was production and warehousing. In these areas the problems with the old systems did not manifest themselves in the daily operations of the factory, as IT was not an essential tool for carrying out the work. With the introduction of the new integrated system this had changed – IT was now essential. Operational routines within the production and warehousing zones required considerable modi cation.

Workers in these areas now had to accept terminals on the shop  oor into which key performance information was input. The manner of working became even more painful when the organization realized that, due to the problems with the graphical user environment, the new system would be ‘a character-based green screen’. This brought up some resistance, and as there was not a production manager in place at the time, the departments had the change imposed upon them.

Auto-SupplyCo elected to adopt a ‘big bang’ implementation as opposed to a parallel implementation. The system began to go live in April 1988 with the roll out of the new modules still ongoing in July 1999.

The problems with the Graphical User Interface (GUI) meant that the company was unable to install the intended client/server system and was forced to install an IBM AS400 with 45 dumb terminals installed on the shop  oor and in the warehouse. Networked PCs running New Technology (NT) linked to the AS400 by client software were used in the of ces. In addition, the old networked (Novel NetWare) PCs were still in operation.

This con guration was fraught with dif culties. The three communications networks were supported by an under-resourced IT department and, rather than integrated as originally intended, the system appears to be interfaced.

## Discussion

## The image of systems integration

## The English NHS and North Trust

At a national level the NHS can be seen to have had a technical focus when describing integration. In 1992 the IM and T strategy explained that an HISS would be expected to include: an integrated patient-based IT environment, a data communications network and an order communications system. It then went on to describe different levels of integration which basically means linking various components together in a particular order – a simple technical and architectural image as described in Figure 4.

This image has been reinforced by high pro le events such as the annual British Journal of Healthcare Computing Conference and Vendor Exhibition, providing biased accounts of the UK NHS HISS pilot sites (Thomas et al., 1995). The participants in these projects were dissuaded from putting into the public domain anything controversial which might dispel the integration myth. Even now the new IM and T strategy (NHS Management Executive, 1998) has an implicit underpinning of integration throughout England and Wales as it drives the new concept of the EHR and EPR for the millennium.

## Auto-SupplyCo

Through the use of ERP, manufacturing companies are increasingly buying into technical packaged solutions which  t a preconceived notion of ‘best busines practice’ providing opportunities for competitive advantage. In the case of ERPCo, the ERP packaged solution was based on an industry supply chain reference model. The decision was also fuelled initially by the need to be Y2K millennium compliant. ERP vendors globally have experienced a massive growth in sales due to these problems and have simultaneously promoted the large potential business performance improvements enabled by common integrated software solutions. This has also been reinforced by the industry movement towards globalization (with standardization of the company reporting across worldwide operations) the adoption of standardized business models and the quest for sustainable competitive advantage (Holland et al., 1999b). Thus, the image perceived by Auto-SupplyCo and their consultant advisors was one of massive potential gains in productivity and customer service delivery together with straightforward integration and implementation of the ERP system (even to the extent that a ‘one-stop’ package solution would solve any integration problems).

Due to limited IT resources, Auto-SupplyCo turned to a large international management consultancy to provide advice and an answer to their problems with regard to maintaining a competitive edge. The consultancy proffered the image of a totally integrated solution through ERP even though it might alter the existing business model of the company. In fact the business and its major customer had a relationship which could not be modelled on a best practice business model. Auto-SupplyCo relied heavily on its own customized and  exible production and supply chain operations for its competitive advantage. A contradiction occurred as AutoSupply Co wanted to retain their principal business models and avoid re-engineering the business. The situation became incongruous when the preferred supplier, ERPCo, could not guarantee to deliver a signi cantly customized set of business processes. One of their principle selling points was the use of an industry standard reference business model. These problems manifested themselves when warehousing and production had to be signi cantly reengineered as certain operational issues were not identi ed at early stages of the procurement and implementation life cycle. Customer service was degraded accordingly to the point of risking the loss of their  rst-tier supplier status.

## The absence of models of integration

## North Trust

Probably the greatest dif culty facing the North Trust planning team at the outset of the project was the lack of a model of the proposed integration. They knew the hardware and software to provide a technical solution existed but they had no ability to conceptualize the complexity of what was involved and the associated issues. It was only after visiting other NHS sites and talking to other staff who had attempted to implement the proposed software package that the realities began to manifest themselves to the potential users – exposing political problems and organizational issues.

If they were to take the integration further they needed some way of exploring how the system would impact on them and their role within the organization. This needed to be at the operational level, which could be understood by everyone concerned. Hence, one of the authors adopted a diagramming and process modelling approach as an attempt to introduce a means of understanding the complex web of interactions and implications and also as a means of enabling discourse of the concept, theory and process of integration.

The process models developed were not intended to solve problems but to make clear the dif culties which had to be overcome if the system was to be operated in the way it was intended. Other trusts using the software had failed to implement it as an integrated system so some of the problems were generic and not just restricted to local politics.

## Auto-SupplyCo

The project team at Auto-SupplyCo found themselves in a similar position to North Trust in that they too had no internal business reference models to relate to at the beginning of their project. There was very little understanding of how the implementation would affect the organization nor suf cient thought given to the changes which would need to be made in order to accommodate the new system. By inviting the external consultants to act in the role of expert modellers in developing the business models they lost the opportunity for ownership of the project and for learning about their own operations and potential implementation issues.

The modelling carried out by ERPCo was intended to con gure their ERP modules to Auto-SupplyCo’s business processes in a technically ef cient manner. Any organizational and cultural issues were subjugated in the process. Again, this shows that the dominant theme was technical integration, interfacing the software modules of the new ERP system with legacy processes within the company particularly with regard to EDI and any existing customer supplier links. Some of the most important factors concerned interna organizational integration issues (production and warehousing) as well as external strategic integration with their main customer. Process and data modelling did not incorporate these added dimensions with problems only becoming apparent at the time of the actual implementation of the new system.

## The reality of integration

## North Trust

The reality for North Trust and Community Trust was that implementing an integrated payroll/personnel system was a highly complex and organizationally dif - cult project. Cultures within individual departments were at odds with the principle of integration. The self-perception of the  nance department as being superior led to their derision of the ability of the personnel department to quality control data entered onto the system.

The new working practices suggested were problematic with personnel and with the unions. Much negotiation took place prior to the contract being signed. However, an agreement was eventually arrived at which would not compromise the integration. This may not have been the case if the issues had not been discovered until the system was due to go live.

The process modelling did raise the issue as to whether the departments should be merged and whether there was a need for ‘personnel clerks’ and ‘ nance clerks’ and not just ‘data entry clerks’. Thi

issue also brought into focus the problem of who had overall control of the new system. These dif culties had to be faced and were not solved by a computer. The hospital took steps to try to address the problems by creating ‘new’ posts and asking staff to apply for them. This can often lead to ‘bad feelings’ and local unappointed staff ‘taking their bat home’. ## Auto-SupplyCo

The reality for Auto-SupplyCo too was that integration is highly complex but in the private sector getting it wrong can jeopardize competitive position. Even though they took an industry standard approach to planning and implementation consisting of a high level multidisciplinary team which was business and top management led, the use of state-of-the-art process modelling tools and techniques and advice and project management from a top-level consultancy  rm, the controlling emphasis was on a technical con guration as opposed to identifying the strategic and organizational dimensions. Hence, interfacing solutions had to be adopted for the external supply chain. In addition, organizational factors arising from imposing standard business models on existing operations in reality decreased operating  exibility as opposed to providing any competitive bene ts.

## Conclusions

There is much evidence now that many organizations are failing to gain the strategic bene ts of integrated systems and even fall short of making operational improvements (Zammuto and O’Connor, 1992; Coopers & Lybrand, 1994a; Davenport, 1998; Holland et al., 1999b). Many reasons for failure have been identi ed, focusing largely on the implementation process and a range of managerial and organizational issues (Voss, 1988). Rarely are these failures technical in nature (Voss, 1992) but arise from problems with purchasing and implementation and also ‘. . . pre-installation and post-implementation factors (Voss, 1986: p. 213).

Savage (1988) conducted a survey among 1200 senior executives in order to de ne their major issues as relating to CIM implementation. The  ndings stated the most important issues (in rank order were) as a lack of understanding and knowledge of CIM, lack of man agement support and commitment, resistance to change, cost justi cation, control, job security, retrain ing and education, and organizational restructuring. These  ndings may be compared to more recent research by Hassard and Forrester (1997) who identi- ed four critical recommendations for CIM implementation: (1) identify the role of CIM in the context of business objectives, (2) derive appropriate measures of performance for the project, (3) exercise effective project management throughout development and (4) evaluate system operation in an appropriate manner. This research, now reinforced by more recent case studies on critical success factors for ERP implementation (Holland et al., 1999a) spanning over a decade, identi- es the key issues for integration as being the appraisa of organizational structures and process in order to eliminate departmental barriers whilst engaging senior management in both the design and implementation process, not-withstanding an emphasis on learning from post-implementation review.

These studies provide an increasing awareness and knowledge of the critical issues but often provide few pointers in terms of the management process for dealing with them effectively. Business alignment, senior management education and commitment, political and power effects, organization structure, process and change are repeatedly identi ed as important factors in complex integrated IS implementation projects but scant attention is given to the issue of understanding the phenomenon of integration itself. It is dif cult if not impossible to communicate this to senior management in order for the full implications of the business vision or image of an integrated enterprise to be known. This lack of a learning culture is compounded by the fact that many post-implementation reviews fail to describe in any real detail how the system was implemented and the dif culties encountered (Bayswater Institute, 1994; Coopers & Lybrand, 1994a, b).

The dif culty, which arises for all implementers of integrated solutions, is to try and discover which of the generic issues related to the four dimensions of integration (technical, systems, organizational and strategic) are pertinent to their particular organization. This can only be done through a process of open communications, discussion and learning, which provides a means of understanding and contextualizing an integrated system and allows the issues to be discussed and explored by the people who will be affected by the new system.

The reality of systems integration is far removed from the idealized concepts expounded by hardware and software vendors and integration consultants. The models and de nitions that they use are too simplistic and fail to address the potential issues each organization will face when implementing their own integrated solution. Realizing the bene ts of integration may be too painful for the organization as a whole and this can lead to compromise, partial use of the system or even complete abandonment.

## Biographical notes

Teresa Waring is a senior lecturer at the Newcastle Business School, University of Northumbria. She hold BSc and MSc degrees from Durham and Sunderland Universities respectively and is currently completing a PhD at Northumbria University. With over 15 years extensive teaching and research experience in the area of IS she is currently the programme director of both full and part time Masters courses in IS management and also business IT. Her research and consul tancy experience includes both the private and public sectors with the main focus on the UK NHS and eman cipatory practices for effective procurement of hospital IS.

Dr David Wainwright holds the post of lecturer in IS at Durham University Business School. He gained a BSc from Goldmiths College, University of London then a postgraduate diploma and a PhD from Northumbria University. Having started his career as a geologist working in the oil industry his academic interest turned to the design and development of IS. He undertook research into manufacturing IS development prior to lecturing in IS and becoming a principal lecturer and head of the IS division at the Newcastle Business School. With 15 years experience of researching, teaching and consulting in IS, his current focus is the process of creating IS strategy in both the UK private and public sectors.
