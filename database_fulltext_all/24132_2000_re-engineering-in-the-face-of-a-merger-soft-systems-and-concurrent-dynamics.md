# Re-engineering in the face of a merger: soft systems and concurrent dynamics

STEPHEN F. KING

Leeds University Business School, ESS Building, Leeds LS2 9JT, UK

PHIL AISTHORPE

Stratica Limited, 175 Woodhouse Lane, Leeds LS2 9HE, UK

Business process re-engineering (BPR) was presented as the key to successful organizational transformation in the early 1990s. In this paper we examine a BPR initiative at a medium-sized UK building society in order to explore whether BPR succeeded or failed and to place BPR within the wider context of an organization facing a merger. The study describes the development of a novel BPR methodology which combines both hard and soft modelling approaches and reveals a degree of success in terms of process modelling and gaining consensus on process content and faults. However, two more far-reaching initiatives served to drain away support from the BPR effort: a parallel organizational analysis undertaken by an external consultancy and the hidden (although rumoured) merger talks with a larger partner. Therefore it is inappropriate to view BPR as an isolated, strategic initiative when, in practice, it may be one of several competing change activ ities vying for support within a changing organizational context. The paper concludes by presenting a model of concurrent dynamics which helps to explain why BPR lost momentum.

## Introduction

Whilst de nitions of business process re-engineering (BPR) vary, particularly in terms of the scope of change (e.g. intrafunctional, interfunctional and interorganizational) (Fielder et al., 1994), there appears to be a consensus that BPR is radical change aimed at achieving dramatic improvements in performance and that information technology (IT) is often a key enabler (Hammer, 1990; Davenport, 1993; Earl, 1994; Willcocks and Smith, 1995). The focus on process, such as order ful lment or product development, may result in an attempt to de ne the activities involved in process execution systematically using diagrammatic modelling techniques such as process mapping or value chain analysis. Given the high failure rate of information systems (IS) projects in general (Lyytinen and Hirschheim, 1987; Sauer, 1993) and the dif culty of implementing radical change (Kotter, 1995), it is perhaps not surprising that many BPR initiatives fail to achieve dramatic improvements in performance (Currie and Willcocks, 1996). Indeed, there is a school of thought which views Hammer’s (1990) original evangelical de nition of BPR as so riddled with contradictions as to be largely irrelevant in practice (Grint and Willcocks, 1995; Willmott, 1995). However, empirical studies of BPR have shown that to label BPR a ‘success’ or ‘failure’ is too simplistic (Caron et al., 1994; Willcocks and Smith, 1995; Currie and Willcocks, 1996). Whilst the dramatic improve ments in performance claimed by Hammer (1990) and reported by the CIGNA Corporation (Caron et al., 1994) were contradicted by Currie and Willcocks’ (1996) extensive UK survey, both groups of researchers agree that BPR can provide a valuable lever for change but must be managed carefully. In particular, it is often hard to persuade a successfu organization to change radically no matter how hostile the environment is becoming.

This paper presents a further case study of BPR and makes two important contributions to knowledge of it. The  rst is to describe how a fusion of hard systems analysis and soft systems methodology (SSM) enabled the core processes of a large  nancial institution to be analysed, revealing signi cant  aws in current operations. The second is to show why, despite the importance of these  ndings, the recommendations of the BPR team were not subsequently implemented. In addressing this second issue we use a new model of concurrent dynamics which has general applicability beyond BPR and IS work. The study therefore explains why BPR lost momentum and seeks to position BPR initiatives within a wider contextual framework which acknowledges that BPR, far from being the major driver for change, may instead be but one of several parallel initiatives competing for attention and resources, including far more radical activities such as a potential merger.

## Research approach

An opportunity to study BPR in detail arose when the former employer of the second author commenced a BPR programme in 1994. The company, a mediumsized UK building society, the City, had its head of ce in the north of England with branches throughout the country (the names of the organization and other entities described in this paper have been changed to provide anonymity). The aim of the research was to develop and apply a new BPR methodology in the City. The effectiveness of the methodology was to be evaluated. The research approach adopted was therefore action research (Galliers, 1991; Wood-Harper, 1992), with the second author using his position as leader of the BPR analysis team to provide resources and opportunities for the work to take place within the organization. Evidence was collected in the form of (1) a project diary in which the second author noted his views on events as they proceeded, (2) various documents from the BPR project, in particular the SSMrich pictures and process models that arose from the process analysis workshops, (3) public domain information about the merger activities of the society during the period in question and,  nally, (4) a recent structured interview with the BPR programme manager who has remained with the merged organization and has therefore provided a long-term view of both preand post-merger events. The study thus addresses two research questions.

(1) How effective was the BPR methodology developed at the City?

(2) Why did BPR lose momentum at the City?

## BPR at the City

## Introduction

In this section we describe the key stages of BPR at the City. BPR started ambitiously as a full review of the two major business processes of the building society: savings and loans. This is called ‘project 1’. The results of project 1 revealed signi cant  aws in these processes. However, due to changes in the organization around this time, BPR was descoped and focused on the ‘Quick Wins’ project (project 2). This project did not progress beyond the analysis stage due to further developments in the merger talks between the City and a larger building society. Project 2 was superseded by the Branch Automation project (project 3) which did result in the implementation of signi - cant new technology on a trial basis immediately before the merger. The interplay between the three BPRrelated projects and the merger talks is described in this section and is analysed in the Discussion section at the end of the paper.

## Initiation

The core business of the City is the provision of savings and loans based on the UK housing market. At the start of the BPR programme in early 1994, the City, along with every other building society, faced a dif - cult business environment. The housing market had remained  at for a number of years due mainly to recession in the UK economy accompanied by high interest rates and was not expected to recover in the near future. Provisions for bad debt, though improving, had taken their toll. Industry experts were predicting a general shakeout in the building society sector resulting in no more than 20 survivors in 10 years time. Senior managers at the City therefore had little con dence in its organic growth strategy and believed the only realistic long-term option was to seek a merger with another major  nancial services operator.

Rumours were rife throughout 1994 with many names being linked with the City at one time or another. A continued impressive  nancial performance was essential, both to maintain attractiveness to a potential merger partner and also maintain high credit ratings on the wholesale money markets. In the absence of any real growth in income, therefore, the organization turned to cutting operating costs and BPR was seen as an important element of an effective costcutting strategy.

The City was a long-established organization and was structured hierarchically, with separate divisions for marketing, human resources, IS, etc. The onl point at which the divisions formally connected was in the management committee at the top of the organization (Figure 1). In the face of the dif cult market conditions, the management committee launched a number of strategic projects which aimed to improve performance via a combination of organic growth and cost cutting. The BPR programme was one of these initiatives and was led by a senior manager from the IS division and sponsored by the marketing director, one of the three executive directors who were jointly running the society following the departure of the chief executive of cer in 1993. However, the strategic projects were not the only major change initiatives going on in 1994. The board had also appointed an external consultancy, KeyConsult, to review their costs, income and structure in order to de ne the markets in which the City would prosper and identify suitable merger partners.

Further IS in uence in the BPR programme was provided by the process analysis team which was seconded from the IS planning group within the IS division. The team had recently developed competence in process modelling through the application of the IDEF-structured methodology to a number of business areas including the IS development process and the treasury function. However, the team was schooled in the use of hard modelling techniques and had no skills in the exploration of the softer issues central to the successful management of change. To counteract this, the process analysis team leader, the second author, decided to apply IDEF analysis within a framework based on the SSM (Checkland, 1981).

## Project 1: BPR feasibility study of savings and loans

## Process models and project scope

Project 1 set about analysing the core processes of the City (savings and loans) with the intention of instigating radical process change where necessary. Savings and loans are comprised of the following subprocesses.

(1) Understanding market and customer needs.

(2) Designing products and services.

(3) Developing and deploying products and services (including training and marketing).

(4) Delivering products and services (opening, servicing and closing accounts).

(5) Providing customer services (customer queries, arrears and repossessions).

The project focused on the branch channel (including introduced business from intermediaries) and on generic savings and mortgage product sets. Corporate centre processing was not included and neither were any support processes.

## Analysis activity

Five process modelling workshops were undertaken, one for each of the subprocesses. The workshops involved staff representatives, who were generally non-managers with in-depth operational experience. Each workshop was attended by between  ve and seven representatives. Attendees with direct customer contact represented customer views. Checkland and Scholes (1990) described two streams of enquiry which together comprise the current form of the SSM. Firstly, the logic-based stream of analysis enables participants to envision a number of potential purposes (or root de nitions) for the system under consideration. For example, a mortgage sales process may have the purpose of boosting the income of the building society, of providing funds to secure a roof over the heads of the homeless, of stimulating the sale of other related products such as buildings and contents insurance, etc. Each of these systems can then be modelled as a process and compared with perceived reality in order to identify process improvements. Given the recent success in applying IDEF, the process analysis team chose to use IDEF to model the processes that corresponded to the root de nitions. Project 1 ran from mid-July to the end of August 1994. By the end of the project rumours of merger negotiations had become rife and this, along with the suspension of the BPR programme pending the announcement of the result of the KeyConsult Structure project, had a generally unsettling effect on the whole organization. This lowering of morale was not countered by another factor: strong  nancial results at the society’s year end of 30 September, which were seen more in terms of making the organization a better prospect for acquisition rather than forming the basis for strong future organic growth.

Furthermore, the rich pictures had caused many stakeholders to take the results of the process analysis workshops seriously and the IDEF models, although clearly simpli cations of complex processes, were regarded as very useful starting points for discussion. Indeed, this was the  rst time that the major business processes of the City had been analysed systematically. Within the BPR programme itself, the output from project 1 was generally accepted, but proved too strong for at least one individual, a senior product manager, who rejected every observation relating to his own area of control while agreeing with criticisms of areas outside of his responsibility. However, there were other signi cant political changes in the context which did have an effect. The KeyConsult project on costs run by the IT director reported and, as a result, job cuts were announced. However, what had been expected to be a wide-reaching scheme of voluntary redundancy, compulsory redundancy and early retirement involving branch staff and some 25% of higher graded head of ce staff was reduced to a more limited scheme involving voluntary redundancy only. This only strengthened the belief that merger was imminent and that job losses had been suspended as a result.

## Project 2: ‘Quick Wins’ in the branch mortgage sales process

The BPR effort, now rapidly losing support, was descoped and refocused by the BPR programme manager on the subprocess where quick results were most likely to be achieved – the branch mortgage sales process described earlier.

## Project aims and scope

The aims of the project were to focus on the acquisition and subsequent processing to completion of direct mortgage business within a branch. The overriding process design aim was to provide a more cost-effective, job-enhancing and customer-responsive mortgage service; particular emphasis was to be placed on those measures which could be implemented prior to the  rst quarter seasonal upturn in business. The supporting objectives were as follows:

(1) To improve process productivity, i.e. higher throughput at current or lower cost.

(2) To increase the number of cases in the system by raising the conversion rate at  rst customer contact and by improving subsequent rejection criteria.

(3) To reduce the average mortgage application process time.

(4) To create variation in the service in response to individual customer characteristics (customer segment) and seasonal, weekly or daily variations in demand.

(5) To migrate branch staff from non-value-adding activities to value-adding activities.

(6) To set measurable targets for the above objec tives.

## Analysis activity

The justi cation for running this project was based on the output from project 1, which is presented in

Figures 2 and 3 and the associated root de nition. The extent of the process faults in the Deliver Products (mortgage sales) subprocess is detailed in Table 1.

The analysis identi ed the following key features.

(1) New business could arrive from a number of different channels.

(2) There was very little added value for the customer in the process.

(3) The process consisted of two virtually independent subprocesses, validating the customer and validating the property. Validation was the domain of risk management and, consequently, changes in management policy would be required as part of any radical process redesign.

Furthermore, the process was linear and, hence, had an unacceptably long cycle time which could best be addressed by the twin strategies of automation and parallel processing. Finally, unacceptably high number of prospective customers (75%) failed to buy even after showing great interest in many cases. Via further workshops, the process analysis team identi ed a number of solutions to these problems including (1) the development of a lead tracking system for tracking prospective customers in order to reduce the loss rate, (2) the introduction of simpli ed mortgage application forms which mirrored the new process steps, (3) the initiation of customer credit checking at  rst contact, in parallel with application progression, (4) measures to ensure there was no con ict of interest between product campaigns, (5) the bringing of insurance underwriting in-house and (6) the redesign of the valuation subprocess (the latter two representing very signi cant changes).

## Discussion

The research reported here began as an action research study which aimed to develop, apply and evaluate a novel methodology for BPR. It subsequently evolved beyond that initial aim to embrace the wider organizational changes that were taking place whilst BPR was under way.

## The effectiveness of the BPR methodology

The BPR team attempted to further its own competence in process analysis and design by developing a BPR methodology which could be used to address both hard and soft process issues. The resulting methodology, a combination of SSM and IDEF, enabled the process analysis team to discover a number of faults in the core savings and loans processes and, in particular, in the branch mortgage sales process. Everyone believed there were problems, but statistics such as 4–6 weeks delay in mortgage application processing and 75% drop-out rates at initial customer contact were surprising to many. The BPR programme manager identi ed three types of reaction from the City managers to these results.

(1) I instinctively knew this; but it is good to have it con rmed.

(2) ‘Rubbish’: I do not believe this (especially where the manager’s area was implicated).

(3) ‘Good God’ I did not realise this!

Similarly, the revelation that at least 50% of new business came from intermediaries (brokers and estate agents) seriously contradicted policies which had actively attempted to run down indirect business in favour of direct business and had invested heavily in branch refurbishment. As these results were being rolled out during the project, the head of group marketing, when confronted with yet another rich picture, remarked ‘Oh no, I hate these diagrams because they always tell the truth!’. Whilst the process analysis team took great pride in their ability to capture the key issues concisely via the rich pictures, the BPR programme manager had a different view of the impact of the methodology:

IDEF is too technical for business managers, but good for staff immersed in the work, both IT and business. SSM-rich pictures were fabulous for directors who were interested in the initiative, but some (less committed managers) saw them as silly or frivolous.

In short, the BPR methodology was effective in enabling front-line staff and the analysis team to capture numerous process issues, but could not arrest the development of the merger.

## The implications of concurrent dynamics

We turn now to the interplay between the BPR programme, the KeyConsult projects and the merger talks. The purpose of the KeyConsult projects, according to the BPR programme manager was

To advise the Board on the City’s long-term strategy regarding markets and partners. Also the future of the  nancial services industry. KeyConsult ‘took over’ the strategic planning process for that year and concluded that the City was too small to be a national player, too big to be a niche player. I was party to those discussions.

From the programme manager’s perspective, the KeyConsult projects fed in to the merger talks; therefore, the two initiatives were very closely related. Furthermore, these activities did not con ict with the BPR programme: ‘KeyConsult were looking at the long-term strategic position, BPR was looking at operational excellence. Both were valid and complimentary initiatives’.

As for sponsors, the whole board was involved in the merger talks, whilst two of the three executive directors were involved directly in the other two initiatives: the marketing director sponsored the BPR programme whilst the technology director sponsored the KeyConsult activities. Furthermore, the technology director was also the BPR programme manager’s line manager and was kept informed of BPR progress via that connection: ‘In practice, all three executive directors were interested in the BPR programme and were kept informed’.

And so it would seem that BPR was perceived by senior management at the City as a way of striving fo internal, operational excellence whilst external consultants were tasked with reviewing the long-term strategy regarding markets and partners. This was beyond the remit of BPR. The message to merge from KeyConsult was heard clearly in the boardroom and was mor pressing than the revelations portrayed in the rich pictures emerging from the BPR team. In order to generalize from this single case, we conclude this discussion by presenting the results using a new mode which speci cally addresses the interplay of concurrent initiatives.

## A model of concurrent dynamics

In a survey of the management and IS literature, Saue and Yetton (1994; 1997) found little attention paid to the issues arising from running two or more organizational change initiatives concurrently. The ‘project in-isolation’ assumption was seen as an inadequate model for describing and explaining what is happening in many organizations in practice because pressures for rapid change in response to turbulent environmental

conditions are forcing more and more organizations to undertake concurrent change initiatives. Those authors used case studies of Australian  rms to build an initial framework for describing concurrent change. They used the term Dynamic to describe

\_ a single organisational change process which may consist of multiple related activities pursued over time and based around a goal or intent and which may be fully planned or emergent.

## (C. Sauer and P. Yetton, unpublished p. 6).

Examples include the development of a mass customization capability, the prosecution of a BPR initiative and the introduction of a manufacturing resource planning (MRPII) system. Elements of the organization, such as its strategy, structure, technology, etc. (Scott Morton, 1991), are changed by the dynamic and moved towards an ‘end- t con guration’. In the case of a single dynamic, it is crucial that the end- t con guration is in line with where the organization wants to go. With multiple, concurrent dynamics the picture is more complicated. The individual dynamics should each be aiming to move the organization towards its preferred con guration (some may not) and, in doing so, the dynamics should cooperate rather than compete. Dynamic 1 is changing the strategy and structure of the organization, whilst at the same time dynamic 2 is altering the structure and the technology towards a different end goal.

## Application to BPR at the City

The dimensions outlined by Sauer and Yetton (1997) help to explain events at the City. The three dynamics in question, i.e. the BPR programme, the KeyConsult projects and the merger talks, were all aimed at improving the performance and longevity of the organization against the backdrop of an increasingly competitive  nancial services sector in the UK. It would seem that the ultimate end- t con guration for the City, as seen by the board of directors, was of an organization merged with another major  nancial services  rm. The KeyConsult projects were compatible with this goal and provided the background analysis for the merger talks. In contrast, the BPR programme sought to build upon and improve the existing strengths of the City:

The Town had poorer quality IT systems and less empowered staff at branches. Branch mortgage advisors at the City could underwrite mortgages at point of sale; Town staff had to pass the application paperwork back to the centre for processing

## (BPR programme manager).

Thus, the BPR ‘end  t’ was not in line with the board’s goal and would have entailed a considerable investment in improving the City’s current processes. This investment would have been even greater for the Town, with its less advanced, less customer-focused branch processes.

The scope of the BPR programme was less prob lematical as it did not overlap with the other initiatives. BPR looked at the internal City processes whereas the other initiatives looked at the market-place and potential partners, but not at the process leve of detail. As for the approach to change, ‘BPR was planned; the KeyConsult work was also planned in the sense of a full market/partner analysis but emergent in the  nal “wooing” of the partner’ (BPR programme manager).

The ultimate pay-off for the KeyConsult work was, of course, achieved fairly quickly: the societies merged. Thus, the more radical change, the merger, was seen as quicker to implement than the proposals emanating from BPR project 1, although, of course, post-merger reorganization has taken a little more time to settle down.

Propinquity appeared to in uence the relative progress of the BPR and KeyConsult dynamics in two ways. Firstly, the BPR analysis was undertaken using a traditional workshop-based approach. This did not disturb the normal workings of the staff involved, who could attend the workshops, provide their input and return to their jobs as if nothing had happened. Indeed, the process analysis team remained the owners of the workshop output not the staff. Secondly, the BPR workshops involved junior staff, with the BPR programme manager and the BPR sponsor receiving weekly feedback on progress from the analysis team. The BPR programme had suf cient support from the directors, but missed out middle management: ‘We preferred to focus on the workers who knew the process on a daily basis. We fell into the trap of thinking middle managers were the problem. With hindsight they should have been involved’ (BPR programme manager).

In contrast, the KeyConsult projects explicitly targeted the board. Visibility was high at the board level and the dynamic was given a priority over the BPR dynamic. Unlike BPR, the lack of involvement of middle managers was not seen as a problem: ‘General managers were not told because they could have lost their jobs and it may have affected morale and stalled new initiatives. Also, the merger talks with the Town may have failed, therefore there was no point in alarming people unnecessarily’ (BPR programme manager).

Finally, we come to timing. From the foregoing analysis it would appear that the BPR dynamic and the KeyConsult dynamic were competing, with the latter winning out due to greater visibility and support at the board level and the less complicated proposed end- t con guration. Maybe the fact that the Town was larger than the City and was effectively taking over the City meant that the City’s end- t con guration was about to undergo a radical review in order to ensure that it was compatible with the con guration of the Town. Therefore, there was no point in attempting to change the City’s con guration in the meantime. This may explain why the radical changes to the core business processes proposed in BPR project 1 were simply unacceptable to the board and why project 2 was sanctioned in order to improve performance quickly (before the merger took effect?) but with little cost (in case the changes were overridden by the Town’s preferred way of delivering savings and loans?). Project 2 failed because, even with a reduced scope, its resource requirements were still too great for a potentially ‘temporary  x’ to the process, whereas project 3 was less threatening because it focused on a technology update which was seen as necessary regardless of the con guration or timing of a merger. This also explains why project 3 survived the merger: its end- t technology con guration was highly compatible with a potentially competing fourth dynamic, the parallel Town technology project.

## Conclusion

BPR, despite its lower pro le in recent years, remains an important approach to organizational change. If we view an organization as a means whereby people (staff) deliver products and/or services to customers, it is the processes which provide the linking mechanism. And so, whilst management and IT fashion has inevitably moved on since the early 1990s to new areas such as knowledge management and electronic commerce for instance, organizations will still deliver their products and services via a set of interrelated activities. The process, as a basic organizational building block, is not dead. However, radical BPR, as espoused by Hammer (1990) is more problematical:

BPR is viable if the culture is right to accept and absorb radical change. This requires either a very open,  exible organization or a major danger that needs to be addressed. It is very dif cult for a successful organization to accept radical change (BPR programme manager).

This study shows that a process analysis methodology based on a fusion of hard and soft techniques is very effective in identifying and communicating numerous process faults. The key stakeholders listened, but their minds were elsewhere. BPR was not the only show in

town and was in many ways a less radical but super- cially more complicated approach to change when compared with the approach ultimately adopted – to merge with a larger partner. This was seen as a better survival route than re-engineering business processes, which was not viewed as suf cient to move the City out of its dif cult position of being too large for a niche player and too small for a national player. Now successfully merged, the new organization is still using BPR but on a smaller, less radical scale: ‘In general, BPR is emerging in more traditional Town terms, e.g. smaller scope (more like total quality management) and is seen as being internally generated, not imposed from outside or above’ (BPR programme manager).

Separate, potentially overlapping initiatives still arise within the organization and consultant ‘overload’ is sometimes a problem. However, BPR is not a dirty word; it has just become absorbed into the Town’s way of working and, as this study shows, may not be the most radical form of change on the agenda. As for concurrent dynamics:

The only way to manage this is to kill off the excess projects early and then to mobilize full organizational support for the remaining initiatives. Full support is needed because implementing change is all about buy-in and commitment (BPR programme manager).

No methodology, be it hard or soft, can guarantee that.
