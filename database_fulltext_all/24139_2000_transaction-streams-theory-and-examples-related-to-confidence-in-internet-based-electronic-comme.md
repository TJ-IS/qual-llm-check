# Transaction streams: theory and examples related to con dence in internet-based electronic commerce

BRIAN SUBIRANA and PATRICIA CARVAJAL

International Graduate School of Management, University of Navarra, Avda, Pearson 21, 08034 Barcelona, Spain

This paper analyses how transactions related to the exchange of goods and services are being performed on the Internet. The adoption of electronic markets in an industry has a disintermediation potential because it can create a direct link between the producer and the consumer (without the need for the intermediation role of distributors). Electronic markets lower the searches cost allowing customers to choose between more providers. This ultimately reduces both the costs for the customer and the pro ts for the producer. In this paper it is contended that electronic markets on the Internet have the opposite effect, resulting in an increase in intermediators. It introduces transaction streams which model how transactions are being conducted and helps explain the types of new intermediators that are appearing on the Internet. The mechanisms by which companies are exploring ways of extending transaction streams are also described. To illustrate the model, the paper brie y analyses transaction streams in the insurance industry and provides examples related to con dence in electronic commerce.

## Introduction

The market value appreciation of Internet services initial public offerings YTD (1980–1997) is \$4.773 million (Meeker, 1997). In only 5 years, the Internet may reach 150 million users. The Internet is used in practically all industries, in ever-more global and sophisticated manners. The convergence of industries such as telecom, of ce equipment, consumer electronics, computers and media and publishing into emerging information industries is having profound effects on the everyday businesses we take for granted. It is no longer necessary to buy newspapers at the local newsstand because they can be read over the Internet. Encyclopedias are not mammoth books collecting dust on a shelf, rather they are small interactive CD ROMS or up-to-date interactive web sites to be enjoyed with a computer. And airline tickets do not need to be reserved through a travel agent for a fee; they can now be ordered directly through a computer.

Such market value appreciation is in part due to novel business practices which are enabled by various Internet functionalities. In particular companies are exploiting innovative transactions processes. This paper analyses transaction processes and presents transaction streams which model novel transaction schemes which take advantage of such Internet functionalities. Understanding the current success stories on the Internet is an interesting endeavour for two reasons. First, it is an interactive environment with the full functionality of many other interactive environments (with some bandwidth limitations which in some instances restrict the exchange of real-time information such as video). This means that by understanding market dynamics on the Internet, one should be able to extrapolate and apply market behaviour concepts in other interactive environments. Second, it is based on open standards and is largely unregulated. Thus, it is in constant  ux, adapting itself to the evolving characteristics of the demand. We could say that the Internet is a lead user interactive network (Von Hippel, 1985, 1988).

Including this Introduction, this paper has seven sections. In the next section it outlines some functional properties which characterize the Internet, concentrating on those impacting on the way transactions are being performed. In the third section, it reviews the existing literature on electronic markets in order to view how information technology (IT) is changing the way companies operate. The transformation of organizations and markets impacts on how transactions are performed. In the fourth section,  ve fundamental steps in performing a transaction are described. In the  fth section, transaction streams are introduced and diverse examples of real transaction streams are provided. The sixth section presents examples related to insurance and con dence in electronic commerce. Some examples of novel company roles which are emerging as well as some of the infrastructure mechanisms underlying transaction streams are given. The  nal section discusses the implications of the  ndings presented.

## Internet functional properties

The Internet is a special medium because it has important properties which facilitate economic activities. In this section six of them are reviewed (not an exhaustive list, merely some of the main properties). The  rst three properties describe the reach-enabling functionalities of the Internet. The next two are the customization functionalities enabled by the Internet. The last property is the low entry cost.

The  rst property is that it can be used all the time, 24 h a day, practically from anywhere and by anybody. This is a very powerful paradigm. No matter where customers are, they can be reached. This has enabled the Internet to create communities of people with speci c interests and low geographic density. An example is HotHotHot, a store devoted to spicy food. HotHotHot features a very complete selection of books and hot sauces. It includes a search engine and multiple delivery options. Operating a store such as HotHotHot in every city around the world would probably not be pro table; however, the Internet enables the interaction between the centralized store owner and the scattered customers. The Internet’s second property for facilitating economic activity is its capacity for enabling various forms of interactive communication between players (Armstrong and Hagel, 1996; Nissen, 1996; Kosiur, 1997). The medium is extremely powerful as a communication vehicle. Through e-mail (asynchronous one-to-one communication), chat (synchronous many-to-many communication), newsgroups (asynchronous many-to-many communication) and even voice and video it has demonstrated its potential for establishing close personal relations. Third, the Internet can be used to provide services combining and selecting the offers of various providers. For example, on the same web page, Yahoo! combines three services from three different companies: Altavista, Amazon and Geo Systems Global Corp. In this sense, it can be used to select the players that one wishes to invite to a given economic activity. This is important because sometimes, the selection can be done automatically by a program which helps an individual navigate through the Internet. An emerging trend in electronic market-places is the aggregation and disaggregation of information-based product components (Bakos, 1998).

Fourth, a software robot can navigate on its own performing a sort of ‘personal butler’ role (Negroponte,

1995). This is crucial because robots can be constructed to perform a myriad of relevant tasks such as selecting vendors for a given product (Beam and Segev, 1997; Guttman et al., 1998). This means that robots can reduce the search costs to a simple time delay related to the interval required by the robot to query the different stores. Fifth, the Internet has developed tools such as Java so that Internet interaction can also be customized with a program running on the client machine. By customization it means that the interaction between a company and its customers is tailored to the end-user. For example, advertising networks are able to track how many times a user has seen a given advertising banner. This information is then used to customize subsequent banner placements. The issue of customization is becoming important beyond the advertising and interface levels (Noland and Galal, 1998).

Finally and most importantly, it is very inexpensive for both the user and the service provider. The entry barriers from a technical standpoint are very low. To start an Internet site, one can rent space on an Internet service provider for a very low price. In fact, there are some services such as Tripod that offer free web page hosting. Thus, everybody can afford a web site. Because it is based on open standards, there are many competitive products covering most of the possible demand needs. This means that some new business concepts can be tested on a small scale at low cost relative to traditional testing arenas.

## Electronic markets

IT has had signi cant effects on some organizations (Sarkar et al., 1995). Some companies are changing the way they operate due to signi cant reductions in the cost of obtaining, processing and transmitting information. This implies continuous evolution of a  rm’s value chain, as IT transforms not only the way the activities are performed, but also the nature of the linkages between them (Porter and Millar, 1985). Transaction costs theory (TCT) helps us to understand how markets and hierarchies are choosen (Wigand and Benjamin, 1995). According to TCT, a  rm has two options in organizing its economic activities: an internal hierarchical structure where it integrates the activity into its managerial structure or a market-like relationship with external  rms (Williamson, 1975). Malone et al. (1987) were among the  rst to link TCT to electronic communication (Sarkar et al., 1995). In free market economies two basic mechanisms for coordinating the transactions related to the  ow of materials or services through adjacent steps in the value-added chain can be observed: markets and hierarchies (Malone, et al., 1987). Markets coordinate the  ow through the supply and demand forces between different individuals and  rms.

Malone et al. (1987) contended that, by reducing the costs of coordination, the evolution of IT is leading to a shift towards proportionately more use of markets than of hierarchies to coordinate economic activity. When the market mechanism is at work, the  ow of materials and services takes the form of external transactions and is coordinated by market forces. If the transaction costs are low, the market is the optimal choice. Firms will choose transactions which economize on coordination costs and if IT continues its rapid cost performance improvement,  rms will continue to  nd incentives to coordinate their activities electronically. Lower coordination costs favour electronic markets by simplifying complex product descriptions and reducing asset speci city.

According to their view, electronic markets will evolve from electronic single-source sales channels to biased markets, where one of the providers uses the market transaction mechanisms in its favour, to unbiased markets and,  nally, to personalized markets. Personalized markets are those in which the customers can use customized aids in making their choices. For example, some airline reservation systems allow the user to set preferences such as departure time, seating assignment and rates which are then used in subsequent transactions. The airline market is therefore customized to the users – different users have different options depending on their preferences.

Bakos (1991, 1997, 1998) analysed the impact of electronic markets by analysing search costs. Buyers must, directly or indirectly, pay search costs to obtain information about the prices and product offerings available in the market. Electronic markets have a vast impact on search costs because of the coordinating effect of IT. Using economic theory, Bakos (1991) showed that a reduction in search costs plays a major role in determining the implications of these systems for market ef ciency and competitive behaviour. This reduction results in direct ef ciency gains from reduced intermediation costs. All other things being equal, fewer intermediators means less cost for the system. In addition, electronic markets result in indirect but possibly larger gains in allocation ef ciency from better-informed buyers. Buyers have a central location for  nding product information so they can allocate their purchasing budgets better. The bene ts realized by market participants increase as more organizations join the system, leading to network externalities (Katz and Shapiro, 1985).

However, Bakos’ (1991) argument was based on  ve economic characteristics of electronic market systems (search costs, increasing return, switching costs, entry costs and maintenance costs) which have been changed by recent market and technological developments around the Internet. The  rst two characteristics that he analysed have been enhanced. First, search costs, as stated above, are reduced (through browsing or robot shopping) and, second, the bene ts realized increase as more organizations join the World Wide Web (WWW) electronic markets. However, the last three have been drastically changed by the emergence of open standards such as TCP/IP (Transfer Control Protocol/Internet Protocol), HTML, XML, Java and the vast market adoption of the Internet as a platform for economic activities. Indeed, Bakos’ (1991) third argument was that ‘Electronic marketplaces can impose signi cant switching costs on their participants’ (p. 297). However, in most Internet-enabled marketplaces, the switching costs are often reduced (or nil). This is because the Internet provides an open standard for information transmission (i.e. a dedicated line is no longer an entry barrier) and Java can be used to create an interface which can reduce the switching costs by translating between alternative interface options (i.e. the interface software is no longer always a switching barrier).

The entry costs (point four of Bakos’ (1991) argument), de ned as large system development and maintenance costs have also been substantially reduced. Many open solutions exist which can be adopted and integrated into a solution with a fraction of the development cost that was necessary in the past. Products such as Front Page and InterDev have signi cantly altered the cost of developing powerful WWW applications. Pre-WWW network technology was expensive and proprietary. This explains why early success stories such as airline reservations and hospital supplies took place in markets with a great demand for immediate and distributed coordination.

There is other work related to electronic markets which is relevant (Rheingold, 1993; Hagel and Armstrong, 1997). Benjamin and Wigand (1995) evaluated how the emergence of a national infrastructure such as the Internet can change the different segments of an industry value chain. The analysis was centred around the National Information Infrastructure (NII) initiative and evaluated the vision described in an Of ce of Technology Assessment report (1994): ‘The network will, in many instances, serve as the market. When this occurs, market structure will depend as much on network characteristics and the economies of networks as it does on relationships among  rms’ (p. 63).

In the next section we will provide a de nition of what constitutes a ‘transaction’ and present a novel  ve-stage model of transaction processes. De ning these processes will lead us to introducing transaction streams in the following section. Transaction streams fully exploit the six functional properties of the Internet presented in this section.

## Transaction processes

By ‘transaction’ we understand the establishment (or cancellation) of some form of contract between different parties which includes a given economic activity (Haanes and Lowendahl, 1997). By ‘contract’ we mean an agreement between a set of agents to perform a course of actions (usually with detailed implicit and explicit conditions and alternative paths). For example, transactions occur when purchasing a cinema ticket, placing an advertisement in a newspaper, purchasing a book, making a  ight reservation, etc. When a customer makes a  ight reservation a contract is established between the customer and the airline. The service involved in this contract is the air transport of the passenger. Electronic markets such as airline reservation systems have been developed to facilitate such transactions. Often, an intermediary operates the market – in the case of air travel it is a CRS (computer reservation system) such as SABRE or Amadeus.

In general, an on-line transaction involves at least three roles (see Figure 1): buyer (customer), seller (supplier) and shipper (distribution service). A market transaction corresponds to a  nite number of interaction processes between market participants in various roles. IT systems hold the potential to change interactions between participants by helping to leverage buying power and streamline complex and inef cient processes (Gebauer and Segev, 1998). The interaction processes involved can be grouped into classes and form the phases of a market transaction (Schmid and Lindemann, 1994).

This paper suggests that interaction processes related to a given transaction may be grouped into  ve transaction processes: player selection, contract condition setting, contract signature, contract storage and transaction action. They will be referred to as the transaction processes. These transaction processes can be represented graphically, as shown in Figure 2.

Each of these processes is now reviewed in turn. Player selection involves the selection of the economic agents that will be involved in the transaction. This is a critical component because it narrows the number of  rms that will compete to obtain the contract to perform the action. CRSs select the airlines that are connected to their systems and, in a given transaction, display only those that are relevant for preset travel itinerary conditions.

For example, in the airline reservation system, SABRE provides the choices for given itinerary parameters. In other words, the process that details the clauses of the contract has implications for the way the market operates. Before a house purchasing transaction can be completed procedures involving various tax forms, price negotiation, loan applications and registry veri cation need to be performed. The last three processes of the sequence have to do with the contract and activity execution. Contract signature refers to the binding step in the process in which the transaction players agree on a course of action which clari es how the transaction activities will be performed. This can be a short and standard agreement or a long, detailed and customized contract. Often a market intermediary sets some conditions to ensure the contract validity (identi cation, down payment, etc.). For example, a travel agent uses a CRS to close a travel contract by con rming the reservation in the system. Once this has been done, in the traditional CRS the contract is issued as a paper document (ticket) and the relevant data stored in the airline database. Finally, the transaction action is the process by which the activity referred to in the contract is executed.

## Transaction streams

In this section, transaction streams are introduced. First, an example of a transaction stream related to the book distribution industry market is presented. Then transaction streams are de ned and different schemes used to enable transaction streams are described.

There are thousands of bookstores on the Internet (Subirana, 1999). Their initial modus operandi appears fairly simple. An entrepreneur decides to make a book database available on an Internet site with the hope that customers will start sur ng the site and orders will land on his or her desk. This arrangement appears very similar to the sale of airline tickets through a traditional CRS and is pretty much what Jeff Bezos (founder of Amazon) did when he left his job as a principal at D.E. Shaw in early 1994 to pursue retailing on the Internet. Music and books are some of the categories most suitable for Internet consumer retailing. There are more titles than any physical store can stock and a selection can be easily made by querying a title database. Books are also well-known commodities and easy to ship. In fact, in its  rst 30 days Amazon shipped books to customers in all 50 US states and 45 countries around the world. The book market is also highly fragmented from the point of view of distribution, authoring and production. Some threat exists from large presence-based retailers such as Barnes and Noble and Borders because they may leverage their brand recognition with their warehouse and book logistics experience. The market is valuing Amazon more than other retailers. With comparable sales, the Internet Bookshop only received marginal market appreciation. With 1996 sales of \$17 million and \$6 million in losses, Amazon had an IPO market value of \$540 million. This difference cannot therefore be explained through an NPV (net present value) analysis of book distribution based on a simple database and a warehouse. Indeed, there is more to book distribution on the Internet than most would have predicted just a few years ago.

One of the novel and effective tools that Internet bookstores such as Amazon.com exploit is the associate program membership. Through the Amazon associates program, Amazon provides the opportunity at linking associate Internet sites to their own database. This means that the users of the associate site can purchase books pre-selected in the associate site. For example, in Netscape’s developer area, there is a section with reviews about different books. The user can select a book and be redirected to the page which sells the book. Netscape has become an independent bookseller at practically no additional cost. The transaction pro t is shared and Amazon gives a 5–15% share to the ‘associates program seller’. So far Amazon claims to have over 150 000 sign-up associates (1998).

Observe that the associate program changes two of the  ve processes described in the previous section. First, the player selection is performed by the associate partner, Netscape in the above example. Then, in the condition setting, Netscape performs a prescreening of all the books of the Amazon catalogue. By sharing part of the revenue, Amazon is bene ting from a very inexpensive and enthusiastic sales force. This is very attractive for organizations such as Netscape which have access to a customer base interested in speci c titles and allow access to the appropriate books when its customers want it. In some way, this can be considered a response by providers to reverse markets (Hagel and Armstrong, 1997). To prevent the power shift to the customer, providers work together on a combined sales effort.

In fact, when Amazon buys advertising space in a search engine such as Altavista, the combined effect is often that of converting Altavista into a sort of up-front paid partner. Advertising banners can now be very targeted. For example, when a customer searches for the word Netscape, an Amazon banner could display the amount of Netscape-related books that are on its database. Furthermore, Amazon can create a banner with a search space so that the customer can choose to search either on the Altavista site or on the Amazon database. This case is interesting because the user is provided with alternative transaction paths while the involved  rms are competing for his or her attention. The paper equivalent of directory search does not enable the dynamic player selection and condition setting exhibited by such successful Internet companies.

Altavista also has an associate program to stimulate the usage of its database. Companies around the world are performing specialized queries into its database. The logic to support such a program is that associate companies have better information about the user than Altavista. This means that they can use such information to design better queries while retaining customer attention. Observe here that Altavista is only involved when the transaction action is performed. Since this program can be combined with the associate program of Amazon and others, the number of players involved in each transaction is not limited. In fact, some of the queries delivered by Yahoo! are managed directly through an associate program by Altavista.

We de ne transaction streams (Subirana, 1998) as electronic markets in which more than one organization controls the  rst four transaction processes. For example, in the Amazon associate program, the referring Internet site, such as Netscape, controls the player selection while Amazon controls the rest of the transaction processes. Underneath each process many related actors could take part and create more relations and transactions as well. This transforms the traditional model described in Figure 1 to a series of transactions in which more than one organization controls the  rst four transaction processes. What is fundamental about these examples is that multiple organizations are involved in a transaction and that is the distinctive property which de nes transaction streams. More examples are given in what follows.

Advertising is another area where transaction streams are common. An Internet advertiser is anyone who promotes their product and/or service via the Internet. This is often accomplished by placing banners (small advertisements which link to the advertiser’s web pages) on sites throughout the Internet. Advertisers normally pay the publishers of the sites on which their banners appear. Individual advertisers, traditional advertisement agencies and cyber-advertisement agencies are all active advertisers on the Internet.

DoubleClick (founded in March 1996) provides Internet advertising sales and management services and manages an Internet advertising network. Double-Click’s objective is to bring its network sites, Internet users and Internet advertisers together. In order to do so, it has created a comprehensive database of Internet user and organization pro les which are used for advertisement campaign targeting purposes. In addition, DoubleClick categorizes every Internet page displaying. DoubleClick advertisement banners in order to promote af nity targeting. When an advertiser wants to place banners on the Internet, it must select among a host of available media – in the tens of thousands in most cases. Such a selection is not as straightforward as selecting the airlines that are available to perform a given itinerary. Issues such as medium af nity, availability, etc., must be taken into account. A transaction stream is produced in this example because DoubleClick is in charge of deciding who are the players (advertisers) involved in a given page view. Such a selection process is at the heart of DoubleClick’s business and it claims it serves over 100 million banners per day (1998). This means that it is computing over 100 million selection choices.

So far, this section has described various examples of transaction streams. Note that most of the examples shown involve two companies. However, often more players are involved. Table 1 shows three transaction stream examples and the  ve processes involved in the transactions. The  rst is a CRS reservation system based on a single market operator. In the second a transaction stream with only one associate program, termed a single transaction stream, is included. In this example, the agency sells travel reservations enabled by the Amadeus associate program. The third is a transaction stream for a banner placement transaction. This last example is termed a ‘multiple transaction stream’ because the number of involved players is larger than four. In this example, Adsmart, another advertising service, also participates. Adsmart contains a database of demographic and media data that can be used to select what are the most desired media. It enables also the creation of a full media plan which is then used to select the appropriate banner placement locations. In the case of banner placement, the individual media (sometimes directly, sometimes through advertising networks such as DoubleClick) provides the availability of the different desired services. In this multiple transaction stream example four players can be involved in an advertisingrelated transaction stream: the advertiser, Adsmart, DoubleClick and the content site. There appears to be no limit to the number of players involved in transaction streams.

There are various types of emerging tools and models that companies use to enable transaction streams. The following are  ve of the most common.

(1) Associate programs. As described above with the Amazon and Altavista examples, this functionality allows companies to extend their reach by sharing audience and revenue with other Internet sites. For example, HotHotHot (described in the previous sections) allows thirdparty sites to provide links into the HotHotHot home page. It offers 5% of the revenue generated by the customers coming through the link provided. The HotHotHot associate is involved in player selection (by redirecting suitable customers) and in contract condition setting (by redirecting to the relevant product page).

(2) Interest links. The special-interest, mail-order world has received revamped impetus with the Internet. The player selection process used to be very ineffective as customers had to  nd and subscribe to a special-interest publication, order a catalogue, choose the merchandise and mail order it. The process took weeks, if not months. However, the Internet, allows transaction activity players to trigger the player selection process via links in speci c Internet pages. Associate programs can be seen as one special way of sharing the revenue of interest links where the associate program managing company is the one performing the contract signature. Interest links participate actively in the player selection process.

(3) Yellow Pages. The All-Internet Shopping Directory provides extensive listings of Internet stores. Categories include hobbies, malls, services and business to business. Just like with Yellow Pages, the directory provides various levels of services ranging from free hyperlinks to ‘focus’ listings and banner advertisements. In its robust implementation, one could conceivably peruse a Yellow Page service for information up until the actual product description. Some Yellow Page services, such as GTEsuperpages.com, are trying to host extensive product information and selling services which results in their involvement in the contract signature process.

(4) Robots. Robots are programs which can perform ‘intelligent’ tasks such as  nding the cheapest publicized price for a given product or the most up-to-date post code for a certain address (Koster, 1997). Shopping robots come in two varieties: as browsers running on client machines or as server software (Subirana, 1997). Robots can be used to extend and automate the functionality of all transaction processes. Robots basic involvement is in player selection and contract condition setting.

(5) Pro le information. www.f y.com (FFly) claims to have a database of over 1 million users (1998). Each user has a Fire y Passport which includes detailed user pro le information (preferred  lms, food bought, etc.) which is permanently updated as the user performs transactions. The passport contains user information which can be carried to other sites to tailor server responses. This technology can be used to lower search costs and can ensure anonymity for certain transactions. This is very relevant as it can serve as the basis for reverse markets (Hagel and Armstrong, 1997). In the future, companies such as FFly may be involved in negotiating, closing and storing the contract. In the most extreme case, the seller may not know the true name of its customer. FFly may develop two separate transactions, one with the customer and one with the provider. In this reintermediation model, FFly would be involved in the  ve transaction processes.

One area in which there has been an increase in the number of tools and models which support transaction streams is in the insurance and endorsement related to contract signature and storage (Hodges, 1997; Markey, 1997). In the next section the issues related to insurance and con dence in electronic commerce transaction streams are reviewed. Assurance credentials and other tools and models which can be used to manage con dence in such transaction streams will also be presented.

## Transaction streams: examples related to insurance and con dence in electronic commerce

In the presence-based world, consumers’ con dence is based in part on brands and on endorsements provided by third-party entities such as the American Automobile Association or the Michelin guides. However, on computer systems which span multiple administrative boundaries, and in particular on the Internet, such trust has proven dif cult to establish (Choi et al., 1997). Others believe that security poses serious challenges to the growth and wide adoption of electronic commerce (Bhimani, 1996; Froomkin, 1996). So far, global brands have been useful in providing assurance to customers. The lack of effective ways of managing the need for endorsement, security and insurance is a well-recognized barrier to the full acceptance of transactions on the Internet (Tapscott, 1996; Schwarz, 1997). To respond to this transaction endorsement need, distributed approaches have been developed and technological tools have been proposed such as cryptography,  rewalls and digital certi cates. Even if there are solutions in some of these areas, they have not gained users interest or trust so far (Pernul and Rohm, 1997).

The lack of con dence stems, in part, from time asymmetry and information asymmetry. In effect, even if the Internet does reduce the time for some transactions to be completed, most real-world transactions require some form of movement of goods or services, thereby introducing time asymmetries in the process of exchange of assets between transacting parties. Time asymmetry brings the notion of risk (perceived or real) to each of the agents involved in the transaction who must invest resources before receiving a return (Salam et al., 1998). In many situations, information asymmetry has signi cant implications for the operations of markets (Caillaud, 1990; Greenwald, 1990). Information asymmetry also affects the on-line market; in electronic markets the physical product is not examined, only a representation of it. Information asymmetry in this case means that the seller has more information about the product than the buyer. This situation imposes certain limitations on knowledge of the product and, therefore, uncertainty regarding its quality or that of the vendor (Choi et al., 1998; Grace, 1998).

Con dence is pervasive in all of the client relationship process. The notion of con dence implies the deposit of resources (money, time and personal information) into the hands of another party for use for his or her own bene t or that of the buyer or both. Without the appropriate level of con dence, the exchange of information between individuals and organizations will be limited (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development, 1997). There are at least three essential aspects related to con dence: to be led to a selection in which the end is foreseen as a happy or a painful event, to realize that the event depends on the behaviour of the other party and,  nally, to perceive the intensity of the negative event superior or inferior to that of the positive one.

The management of con dence has been historically affected by brand name recognition. In the non-virtual world, the mere presence of a brand name is suf cient to create con dence in relatively unimportant decisions as, for example, the purchasing of small amounts of products or repetitive purchases and in generic products of easy speci cation. In effect, brand name recognition performs different functions (Leon, 1991).

(1) An identi cation function: the brand identi es the product according to its main characteristics. It also leads to a speci c con guration of attributes. Therefore, the brand itself constitutes valuable information about the characteristics related to a speci c product offer.

(2) A reference function: the brand helps the buyer to identify him or herself, and contributes in structuring and organizing the market offer.

(3) A guarantee function: the brand is a public commitment of quality and performance. It is a given promise. It assures the permanence of quality that is expected of it.

(4) A personalization function: the selection of certain brands allows individuals to place themselves in relation to their desired social status. In making a selection a person shows their desire to be different from their peers or, on the contrary, to integrate themselves.

(5) A playful function: it corresponds to the pleasure that is experienced when purchasing. The presence of multiple brands makes certain buyers experience a feeling of true animation and results in a source of stimulus.

(6) A practical function: instead of having to repeat a complete decision process on each occasion, the brand facilitates the memorization of previous selection processes and the conclusions of consumption experiences. The brand is in this sense, a summary of information related to past purchasing experiences. Taking into consideration that the brand name is memorable and easy to recognize, it gives the buyer the possibility of following repetitive processes and, as a result, generates con dence in the client.

## Transaction streams

In non-electronic transactions, when such con dence is limited, users can try to complement it with endorsements, licences and insurance, particularly in important transactions (e.g. those with high monetary value) or in cases where brand con dence is low. The insurance model does not apply directly to electronic commerce when transaction streams involve many players and transaction processes are completed in short periods of time. The issues related to insurance and the  rst three transaction processes are now reviewed. Assurance credentials and other tools and models are then presented. These are natural extensions of the traditional insurance services and can increase end-user con dence in transaction streams.

Player selection involves the selection of the economic agents that will be involved in the transaction. An insurance contract represents an agreement between the service provider and the party insured. It can also represent an agreement between at least three parties, the surety, the obligee and the principal. Often more parties are involved and various bilateral contracts are signed. This contract is called a policy and speci es the obligations of the parties involved.

The contract condition setting refers to the process by which the parties involved negotiate the details of the action to be performed. This activity is related to the interactive exchange of messages between the players involved (Runge, 1998). The policy covers damages in icted by the insured upon a third party if the damages were caused by an accident or occurrence (with exceptions as speci ed in the policy). The insurer does not relieve the insured from responsibility for committing malicious acts. When an insurance policy is requested, insurance providers calculate the risk to be assumed by the insurance policy and  x the premium at an adequate level to re ect this risk. Insurance providers will need to assess a service provider about past behaviour to determine risk. A policy requires an assessment of the assumed risk and settling claims requires judgement, neither of which are easily automated. In other words, policy purchasing transactions are, in general, too complex for transaction streams in which there are no clear condition setting rules.

Contract signature refers to the binding step in the process in which the transaction players agree on a course of action which clari es how the transaction activities will be performed. This can be a short and standard agreement or a long, detailed and customized contract. Policies are agreements certi ed by the signatures of the parties. The electronic representation of a policy will include (1) the names of the parties, including the name of the insurance provider, the name of the service provider and a description of the obligee (if needed), (2) the subject of the insurance and the insured risks, (3) the period during which the policy will be in force and (4) the limits of liability (an individual limit, an aggregate limit of liability or possibly no limit in cases of endorsement or licence).

One tool which has been suggested to increase con- dence in transaction streams is the provision of assurance credentials. It can be used to provide an independent means of verifying the source or the content of a given information exchange. Figure 3 shows an example of the streams of messages exchanged to retrieve and verify assurance credentials. In message 1, the client requests proof of assurance from the candidate service provider. The assurance is presented to the client in message 4 (the form varies with different proxy implementations). If the service provider requires a separate credential for each client (conventional cryptography), it requests and retrieves such a credential in messages 2 and 3. The authentication server returns a new credential derived from the original one, encrypted in a key that may be veri ed by the client (Lai et al., 1997). After validating credentials from the service provider, clients may keep them until their expiration.

Before the contract signature process is completed, an assurance credential may be granted to a server after meeting the requirements imposed by the server issuing the credentials. The credential mechanism can be more tailored to the transaction needs than standard of cial licences. They can also be tailored to the user so that the transaction risk is managed dynamically by the user and not by the regulatory environment exclusively. Furthermore, proxies can be set in such a way that all the transactions realized by a given organization are endorsed and certi ed by an independent and trusted  rm. Observe that the mechanism behind this process is similar to the FireFly passport mentioned earlier but with a different purpose: instead of exchanging user demographic information to manage the transaction stream, the system exchanges endorsement and certi cation preferences. Thus, assurance credentials can be de ned as proxies. Furthermore, proxies can be set in such a way that all the transactions realized by a given organization are endorsed and certi ed by an independent and trusted  rm.

The veri cation of the assurance credential shows an example of how transactions streams are implemented. When an assurance credential is received from a service provider, the client validates the credential in two steps. First, the proxy is veri ed cryptographically and may require further interactions with others servers. Second, the information presented in the proxy is extracted and compared against the user’s and application’s policy for server selection. Furthermore, the service provider must authenticate itself to the client using the authentication protocol used by the system (Lai et al., 1997).

In addition to assurance credentials, licensing and endorsements are mechanisms which may be used to gain con dence in the service provider and play the role, as described by Ba et al. (1998), of a trusted third party for electronic commerce transactions. A licence is a credential which indicates that a service provider is legally authorized to provide a service. It indicates that the service provider has been found to meet certain minimal quali cations required by law and that it is subjected to regulations and sanctions if it violates the law. An endorsement provides assurance that a service provider meets the more rigorous requirements determined by the endorser and usually provides information about the quality of a service provider. The con dence in an endorsed service provider depends in part on one’s con dence in its endorsers. Endorsements do not provide compensation for damages incurred while interacting with service providers. They provide a mechanism for clients to evaluate and reduce the risk involved in dealing with service providers better.

The concepts of insurance policies, licensing and endorsements are related, differing mainly in the limits and source of compensation in the event of a loss. Insurance policies provide a contractual responsibility to the insurance providers. The development of ‘institutional trust’ will lead to a decrease in the consumerperceived level of risk of transactions over the Internet (Sarkar et al., 1995; Salam et al., 1998). The presence of assurance, licensing and endorsement credentials is a  rst step in improving con dence in a service provider. This con dence must depend in part on the con dence in the endorser or insurance provider. An extensive network of relations can be activated to create con dence. Lai et al, (1997) proposed a system based on a network of trust relationships which includes service providers, licensing agencies, insurance providers and endorsement agencies. In their example (Figure 4), client C requests service from service provider S2. To provide this service, S2 subcontracts to service provider S1. C’s con dence in the composite service depends on the assurance provided for both S1 and S2. To improve customer con dence, S1 and S2 obtain a liability insurance policy from insurance provider I1. As long as C has con dence in I1 it is assured that C will be compensated in the event of damages caused by S1 or S2. In this example C does not have con dence directly in the insurance provider, but will accept the endorsement of E3, an organization which rates insurance companies. Client C will also  nd that service providers S1 and S2 are licensed by licensing agency L2, indicating that L2 has found each server competent in offering its services. The licensing authority L2 has not been endorsed directly, but is recognized as the appropriate licensing agency by C.

In this section various tools and models related to con dence in transaction streams have been discussed: insurance services, assurance, licensing and endorsements credentials and networks of trust relations. It concludes by presenting a hypothetical example (see Figure 5) which provides evidence of the potential complexity that results when advertising and other services are combined with those concepts. In the example the players involved when a sample user ‘clicks’ on an insurance robot to search for an insurance product are plotted. The robot itself calls a set of licensing agencies, endorsement agencies and other companies such as modelling and advertising  rms. Licensing and endorsement agencies in turn search for insurance brokers which, through third-party predictive modelling and endorsement agencies, assess what is the risk premium that should be used. Insurance brokers nurture their systems with the information provided by other companies to increase their hit ratio while minimizing the risk. The robot then collects all the answers and provides the aggregated response to the user.

## Conclusion

This paper describes a fundamental shift in industry structure for transaction-based economic activities. Such a shift is demonstrated by the fact that player selection, contract condition setting, contract signature, contract storage and transaction actions can be performed on the Internet by different players. In these cases, when a user purchases a product or a service, a transaction stream is triggered. It is argued that electronic markets are evolving from an intermediary-based model into a transaction stream model in which more than one agent controls the transaction contract process. The number of players in a transaction stream is bound to increase and there is no limit to such numbers.

In fact, in the context of this paper, electronic commerce can be de ned as the set of economic activities in which contracts are developed almost instantly by machines exchanging information through a network and resulting in transaction streams involving more than three players. Transaction streams related to electronic commerce occur in short periods of time (e.g. less than 1 s) and most of the economic agents involved can be selected via prede ned computer rules or through other methods such as ‘instant’ auctions.

Transaction streams are present in many industries (book distribution, advertising, company directories, etc.). Managers must then seek to bene t from transaction streams by integrating them into their current business models or developing new ones. For example, traditionally exclusive distributor arrangements such as those existing in book retailers must be complemented with an Internet presence through the incorporation of some of the transaction stream schemes presented above. The Internet provides tools for facilitating such a process and enables different transaction participants to establish protocols in order to perform each of the  ve basic transaction components in a myriad of different ways. Associate programs, interest links, Yellow Pages, robots and pro le information are just a sample of the tools and models being used. Different players add value through content, editing, navigation and distribution. As transaction streams evolve, new needs will appear.

Companies participating in existing electronic markets must question whether bene ts can be derived from operating the market within the Internet transaction stream context. Transaction stream opportunities may increase the amount of transactions being performed. However, the broader reach may attract new players and bring pro ts down. In addition, transaction streams may become increasingly competitive. For example, the pricing policy through each of the transaction stream players deserves increasing attention. Pricing and services packaging practices can become more complex as competition increases and experience is accumulated. Companies must  nd new ways to add value in their transaction streaming activities. Dell claims (1998) to sell over \$4 million daily through in-company associate programs which rely on their ‘business centre solution’. Their service currently (1998) supports over 100 companies in the management of their PC ordering needs. Furthermore, Dell is working to increase the value added to each customer by customizing current functionality offerings such as company reports, account team information, product information gathering and budget composition. These custom sites support the entire selling cycle, from pre-sales to post-sales. Using this in-company associate program, Dell is gradually transforming supply chains into seamless direct relationships between suppliers and manufacturers and their customers. Dell could provide the tools to foster third-party players to develop modules which enhance transaction streams involving Dell’s products. This is an example where the search for new value-adding activities has the effect of transaction streams being present well beyond the marketing function. More research is needed to understand the systems, infrastructure and business models which can support cross-industry, cross-functional transaction streams.

Figure 6 illustrates an infrastructure which could underlie cross-industry, cross-functional transaction streams. As can be seen, many players may be involved in simple, routine transactions. Nine layers have been identi ed: a customer layer, customer management layer, service provider layer, payments layer, attention management layer, navigation selection layer, insurance layer, predictive modelling layer and information layer. Observe that the information layer is similar for the advertising and insurance streams while the modelling techniques are of a different nature: the former is geared towards managing attention while the latter results in insurance quotes. In both cases, click-yield management techniques are required to optimize the available ‘click inventory’.

The insurance layer can provide a means of managing con dence from both user-centred and providercentred views. Insurance providers should then seek opportunities in linking their products and services with such transaction stream layers. The need for con dence may also generate a rapid and sustainable growth in insurance, potentially diminishing in importance the value of global brands. There is a booming of companies constituted as con dence drivers, such as Bizrate.com and @guard which may be related to the need for better con dence management approaches. Licences, endorsements, assurance credentials and insurance services are mechanisms which can also increase con dence in electronic commerce.
