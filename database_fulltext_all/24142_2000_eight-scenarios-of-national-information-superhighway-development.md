# Eight scenarios of national information superhighway development

MARIOS C. ANGELIDES and HARRY W. AGIUS

Department of Information Systems and Computing, Brunel University, Uxbridge, Middlesex UB8 3PH, UK

Leading industrialized nations are now involved in the full implementation of national information superhighways. Their efforts will depend largely on the roles and levels of involvement of public institutions and how this is perceived and reacted to by the private sector. Through the evolution of eight scenarios of public institution involvement, this paper extrapolates the consequences that each brings about in the development of a national information superhighway. In particular, this paper examines the consequences of the development of an information superhighway for the UK within the context of each scenario and draws comparisons with developments in other countries. Contrary to popular belief, we show that there is no full solution for the shortcomings experienced in the development of the UK’s information superhighway; rather, each scenario offers solutions for a subset of the shortcomings.

## Introduction

At the 1995 G7 Ministerial Conference on the Information Society in Brussels, the G7 nations agreed a statement of eight ‘core principles’, backed up with a series of pilot projects, to promote a global information infrastructure to far surpass the Internet and its likes (Marshall, 1995): promote dynamic competition, encourage private investment, de ne an adaptable regulatory framework, ensure open access to networks, ensure universal provision of and access to services, promote equality of opportunity, promote diversity of content including linguistic and cultural diversity, and recognize the need for worldwide cooperation, particularly with developing countries. These principles have since been used as guidelines for the development of national information superhighways in Europe (European Commission, 1997), North America (Berendt, 1998) and East Asia (Langdale, 1997).

Our empirical research reveals that information superhighway developments in G7 countries are a fusion of developments in four areas (Angelides and Agius, in press).

(1) Communications and protocols consists of conduit technologies, degrees of bandwidth and communications services such as asynchronous transfer mode (ATM), asymmetric digital subscriber line/loop (ADSL) and  bre-to-the-home (Reinhardt, 1994; Basile et al., 1995; Dixit, 1995; Large, 1995; Pospischil, 1995; Angelides and Dustdar, 1997).

(2) Hardware receivers and transmitters consists of hardware and user requirements and potential devices for receiving and transmitting information (Kupfer, 1994; Fox, 1995; Natarajan, 1995; Ninomiya, 1995; Braham, 1996; Reed Electronics Research, 1998).

(3) Consumer services and applications consists of core, business and specialist applications or those of innovative content (Housel and Davidson, 1991; Browning, 1994; Cane, 1994; Cats-Baril and Jelassi, 1994; Rawsthorn, 1994; Verity, 1994; Benjamin and Wigand, 1995; Blank, 1995; Cane, 1995; Lucas et al., 1995; Stiles, 1995; Ward, 1995; Bradbury, 1998).

(4) Information infrastructure is the topology of the superhighway. It consists of the broadcast models used, issues of application and conduit development, storage, communication and access of content, who the content providers and users will be and their roles and security issues (Cats-Baril and Jelassi, 1994; Cooper, 1994; Reinhardt, 1994; Dixit, 1995; Lucas et al., 1995; Sugaya, 1995; Fransman, 1997).

Unlike the Internet, where there is now very little governmental involvement (Angelides, 1997), the successful development of a national information superhighway is critically dependent on the levels of involvement of public institutions in their roles as consultants, regulators and users in each of the above areas, and how this is perceived and reacted to by the private sector (Department of Trade and Industry, 1991, 1994; Housel and Davidson, 1991; Crandall, 1992; Noam,

1992; Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development, 1992; Clinton and Gore, 1993; Cats-Baril and Jelassi, 1994; Central Computer and Telecommunications Agency, 1994; Commission of the European Communities, 1994; Frazier, 1994; Grif th, 1994; Harvard Law Review, 1994; Kettinger, 1994; Lewyn, 1994; Of ce of Telecommunications, 1994, 1995; Telecommunications Council, 1994; Blackman and Preston, 1995; Gershon and Kanayama, 1995; Ghertman and Quélin, 1995; Gore, 1995; Harper, 1995; Hatta and Takeuchi, 1995; Heldman, 1995; Information Highway Advisory Council, 1995; Schement, 1995; Schoechle, 1995; Schoof and Brown, 1995; Stiles, 1995; Van Cuilenburg and Slaa, 1995; Ward, 1995; Carlson, 1996; Miller, 1996; Omura, 1997; Nihoul, 1998). The intensity of involvement of each institution within these three roles depends on interactions with other key players. Consequently, there are several combinations of involvement in each area of development, with each combination resulting in a different development scenario.

The objectives of this paper are twofold. First, it aims to present our analysis of the  ndings of the empirical work undertaken and, in particular, that which relates to the three roles for public institution involvement in national information superhighway developments. Second, it aims to discuss the application of these scenarios to developments within the UK in comparison with developments in other countries, in order to determine the consequences of each scenario which result from the interaction of public institutions in the three roles and the reaction of the private sector.

## Research method

## Data collection strategy

A multiple case studies approach was used as the primary data collection strategy for this research. Data were gathered from G7 countries, through both interviews with government advisers, departments of trade, national telecommunications agencies and national information infrastructure task forces, and through academic research. All the initial interviews were conducted immediately after the end of the G7 Ministerial Conference on the Information Society, which took place in February 1995 in Brussels. This enabled us to cue the interviewees’ responses to the conduct of the conference that had just been concluded. The initial interviews helped both to unwind the roles public institutions may serve in the development of an information superhighway and to identify those public institutions which have or could serve in those roles in each G7 country.

Three roles were established: consultant, regulator and user. In the case of the UK, the matching public institutions are the Central Computer and Telecommunications Agency (CCTA) for the role of consultant, the Department of Trade and Industry (DTI) and the Of ce of Telecommunications (OFTEL) for the role of regulator, and most major government departments for the role of user. Structured interviews were then conducted with key members of the public institutions identi ed for the purpose of developing, initially, a deeper insight into the issues and contextual forces of national information superhighway developments and, potentially, a framework for investigation and presentation. While each of us independently gathered data on and from the public institutions, the structured interviewing technique ensured consistency in data collection across the board. We spent several months interviewing in G7 countries and the data collection process lasted over 18 months.

At the end of the structured interviews, a factor analysis and re nement of the interview data revealed four underlying areas in national information superhighway developments which served as the framework for further analysing the  ndings in the combined context of the three roles of public institution involvement: communications and protocols, hardware receivers and transmitters, consumer services and applications, and information infrastructure.

## Data analysis strategy

The data gathered during the interviews were analysed using qualitative data analysis techniques recommended by Miles and Huberman (1984). First, after completing the interviews, word-processed summaries of the interviews were given to the interviewees for feedback, corrections and the  lling in of any gaps that may have resulted. Once all data from the various advisers, departments, task forces and national telecommunications agencies had been analysed and documented using the resulting framework, a crosscountry analysis was performed for the purpose of supporting our investigation and for cross-reference analysis.

Although each public institution makes a unique contribution towards the development of a nationa information superhighway, it does not exist in a vacuum. The role each institution plays depends on the level of involvement of the other key players. This was highly evident from the data collected. Therefore, any investigation regarding the development of a national information superhighway needs to consider collectively the level of involvement of each player in each area of development. Since there are three roles and various levels of involvement for each role, there are thus several possible combinations of involvement in each area of development.

After an individual investigation of each role in the following section, the paper then investigates the development of an information superhighway for the UK in each area of development and with each combination of public institution involvement. This investigation is an amalgamation of current developments and problems and potential solutions and lessons drawn from other G7 countries.

## Three roles for public institutions

There are three roles for public institutions which impact on the development of an information superhighway: consultant, regulator and user. In the role of consultant, public institutions serve as a source of knowledge and expertise and lead national efforts in the development of an information superhighway. In the case of the UK, the role of consultant is ful lled by the CCTA. As a regulator, public institutions control the environment in which these developments take place. In the UK, this role is undertaken by the DTI and OFTEL. In the role of user, public institutions implement and use superhighway applications. Any public institution may be a user. For example, the Inland Revenue, Department of the Environment and Department of Health (as well as the CCTA, DTI and OFTEL) may all implement and use UK superhighway applications. Public institutions undertake all three roles and the intensity of their involvement in each role may be high or low. This yields eight possible permutations, each of which is a scenario of institutional involvement, implementation of policy, and private sector reaction.

## Public institutions as consultants

As consultants, public institutions can either serve the low role of facilitator or the high role of leader. As a facilitator, public institutions allow the market to develop and control the information superhighway, leaving much scope for individual projects; as a leader, they coordinate information superhighway development within a macro-plan, thus serving as ‘product champions’. The role of public institutions as macrocoordinators has been important in in uencing the direction of the information superhighway in other countries (International Telecommunications Union, 1996). For example, the role of the Information Infrastructure Task Force in the United States, France Télécom’s research and development laboratories, the Centre Nationale d’Etudes des Télécommunications (CNET) in France, the Post, Telephone and

Telegraph (PTT) Ministry in Germany and the Ministry of Posts and Telecommunications (MPT) in Japan.

The CCTA serves the role of macro-coordinator in the UK. As one CCTA of cer informed us with great conviction ‘. . . the CCTA is championing developments in the information superhighway for the UK and we are encouraging wide government and commercial participation . . .’. However, the CCTA has been weak in this role compared to similar institutions in other countries, particularly because its power has been diluted by the interests of other UK public institutions. The CCTA views the responsibility of government as improving public services through the application of information systems. It suggests that consultation should be held with other public and private institutions about the possibilities for improved services and open government through the exploitation of the information superhighway (Central Computer and Telecommunications Agency, 1994).

## Public institutions as regulators

Through the regulation of information superhighway developments, public institutions cater for the interests of four groups: information users, information providers, information investors and national and global organizations. Information users are given access to services at an affordable price and offered protection against exploitation of their intellectual property and personal data. Information providers are allowed non-discriminatory access to the superhighway for the provision of consumer services and applications and are protected from abuse by other dominant operators. Information investors are assured of a stable regulatory environment because of the high capital sums and long pay-back periods involved in the  nancing of developments (Jameson, 1996). National and global organizations are provided with a stable telecommunications infrastructure which meets social, cultural and commercial obligations (International Telecommunications Union, 1996).

High regulation by public institutions sustains a monopoly, duopoly or oligopoly in the market for information superhighway development. Where this is the case, the main monopolizing organization is typically the national PTT. High regulation offers protection to information users, but may not be optimal in terms of access, service and price levels. For information providers, a monopoly, duopoly or oligopoly results in abuse from and discrimination by the monopolizing organizations whose operating ef ciency and experience exceeds their own. In addition, their involvement is restricted, which limits their ability to provide innovative services. Most of the service innovation is thus initiated by the monopolizing organizations. Information investors, although assured of a stable regulatory environment, are prevented from investing freely. National and global organizations are provided with a stable infrastructure, by which social, cultural and commercial obligations may be met (David and Steinmueller, 1996).

Low regulation by public institutions results in a deregulated market. Information users are legitimately discriminated against and are offered little protection against exploitation of their intellectual property and personal data. In addition, although free-market competition may result in price levels which are initially high, as the market stabilizes prices decrease, although not necessarily to an affordable level. Dominant information providers can exclude new entrants through predatory pricing and gain competitive advantage in one area through the use of cross-subsidies from other areas. However, because involvement is not restricted, there is market competition which results in high service innovations (Van Cuilenburg and Slaa, 1995) and allows information providers to enter new areas. For information investors the regulatory environment is unpredictable, which serves to deter many potential investors. Similarly, national and global organizations are provided with a telecommunications infrastructure that changes as rapidly as technology and business objectives change, making it dif cult to meet social and cultural obligations. For this reason, the European Union (EU) is seen as being the most appropriate regulation level for European member states (Commission of the European Communities, 1994; European Commission, 1997).

An interviewee from OFTEL seized the real ‘heart of the matter’ when they commented on regulatory environments for information superhighways: ‘On the one hand, we don’t want another unregulated Internet, but . . . at the same time, we don’t want to see a return to the monopolistic telecommunications market of the 80s. The most appropriate form of regulation would probably be a compromise between the two.’

## Public institutions as users

In the role of user, public institutions use the superhighway for the development of applications and services. High-use public institutions initiate research and  nance applications and services development. This ensures that public institutions target the ef - ciency of their operations and remain in control of public sector application and service development (Benjamin and Wigand, 1995). Commenting on recent systems implementations at the Inland Revenue, one interviewee said that the systems had ‘. . . opened our eyes to what is possible and what we want things to look like over future information networks’. Low-use public institutions are ‘observers’ which have little control over application and service development. Thus, the target of the development of applications and services may not be the ef ciency of public sector operations.

## Eight scenarios for public institution involvement in national information superhighway development

In this section, we present the eight scenarios that arise from the combination of the three public institution roles (which are summarized in Table 1) and examine each scenario in the context of the UK and in comparison with relevant developments in other countries.

## Scenario 1: ‘dominance’ (high consultation, high regulation and high use)

In this  rst scenario, the pro le of public institutions is high. They lead the development of an information superhighway through the monopolizing organizations and  nance and direct applications and services development. In this scenario, the public institutions become the ‘product champion’ collectively.

If the government, through its public institutions, adopts a dominant stance, then the information superhighway becomes one which is primarily for its own use. An example of such dominance is that of the French Government during the implementation of the Minitel network and, subsequently, during its use. However, contrary to the above, in the dominance scenario public institutions promote and sustain their interests, thus sti ing innovation in services and applications to those within its interests. With such a high degree of public institution involvement, applications and services will also be slow to change. It is thus dif - cult for any  rm to lead the  eld and so competitive advantages become harder to attain. This is further ampli ed by business being unable to commence until the infrastructure is in place, which is essentially determined by the government and so is beyond the control of individual  rms. However, the high degree of public institution involvement allows the information superhighway to develop at the necessary pace and also ensures that it reaches most areas; thus, the potential critical mass sought by information providers is assured.

In the area of communications and protocols, dominance in the UK results in public institutions using the national PTT, i.e. British Telecom (BT), for the laying of the conduits over the many independent cable operators. BT, being assured of a return on its investment, lays  bre-to-the-home to enable high bandwidth and phases out the use of ADSL and  bre/copper hybrid (Haslam and Grover, 1996). As a result, satellite communications are less intrusive because the proportion of terrestrial communications in use in the UK considerably exceeds that of satellite (Collins, 1990; Reed Electronics Research, 1998) and terrestrial communications are far less expensive and more stable.

In the area of hardware receivers and transmitters, the absence of a market-led strategy and, consequently, the use of a publicly controlled BT, results in a single, standard hardware receiver and transmitter platform available from one leading player. This ensures that the critical mass is achievable. However, a High-De nition Television (HDTV) standard remains elusive, since BT would lose the necessary critical mass by requiring the purchase of new HDTV sets by information users. Limited use of cable set-top boxes (Klingler, 1994; Bell et al., 1996; Braham, 1996) is no longer a problem as it is the use of BT’s platform that affects business interests, which will be high because of the public support BT has.

In terms of consumer services and applications, high public institution use of the information superhighway increases the number and spectrum of trials and pilots, with public sector applications and services emanating from public institutions, while information and entertainment originates from BT.

While the information infrastructure is stable, high regulation, absence of a market-led strategy and lack of competition within the market limits information investment and reduces innovation. High regulation therefore leads to stagnation in this scenario.

## Scenario 2: ‘nanny’ (high consultation, high regulation and low use)

This scenario differs from that of dominance in that public institutions exercise little control over the development of applications and services and, thus, the information superhighway is not primarily for their own use. Instead, the public institutions act as a ‘nanny’ by leading and regulating information superhighway developments, leaving the private sector to compete freely for innovative application and service development. The low use by public institutions acts as a catalyst for a high level of information investment from businesses.

As a result, applications and services are highly responsive to changes in demand and there is also high competition among information providers and investors (Benjamin and Wigand, 1995). Those applications and services prototyped during trials with information users become the most widely used after the infrastructure is in place, for the short term at least. However, without the government acting as a model user through its public institutions, information providers  nd it dif cult to generate interest for public applications and services among information users and, therefore, fail to attract a critical mass of users. Fullscale commercial activity is frustrated until the infrastructure is complete (as in the dominance scenario), which is essentially determined by the government and so is beyond the control of individual  rms.

In the UK, this scenario results in a situation resembling that of dominance. However, whereas in the dominance scenario only information and entertainment consumer services and applications originated from BT, in this scenario BT is free to compete in all areas of the consumer services and applications market. This acts as a deterrent to some information providers and investors seeking entry into the market (Jameson, 1996).

## Scenario 3: ‘laissez-faire’ (high consultation, low regulation and high use)

In the ‘laissez-faire’ scenario, the development of an information superhighway is ‘championed’ by public institutions and low regulation results in free-market competition determining who shall develop the information superhighway. However, the public institutions determine much of the information content through the  nancing and direction of applications and services development.

An example of the ‘laissez-faire’ scenario is that of the USA (Clinton and Gore, 1993; Information Infrastructure Task Force, 1993; Kettinger, 1994; Information Highway Advisory Council, 1995; Miller, 1996), the ‘birthplace’ of the information superhighway, where the federal government is not only a national ‘product champion’, but also leads developments throughout the rest of the world. The USA has a low regulatory environment in order to encourage private sector investment in the development of its information superhighway. It funds pilot applications and services through the National Telecommunication and Information Administration and the National Science Foundation (Egan, 1991). The federal government’s regulatory role is largely to advise potential information providers and investors.

Gore (1995) emphasized the need for a new political and regulatory climate within the USA:

. . . the onslaught of technology is causing  erce competition between once separate entities in telecommunications. The telephone companies want to transmit entertainment; the cable carriers are asking to get into the communications business. And businesses are turning to satellite communications carriers or installing private  ber-optic networks. All are regulated by vague, outdated, con icting, constantly changing government telecommunications policies. The result is that the private sector hesitates to jump in and make the investment. We face the classic ‘chicken and egg’ dilemma. Because there is no network, there is no apparent demand for the network; because there is no demand, there is no network (p. 159).

One US Government of cial we interviewed before the introduction of the 1996 Telecommunications Act argued the need for a higher championing of the information superhighway by the US Government,

The American people want a superhighway that allows them to conduct their business and get good quality infotainment . . . and they don’t care who provides it . . . but businesses cannot afford to invest in the development of an information superhighway when the environment is unstable and fast changing, like the Internet.

This need for stable, clear and up-to-date government policy was enshrined in the United States’ Telecommunications Act of 1996, the main objectives of which are those of deregulation and competition, within a framework of universally available and affordable applications and services. While regulation is low, the Act does state that there should be speci c, predictable and suf cient federal and state mechanisms to preserve and advance universal availability. It achieves this by legally binding telecommunications carriers who wish to provide interstate telecommunications applications and services to contribute  nancially to the preservation and advancement of those telecommunications. However, no state or local government is permitted to restrict market entry, except in some rural areas, and rural telephone companies may acquire waivers for meeting most requirements of the Act (Egan, 1996).

As a result, telephone companies and cable companies are now competing head-to-head, with both entering the markets for long-distance telecommunications and information, video and programming services. However, telephone companies are obliged to provide unbundled equal access and interconnection to the public telephone network, as well as facilities such as number portability and White Pages listings for competitors. In addition, electric utilities are now providing telephone services, and cross-ownerships between telephone, cable, radio, television and newspaper companies are also now permitted.

In the ‘laissez-faire’ scenario, although developers are chosen through competition in a free market, public institutions lead applications and services development so that the information superhighway is still primarily for their own use. The ‘laissez-faire’ scenario encourages innovation in the development of the information superhighway, not in applications and services. Thus, the promotion of public institutions self-interests sti es innovation in services and applications, making them slow to change, as was the case in the dominance scenario. Although  rms may lead the  eld in the development of the communications and protocols, hardware receivers and transmitters and information infrastructure, it is dif cult for  rms to lead in application and service development because of the high degree of control by public institutions.

Business determines the development of the infrastructure in this scenario and so it is not restricted by government intervention, as was the case in the  rst two scenarios. However, the low regulatory role of public institutions means that the information superhighway may not develop evenly across the nation and may not reach remote areas; thus, the necessary critical mass of information users is not assured.

## National information superhighway development

‘Laissez-faire’ in the UK, in terms of communications and protocols, may result in cable operators continuing to lay the conduits, with the extent of BT’s involvement uncertain. While BT may compete for development of the information superhighway, cable operators may remain in control of communications on the information superhighway. Moreover, satellite communications are able to penetrate further because of low regulation and price competition among terrestrial operators.

The lack of a publicly controlled operator leads to the emergence of a number of hardware receiver and transmitter platforms from a variety of  rms and, thus, a plethora of standards. This restricts the emergence of a leading player, making the standardization of HDTV of reduced priority. The dominance of cable operators increases the use of set-top boxes and, thus, increases business interest in television as a platform as a result.

In the area of information infrastructure, while BT may compete for the development of the information superhighway, cable operators may remain in control of communications on the information superhighway and its infrastructure. The result is a failure to provide the stable infrastructure required by national and global organizations (David and Steinmueller, 1996; International Telecommunications Union, 1996; European Commission, 1997).

Moreover, the lack of a stable regulatory environment acts as a deterrent to potential information investors in consumer services and applications.

## Scenario 4: ‘host’ (high consultation, low regulation and low use)

In this scenario, although public institutions lead information superhighway developments, the low regulation and use they exercise results in free-market competition determining who shall develop the information superhighway and consumer services and applications. Thus, the public institutions merely act as a ‘host’ for the development of an information superhighway, leaving the private sector to compete freely for innovative communications, hardware receivers and transmitters, information infrastructure and application and service development.

A host scenario for the UK has the following effects within the four areas. In communications and protocols, the host scenario results in many leading players competing for a stake in information superhighway developments through the laying of conduits according to current trends and predicted demand. All operators are free to lay  bre-to-the-home and satellite communications become increasingly pervasive as the terrestrial operators compete among themselves. This scenario may result in more than one information superhighway, each controlled by a different type of operator (e.g. cable, telephone, television and satellite). For example, Energis, a company which is owned by the 12 regional electricity companies in England and Wales, has already constructed a nationwide  breoptic network along the power grid (Steinbrink, 1994; Leung, 1995).

The existence of many superhighways results in no de nitive hardware receiver and transmitter platform and no HDTV standard. With business determining the development of the infrastructure, no restrictions are imposed through government intervention. However, there is an increased interest in television as a platform because of the high involvement of the cable companies.

The many leading players also compete through the development of various consumer services and applications, in which there is therefore a high degree of innovation. However, the result is that the majority of applications and services development is information and entertainment centred, with very little development of public sector applications.

Without a stable information infrastructure, many potential information investors are deterred from entering the market unless leading players seek to form cross-industry cartels and mergers for stability. This scenario is closest to the current situation within the UK and we are now beginning to witness cartels and mergers between the television, telephone, cable and satellite operators. For example, with Cable & Wireless’ merger with Telewest and NTL (which may also involve Deutsche Telekom and France Telecom) (Thapar, 1999), we can expect this new almagamation to take over as the leading provider of applications and services in the UK. As one government analyst argued during an interview, ‘We don’t have national oil companies anymore. Why on earth should we have national phone companies? Since this superhighway will primarily carry business and personal information applications, the private sector should develop this.’

## Scenario 5: ‘coercion’ (low consultation, high regulation and high use)

The combination of low consultation with high regulation in the  fth scenario sees the responsibility for initiating and leading developments of an information superhighway transferred from public institutions to the monopolizing organizations. Thus, the public institutions do not act as ‘product champions’ and they do not lead efforts or follow a macro-plan; instead, the monopolizing organizations develop and lead the information superhighway efforts and they determine the infrastructure exclusively (International

Telecommunications Union, 1996; Jameson, 1996). In their low consultative role as a facilitator, public institutions coordinate these efforts. In this scenario, the primary objective of public institutions is to improve public sector applications and services, while development is undertaken by the monopolizing organizations. Thus, the monopolizing organizations are coerced into assuming the leadership role that the public institutions would have had if their consultative role were high, as is the case in the dominance scenario.

Coercion in the UK, in terms of communications and protocols, results in BT laying  bre-to-the-home and phasing out ASDL and  bre/copper hybrid, as in the dominance scenario. The proportion of terrestrial communications in use increases, thus limiting the penetration of satellite communications.

A standard hardware receiver and transmitter platform is provided through BT, which ensures that a critical mass is at least achievable. Consequently, as in the dominance scenario, there is no HDTV standard. However, unlike the dominance scenario, in this scenario the use of cable set-top boxes diminishes as BT aggressively promotes its own hardware receiver and transmitter. Business interest thus remains low in television as a platform since new information providers and information investors are deterred by the dominance exercised by BT through its standard.

Although high regulation and high use by public institutions results in information infrastructure stability, competition is limited and, thus, innovation is sti ed. As in the dominance scenario, high use by public institutions increases the variety of pilot consumer applications and services.

## Scenario 6: ‘laissez-passer’ (low consultation, high regulation and low use)

The ‘laissez-passer’ scenario differs from coercion in that public institutions do not seek to lead the research and development of applications and services and, thus, the private sector competes freely for innovative application and service development. Consequently, the information superhighway may not serve the interests of the public institutions. Instead, public institutions only serve to regulate information superhighway developments in order to ensure that the interests of information users, information providers, information investors and national and global organizations are safeguarded.

Moreover, the low use stimulates a high level of information investment resulting in high competition among information providers and investors and, thus, applications and services that are highly responsive to changes in demand. However, public institutions do not ful l their roles as leaders and model users and, thus, information providers  nd information-user interest lacking in public applications and services. They therefore fail to attract a critical mass of users.

This scenario is typical of German efforts, where low consultation and government use, combined with high regulation, enabled Deutsche Telekom to play a leading role in the construction of Germany’s information superhighway (infobahn). Fibre-in-the-loop was installed on a large scale across both eastern and western Germany (Pospischil, 1995) and the  bre-optic network was built up through the introduction of broadband communication services (Steinbrink, 1994). Deutsche Telekom’s telephone based informationservices presence makes a hybrid television/telephone hardware receiver and transmitter most likely (Lai and Reeh, 1995). Despite the deregulation of the telecommunications markets in Europe, Deutsche Telekom is still three-quarters state-owned. However, the lack of experience by Deutsche Telekom in open markets has meant that it has lost a large slice of market share and revenues. One German Government of cial we interviewed, predicted this loss:

Here in Germany, the centralization of our telecommunications services has survived for many years . . . but when, as we expect, telecommunications markets are opened up in Europe, Deutsche Telekom will have to quickly recognize, and react to, the implications of competition . . . or face losses . . . The German people are very interested in enhanced information and entertainment services and if Deutsche Telekom cannot provide it, then there are many cable [and telephone] providers in Germany that can.

In the UK, the ‘laissez-passer’ scenario is similar to that of coercion, except that there is now room for competition in consumer applications and services and, thus, scope for innovation, but there are few plans for the development of public sector applications because of low use. However, BT is now also able to compete in all areas of the applications and services market. With BT in control of the hardware receiver and transmitter platform and the information infrastructure, it is able to protect its market interests and deter other information providers and investors from entering the market. This is similar to when the government  rst lifted the telecommunications monopoly on BT to allow other operators to compete in providing telephone infrastructures and services when BT’s domination did not decrease signi cantly.

## Scenario 7: ‘minimalism’ (low consultation, low regulation and high use)

In this scenario, public institutions play no signi- cant role in the development and regulation of the

information superhighway other than to facilitate research and development. The resulting market is likely to be very competitive, making strategic advantages dif cult to attain. Thus, the forming of alliances between the operators and information investors becomes an attractive business opportunity and also a necessity for survival in the market (Carlson, 1996). Therefore, minimalism may lead inevitably to the development of more than one information superhighway.

The minimalism scenario re ects the least effect of the public institutions on the superhighway while still permitting some role. The development of the infrastructure is determined by a market-led strategy; however, low regulation leads to uneven national development, thus challenging the achievement of the necessary critical mass of information users. In this scenario, the public institutions initiate research and  nance applications and services development to ensure an increase in public sector ef ciency and so they remain in control of the developed applications and services.

Japan’s efforts are typical of the minimalism scenario. The Japanese Ministry of Posts and Telecommunications (MPT) facilitates competition between telecommunications and cable companies’ infrastructures and assists in establishing government and business partnerships (Telecommunications Council, 1994). This market-led strategy has resulted in many hardware receivers and transmitters, including enhanced- and improved-de nition television sets, personal digital assistants (PDAs), car navigation systems, multimedia home terminals, set-top boxes and cable television servers. In addition, the MPT is developing and introducing public sector applications and services. These high-pro le applications and services have dampened the development of business applications and services. As is expected in the minimalism scenario, Japan has a number of competing information superhighways, including satellite, telephone, terrestrial television and  bre-optic cable (Ishizuka, 1994; Nakamoto, 1994; Gershon and Kanayama, 1995; Hatta and Takeuchi, 1995; Ninomiya, 1995; Stiles, 1995; Sugaya, 1995; Van der Staal et al., 1995; Fransman, 1997; Omura, 1997). However, one vision in Japan dominates: ‘All business and consumer applications should be delivered primarily through television, with access also through mobile devices, such as watches, mobile phones and PDAs’.

Minimalism in the UK means that, in terms of communications and protocols, cable operators may continue to lay the conduits, together with BT, while satellite communications penetrate deeper, as is the case in the ‘laissez-faire’ scenario. However, unlike the ‘laissez-faire’ scenario, where UK Government institutions served as product champions and coordinated developments, in the minimalism UK scenario the cable and satellite operators and BT are left to champion their own information superhighways, with the public institutions simply facilitating developments. Thus, minimalism leads to an unstable UK information infrastructure and regulatory environment which deters potential information investors.

The lack of a publicly controlled operator together with a lack of public institution leadership also leads to the emergence of a number of hardware receiver and transmitter platforms and, thus, no uniform standard. This reduces the necessity for HDTV standardization, as was the case in the ‘laissez-faire’ scenario. With cable operators increasing their dominance and, thus, the use of set-top boxes, business interest in television as a platform is increased as a result.

For consumer services and applications, the high use by the public institutions increases the development of public sector applications and increases the overall number and variety of trials. UK public institutions have the choice of more than one information superhighway on which to run their applications and services, and, therefore, cable and satellite operators and BT are unsure of exactly who will be delivering these applications and services to information users and on which superhighway they will be delivered.

## Scenario 8: ‘lacklustre’ (low consultation, low regulation and low use)

The  nal scenario sees public institutions adopting no signi cant role in national information superhighway efforts. Low regulation means that any interested operator and information investor or alliance of operators (as in the minimalism scenario) may pursue the development of the information superhighway, which is facilitated by the public institutions. The public institutions themselves simply act as observers to the development of applications and services. This scenario is not one where there is lack of interest, but one where the public institutions elect to ‘sit back’, such that the development of a superhighway and of applications and services is entirely market led.

The lacklustre scenario for the UK leaves all willing operators (including cable, terrestrial and satellite operators and BT) free to lay conduits and compete for a market share via communications and protocols. With no leading players, the result is a plethora of platforms and standards for hardware receivers and transmitters and, thus, a lack of a de nitive HDTV standard. However, there is increased interest in television as a platform because of the high involvement by cable operators and the wide variety of choices offered to information users reduces the market share that each information investor and provider may achieve. Thus, the crucial critical mass of users becomes dif cult to achieve.

The increased competition leads to innovation in all information infrastructure developments, consumer applications and services development and use of the information superhighway. The number of trials and pilots thus increases, but these may not necessarily include public sector applications and services. The establishment of applications and services is thus demand led, instead of being led by the information users’ needs (Bubley and Bennett, 1994).

## Conclusions

The discussion within each of the eight scenarios has highlighted a number of shortcomings in national information superhighway development within the UK. These are summarized in Table 2.

Figure 1 shows which of the shortcomings in Table 2 are met and which remain unresolved, as they arise from the discussion within each scenario.

In the dominance scenario, shortcomings 8 and 14 remain unresolved. The high level of government intervention results in no open competition and, thus, a lack of innovation. This level of intervention assumes a publicly controlled BT with a single, cost-effective, standard hardware receiver and transmitter platform for the critical mass of information users, which cannot, therefore, be an expensive HDTV.

In the nanny scenario, shortcomings 8 and 14 continue to prevail, although the private sector is now free to compete in the development of applications and services. However, shortcomings 5 and 12 also prevail. The low use role played by public institutions results in few plans for the development of public sector applications. Consequently, while high regulation should ensure a critical mass of information users, low use discourages interest for public applications and services among them.

The low regulation in the ‘laissez-faire’ scenario leaves the majority of shortcomings unresolved. Freemarket competition encourages cable operators to continue laying the conduits and leaves satellite communications free to expand further, both of which discourage BT from investing in  bre-to-the-home. The variety of players results in a non-uniform infrastructure and many standards and development platforms, thus disorienting information users and restricting the emergence of a leading player. This leaves the future of an HDTV standard uncertain.

The host scenario leaves an additional shortcoming (number 12) to those in the ‘laissez-faire’ scenario unresolved. Because of the low use role, public institutions do not promote the development of public sector applications and services.

The low consultation of the coercion scenario adds an additional two shortcomings to that of dominance (numbers 9 and 10). The prevalence of BT’s hardware receiver and transmitter limits the use of cable set-top boxes and, thus, discourages business interest in television as a platform.

The ‘laissez-passer’ scenario is similar to that of coercion, but with an additional shortcoming that remains unresolved (number 12) because of the low use, which results in limited plans for the development of public sector applications and limited scope for competition and innovation in applications and services only.

Minimalism compares with the ‘laissez-faire’ scenario except that the combined effect of low regulation and consultation creates an unstable regulatory environment which deters information investors. This leaves shortcoming 14 unresolved, since the reduced number of investors reduces the level of competition and, thus, sti es innovation.

Similarly, the lacklustre scenario compares with the host scenario except that, as with the minimalism scenario, the combined effect of low regulation with low consultation creates the unstable regulatory environment that leaves shortcoming 14 unresolved.

The future of HDTV is placed in serious doubt from the  ndings of this research, since none of the scenarios accommodate its standardization. This is not surprising considering that standardization of HDTV has been a problem throughout the G7 nations (Basile et al., 1995; Fox, 1995; Large, 1995; Ninomiya, 1995; Bell et al., 1996; Berendt, 1998; Reed Electronics Research, 1998). It appears that none of the scenarios achieve a full solution for the UK’s information superhighway. Some of these scenarios may offer a short-term solution for other countries, but all G7 countries experience their own shortcomings (European Commission, 1997; Omura, 1997; Nihoul, 1998).

As a result of the recent liberalization of many telecommunications markets in the world, scenarios 1, 2, 5 and 6 are unlikely to prevail for much longer and those countries that have been building their superhighways within those scenarios are likely to  nd themselves isolated within the global market, unless they shift into one of the other four scenarios. However, on the one hand, although a high-regulation scenario, such as the dominance scenario, solves more of the problems of the UK than the low-regulation scenarios (as shown in Figure 1) and led to great success in the case of the French Minitel system, it nevertheless led to reduced quality and level of service in telecommunications in the UK during the 1980s. On the other hand, in those nations where regulation is currently low, e.g. the USA, UK and Japan, satellite penetration rates have tended to increase, thus making a satellite-based infrastructure a serious challenger to the more prevalent terrestrial  bre-based counterpart, with high bandwidth and interactive many-to-many multicasting.

The UK has currently been operating mainly within the host scenario. However, since the current UK Central Government has begun to shift its focus away from developing the national information superhighway to the study of divisions in ‘information classes’ in order to try to soothe the problem of social exclusions, there have been indications that it may also fall into any of the remaining three low-regulation scenarios (lacklustre, minimalism and ‘laissez-faire’). The study assesses the levels of use and accessibility of the public sector applications and services that the government is introducing through the Internet (as a pilot for the applications and services it will introduce on the information superhighway). This may shift the UK into a ‘high-use’ category, in which case, combined with a revived championing by the CCTA, it will see the UK moving into the ‘laissez-faire’ scenario, i.e. into the scenario of the USA. Without a revived championing by the CCTA, this would see the UK moving into the minimalist scenario of Japan.

The study, however, was the personal responsibility of ex-state secretary Peter Mandelson and currently there are no clear indications that this study will conclude in favour of high use, if it concludes at all. Should the study fail to bear this fruit, the UK will fast fall into the lacklustre scenario, at which it is currently teetering over. The initiation of the study saw the consultative role shifting from the CCTA to central government. While this has given the information superhighway higher visibility, the role has been taken away from an information systems specialist centre towards the more generic central government. With the huge number of tasks central government has to undertake, championing of the information superhighway could easily become diluted or gain reduced priority, as other more politically pressing issues (e.g. health and education) arise.

The DTI and OFTEL are currently busy overseeing the widespread application of deregulation in the telecommunications market in the  rst instance before moving into the information superhighways market. This role, combined with low regulation, helps the industry and, in particular, the cable, telephone and satellite companies in the UK to form alliances to get on with the business of determining the future ‘ avour of the information superhighway. However, without one dominating ‘ avour’, the end result is likely to be Neapolitan! In this case, infotainment is more than likely to dominate in the home, with basic on-line consumer information management services such as shopping and banking.

The increased UK interest in the development of a national information superhighway since the  rst G7 meeting,  rst championed by the CCTA and now by central government, along with the recent study, suggest that it will not be surprising if the UK settles within the US-style ‘laissez-faire’ scenario. However, this is not to say that the end results for the two countries will be the same. There are some stark differences between the US and the UK information superhighways at this time.

First, the USA has always operated comfortably within the ‘laissez-faire’ scenario, whereas the UK information superhighway began its life in the host scenario and looks likely to shift from that to ‘laissezfaire’. Second, pilot applications and services in the USA are funded jointly by the National Telecommunication and Information Administration, the National Science Foundation and the private sector, whereas, in the UK, funding of pilot applications and services by the government is lacking, leaving private investment to carry the full weight of this task. Therefore, the emphasis is on commercial applications and services. Third, although both the USA and UK have increasing satellite penetration rates, these are far higher in the UK (and Europe as a whole) than they are in the USA, therefore making a satellite infrastructure a much more conceivable alternative in the UK and Europe than it is in the USA. Fourth, in the USA, the vice president has championed the information superhighway consistently from the very beginning, whereas, in the UK, there is no such leading  gure and the change of government in May 1997 has seen the shifting of the championing task from the CCTA to central government and then diluting it into a study that has yet to conclude. Fifth, while both countries have deregulated telecommunications markets, the USA was speedy in introducing relevant legislation in 1996 in anticipation of the early-1997 worldwide deregulation ‘pact’, in order to set in place a suitable political and regulatory environment quickly; whereas the UK has stood  rm in not wishing to review its present situation until 2001. Until then, BT may neither compete fully with other providers, nor develop a national information superhighway in the G7 sense.
