# An exploratory study of information systems in support of employee empowerment

ANNA PSOINOS

PricewaterhouseCoopers N.V, Management Consultants, Archimedeslaan 21, PO Box 8283, 3503 RG Utrecht, The Netherlands

THOMAS KERN

Department of Information and Decision Sciences, F1± 16, Erasmus University Rotterdam, Burgmeester Oudlaan 50, PO Box 1736, 3000 DR Rotterdam, The Netherlands

STEVE SMITHSON

Information Systems Department, London School of Economics and Political Science, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, UK

The notion of employee empowerment in today’s organizations is becoming increasingly important with the growing degree of responsibility individual employees hold. This paper presents the ® ndings of a longitudinal study which examined the relationship between computer-based information systems (IS) and empowerment in detail drawing heavily on empirical work carried out in the British manufacturing industry The paper elaborates and discusses the conceptual issues which are relevant to the idea of empowerment and its relationship with IS, before determining empowerment to be in essence the decentralization of decision-making authority to lower levels of the organization. The lack of any research on this topic led us to carry out a combination of empirical research. A large-scale postal survey of 450 UK manufacturing companies followed by a series of 20 in-depth interviews in 18 top UK manufacturing organizations formed the basis for this paper. The ® ndings con® rmed that empowerment is indeed pursued by many UK manufacturing organizations within their various efforts at improving their organization of work. The research participants viewed IS as an important enabling tool for empowerment offering many opportunities, but clearly noted that the role of IS in this is supportive rather than initiating; IS do not lead to employees becoming empowered. However, the interviews revealed that manufacturing organizations are facing a wide range of problems and dif® culties with their existing IS raising a number of constraints. The paper concludes with a number of practical suggestions for using IS as a supportive means for empowering employees.

## Introduction

This research explores the support which computerbased information systems (IS) provide to employees in manufacturing organizations which are adopting employee empowerment (Byham and Cox 1991; Bowen and Lawler, 1992; Hammonds, 1994; Mills, 1995; Blanchard et al., 1996). Empowerment is one of the management ideas which recently received signi® cant attention due to the impact it can have on an organization’s effectiveness (Conger and Kanungo, 1988; Vogt and Murrell, 1990; Ketchum and Trist, 1992; Clutterbuck, 1994; Ehin, 1995; Mills, 1995; Blanchard et al., 1996; Jenkins, 1996; Malone, 1997; Argyris, 1998). It is evident that the encouragement of empowerment in an organization in¯ uences many organizational aspects, but primarily work practices. Information systems (IS) and information technology (IT) applications are introduced in an organization with the goal of facilitating the work and functions of employees. As organizations continually change to keep up with the market dynamics, the work and functions of employees change too. This research sets out to assess empirically how existing IS support these different work practices. By IS in this paper we refer to \`the means by which organisations and people, utilising information technologies, gather, process, store, use and disseminate information’ (UKAIS, 1997, p. 5).

As information is one of the necessary elements for employee empowerment (Kanter, 1984; Bowen and Lawler, 1992), the role which IS can play in empowerment seems intuitively important. However, although the importance of information as a resource has been pointed out, there is relatively little literature which explicitly links empowerment and IS (Brousell, 1992; Clement, 1994; Wareham et al., 1997a). This research aims to address this gap in the literature by examining the ways in which IS can support employees in their enlarged responsibilities. As we wanted to avoid the overly optimistic and technologically deterministic accounts of the potential of IT which have characterized much of the hype surrounding IT and IS yet which seem quite remote from the everyday reality of many organizations, this research focused on how existing and established IS ful® l the new requirements. Hence, the research aimed to identify whether and how existing IS in manufacturing organizations support the work practices created by the adoption of empowerment.

Existing research suggests that empowering management practices are adopted more frequently in manufacturing than in service ® rms and their impact seems to be stronger than that in service ® rms (Bowen and Lawler, 1992, 1995). In addition, historically, manufacturing ® rms have been the leading adopters of employee empowerment practices (Lawler et al., 1992; Stewart, 1992) and, therefore, can provide a mature context for their study. Furthermore, the manufacturing industry constitutes an important application area for the study of IS. Yet, we join others in noting that it has received little attention compared to other areas in the services industry, such as banking for example. Kling et al. (1992) noted that

Unfortunately, it [manufacturing] has not been well studied empirically by information systems researchers. Given the crucial importance of manufacturing to national economic welfare, the lack of detailed research on the effective role of IT for facilitating high performance manufacturing constitutes a serious shortcoming in our ® eld (p. 39).

Therefore the role of IS in the support of empowerment in manufacturing is a topic which appears extremely important and of relevance to IS and one which has not yet been explored. For management the issue at stake is harnessing the technology for supporting empowerment and being aware of the potential management and organizational changes the introduction of IS may entail in order to support employees in their empowered roles.

This paper, in turn, examines the relationship between empowerment and IS, drawing extensively on the existing management and IS literature. In the ® rst section we discuss the way the empowerment concept has been developed theoretically and identify its main weakness. Consequently we put forward our view of empowerment, which forms the basis of this research and which also leads us to the next section in which we assess the theoretical relationship between empowerment and IS. The resulting conceptual dimensions we identify as de® ning the main relationships between empowerment and IS are then used as the basis to investigate empirically the support of IS in empowerment in British manufacturing organiza tions in the fourth section. In the ® fth section we discuss the ® ndings and elicit the constraints and opportunities which arise with IS support for empowerment before rounding off the paper with a number of management considerations.

## Empowerment ± a different management paradigm

Empowerment as an identi® able concept originated approximately 10 years ago from social work studies (Mullender and Ward, 1991; Humphries, 1996). Since then it has been de® ned in various ways in the business and management literature:

Its central meaning is to enable people to do things that they would otherwise be unable to do. It means to remove the restrictions ± arti® cial or otherwise ± that prevent people from doing the things that it is within their ability to achieve (Jenkins, 1996, p. 37).

Empowerment is, in essence, the transfer of power within organisations from top management to middle management and so on, all the way to the front line employees (Clutterbuck, 1994, p. 12).

Empowering people means encouraging them to become more involved in the decisions and activi ties that affect their jobs. It means providing them with the opportunity to show that they can come up with good ideas and that they have the skills to put these ideas into practice (Smith 1996, p. 9).

The roots can easily be traced back to the sociotechnical systems approach (Trist and Bamforth, 1951; Emery, 1969; Cherns, 1976; Emery and Thorsrud, 1976; Pugh and Hickson, 1989; Ketchum and Trist, 1992), to the Scandinavian participative approach and the industrial democracy movement of the 1970s (French et al., 1960; Blumberg, 1968; Emery and Thorsrud, 1976; Sandberg, 1982) and even further to the human relations school founded by Elton Mayo in the late 1920s (Mayo, 1933, 1949).

Nowadays, there are various practice-driven case studies available from around the world, describing in essence how organizations promote empowerment. For example, the Brasilian Semco (Semler 1993), Xerox’ s well-documented Leadership Through Quality programme (Kearns and Nadler, 1992; Walker, 1992), Gaines’ pet food plant in Kansas (Ketchum and Trist, 1992) and Volvo’s Uddevalla plant (Berggren, 1994). The list is continually growing and pervading numerous industries.

## IS in support of employee empowerment

Empowerment is said to have a range of effects, depending on the industry and organization. In manufacturing empowerment is characterized by its work design which is noted as being a radical break with Taylorism and Fordism (Berggren, 1994). Team autonomy and the decentralization of decision authority to the shop¯ oor teams are central features (Shipper and Manz, 1992). Work design is usually coupled by open internal communication (Pacanowsky, 1988), a ¯ atter hierarchical structure, highly skilled employees (Adler and Cole, 1993; Berggren, 1994) and an environment of trust. The result for many employees is increased job satisfaction, high morale and a drive for innovation and taking initiatives. As empowerment becomes more popular, many successful cases have been reported which \`® t’ the former characteristics in general terms (e.g. Kanter, 1984; Dumaine, 1992; Ketchum and Trist, 1992; Shrednick et al., 1992; Gleckman, 1993; Zellner, 1994).

However, not all implementations have been successful. As the ideas ® nd more application, problems begin to arise. Dif® culties were reported in an empowerment effort which faced the strong resistance of senior management (Rothstein, 1995). More recently, Argyris (1998) claimed that, despite all the attention and effort paid to it, empowerment has not delivered the bene® ts it promised and still remains mostly an \`illusion’. This failure is attributed to the traditional management systems and their contradictions with the empowerment philosophy and to the change programmes which are usually followed in order to introduce empowerment, which increase these contradictions. In summary, many researchers argue that empowerment seems to be more of a myth rather than a reality in actual practice (Claydon and Doyle, 1996).

These myths were in essence identi® ed as a result of three reccurring ambiguities. Firstly, the term has often been used for rhetorical purposes, as a catch all for solving all ills within the organization and is therefore not well understood (see Conger and Kanungo, 1988; Mullender and Ward, 1991; Mondros and Wilson, 1994; Russ and Millam, 1995). Secondly, it is often not clear in theory and practice whether there is a difference between \`participation’ (French et al., 1960; Marchington, 1980; Mumford, 1983; Russell, 1988; Tausky and Chelte, 1988), \`involvement’ (Millward et al., 1992) and \`empowerment’ or whether they are al synonymous (Batstone, 1984; Marchington et al., 1993). Thirdly, there are practical inconsistencies and confusion in the implementation of empowerment, as it is often considered to be an integral part of a business process re-engineering (BPR) or total quality management (TQM) change initiative, yet it is not exactly clear how it is operationalized (Lawler et al., 1992; Sewell and Wilkinson, 1992; Hammer and Champy, 1993; Bowen and Lawler, 1995; Psoinos, 1998). Together these ambiguities and myths surrounding empowerment demonstrate an urgent need for clari® cation.

## To be empowered or not ± what do we mean and how does IS ® t in?

Empowerment in its original meaning, to empower, means to \`authorize, give power to’ (Tulloch, 1993). Accordingly, we argue that central to the concept of empowerment is indeed the delegation of power to staff/employees in order to make and implement their own decisions. These decision-making responsibilities range from those which are invariably involved in task execution to those involved in task design. Thus, employees assume a greater degree of authority in controlling the coordinating, allocative, improving and control functions associated with their tasks.

Although as Argyris (1998) rightly noted, empowerment is a goal which organizations can work towards, approximate and may never quite reach, we believe it is critical that attempts at empowerment are focused on decentralizing decision-making authority to employees. By de® ning empowerment as the decentralization of decision-making authority we can capture the broader changes which are necessary for empow erment to be anything more than rhetoric. In empowerment the decisions made by workers do not merely concern the internal regulation of the system but may go far beyond that. Hence, empowerment challenges the effectiveness of traditional management roles and the organizational structures.

A change towards empowerment in turn involves several initiatives for altering structures, procedures, relationships and culture (Neumann et al., 1995). According to our research the best way of approaching the necessary changes for operationalizing empowerment is to follow what most current supporters of empowerment proclaim as being the four core aspects which characterize empowering organizations: the provision of information, power, knowledge and rewards to employees (Bowen and Lawler, 1992; Bridges, 1994; Lawler et al., 1992). (These core aspects are obviously related to the various means by which organizations have attempted to enhance employee interest and commitment to management goals in the past. For example, employee reports which aimed to provide company information directly to employee have been popular since the 1970s (Marchington et al., 1993; Batstone, 1984).) We strongly argue that, to enforce these changes, an effective and appropriate

IS could support the necessary distribution of information and knowledge:

Lots of companies talk about empowering their employees ± giving them all this authority and responsibility ± but if they don’t have the information to back up the responsibility and the authority, they don’t have the where-withal to act (Brousell, 1992, p. 121).

It is on this potential relationship between IS and empowerment that we will focus in the remainder of this paper.

## The relationship between IS and empowerment

Although recently a few papers have emerged which deal explicitly with IS and empowerment, they are each the outcome of a long line of work in IS and various related aspects of organizations. This work can be divided into three broad categories. The ® rst addresses the impact of IT/IS on the structural elements of organizations and, therefore, approaches organizational issues at a macro level (Swanson, 1987; Markus and Robey, 1988; Orlikowski and Robey, 1991). The second looks at the impact of IT/IS on individuals and, thus, focuses at a micro level. These two directions are the result of the strong divisions between structuralism and objectivism which have characterized the social sciences in this century (Giddens, 1984; Orlikowski and Robey, 1991). Finally, the third focuses on recent technological developments which complement empowerment.

## Macro level ± IS and decentralization

New ITs are suggested to lead to decentralization and empowerment. Recent studies by Wyner and Malone (1996) and Malone (1997) discussed a model linking IT and the structure of organizational decision making, which provides support for the argument that new IT are leading to decentralization and empowerment. However, these papers are only the most recent interpretations in a long line of work examining the impact of IT on the structure of organizational decision making which was set off by Leavitt and Whisler’s work in 1958 (see, e.g. Emery, 1975; Grochla and Szyperski, 1975; Attewell and Rule, 1984; Huber and McDaniel, 1986; Huber, 1990; Wijnhoven and Wassenaar, 1990; Gurbaxani and Whang, 1991; Ang and Pavri, 1994).

The four key positions in the centralization debate were nicely summarized by George and King (1991) as (1) computerization causes centralization, (2)

computerization causes decentralization, (3) decisionmaking structure is determined by factors other than computerization (known as the \`no inherent relationship’ view) and ® nally, (4) computerization re¯ ects rather than causes (de)centralization as organization shape their computerization efforts to conform to their pre-existing structures. Based on a \`managerial action imperative’ model which takes into account the external, historical and cultural constraints, they recognized a tendency towards the use of computerization as a means of reinforcing and re¯ ecting the decisionmaking status quo.

The decentralization effect of IS in turn impacts not only the organizational structures, but also changes the roles of employees and managers. A dissemination of power in part emerges, suggesting employees are empowered to a greater degree.

## Micro level ± the effects of IS on individua employees

In the second group, the research focuses on the impact of IT/IS on individuals (of particular interest is the study of middle managers and employees) because its main unit of analysis is the agent (individual employee) as opposed to structure (organization). As noted above, Bowen and Lawler (1992) saw the dissemination of information about organizational performance (e.g. operating results and competitor performance) to the lower levels of the organization as one of the four key features of empowerment. Since IS can support this, they seem to be directly relevant to empowerment. Jarvenpaa and Ives (1994) put forward examples of ® rms which report daily performance indicators (such as worldwide sales or current stock price) through sophisticated IS directly to employees: \`Such system can help focus employee and team efforts toward the problems and opportunities facing the broader organisation’ (p. 41).

In a similarly futuristic and optimistic tone, Hoffman (1994) set out an IT vision for supporting a federated organization: \`to support worker empowerment throughout our enterprise, we will be prepared to provide every worker with all information relevant to that worker’s job and its effect on the company as a whole’ (p. 55).

The importance of information for empowerment has made researchers declare IT and IS as critical. Clement (1990) examined the use of desktop computers by secretaries and the cooperative solutions they developed to deal with the dif® culties they faced in their use of computers. In this context Kanter (1977) noted that \`the powerful are those with access to the tools for action’ (p. 166), yet Clement (1990) seemed to imply that the greater control over their

interaction with computers led secretaries to assume greater power in their dealings with management. The role of IT in all this is questionable: the strengthening of social relations through collaboration seems more responsible for their \`empowerment’ compared to the use of IT. Furthermore, we can envisage similar social dynamics developing around any other form of technology; the secretaries seem to be particularly concerned about the way they had been treated over computerization, but this could have happened with the introduction of any new technology. This point precisely was picked up by Clement (1994):

Computerization serves not so much as an empowering tool, but as the catalyst and occasion that expands the possibilities for organizational realignment and empowerment. The constraints and opportunities presented by technological change help in opening \`spaces’ in organizational life within which the staff can bring to the surface long submerged concerns (p. 61).

The background to this line of work is the literature on the effects of IT/IS on individuals; employee job satisfaction, productivity and skills have been studied since the ® rst days of computerization (Attewell and Rule, 1984; Kling, 1991; Orlikowski, 1992). Although there has been substantial work on the impact of IT/IS at the individual level, clear conclusions have not yet been reached (for reviews of IT impact research see, e.g., Wijnhoven and Wassenaar (1990); Orlikowski (1992) and Ang and Pavri (1994)). More recent empirical work which employed the job characteristics model developed by Hackman and Oldham (1980) has suggested that computer-based IS have a positive effect on the job satisfaction and motivation of end-users (Ryker and Nath, 1995). Simons (1995) examined the ways interactive control systems \`focu[s] attention on the strategic uncertainties that managers want everyone to monitor’ (p. 88), while maintaining control at all levels. Thus, the role that IS can play in ensuring control in an empowering organization can range from the tight constant surveillance of operators to the more indirect provision of information in order to ensure that the efforts and attention of all members are focused on the right priorities. Once more, it is the empowering organization which decides how and for what purpose the technology will be employed to support employees.

## Technological changes complementing empowerment

However, both the above noted groups of work on the relationship between IS and empowerment (or decentralization) ± except perhaps the latest paper by Clement (1994) ± seem to aspire to a \`moderated technologically deterministic view where the technology is seen as the independent variable which impacts on organizational aspects at various levels (George and King, 1991; Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991). Recently, more sophisticated approaches to the relationship between IS and empowerment have emerged, which tend to study technological advances ± usually with the introduction of a new IS ± which are complemented by organizational changes in the direction of empowerment, or vice versa.

For example, Wareham et al. (1997b) described a case study where the introduction of a performance monitoring system tracking the activities of over 150 service technicians was complemented by an organizational redesign based on empowerment. When the IS was initially introduced, it was met by strong resistance from the employees who were essentially troubled by the use of measurements in demonstrating their inadequate performance. Thus, the company decided to embark on an empowerment programme which transformed the traditional hierarchical struc ture to a three-tier team structure and the \`bottomup’ information ¯ ows which were characteristic of the traditional control context were replaced by an interactive control mechanism with a two-way information ¯ ow. The study exempli® ed the need for aligning IS with organizational structure and policies and stressed that the introduction of IS cannot cause the categorical elimination of traditional hierarchies (Wareham et al., 1997b). It rather suggested that \`the manner in which the information is utilised within the organisation is entirely a matter of management discretion’ (Wareham et al., 1997b, p. 1404), but was also critical of the realization of the bene® ts of IS.

Sayer and Harvey (1997) discussed a case of the use of electronic mail which demonstrated the power of organizational actors in using the same IS to totally different effects. Mitev (1996) similarly discussed the role of IT and IS in BPR and suggested that ITinduced organizational change reinforces management control and \`increases ef® ciency through oiling and diluting issues of power relationships by providing technology-based ©equalº access to organisationa information and knowledge’ (p. 64). In turn, technological advances and IS can initiate, enforce and complement empowerment in organizations.

## The relationship between empowerment and IS: the research issue

From our analysis of the existing research, it is evident that there is no single, unequivocal relationship between IS and empowerment, whether this relationship is approached at the level of structure or action or from a technologically deterministic perspective or not. We identi® ed six areas which de® ne this relationship (see Table 1).

IS was shown to support employees by providing information or promoting the delegation of decisions, while at the same time IS can be used to control and monitor employees. Hence, a \`dichotomy’ or rather a continuum appears between the \`positive’ and \`negative’ support which IS can provide for empowerment. We believe this continuum is at the core of the issue and needs to be explored further.

A further point which merits clari® cation is that, as in some cases IS can be supportive for empowerment, can we regard them as able to empower employees? The ubiquitous yet unquestionable reference to the relationship between empowerment and IS (particularly in the popular IS literature) and the discussion of IT’s \`empowering potential’ seems to suggest so. For example, Pfeffer (1994) noted that sharing information is a necessary precondition to empowerment but did not go any further in qualifying this claim.

Together these research issues were empirically explored in the context of the British manufacturing industry.

## Research methods

In order to explore the research issue in British manufacturing organizations, we undertook both a large-scale exploratory postal survey and a number of case study interviews. The integrative, quantitative and qualitative research approach not only gave us a large data set, but it also strengthened our ® ndings’ representativeness for exploring the relationship between IS and empowerment in the UK manufacturing industry. Our research is supportive of Robey and Hartwick’ s (1994) argument that researchers should use multiple research methods, including both variance and process research strategies, when investigating social processes. Attewell and Rule (1991, p. 304) corroborated this view, emphasizing the strong \`complementarity between survey and ® eldwork approaches to studying information technology’ and the suggestion that \`each is incomplete without the other’. In turn, our research approach focused on the integration of a quantitative approach such as a survey with case study interviews in order to combine ultimately an objective view with a more subjective individual view.

## Survey research

In 1996 we undertook \`a survey of empowerment in British manufacturing’ of the top 450 UK manufacturing companies. For that, a questionnaire was devised covering three main areas from the personnel and human resource (HR) director’s perspective: (1) changes in work organization, (2) employee empowerment and (3) IS in empowerment. We were interested in primarily exploring the implications of IT in support of empowerment within these three sections. In turn, the purpose of this survey was essentially exploratory in nature.

The mailing list was selected from the 1995/6 Times 1000 company guide and the 1995/6 Financial Times UK 500 companies and counter-checked for correctness against a ® nancial company database One-Source. Both public and private sector organizations were selected. The organizations were chosen primarily according to their annual turnover. Personnel and HR managers were selected as addressees since we found these to be most knowledgeable about employee empowerment issues and their access to IS. An overall response rate of 29.8% was achieved, albeit with only 23% of the questionnaires usable. Those where the respondent had essentially not correctly completed the questionnaire were unusable. This paper, due to length limitations, focuses primarily on the detailed additional comments the respondents made on the questionnaire. The comments were recorded in their original phrasing and are presented intact in the following ® ndings section. In her unpublished thesis, Psoinos (1998) discussed the overall survey ® ndings in greater detail.

## Case-study interviews

Following the survey, a number of case study interviews were undertaken. Using a semi-structured interview protocol we interviewed a range of participants from long-established UK companies in order to uncover the full range of dif® culties which emerge with the encouragement of empowerment and with IS support in the new context. Early on, our research suggested the situation was likely to be different in newer, less-established companies. Thus, the companies selected were all going through changes in their work practices, although not necessarily as a result of a formal, intentional change programme (as noted in many cases empowerment has a more evolutionary character). Furthermore, the selected companies had varying budgets and extent of involvement in IT. Six out of the 18 companies which participated in the research fell within the 100 companies with the biggest IT expenditure in the UK.

The interviews were all scheduled for 1 h but in many cases lasted anywhere up to 2 h. All of the interviews were tape recorded, transcribed and sent to the interviewees for veri® cation. We then developed a higher level of abstraction and interpretation by applying the precepts of intentional analysis to the transcripts (Sanders, 1982). In addition, we sought supporting documentation in order to develop a deeper understanding. This included annual reports, ® nancial documents, presentations and a number of interna memorandums and reports. These sources and procedures allowed us to form a qualitative, interpretative approach in developing a number of case studies (Walsham, 1995). Thus constructed, the case study ® ndings integrated with the survey data will now be detailed in the next section. To guarantee the request for anonymity all names have been omitted. ## Exploratory ® ndings on IS and empowerment in the UK manufacturing industry

In order to set the scene, we provide a broad overview of IS use in manufacturing organizations before concentrating on IS support in empowerment. The section concludes with an outline of the main constraints which organizations noted regarding IS support for empowerment. ## General IS context in manufacturing

As expected (since the companies were in part selected for the extent of their use of IS) all companies were heavily involved in IS. Regarding their technology, most companies had a combination of mainframe and PC systems. Most core systems were still run on mainframes, while PC-based systems were becoming very popular. Local area networks and wide area network were usually in place with a noticeable move for many companies of migrating to Windows NT, due to its general compatibility with Windows applications. Electronic mail was established in all 18 companies and was widely used in most cases and some companies had introduced Lotus Notes. A signi® cant rise in PCs was noted in most companies: one fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG) manufacturer had gone from 200 to 1200 PCs in 2 years, while in 1992 on a site of 700 people they had only four PCs. Similar trends were noted in all companies: another had installed more than 300 PCs in 1 year on a 2000 people site, while another had 400 PCs scheduled for implementation. The use of PCs, although most prominent in of® ces, was rapidly expanding into production areas.

Most companies had a hybrid IT organization in place comprising a central IT department (which sets out IT strategy, coordinates activities, sets standards and so on) and a few IT people based at each manufacturing plant, depending on its size. A couple of companies had outsourced and, subsequently, their IT organization was quite different.

## Empowerment in manufacturing

As part of the postal survey, we asked whether the companies were engaged in empowerment initiatives, why empowerment had been embarked upon and how empowerment ® tted into the overall strategy of the company. Of the 103 respondents, 91 (88.3%) had introduced various change initiatives and, of these 91 companies, 79 (86.8%) had led to some employee empowerment. For most of these companies, empowerment had come about as part of BPR, TQM, downsizing or delayering. The aims of these change initiatives were typically to improve quality, increase productivity, increase ¯ exibility and reduce costs. Companies were speci® cally trying to improve employee job satisfaction or increase worker autonomy in far fewer cases. In other words, for these companies, empowerment resulted from a desire to improve business performance and not as some form of charitable gesture.

## IS in support of empowerment

Employee decision making is critically dependent on distributing the right information at the right point in the business process. The delegation of responsibilitie necessitates skills and knowledge, but \`knowledge’ was found to consist of two elements: the underpinning, in a sense the \`static’ knowledge an employee has regarding the task at hand and the dynamic information which links the task to the whole process. Both are absolutely necessary and, if one is missing, then empowerment cannot operate successfully:

We have got a lot of very skilled, very bright individuals in our organization and they are more than capable of making the right decisions along those business processes. And quite often they just do not have the information to make that decision (IT manager, pharmaceuticals manufacturer).

We grouped the core data surrounding the most important IT support functions exactly as they emerged from the interviews.

## IS in support of employee empowerment

## Support for decision making

IS can enable employees to attain access to the data and information required to make decisions and may even lead to the delegation of decision making, providing the organization so wishes: \`As con® dence grows in the new operational procedures there can be a steady ¯ ow down of decision making, e.g. on material call-off, shift pattern, etc.’ (questionnaire response). These decisions can range from everyday operational ones to more complex and signi® cant ones depending on the task at hand. For example, employees on a car production line needed information on speci® c customer orders:

But clearly the IS role here is to make sure that people are getting information through as fast as possible from the customer and that it is presented to the local management or ± in some cases ± the actual local team leaders at the level which is appropriate to respond to the need so the complexity of the IS is around taking that order and breaking it down to local decisions’ (IT strategy manager, car manufacturer).

On the contrary, in the more complex production process of aircraft engines, IS can also provide access to the knowledge that is needed for making decisions and which employees may not possess off hand: \`people lower down the organization have access to the information and knowledge necessary to make decisions’ (questionnaire response).

There is a lot of that wisdom in the senior levels who are actually able to say: \`well I do not think that will work there’ . That sort of wisdom can actually save millions of pounds but it does mean that lots of decisions to change designs, all the rest of it, kind of get sucked up at the top. Now one of the changes that we expect IT to bring is to make some of that wisdom available . . . to the person making the decision so they do not have to refer the decision upwards, they can access the wisdom and integrate that into their decision process (personnel manager, aerospace company).

In other words, IS provide a useful distributing and access function to essential data and information, which supports employees in their decision-making tasks.

## Access to general information

Our ® ndings revealed that access to all the necessary information is critical for empowerment: \`Accessible information is essential to the attainment of empowerment goals’ (questionnaire response). Information provision can support employees in many ways in their day-to-day routines and jobs. IS, for example, were found to support business performance reporting on many items such as sales volumes, pro® ts, market share, prospects, business plans and so on. All this information is related to enhancing employee understanding and involvement in the business.

You go around some of our factories and you see quite a lot of charts about scrap levels or product delivery performance or improvement group activities, computer-produced. And you ask who has done it; it is quite often the operators (personnel director, cables manufacturer).

Information about product performance and quality is in some cases disseminated down to the shop¯ oor through IT. In the continuous improvement process of a car manufacturer, IS assist in providing better, quicker and more accurate and relevant feedback from customers: \`IS provide accurate, timely and relevant information to the right place at the right time’ (ques tionnaire response). In line with the continuous improvement ideas though, the customer is not merely the person who buys the car. The employee who receives the output of one’s work immediately and the next person on the conveyor are all considered customers and their feedback is important. IS can assist in improving the form the necessary information is presented in by analysing the information and putting it in a more readily understandable and usable form. This appears to be considered more useful compared to paper-based systems.

Companies seemed aware of the potential bene® ts that the provision of information holds in promoting employee interest through linking their work to the customer:

and nowadays we tend to give people more infor mation. If there is something going through the shop for a speci® c customer, you can ® nd out who the customer is. You know and in the past it was jus something you had got to make. But now you can see who it is going to much more. So in some ways we are giving out more information to make the job more interesting for people on the shop¯ oor (IT manager, engineering company).

## Task automation and facilitation

IT and IS have facilitated employee tasks in various ways. Shop¯ oor staff can, for example, enquire about up-coming jobs, check whether an item is on stock without having to walk to the stores and receive process instructions on screen for how to do things. IS can in turn provide a single source of data (i.e. databases). The facilitating impact that IS thus appeared to have relates essentially to timeliness and availability:

what we want to do is ensure that the vital information that a person needs for their job, they get it in a timely way and it is available to them. Whereas, previously, it sometimes was not available to them. It sometimes was ® ltered through various people and over time (personnel manager, car manufacturer).

IS also automate and facilitate many of their everyday tasks, thus saving time and effort. This is quite signi® cant and should not be underestimated, as in empowering organizations employees still have to perform all their usual traditional tasks alongside their \`new’ responsibilities. A common result is that employees are overworked and \`burnt out’ by the intense workload: \`[IS] free managers from chasing around getting, reporting data, gives them more time to devote to their people’ (questionnaire response). In many continuous improvement applications, employees had assumed quality control tasks. In a car manufacturer, shop¯ oor operators input data related to quality assurance monitoring the system and received product quality-related information. A communication network relayed information, which was gathered at the quality check areas, to a central computer. Team members using remote PCs located throughout the plant could then access details of any vehicle on the line: \`Now we share all that information. There was a time when we did not share that information to employees. So now most things that we can measure, we share with employees’ (personnel manager, car manufacturer). Operators tended to receive data on machine performance in terms of quality and ef® ciency. Information regarding what job orders were coming or quality-related data could enable employees to be more pro-active rather than reactive and could warn them about events which were in most cases preferable for the organization, but also for employees as it allowed them better control over their job: \`This information now is more readily available or available to the people who are doing the job, we expect them to act upon it’ (personnel manager, car manufacturer).

The fact that information was provided to employees in a timely manner enabled them to be more proactive, but it could also be used by managers to press for even quicker employee responses to it. In production environments such as car assembly, there can be a signi® cant danger of IS contributing to constraining employee latitude and freedom further.

Finally, IS were also found ± providing the organization allowed it ± to facilitate breaking through the barriers between departments and enabled employees to access wider parts of the organization than those traditionally available to them.

Sort of 5 years ago, if I wanted to know who had spent what, it all came in hard copy. If there was an excess I used to go and phone up the ® nance department and say, who spent this and this cost so much and what on earth is it? Nowadays you do it by the screen. You go all the way down and it comes out and it says . . . I am not frustrated waiting for someone else to tell me the answer. And most people can do this (IT manager, engineering company).

## Communication

IS were widely and commonly used by employees for communication purposes. In a car assembly plant if, for example, a team which was responsible for assembling a particular component had a problem as a result of a faulty subassembly further up the conveyor, they used the network to relay the relevant information to the team responsible for the subassembly.

IS can also be used to facilitate communication between various departments:

There is no reason why if we had screens out in the plant, employees cannot come in and enquire. So he has got a payroll query, he can key in and possibly ® nd the solution, the answer to his query. And he has got access to his ® le (personnel manager, car manufacturer).

Similarly in an electrical products manufacturer which had had to cut down on support jobs, shop¯ oor operators had access to the entire ordering system to order parts for the line and were encouraged to do their own absence statistics and their holiday booking direct on-line in order to minimize the amount of work for the personnel department and facilitate internal procedures. Nevertheless, the two companies which mentioned that they were considering allowing employees access to input data into their own personnel ® les had not implemented the idea yet.

Although many companies mentioned the use of computerized process instructions (regarding assembly of components, etc.), concerns about their actual value were expressed. It is worth noting though that, in one car manufacturer, the process instructions were used as a basis for team discussions.

we have recently implemented a new system whereby the guy has access to computerized process instructions rather than paper ones, so they tell him how to do it; by now they have the ability to use screens to look at assembly drawings and the like. The extent to which that is really useful in the envi ronment of an assembly line is interesting. I do not think we have quite yet cracked the technology which gives an assembly worker useful instruction

at the point of assembly. My understanding of the way they use this new facility is they intend to use it in team discussions about quality rather than actually to support that dimension (IT strategy manager, car manufacturer).

Apart from these support functions, however, during the course of the research we developed a proposition that IS can support empowerment by bringing the idea of the customer into the organization and providing a stronger link between an individual’s tasks and the customer of the ® nal product. As noted above, the understanding that the customer really drives the business is held as a critical factor in the success of empowerment. In most cases though the division of tasks had removed the idea of the customer further away from the production operations. For example, it is fairly standard practice in most industries for operators to receive a fairly precisely formatted manufacturing view of the order.

So, for instance, in terms of what parts to ® t we have a mechanism whereby what they actually see is not a part number as such but a bin number reference so they say the harness is in bin A4 for this vehicle and the next vehicle the harness is in bin A6 (IT strategy manager, car manufacturer).

Thus, employees do not even see the component as part of a car that they are building but rather a part coming out of a bin. Therefore, if IS were found in any way to create a link with the customer, then we could claim another support function. However, we did not ® nd evidence for such a claim apart from only one instance where the customer’s name and details accompanied the car on the line.

## Problems and dif® culties encountered

Although the interviews provided ample evidence of the supportive role of IS in relation to employees, a wide range of problems and constraining aspects also frequently emerged. These surfaced either as elaborations of responses to questions in the questionnaire or in the unstructured discussion. In most cases, the constraints cannot be unilaterally attributed to either technological or organizational factors but rather seem to involve many interrelated aspects. The core constraints are detailed below.

## Access constraints

The respondents noted many dif® culties and inadequacies regarding access to information. In most situations, the participants noted that the speci® c tasks which an employee had to perform traditionally de® ned the basis on which information provision had been determined: \`If I am an operator then the business’s IS are not particularly accessible to me’ (HR manager, FMCG manufacturer).

For manufacturing operatives their information and systems access was mainly through using production computer systems. They may have had access to computer screens in terms of production control, programming ingredients, process operating instructions, necessary materials, ordering parts and equipment and informing them that the production line had stopped. However, these facilities are essentially for speci® c, production-related activities and are much more for input purposes (e.g. reporting completed jobs, assemblies, etc.). Other than that, operatives usually did not communicate more widely (e.g. via e-mail or other communication systems) or have access to IS of other functions.

They [lower-level employees] have access to systems that they are allowed to and there is very strict security on our systems, that people have access only to those systems that are speci® cally relevant to their job (personnel manager, aerospace company).

They may have access to computer screens in terms of programming ingredients, but in terms of, do they have electronic mail, do they receive communications that way, no they do not. It is still very traditional in that sense (personnel manager, FMCG manufacturer).

These speci® c screens were essentially dedicated terminals running one system and what operatives saw was predetermined; there was no sense of exploration or the user having control of the interaction. In most cases the user’s role was passive and the system prescribed the screens he/she could view.

We found that the limited access and the principle of \`need to know’ as the basis of information provision was a major constraint in the use that employees could make of IS in empowerment. We use a powerful example to illustrate the point:

So quite a lot of the [new] system is about open information that says need not to know rather than need to know is the criterion for deciding what is in there. In other words only the things that people must not know, like details of a new product, are not there . . . you can, as an assembly line worker, not just get the customer’s name on the order but you can actually ® nd out the total history of that order if you want to. Whether the guy really wanted blue paint and he settled for green because he was told that blue paint was going to delay the order for a week. That sort of little piece of information is the sort of thing that might be able to make a team leader say \`why should paint be an issue? I can change the paint in 5 minutes time. Who is it that’ s making that decision?’ (IT strategy manager, car manufacturer, emphasis added).

## Information inadequacies

The lifting of access restrictions and the unlimited provision of information does not necessarily mean that IS can effectively support employees. In such circumstances, information overload and the dif® culty in locating the right piece of information when it is needed can again constrain employees.

Quite often the task of ® nding the appropriate information for their purpose overwhelms people. You do not do it, you therefore make a guess or a judgement or whatever based on whatever relevant information you have got or incomplete information you have got (personnel manager, aerospace company).

ensuring the right information is available at the right time to the right people is not always possible (questionnaire respondent).

Furthermore, an overload of information was normally found to be detrimental rather than bene® - cial. Particularly regarding electronic mail messages, users often noted an abuse of the system which replaced even simple communications which could be easily conducted face-to-face.

It seems that the companies were facing dif® culties in ensuring that employees had the correct, relevant information. One way of overcoming this dif® culty is perhaps to have information \`sorted out’ for employees. \`There is a risk with IS that you further overload people with information so you have to put in tools to allow people to search quickly and effectively for the relevant information’ (personnel manager, aerospace company).

However, whatever the solution, the amount and type of information that is provided to employees beyond that which is absolutely necessary for their tasks has to be carefully thought through. It is not merely that organizations need to give out information, what they give out is also important.

in our organization, particularly within the manufacturing operation, there is a temptation to measure everything that moves. And at the base level I do not think we understand enough about our process to identify what is an important measure and what is not. So one of the risks associated with the increase in ease of access to information and the technology, is that we are simply going to measure more things quicker and generate more pieces of data. While data is great, information is helpful, so that I think is our challenge. There is however a clear insight into our need to manage data systematically and consistently (HR manager, FMCG manufacturer).

The form that data is presented in is equally important for employees. As noted above IS can support business performance reporting on items such as sales volumes, pro® ts, market share and so on. As for employees though, these items of data often have a \`remote’ and abstract feel; the challenge here is that they need to be assimilated in a format that people can read and internalize easily.

## Systems architecture/structure

Closely related to restrictions of access is the issue of separate IS run in the various parts of the organization. One of the most important concepts for empowerment is the need for the employee to take on board a much broader set of responsibilities than the ones determined by the narrow speci® cation of his/ her tasks. IS can (in theory) support the ability of employees to see the whole task from start to end. However, at the moment this is not possible due to the limitations of the IS infrastructure in most manufacturing companies. As manufacturing organizations have been built around functions for years, separate systems were designed and built to support each speci® c function. These promoted a \`compartmentalized’ view of the organization and, when employees need to have a look \`across’ the organization, it is simply impossible. For example, in an order ful® lment process, each stage ± the customer service department, the planning process, the delivery process, the invoice payment ± is usually supported by different systems. If someone wants to go back and check why something went wrong, the systems do not allow one to see the whole task and whole process.

If somebody has a problem here if you enable them through some sort of system to go back and analyse where the problem came from, they may be able to rectify it themselves. . . . So I think hugely that will help in terms of people understanding where their work ® ts in (HR manager, oil company).

This new company is made up of four old environments. We have got four sets of different legacy systems. So the ability to see information right across those, those four old companies if you like, is damn-near impossible (IT manager, pharmaceuticals manufacturer).

For managers the situation is the same; for them though this does not pose such a big problem because they possess the broader picture and an understanding of how things \`® t together’. Employees that have been doing one or a few tasks for 20 years usually do not know where their task ® ts in and how what they do in¯ uences everything else.

But until we have a different not necessarily organizational structure, but different ways of communicating, looking at cross-functional processes and using the IT systems. . . . It is not about getting new IT systems, it is actually using what those IT systems are very capable of doing to our advantage, we will not get the bene® t of this crossfunctional approach. . . . some of the systems we actually have go across, but because of the structure people do not use them that way. They are only interested in their little bit of the process or their customer or supplier or whatever (HR manager, oil company).

Even in the cases of some systems which do cut across, organizations tend not to use them in that way. This is where we hit boundaries which are set up and closely guarded by each functional department which de® ne their territory and prohibit employees from other departments entering their IS. Thus, this issue is not simply technical but also determined by organizational factors which seem to create and re-create the situation. \`When you talk about integrated packages, so that everything is under the same umbrella, the same supplier, the biggest problem is people still trying to run them in the departments they used to have’ (IT manager, engineering company). Similarly, IS are also usually designed based on the discrete, different tasks within a certain function.

It is clearly necessary for various systems to talk effectively to one another. In my own area we have a human resource system, we have a payroll system, we have a pension system. Historically those three never talked to one another; we had to manually transfer data between them. We are now just getting to the point where they do all run off the same database (HR manager, health science company).

## Too much prescription by system

Empowerment encourages a minimum speci® cation of actions in promoting creativity and ¯ exibility. IS, however, are often viewed as imposing some sort of control over work processes ± what has been termed in the IS literature as \`social control’ (Land et al., 1983). This is regarded differently depending on the type of work process: \`it is probably a good discipline in our manufacturing areas which need to be regulated, they need to be more routinized than they are today. I think it is less desirable in an R&D group’ (HR manager, health science company). The problem with standardization was explicitly addressed by one of our interviewees.

I mean sometimes people try and design computers to do certain things that are not necessarily best done that way. It goes back a little bit to trying to put too much into them sometimes. But resulting in almost a very rigid, mechanistic way of carrying out a process and that I would class as inappropriate. We try and try to bring people to think about how to get things done better, but every action you take is constrained by the computer system even where you could legitimately have a thought process to go through (personnel manager, aerospace company).

Moreover, the issue of technologies’ prescriptive nature was further exempli® ed in one case where an IS was used to try to tell a storeman almost exactly where to put a part.

and so the amount of information, the amount of prescription, if you like, in the system, was enormous. Now we deliver the part to the team and allow them to . . . make the decisions about, well, today, it is better to have this part and this part of my work space. But next week, because we have got a different mix, I want it over here. So we have almost backed off in terms of prescribing information to people and let them do their own thing, where the scope ought to be. . . . In the factory we used to have a location, a left-hand side, a righthand side of the track for example. And the system expected that information to be accurate. But the guy who was actually bolting it together did not understand why that was important. And the reality was he was right, it was not important (systems manager, commercial vehicle manufacturer).

## Technical dif® culties

The technology had been causing problems for the vast majority of companies. For example, the respondents often noted the dif® culties with mainframe technology.

It is really impossible to get information out of a mainframe (team leader, personnel, car manufacturer).

They were never really quite right for anybody’s problem but you kind of made do with them. I think now we are kind of seeing a trend where there is more capability for bespoke tailoring within IT systems and I think that is what I meant by ¯ exibility. The more I can take something and tailor it to what I need and my colleagues can do the same, the better (HR manager, health science company).

There were also signi® cant dif® culties with the user-friendliness of systems: \`Within a very traditiona industry, the systems require to be developed to allow greater user-friendliness’ (questionnaire response). Many respondents stressed the need for decentralized, distributed systems and intranets. In one of our case interviews an intranet had recently been set up, giving research scientists access to data held on different platforms.

## Inadequate user skills

The skills that the employees possessed regarding the use of IT strongly constrained the support that IS could provide to employees: \`one of the things that holds the organization back is our general IT literacy’ (HR manager, health science company). Naturally IT skills were not equally distributed and differences were to be expected; these can have various consequences: \`There has been a certain disconnect in that the majority of the workforce have been far more computer literate than the people running it’ (personnel manager, aerospace company) and \`There is an urgent need for higher levels of IT literacy of operatives’ (questionnaire respondent).

This has certainly acted as a constraint in the support that IS provide for empowerment as senior management did not appreciate the value and usefulness of IS as much as their staff did. Our ® ndings highlighted that age is strongly correlated with IS skills in the majority of cases.

A lot of people coming into the organization in their twenties are extremely IT literate. A lot of people who have been in the organization for some time are less so and I think like most organizations we have a considerable task in making people more IT literate so they can see how systems can help them in whatever it is they are doing (human resources manager, health science company).

I think for lots and lots of people IT, certainly for anybody, say over 35, it is actually quite dif® cult (personnel manager, FMCG manufacturer).

## IT department

The IT/IS department is usually responsible for user training. However, their role stretches far beyond than just providing training. The IT department is usually critical in the successful use of IS in empowerment. Nevertheless, in many companies, users noted dif® - culties with their IT departments. For example, users were usually not aware of the tools which could support them in their work and expected such assistance from the IS department: \`A better understanding of what is available’ (questionnaire response) and \`Easy identi® - cation of computer tools to use at work’ (questionnaire response).

Similarly users often \`complained’ that their IT department did not pay particular attention to their tasks and work practices:

the IS department has not really shared the same agenda that the manufacturing group did when they were embarking on their change programmes and were a reluctant partner. And spent an awful lot of time focusing on ® nancial control systems or ® nancial accounting or sales order processing. But did not get incredibly close to the manufacturing agenda. And some of that has to do with deeprooted perspectives on where their priorities lay and on managing the business’ (HR manager, FMCG manufacturer).

In other cases the IT department’s attitude to user involvement in the development process caused friction and failed to deliver satisfactory systems:

most of the users in our company, because they are effectively shut out of the IT process, they are asked what their requirement is for their computer system but they then have no in¯ uence in terms of how it is actually happening. They then get the product delivered here, this is what you asked for and they go and test it and it does not work, it does not quite do what they wanted because they were not really involved in the project in the ® rst place (personne manager, FMCG manufacturer).

One respondent believed that \`giving control of development to users’ (questionnaire response) is essential in enhancing the support that IS provide for empowerment.

I have been working on a project for introducing some HR software into the UK and into various locations in northern Europe. . . . At the end of that project, there was a big project meeting in IT . . . you know all the people that had worked on the project, they all had a meeting and they would not invite me, because they said I was a user, I was not actually part of the project. . . . they were not prepared to come and sit down and talk to me as a user, which I thought was just terrible (personnel manager, FMCG manufacturer).

With the IT department \`imposing’ solutions on the users, the latter are likely to feel less in control of their work and the tools they use and such feelings can extend beyond the use of the individual system and constrain the support that IS provide for their work.

There were also various other issues which pertained to the link between the business needs and IS implementation issues which companies noted as critical in enhancing the support of IS for empowerment. These obviously suggest that users are facing problems with IS projects:

Continued focus on business sponsorship and delivery to business plan and time-scale’ (questionnaire response).

A more coordinated approach across the organization to avoid \`ghettoes’ of limited progress (questionnaire response).

Increased senior management support for major projects (questionnaire response).

## Users’ attitudes towards IS

The various attitudes which employees develop regarding IS and information can be critical in their use of IS. These attitudes depend on internal as well as external factors (Orlikowski and Gash, 1994; Culpan, 1995; Roberts, 1987). The employee attitudes towards IS were beginning to change in some cases; employees were interested in taking on IT training in out-of-work hours without getting paid for it:

and this is in their own time, so it is their own selfdevelopment. . . . a lot of people are gathering more and more knowledge and are keen to get involved. . . . So it is not something that we have to, I suppose, any more encourage. It is self-generating. Whereas when it ® rst started, people were \`how much are you going to pay me for doing this’ (personnel manager, car manufacturer).

However, the situation is still quite \`shaky’ and organizations have to cultivate users’ attitudes towards IS carefully and consistently, particularly since, in manufacturing, computers have a history of replacing workers (Shaiken 1984; Hodson and Parker 1988). For example, instances of inappropriate IS use by managers can make employees wary of IS: \`the slight danger is that some managers I think are inclined to use IT as a control way and I remember one job manager who used it as a sort of weapon of terror’ (personnel director, cables manufacturer). Or, on the other hand, too many and frequent changes of the systems which are used might result in making employees feel uncomfortable:

perhaps we should skip out a few generations of software occasionally; we do not necessarily have to have the version 6.1 and then the version 6.2 and then the version 6.2a. We can perhaps jump from version 6 and wait for version 7 to come along and go in big chunks, because I think ± again I say this from a user’s point of view ± it is just as disruptive to take these little steps as it is to wait and take a big step (personnel manager, building materials manufacturer).

Too frequent changes of systems can impede effective incorporation of IS in the everyday practices of employees, particularly as, in manufacturing, lower level employees have not been using IS for a long time and are thus not totally comfortable with them.

In a very traditional car manufacturer senior production managers just refuse to use electronic mail:

I have a lot of problems with a couple of managers. Usually the ones ± the production managers who do not read their PROFS [e-mail system], fullstop. So it makes my job a lot harder because what then I have to do is chase up like literally, physically go to the production of® ces, try to ® nd the manager to let him know (personnel of® cer, car manufacturer).

Such attitudes can pose signi® cant constraints to the support that electronic mail can provide for communications between employees.

## Analysis and discussion of the relationship between IS and empowerment in manufacturing

The interviews and survey data provided a broad assessment of the reasons why these companies embarked on employee empowerment initiatives as well as the role of IS in relation to empowerment. For the manufacturing companies concerned, we found a very high level of awareness and knowledge of empowerment based on their experiences. For them, empowerment and IT/IS was an essential component of strategies such as BPR and TQM aimed at improving business performance. Empowerment as such was not usually a separate strategy. More common was the view that empowerment initiatives, in addition to other factors, has to entail an element of IS to achieve success. The reason being, as emphasized by our ® ndings, as employees assume the broader tasks and responsibilities of making decisions, solving problems and improving operations, the support that IS can provide is fundamental in operationalizing empowerment. In turn, our ® ndings clearly suggest that, contrary to some of the more populist literature and Clement (1990) (see Table 1), IS does not itself empower or lead to empowerment. IS instead were shown to support employees in ful® lling the responsibilities which have been brought on by empowerment. The use of IS, however sophisticated such systems may be, thus cannot be said to \`empower’ employees, but rather empowerment is complemented by IS as Wareham et al. (1997a,b) (see Table 1) found.

There was clear evidence from the comments of the respondents that, under favourable conditions, IS could provide support for decision making through the provision of tools and speci® c decision-related information and by providing access to more general information in order to improve the working lives of the employees. Similarly, IS could provide the tools and information for facilitating or automating certain manufacturing tasks signi® cantly as well as improving communication between individuals and teams. These broadly corroborate Pfeffer’ s (1994) view that increased information ¯ ow and access will aid the process of empowering employees in their tasks, but also supports Wyner and Malone’s (1996) and Malone’s (1997) suggestion that IS can facilitate the decentralization of decision-making power in the contexts of empowerment initiatives (see Table 1). Here, the true opportunities which IS can provide in support of empowerment are evident in the increased decision-making process speed, which enables manufacturers to respond rapidly to external and internal business demands and pressures.

Recalling the premise from above that empowerment requires the provision of information, power, knowledge and rewards, we can see that current IS go some way towards the provision of information. However, problems remain in the inadequacy of some of the information, the systems’ architectures and structures, the prescriptive nature of some IS and other technical dif® culties which typically arise from the unfriendly nature of legacy systems. These problems are all surmountable, given the will and resources of a manufacturer. However, a mere change in the IS will not be suf® cient. Rather, as our ® ndings suggest, which indirectly also corroborate Hoffman’s (1994) (see also Table 1) suggestion, empowerment will depend on the adoption of a new IT strategy and IT architecture which supports information dispersion.

However, IS make little direct contribution to the provision of power, knowledge and rewards ± the other three core requirements for empowerment according to Bowen and Lawler (1992), Bridges (1994) and Lawler et al. (1995). We can see evidence of the impact of these core requirements in the constraints highlighted in the study. Access constraints are more often due to managers’ reluctance to cede power than to constraints inherent in the technology. User attitudes are clearly strongly in¯ uenced by perceived power and reward imbalances. Negative attitudes are highly probable if employees feel that empowerment is a mere rhetoric and that they are not being given suf® cient power and rewards for discharging their new responsibilities effectively. Inadequate user skills, on the other hand, re¯ ect a shortfall in user knowledge which prevents them from taking advantage of the information and IS tools available. Issues concerning power, knowledge and rewards may also provide an explanation for the poor contribution to empowerment from some of the IT departments involved. Even if the IT department has the necessary knowledge and motivation to support empowerment (which is not always the case), compared to departments of ® nance or production, IT departments often lack the organizational power for implementing their policies. Empowerment without the transfer of power to employees is evidently meaningless and it is likely that the reluctance to decentralize decision-making power enthusiastically explains the failure of some of the organizations addressing the constraints seen in our study.

Power is very much related to organizational structure and culture. Any attempted implementation of empowerment without appropriate changes to traditional bureaucratic hierarchies is unlikely to be successful. Empowerment and its supporting IS demands a ¯ atter organization with fewer levels of management and improved links between teams, particularly where such links cross traditional departmental boundaries. Organizational culture, in terms of the norms and values accepted by the organization, is

## IS in support of employee empowerment

equally important and strongly in¯ uences the success of empowerment initiatives and their supporting IS. While in some cases the technology is in place but the change in organizational culture is not forthcoming, in other cases the reverse is true:

we had this situation where we had major investment in systems that was delivering rigid processes, but we had this change in the culture that was trying to become less regimented and less rigid. And the reality was, in some areas the systems inhibited that (IS manager, commercial vehicle manufacturer).

Hence, our ® ndings and analysis clearly demonstrate that successful empowerment requires the appropriate provision of information (through IS), power (through changes in structure and culture), knowledge (through user training) and rewards (in the shape of improved satisfaction, status or ® nancial rewards).

Moreover, our ® ndings highlight the fact that both organizational and technological factors in essence constrain the effectiveness of IS in the support of employee empowerment. These relate to both the design of the systems and the established uses they have within the organization and they have to be addressed if IS are to help employees with their responsibilities. Our suggestion would be that senior business and IT managers should work closely together in order to map out the new work practices and address the constraints identi® ed in Table 3. This may involve fundamental changes in an organization’s culture and structure, if these have not already been addressed. Such a strategy would ensure that IS support employees rather than constrain them in their empowered roles.

In terms of the technology, new systems such as extranets, intranets and distributed database systems (such as Lotus Notes), which are becoming increasingly popular, might considerably alleviate the constraints of legacy management IS. Here we emphasize, as Clement (1990) did, that computerization or IS can present an occasion where users can gain more control over their work (see Table 1). Intranets, for example, facilitate the distribution of information and could overcome many of the traditional access constraints. Furthermore, search engines could support employees in locating speci® c information and new enterprise resource planning systems could perhaps help overcome the dif® culties with the \`silo’ structure of many IS infrastructures in manufacturing. The open structure of Internet technologies lends itself in particular in encouraging the integration of different platforms and legacy systems and web interfaces for improving general user-friendliness. In addition, new types of application such as group support systems (Ventana, NetMeeting, etc.) and idea generating systems such as Idea Central (Imaginatik) can prove valuable in supporting employee activities while overcoming many of the limitations of traditional applications and structures (as suggested by Sayer and Harvey (1997) (see also Table 1).

Most UK manufacturing organizations have yet to invest heavily in such new technologies. Instead they may still rely heavily on a combination of 1970s main frame management IS, augmented by more modern client ± server architectures. Further research could examine whether the constraints identi® ed above exist in organizations which have adopted more open and unstructured systems. Organizational changes are often enabled by and associated with technological developments, just as the decentralization and enduser computing of the 1980s was facilitated by the emergence of PCs. It may well be that the new systems and applications of the Internet era might help to ful® the promise of empowerment.

## Conclusion and managerial implications

The key contribution of this study is the rich insight afforded into the relationship between IS and empow erment, an area which has been relatively neglected in the literature. This paper was based on an indepth empirical study of empowerment in the context of UK manufacturing ® rms and involved both a large-scale postal questionnaire and interviews in 18 companies. The ® ndings clearly show that IS alone are insuf® cient and do not empower employees. Rather, where the organizational conditions were right, IS sup ported empowerment through improving the employee decision-making process, providing access to both general and task-speci® c information, facilitating communication between individuals and teams and facilitating and automating tasks. However, our research emphasizes the need and importance of addressing both organizational and technological issues in order to overcome the constraints on empowerment which frequently exist in practice. In terms of managerial implications, the following organizational and technological issues thus highlight those areas which our research suggests need careful attention.

The organizational issues include the changes needed in terms of the provision of the power, knowledge and rewards for empowerment to succeed. This implies the need for ensuring that the organization’ s structure and culture are conducive to empowerment, as well as the provision of resources for user training. This echoes George and King’s (1991) reinforcement politics perspective and complements Wareham et al. (1997b) in highlighting the need for aligning IS with organizational structure and policies. Our study suggests that empowerment is not normally sought for its own sake but is rather part of a business strategy which aims to improve the company’s performance. Although this suggests that organizational issues will be addressed as part of the wider initiative, it does not necessarily mean that such issues will be solved satisfactorily from the perspective of all groups within a company.

Although technology is far from being the whole solution, it is still important. Legacy systems, based on 1970s mainframe technology, re¯ ect a centralized management IS paradigm. Thus, they place severe (and perhaps fatal) constraints on the empowerment initiatives of the 1990s. Clearly, senior users and IT managers need to work together in order to ensure that the organizational and technological conditions are conducive for empowerment. Both the organizational and technological structures need to be ¯ atter and more ¯ exible and, in terms of technology, up-to-date. For these changes to take place, the culture of the whole organization, including the IT department, needs to be involved in the process.

Any research study has its limitations. This study has taken advantage of the breadth of response in terms of the large number of organizations which responded, either to the original postal questionnaire or who took part in the interviews. However, this means that the study lacks the depth provided by longitudinal case studies. Similarly, in most instances, our respondents and interviewees were from a single (but important) stakeholder group: human resource managers. The study cannot claim to be representative of the situation in UK manufacturing organizations. Although the original questionnaire was sent to a large sample of organizations, it is likely that those who responded were more likely to be practising empowerment. Those manufacturers who are unconcerned with empowerment would have been unlikely to complete the questionnaire. Furthermore, we selected the organizations in which to conduct interviews based on their questionnaire responses. The interviews were held with companies who seemed to be involved signi® cantly in empowerment but who also had some awareness of IS. Nevertheless, the results of this study suggest further research will be highly worthwhile. The study could be usefully replicated in other industrial sectors (e.g. ® nancial services) as well as being complemented by further in-depth longitudinal case studies.
