# Practising peer review in organizations: a quali er for knowledge dissemination and legitimization

MAGNUS BERGQUIST AND JAN LJUNGBERG

Knowledge Management Group, Viktoria Institute, Gothenburg, Sweden

ULRIKA LUNDH-SNIS

Laboratorium for Interaction Technology,Trollhättan Uddevalla University, Uddevalla, Sweden

A key issue in many organizations is how to disseminate information in an effective way and, more importantly, how to make use of this information in order to create new knowledge. One way of addressing this problem is to focus on how information is socially transformed into knowledge. This includes how knowledge is handled in practice and how the knowledge produced is quali ed as being something worth knowing and acting upon. Two well-established practices for doing this are the refereeing system and the peer review process. These are used in scienti c communities as a means of validating and legitimating knowledge, for example by reviewing journal papers before publishing or project proposals before granting funds, etc. This paper argues that peer review is a useful concept when looking at knowledge creation and legitimization in organizations. The social meaning of peer review is to legitimize new knowledge by organizationally sanc tioning it and thereby creating a platform for collective sense making. This paper uses an example from a  eld study in a pharmaceutical company in order to illustrate this argument. The study took place in a quality support department where the quality of health care products and processes was assessed. The organization had a need for fast and reliable updating of information that could in uence how the production process of pharmaceuticals should be carried out. In order to cope with these problems the department established an ‘evaluation loop’, which shared several characteristics with the peer review process.

## Introduction

This paper addresses the question of how information is socially transformed into knowledge. Simply because organizational members obtain access to information does not mean that they will relate to or understand this information. The ease with which information spreads can also create an over ow of information that can result in less rather than more useful knowledge for the recipient (Ljungberg and Sørensen, 1998; Schultze and Vandenbosch, 1998; Berghel, 2000). The focus of the discussion here is how knowledge is handled in practice and how the knowledge produced is quali ed as being something worth knowing and acting upon. The creation of knowledge in organizations is a collective process of sense making. Emerging from this process is an answer to the question people in organizations implicitly and sometimes explicitly ask themselves. What counts as knowledge in our organization and why?

This paper will discuss the concept of peer review and how the practice of peer reviewing can be used as a model for both knowledge sharing and quality assurance in knowledge-intensive  rms. The paper will illustrate the arguments with empirical data from a  eld study in a pharmaceutical company. Based on a study of a quality support group, knowledge creation as a social process, which involves an understanding of the organizational context in which actors work and where knowledge is constructed, in this case the work conditions in the quality support group, is discussed. The example considered is one where the organization had formed an organizational framework for disseminating new information to the relevant parties involved in the process of asserting and evaluating information that could potentially change work practices and methods. The consequences of peer reviewing will be discussed more in depth as a model for knowledge sharing and quality assurance.

The analytical method used is one of studying practices that are contextualized through the exploration of connections between empirical observations and theoretical considerations. In the case studied quality assessment is understood as a practice that is integrated in the general work practices at the company. It is the authors’ belief that this is therefore best understood as a ‘corporate peer review process’, which to a great extent draws upon some of the basic principles associated with academic peer reviews.

The paper is structured as follows. In the next section knowledge management is discussed and related to theories about the social construction of knowledge. Then the notion of the peer review process is explored. Thereafter the  eld study is presented, followed by a discussion of the general relationships between peer review and knowledge management. Finally, the paper concludes by considering how the peer review process might bene t the processes of organizational knowledge creation and sharing.

## Knowledge and its management

Since Plato’s articulation of knowledge as justi ed true belief, many different conceptualizations of knowledge have been proposed. However, much of the increasing interest in organizational knowledge could be gathered within the broad and inscrutable term of ‘knowledge management’. The term in itself indicates that there is something called knowledge and that this needs to be managed. But what is knowledge and can it be managed? It is not necessary or even meaningful to attempt to de ne knowledge, but it is necessary to pinpoint some of the dimensions of knowledge that have been discussed in the literature.

## Tacit versus explicit knowledge

Perhaps the most important discourse within the knowledge management area concerns the distinction between tacit and explicit knowledge. Many of the typologies and classi cations of knowledge build on the in uential work of Polanyi (1962) and his notion of tacit knowledge, i.e. people know more than they can tell. However, the commonly used tacit-explicit distinction is not directly derived from Polanyi’s (1962) work. Many authors see explicit knowledge as knowledge that has been codi ed into manuals, procedures and rules and is easy to communicate, while tacit knowledge is hard to articulate and, thus, only exists in people’s minds and manifests itself through their actions. Nonaka and Takeuchi’s (1995) view on knowledge creation as conversions between tacit and explicit knowledge is one example of this dichotomy. Polanyi (1962) did not make such a distinction. Instead, as Tsoukas (1996) pointed out, Polanyi (1962) claimed that the tacit and the explicit are mutually constituted and should not be treated as two separate types of knowledge. Tacit knowledge is thus not, as in Nonaka and Takeuchi’s (1995) view, explicit knowledge that is internalized, but a necessary component of all knowledge.

## The locus of knowledge

Where does knowledge reside? Spender (1996) argued for a distinction between individual and social knowledge. Another highly cited typology (Blackler, 1995) is an elaboration of Collins’ (1993) categorization of knowledge types, corresponding to images of knowledge found in the literature. Rooted in Simon (1964), one of the most common images of knowledge is that it resides in the heads of individuals. Some even argue that knowledge is inseparable from people (Grant, 1996). Nonaka (1994), for example, viewed knowledge as a speci c entity formed in the minds of individuals. If knowledge resides ‘between the ears of individuals’ (Fahey and Prusak, 1998), processes such as externalization and internalization of tacit knowledge become vital for the creation and transformation of knowledge (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995). Knowledge residing in the heads of individuals in the form of conceptual skills and cognitive abilities is what Blackler (1995) called embrained knowledge. Individual knowledge can also be tied to physical presence, physical cues and experience of sensory information (Zuboff, 1988). This kind of actionoriented, embodied knowledge is residing in the ‘hands’ of people, e.g. the expertise of a craftsperson rooted in action in speci c contexts.

According to Spender (1996), knowledge is less about truth and reason and more about the practice of intervening knowledgeably and purposefully in the world’. This perspective is highly relevant as it highlights the fact that knowledge cannot be divorced from the daily work activities performed by people in the organization. In contrast to this individualistic cognitive perspective, knowledge is also seen as emergent from organizational processes and social practice. This perspective views knowledge as something collective that is embedded in the form of communities of practice performing everyday activities that continuously and collaboratively develop, re ne and make sense of knowledge (cf. Lave and Wenger, 1991). Spender (1996) denoted this as social knowledge, whereas it relates to encultured knowledge in Blackler’s (1995) typology. Encultured knowledge is closely linked to the process of achieving shared meanings emerging from interaction and is collectively shaped (Gergen, 1992). Arenas where this negotiation of meaning takes place are communities of different sorts, i.e. groups of people linked together by shared values, working premises, special interests, etc. Knowledge is thus created, shaped and enacted in, for example, communities of practice (Orr, 1990; Brown and Duguid, 1991), communities of knowing (Boland and Tenkasi, 1995) or discourse communities (Berkenkotter and Huckin, 1995).

Codi ed knowledge is knowledge that is structured into a set of identi able rules and relationships that can easily be communicated (Kogut and Zander, 1992), what Blackler (1995) called encoded knowledge. Typically, encoded knowledge takes the form of books, manuals and databases. Encoded knowledge is separated from the original context of creation or use and it must be interpreted in new contexts. Interpreting and making sense of encoded knowledge in new situations is a social process relating to the practices used in these situations.

Knowledge residing in systemic routines is referred to as embedded knowledge in Blackler’s (1995) typology. Embedded knowledge is the systemic relationship between technology, roles, formal procedures and emergent routines. A similar notion to this is Prahalad and Hamel’s (1990) ideas around core competences, which consider competences at an organizational level in the form of infrastructure, patents, production skills and technologies.

Tsoukas (1996) argued that all of these typologies suffer from inherent dichotomies and present a dualistic worldview. However, despite their limitations, these typologies are widely used and they help to make sense of the ‘rationality’ in many knowledge management efforts. It is also important to note (as will be argued later in this article) that each of these types of knowledge is embedded in social practice and different processes of knowing.

## The management of knowledge

Plato’s de nition of knowledge is far from contemporary post-modern views, where organizational reality is considered as being constructed by the use of linguistic and cultural representations (see e.g. Gergen, 1992). However, many knowledge management approaches still rely on an objectivist view not far removed from Plato’s original de nition. A review of the knowledge management literature reveals that much of the literature on knowledge management is driven by a ‘hard information systems view (Scarbrough et al., 1999; Swan et al., 2000). More substantive perspectives on knowledge (e.g. Blackler, 1995; Tsoukas 1996) are rare in information technology (IT)-focused approaches to knowledge management.

What might constitute a particular knowledge management approach will therefore depend on what perspective on knowledge dominates and what kind of knowledge management activities are going to be included. Hedlund (1994) proposed that knowledge management addresses the generation, representation, storage, transfer, transformation, application, embedding and protecting of organizational knowledge. If activities like these are related to types of knowledge, an overview of knowledge management efforts can be derived.

Encoded knowledge is the type of knowledge that is most commonly handled in knowledge management initiatives. Many of the IT tools intended to support knowledge management build on assumptions of knowledge as objects, i.e. chunks of knowledge that could easily be stored in databases. Storage and retrieval become the most important knowledge management activities, as well as the building of architectures in order to manage a  rm’s codi ed knowledge (see e.g. Zack, 1999). IT support includes expert systems, competence tools and expert seeking tools (Lindgren and Wallström, 2000). In addition, most of the competence tools currently available passively store formalized competence descriptions related to well-de ned tasks (see Lindgren and Wallström, 2000; Sorensen and Snis, 2001).

Knowledge management initiatives that focus on embrained knowledge often relate to the management of human resources (McClure Wasko and Faraj, 2000). However, even within this perspective a rather rationalistic perspective on competence and knowledge still dominates both theory and practice (Sandberg, 1994; Lindgren and Wallström, 2000).

Encultured knowledge could take many forms and has only recently entered the knowledge management arena. Research on managing knowledge that is encultured in communities is quite rare. However, several studies focusing on knowledge sharing in communities do exist (e.g. Orr, 1990; Brown and Duguid, 1991; Wenger, 1998; Kollock, 1999; Ljungberg, 2000). IT supporting the development of encultured knowledge has to some extent been available within the groupware and computer-supported cooperative work (CSCW) area (see e.g. Sproull and Kiesler, 1991; Ciborra, 1996) for some time. With infrastructures such as intranets and the Internet, communication tools such as e-mail and ICQ and design concepts such as web portals (Detler, 2000), IT has become an increasingly important factor in shaping encultured knowledge.

Knowledge embedded in routines has been addressed in quality management programmes such as total quality management (TQM) and the International Standards Organization (ISO). TQM and similar approaches seek incremental and continuous improvement of established work practices (see e.g. Hackman and Wageman, 1995). In a similar way ISO standards seek to establish a certain level of embedded knowledge. These approaches focus on performing routine processes better in small steps and could be seen as single loop learning (Argyris and Scön, 1978). This is in contrast to innovation and breakthroughs in knowledge that can be viewed as double loop learning. However, none of these activities concern the social construction and legitimization of knowledge. Mainstream knowledge management approaches have neglected the notion of knowledge as a dynamic process of making sense of what counts as knowledge (Weick, 1995). In order to account for this notion, the objecti ed view of knowledge must be set aside and how meaning is socially constructed must be focused on.

## The social construction of knowledge

Based on the previous discussion, it can be concluded that knowledge has to be accounted for as a dynamic activity that is not easily understood by an outside observer or something that can easily be stored in databases. Knowledge assumes many shapes and forms and members mould it when it is internalized and enacted. It is therefore appropriate to analyse knowledge in terms of the social process of knowing (Blackler, 1995; Cock and Brown, 1999) This perspective draws on Berger and Luckmann’s (1991) idea of knowledge as socially constructed and enacted in everyday routines. Therefore, in an organizational context knowledge becomes knowledge only when there is a shared recognition of it as knowledge by organizational members. The members of an organization socially construct knowledge as they perform their everyday activities. Knowledge is not there to be discovered or revealed. It is actually fabricated in practice, through talking, writing or acting (cf. Tsoukas, 1996). Talk and interaction enable collective interpretations and negotiations (Brown and Duguid, 1989; Orr, 1996), thereby creating a common understanding and helping to establish a community of practitioners. Knowing is mediated, situated, provisional, pragmatic and contested. Hierarchical organizations must also be understood as relations of power and domination that affect not only  ows of knowledge but also how new knowledge is accepted and enacted (Foucault, 1982; Blackler, 1995). This is particularly important when considering work activities in knowledge-intensive organizations where tensions exist between professionals, managers and established problems solvers (Argyris and Schön, 1996).

Blackler (1995) distinguished between embrained, embodied, encultured, embedded and encoded knowledge; it must therefore be understood that there are  ve different dimensions that are related to how knowledge is constructed. Each one of these is embedded in different practices and enacted in its own distinct mode. Knowledge is a shared entity and the production of knowledge is to a certain degree a matter of cooperation and a sharing process. Traditionally, decisions are made by management and imposed on organizational members, which can create con ict and the rise of counter-activities aimed at reformulating organizational structures. New knowledge can also be created and institutionalized as a result of bottom-up activities, such as ‘skunk work’, where members of the organization impose a concept or an activity based on their own de nition of the relevant criteria, irrespective of the of cial goals of the organization. An important task is therefore to highlight how knowledge becomes reattached to and embedded in the ongoing processes within the organization (cf. Spender, 1996). Typically people select among alternative possibilities in order to interpret a certain situation. The interpretation is connected to the organizational context in a manner similar to that stated in Weick (1979):

Selection is the organizational process that generates answers to the question ‘What’s going on here?’ The selection process selects meanings and interpretations directly and it selects individuals, departments, groups, or goals indirectly. The selection process houses decision-making, but it is crucial to remember that decisionmaking in the organizing model means selecting some interpretation of the world and some set of extrapolations from that interpretation and then using these summaries as constraints on subsequent acting (p. 175).

Thus, understanding knowledge as a social phenomenon carved out as people perform their everyday activities presents a new agenda for understanding the processes of knowledge creation. It means that the concept of knowledge as a gathering of facts has to be abandoned and what Latour and Woolgar (1979/1986) called the ‘social construction of [scienti c] facts (p. 32) be looked at. From the authors’ point of view it is just as important to analyse what is recognized as knowledge in an organization as to analyse the social processes by which information becomes knowledge. This does not mean that knowledge cannot be stored and retrieved and that the processes of knowledge creation cannot be formalized, but that this formalization has to be carried out in accordance with a speci c social context based on work practices in which people attach certain meanings to their everyday activities.

## Peer review: institutionalizing and certifying new knowledge in organizations

When people select meanings they attempt to be reasonable in their selections. However, what seems reasonable to one person may not be reasonable to another. By sharing knowledge and/or sharing practices that lead to new knowledge, different views become aligned and a shared mindset evolves among the participants in a group. This does not necessarily mean that everybody agrees on a certain matter, but at least they can agree on what they disagree about.

In order to frame this relationship the concept of peer review as an example of a ‘social organizer’ of common concepts and ideas among the participants in an organization is introduced. Peer review can work as an abstraction that explains how knowledge becomes socially anchored in an organization. Members can provide their interpretations of a certain case in a structured way and obtain feedback from other members, which in turn generates what is socially accepted as knowledge in the organization (cf. Schultze and Boland, 2000). Another important dimension of peer review is its importance for innovation. Innovations are context bound. In order to be noticed, they have to be communicated. In this case peer review can function as an incentive for raising awareness of novel ideas ‘discovered’ by others that might subsequently become exploitable (Chubin and Hackett, 1990, p. 200).

The peer review process was developed through the establishment of a referee system in the  rst scienti c journals in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Europe. Over time, it has become a system for certifying new knowledge (Ziman, 1968; Chubin and Hackett, 1990; Berkenkotter and Huckin, 1995; Fuller, 1995). As Zuckerman and Merton (1973) put it:

The referee system in science involves systematic use of judges to assess the acceptability of manuscripts submitted for publication. The referee is thus an example of status judges who are charged with evaluating the quality of role-performance in a social system (p. 460).

With the editorial referee system for journal publications as the archetype, peer review is by now a fairly well-established academic procedure for assessing the scienti c quality of work, for assigning candidates to academic positions or for funding or rejecting research proposals. All established researchers are involved in peer review processes as reviewers of books, articles and curriculum vitae or are the subject of reviews. By publicizing knowledge it becomes disseminated in a way that makes it recognizable as ‘new knowledge’. Before articles are published (in conferences, journals, etc.) they are peer reviewed in order to ensure that the  ndings are new and that the quality of the research presented is high. There are no objective criteria for what counts as ‘relevant’, ‘interesting’ or ‘new’ research. The approach is instead to legitimate the quality of the work in an organizational framework, which is often a particular research community. This is most often done with the help of a peer review procedure. The peer review can thus be seen as a social mechanism through which a discipline’s experts maintain quality control over new knowledge entering the  eld (Hernad, 1982; Berkenkotter and Huckin, 1995).

Academic review processes are established due to scarcity of  nancial resources. Academia is characterized by competition, but the social meaning of peer review is more complicated. Reviews are always discussed and contested, but at the same time reviews are important dimensions for the creation of a knowledge base. Academics are readers and writers and objects and subjects in the process of developing new ideas, concepts and products. When actively taking part in and evaluating other peoples’ work, new ideas emerge and existing ideas are re ned. The academic community is in this respect a varied yet common entity that constitutes the academic society as a culture with a shared set of rules and rituals, values and practices.

Peer review can be seen as an activity that makes sense of and justi es certain information as interesting and strategically important knowledge, while some information remains just plain information (cf. Weick, 1995, p. 35). Even though it is a well-established concept and practice, peer review has recently been subject to some debate due to the shift from paper to electronically based publications. While some have argued for the superiority of electronic publications (cf. Ginzhparg, 1994, 1998; Odlyzko, 1995; Harnard and Hemms, 1998), others have stressed that there are serious social implications that need to be considered. Widening scholarly publicizing is not only about making knowledge accessible in the form of electronic media. Practices attached to traditional paper-based journals, e.g. peer review systems as a way of legitimizing knowledge as new, interesting and of high quality, also need to be considered (Kling, 1991; Kling and Covi, 1995; Kling and Lamb, 1996).

Although the peer review process is closely related to the academic community, it has been found fruitfu to reformulate the concept of peer reviewing in order to accommodate other kinds of organizations outside of academia. What are the more general principles and practices of peer reviewing? What should an organizational framework look like? Peer review is a way of legitimating information as knowledge, which in practice means to legitimate it as ‘something worth knowing anything about in the organization’, irrespective of the organization. Peer review is a more or less formalized system for knowledge sharing and for qualifying new facts and ideas as knowledge. Quality assurance is both the means for assuring the quality of knowledge and a way of organizing the research community in terms of resources. The organization of knowledge and the social context have to be understood as mutually dependent. The role of knowledge – how it is handled, valued and deployed – is intimately subjected to the organizational framework as well as work practices. In order to manage knowledge a deeper understanding is therefore needed of how these interdependencies come about, develop and change in different contexts based on empirical studies.

## Method for data collection and analysis

The case study in this paper is based on an empirical study of quality assurance experts working within the central quality assurance support department in a worldwide pharmaceutical company. The pharmaceutical domain is increasingly subject to con icting demands for innovation and both continuously expanding knowledge and adjusting work processes so that  rms can accomplish the challenge (cf. Henderson and Cockburn, 1994). In order to meet these demands participants’ ability to act appropriately in such complex work situations becomes important and this is what we might call knowledge work conditions (cf. Ljungberg, 1999). Although this study is limited to the pharmaceutical domain, the case does provide a useful illustration of how knowledge-intensive  rms can apply and visualize review processes as a method of disseminating and legitimating new knowledge.

The case represents a valuable domain for studying the organization of knowledge work as the individuals involved were a professional group of quality assurance experts. Most of them were chemical engineers, while others were administrative and management people. As a group they were recognized as specialists within validation work and supported other units within the organization. Validation work involves the quality assurance of chemical and biological processes as well as technical equipment when producing pharmaceutical health care products.

In order to obtain a coherent understanding of the speci c knowledge work in this case, the research approach chosen was inspired by ethnographical methods (Hammersley and Atkinson, 1995; Silverman, 1997). By adopting a qualitative approach to the research the focus was on how this group organized their activities that were related to knowledge creation, dissemination and legitimization. It is particularly important to recognize the subjectivity of knowledge creation and legitimation in these activities (Berger and Luckmann, 1991; Blackler, 1995). It was therefore important to identify the processes of collective agreement between the different employees. The process that was applied was a review process and this was supported by an information system called REQUIREMENTS. The use of this system and the review process were tightly integrated and their information and communication technology (ICT) support is analysed in detail later in the paper.

The empirical effort examining the work of the quality assurance experts consisted of eight semi-structured interviews and recurrent observations during a period of 4 months. Furthermore, the authors attended three meetings and analysed on-line archives. The study also included consideration of one knowledgemediating system. The members of the organization referred to this as a ‘knowledge harmonization tool’. In order to clarify themes and conceptions further the analysis was discussed with the core employees involved in this research in several informal meetings.

The analysis of the empirical data was grounded in theories of knowledge creation, the social construction of knowledge, organizational knowledge and communities of practice. This approach is extremely important when investigating settings where knowledge and knowledge creating processes have a tight coupling to both collaborative work and ICT support. Different aspects, such as the technical and social, become interwoven into a complex context and both need to be given due consideration. In general, however, the perspective on knowledge management represented in this paper draws heavily on theories of the social construction of organizational knowledge within communities of practice.

## Knowledge legitimization in the pharmaceutical domain

The pharmaceutical domain can be characterized as knowledge-intensive work due to its heavy reliance on employee expertise. The work is to a great extent regulated by governmental laws and external guidelines and requirements. The regulatory authorities expect pharmaceutical manufacturers to comply with ‘good manufacturing practice’. Therefore, the pharmaceutical organization that was studied was characterized by a constant need for fast and reliable updates of information that could improve the pharmaceutical production process. This implies that there was a need for constant innovation and improvements in the production process that had to be integrated into existing work procedures. A range of information sources such as law regulations and policy documents had to be included into research and development as well as the manufacturing of the  nal remedy. Work activities for documentation and quality assurance were of particular importance in this setting.

In order to deal with this highly regulated and complex industry it was extremely important to provide the organization’s employees with updated knowledge of current regulations, standards and requirements. The demands on the individual experts became increasingly high, for example in terms of keeping up to date with the new or the continuously revised requirements, whilst at the same time keeping up to date within their own scienti c discipline (e.g. biology, chemistry and engineering). All of the people within quality assurance also had to keep up to date with more general knowledge (e.g. validation and quality assurance) within the group.

## Practising peer review in organizations

Thus, collaboration was considered important and an essential part of the quality assurance work. People within the quality assurance group needed to work together in order to create knowledge about their work domain and to provide the capabilities and resources required in complex settings. Their knowledge-intensive work activities often concerned collaborative problem solving and support that required a more effective way of handling information and knowledge between different people, both in ad hoc processes and other (more formal) processes during long periods of time. For instance, in projects, the decision about who to appoint to a project was usually made by selecting several employees that together covered the competencies needed for that project.

The way in which knowledge processes were handled was therefore very important but complex. It became important to ensure that everyone in the group understood the importance of performing engagements, proper documentation and planning issues, key procedures and conclusions. In order to ensure that the validation of their health care products and processes was performed correctly, the validation support had to be properly planned, coordinated and documented in a collaborative effort throughout the organizational processes. For this reason the quality support work group conducted an in-house peer review process in order to improve its quality control system and engagement ef ciency. They applied a well-structured and prede ned organizational procedure for the peer review.

One key activity in the peer review process concerned the audit and review function in which requirement documents were created, modi ed, reviewed and assigned and then all collected in a common information space, which was an ICT tool that was referred to by the employees as a knowledge harmonization tool. This tool supported the handling of the documents to be reviewed, the coordination of steps and work deliverables and communication between the employees involved. The role of this knowledge harmonization tool is described in the following section and then how the peer review process was conducted is subsequently described in more detail.

## The role of a knowledge harmonization tool

In order to accomplish their work practice of creating, organizing, reviewing, distributing and sharing documents they needed a system that would  exibly support the management of documents, process information and review comments. The documents that were collected and handled in this system were referred to as sanctioned and reliable knowledge. The knowledge contained therein depended on the interpretations and guidelines formulated in the general requirements from the government as well as the speci c best practice from the quality assurance employees themselves. This tool could, according to Schmidt and Bannon (1992), be classi ed as a common information space and it supported communication and information sharing between people across geographically dispersed departments. It was easy to access and browse the documents through a web-based client. All documents were uploaded as word-editable  les. Each document had attributes attached in order to describe it more precisely. For instance, the status and the owner of the document could be read from these descriptions. This meta-information was stored in a database together with the documents in order to support and facilitate effective sorting, searching and browsing.

It was decided to use the system as a tool by which the quality support group could coordinate frequent activities and discuss common issues. A well-de ned work  ow was essential for effective routeing of documents amongst the participants. It was named the REQUIREMENT SYSTEM and was supposed to ensure that work efforts were in compliance with current regulatory, external requirements and, thus, aimed to create and disseminate better practices. The REQUIREMENT SYSTEM contained functions that enabled users or groups of users to design the structures of documents in order to suit their own speci c needs. Hence, there was always an owner associated with a document who was responsible for maintaining it. A core characteristic of one such requirement document was that it should add value to the company and that the expert employee in the relevant subject area should accept the added value. It was to be recognized by the employees as the ‘accepted way of working’ in order to thus ensure constant improvements in the quality of both their work processes and results.

All requirement documents contained two main sections. Section 1 discussed the speci c demands and the rationale for a speci c interpretation of a general requirement. This section had to be adhered to wherever the work described was carried out. Section 2 of the document had to be written in such a way that it could be used directly by each organizational site or unit. For speci c needs this section could be written as a checklist and, if relevant, enclosed with references to external archives, i.e. the databases upon which the interpretation was based.

A requirement document was viewed as ‘a template for improvement in practice. As this practice gained respect as a high quality standard by the various actors, re nements were implemented in the requirement document in order to meet local needs. The idea was that, by using the REQUIREMENT SYSTEM, a certain level of harmonization throughout quality support would be ensured and, therefore, this system was conceived of as a ‘knowledge harmonization and dissemination tool’ among the employees.

The most important aspect of the ICT tool was possibly the use and management of it. Since the collection of documents was shared among many people there had to be some support for coordinating the updates and indicating the status of changes. For this reason an ‘applicability matrix’ was included, which was based on a structured pro le mechanism. The units in this matrix de ned their subject areas in terms of their primary organizational function. The same matrix was also used when de ning the pro le of a document. By using the same attributes and attribute values it was possible to see to which unit the document was in principle applicable. The quality support group was responsible for the maintenance of the system, for example managing distribution lists of employees, uploading of new versions, etc. One assistant in the group managed the general tasks, such as uploading  les, updating the document attributes and maintaining e-mail lists. The document owner supervised and thereby legitimated the entire process.

What the workers perceived as a ‘knowledge harmonization process’ can also be viewed as a knowledge legitimization process within the organization. The paper will now look more closely into the actual peer review process as it was typically conducted.

## The process of peer review

The procedure for the review process included prede- ned steps of preparing, reviewing, accepting and assigning the knowledge to be legitimized as shown in Figure 1.

In the preparation step a form had to be completed in order to request a new requirement. The form was available as a template that could be downloaded from the REQUIREMENT SYSTEM. It was then sent to the quality support group by e-mail. In the acceptance step the manager of the quality support group had to accept the request for the new requirement and the request had to be submitted to the quality support group with documentation for the acceptance and allocation of an owner and a partner. An owner was assigned to be the creator of the requirement while the partner was supposed to be responsible for those people responsible for the review process. A responsible assistant at quality support contacted sites and units with information about new requests for requirements. Subsequently, a resource allocation step was followed in which quality support would ask for allocation of resources for the new requirement preparation. Sites and units had to respond to this information. Work group members and reviewers would be allocated and their names would be published in the REQUIREMENT SYSTEM. This procedure created a temporary network or knowledge community where members and reviewers became peers with a mandate for de ning what was considered to be the standard for the new requirement.

Following this activity the next step, i.e. requirement preparation, could begin. The owner decided how the preparation would take place. The owner could either make the decision or delegate it to another work group member. In order to ensure uniformity, the requirement document template within the REQUIREMENT SYSTEM had to be used. First, a draft had to be submitted to the quality support department, i.e. to the assistant, in order to be published in the REQUIRE-MENT SYSTEM. All employees involved in the review had to agree on the contents before the  rst draft could be submitted.

In the coordination step the owner and the partner were informed about the acceptance by e-mail. If the requirement was accepted, the owner had to contact each reviewer with the following information: ‘The review can now take place. Please submit your comments by e-mail no later than…’ The  rst round of reviews aimed at providing initial comments on the  rst draft. These comments needed to be communicated and would subsequently be included within the requirement document. The owner was responsible for collecting and  ling all the comments. If no comments were received from a reviewer, the owner was required to annotate with his or her initials and the annotation ‘no comments received’. Finally, in this initial review, the owner and the partner were required to submit an updated draft to the assistant in the quality support group, who in turn published the draft within the REQUIREMENT SYSTEM.

In the second review process the owner was expected to contact each reviewer again with the following information: ‘The  nal review can now take place. Please submit your comments by e-mail no later than...’ The owner was again responsible for  ling the comments and the procedure from the initial review process repeated itself. The owner was accountable to the partner, to work group members and to the reviewers for comments that might incidentally be left out. As in the  rst round, the owner was responsible for submitting the  nal draft as well as the comments to the quality support group by e-mail in order for the assistant to publish the  nal draft in the REQUIRE-MENT SYSTEM.

The acceptance step included a procedure for informing relevant units about the new requirement. The assistant would distribute a form to all relevant sites and units. This form was a request for acceptance among units to which the requirement applied. The distribution was done in accordance with the ‘applicability matrix’ from which they could read what organizational and business units were interested in or applicable to what requirement document. Further comments on the requirement were not allowed during the acceptance process.

When all forms relevant to other organizational sites had been received, the assistant would again distribute the accepted requirement to the relevant areas for signing. During the signing procedure all relevant people that had accepted the requirement signed the  nal version. This resulted in the  nal and signed version being published within the REQUIREMENT SYSTEM.

In order for the new requirement to come into force, all relevant sites and units had to implement the requirement according to a time schedule. This implementation meant preparing internal procedures, local instructions or other relevant working documents (written agreements, protocols, etc.). If the implementation of the requirement was delayed for any reason, the delay would be published in the system.

Owners were expected to keep the documents updated with respect to changes in the internal and external requirements in the  eld. This represented an ongoing maintenance process. If a requirement needed updating or revision, a request for revision was to be submitted to the quality support group by e-mail. Quality support would then contact the owner and partner in order to initiate an update of the requirement and a new review process. When minor changes were initiated, the owner, partner and quality support could assess in collaboration whether the requirement document needed one or two reviews or only the acceptance round before it could be implemented. This information was always available from the REQUIREMENT SYS-TEM.

## Discussion

Rather than seeing themselves as information processing, decision making and problem solving industries, technologically advanced companies are becoming aware of themselves as knowledge seeking and knowledge creating organizations (Powell, 1998). This is a general change that will affect companies to a lesser or greater degree. Even ‘traditional’ industries will have to become more knowledge intensive in order to progress or at least keep up with competitors and changing conditions. Coexisting with this demand being placed on them, new demands are being placed upon pharmaceutical  rms by the outside world in terms of certi cations or formal requirements that affect the way innovation can be made. Business opportunities are structured in relation to the conditions established alongside the market. However, the case study presented here falls outside these four categories and illustrates additional challenges.

The quality support department in this study asserts the quality of health care products by processes both within and in cooperation with other sites in the worldwide organization. They created networks of partnerships both in the form of interorganizational relations and in the form of alliances with external associates. Decisions are thus made based on the way things are understood and made sense of in this context.

As their work is extremely dependent on their own ability to apply new requirements and standards that are constantly changing the conditions for work procedures, the ability to make sense of this new information and introduce and implement it into the organization becomes crucial. The organization is characterized by a constant need for fast and reliable updates of information that could potentially in uence how the production process of pharmaceuticals should be carried out. Such information includes law regulation and policy documents that must be included in research and development, as well as the manufacturing of the  nal remedy. The various employees of the organization create environments for solving work tasks by seeking and exchanging information, by ongoing communication and by collaborating. However, it is not always clear what particular information needs to be considered and why. The challenge is to relate to the incoming information in a creative way by using expertise knowledge and, at the same time, ensuring that this knowledge operates in compliance with existing procedures in a way that is acceptable to the organization as a whole. As one interviewee stated ‘It is our lifeline in the work of interpreting and anchoring knowledge and procedures’ (quality expert).

The challenge here then is to consider the synthetic policies and guidelines supplied by the external regulators and provide the organization with acceptable new standards and requirements.

Organizations of this kind need to establish imperatives for rapid change in their pattern of activities (Argyris and Schön, 1978, 1996). By focusing on knowing rather than knowledge, the distinction between knowledge and learning can be avoided (Lave, 1993). To ‘know’ is often to learn something new or to use what a person already knows in a new situation. This was exactly what the quality support group aimed to accomplish. In order to maintain high standards in their work, members of the organization created and used different types of knowledge. Knowledge was not thought of as a precise body of truth that was internalized and remained ready for reuse. The dynamic character of their work made some types of knowledge more useful than others at some points in time. Knowing what to do and what decisions to make was not the same from one day to another or in different situations. It was practised among all members in the organization and accepted as ‘their way of working’.

In order to establish a reliable infrastructure that will (1) have new information evaluated and (if it was considered important), (2) have it circulated throughout the organization and (3) have it implemented in both the research and development and production processes, the case organization in this study has established an ‘evaluation loop’ that is organized according to what in academic life would be recognized as a peer review process. The peer review not only ensures that relevant new knowledge is distributed to the different parties involved, but it is also particularly important for converting information into something that is considered to be reliable and accountable knowledge (Harnad, 1982; Berkenkotter and Huckin, 1995). The social meaning of peer review is to legitimize new knowledge by sanctioning it organizationally, thereby creating a platform for collective sense making (Weick 1995). This process leads to what Spender (1996) called organizational knowledge. Our study demonstrates how this can be favourably conducted in practice.

The concept of peer review was used as a model for knowledge sharing and quality assurance within the quality support department. The incentive for quality work was the ability to link and assess knowledge and work performance. A complex interwoven process of interactions between people (Swan et al., 2000), both those who were in charge of review requirements and those who wanted to contribute, re ect and provide comments on them, was staged and carried out. This was seen as crucial to the participants in order to improve the quality of the work carried out and, above all, to legitimize the new requirements and procedures in the organization. This therefore is a question of interaction and social legitimization of what becomes known as knowledge in the organization. As noted earlier, knowledge had to be accounted for as an activity that was not static and easily discovered by outside observers or codi ed into a formal database. The members in the organization constructed what they recognized as valid knowledge in a process that was characterized by continuous negotiation and legitimization. The knowledge created in such a process was the outcome of several practices and fabricated by interactions between various employees. Other expressions that indicated the importance of more organized feedback loops were expressed in an interview.

What I would like us [the departmental group] to spend more time on, in meetings for instance, is feedback and comments on work documents and reports as well as slide presentations and papers that I am going to present in a conference or a workshop.

The outcome of the review process was conceived of as a value adding process of managing knowledge and establishing best practice. As such, those involved emphasized that the application of this organizational method would imply a quali cation process for knowledge creation, harmonization and dissemination among co-workers. The peer review process also established a common view of what information to regard as knowledge in the organization, thereby homogenizing a socially and spatially dispersed work milieu. Thus, partial de nitions based on relations of authority were avoided.

This paper has illustrated actual observations of knowledge legitimization processes and argues that review processes are good examples of how to qualify knowledge in socially constructed interaction processes, e.g. knowledge creation, legitimization and sharing. Whether it is computer supported or not, the importance of interaction processes in which writing, reading and providing comments on work documents are truly important for the negotiation and creation of knowledge should be emphasized. However, when it comes to computer support, it should be these processes that are considered for design implications. There are also different arguments that can be made that can help to explain why the usefulness of the REQUIREMENT SYS-TEM was so highly rated.

Firstly, what makes the process of legitimizing knowledge dif cult is that it takes place in a dispersed organizational context. In this context ‘quality knowledge becomes a strategic trade-off between expert knowledge and what can possibly be made acceptable to others. Social interaction through the IT-supported workspace in the REQUIREMENT SYSTEM, i.e. the document repository, is therefore seen as a good way of qualifying working practice because it is explicit as well as effective. Using the system results in what is seen to be a reduction in the complexity of knowledge articulation.

Secondly, by using the REQUIREMENTS SYSTEM a certain level of harmonization throughout validation of quality support could be ensured. It was used as a template for ‘best practice’, to learn from and be inspired by. All the documents included in the REQUIREMENTS SYSTEM played an important role as formal and applicable knowledge sources about how to get the job done. This current tool also facilitated harmonization and transformation of knowledge by distributing it electronically among dispersed co-workers. It was adaptable and tailor-made to the needs of different users (for instance, the documents were created collaboratively in a co-authoring process and stored in an editable standard format). The system was also applicable to and usable by practitioners, support workers and management.

Thirdly, as shown in this paper, using this system per se did not assure knowledge legitimization and quali - cation. The most important role the system played was that it supported the social process of constructing knowledge within the organization. Reading and reviewing colleagues’ work organizationally constituted this process. The social importance of feedback was also a recurrent theme in the interviews. Even though this feedback process was not fully supported in the system, such as directly adding annotations, comments and references to the document under review or viewing and combining various review comments in one single tool, the need for this functionality was apparent to those involved. ‘Please give me comments on this piece of work!’ would be shouted in the corridor or even sent out in e-mails in a more informal way.

To summarize the discussion, the arguments presented here are now related to a more general discussion of how to use the principles of peer review in different contexts. There are of course major differences between academia and other types of organization. The way knowledge is treated in academia is very much in homology with and a result of the meaning and status as an organizing principle that knowledge has achieved in this context. However, business and organizations outside universities are becoming more ‘academic’ in the sense that the way knowledge is de ned and treated is seen as a crucial asset for the survival and successive elaboration of activities (cf. Drucker, 1988; Grant, 1996). The quality assurance of knowledge is becoming more important as well as the managing of knowledge at an organizational level.

De ning processes of quality assurance as a similar type of activity as the peer review process that is found in academic life draws attention to different dimensions of the management of knowledge than is usually the case when discussing ‘knowledge management’ in organizations. In the work practice studied, knowledge quality was not seen as an objective ‘fact’. Instead, it was  rst negotiated and then rejected or acknowledged as being recognized or not as knowledge by the people involved in the quality assurance process. They were all experts in their particular  eld and were thereby a legitimate part of the process of de ning new knowledge and deciding how to act based on the de nitions produced.

## Conclusion

From the  ndings presented here it is concluded that peer review is a constructive way of conceptualizing the process of quality assurance and how it is socially organized in organizations outside of academia. By disseminating information in the organization and legitimizing it through anchoring it in different nodes of the organization, information becomes recognized and accepted as knowledge during this process. Formalization and accessibility of knowledge are central aspects to work performance in this context. The review procedure plays an important coordination role in workers’ daily knowledge activities. Furthermore, these efforts are tightly coupled and integrated into a common ITsupported, ‘legitimized’ practice. Therefore, a particular challenge in this process seemed to be studying the collaborative effort of reviewing and legitimizing knowledge rather than trying to de ne what knowledge ‘is’ or how it could best be stored and retrieved.

When considering IT support, it is argued here that it is these processes that should be the focus of attention. The  ndings presented here clearly show that technologies that enable and facilitate communication and interaction among people who may work temporally as well as spatially dispersed are necessary in order to structure the processes of legitimizing information as knowledge. Attention should be directed towards the interactive processes of communication, negotiation, reviewing and commenting instead of focusing simply on what information should be fed into databases. The quality of knowledge is, from this point of view, not seen as inherent in a piece of information or constitutive for what further activities need to take place. Instead there should be more focus on how common views are established and the constitution of the social context in which the legitimization process operates. The concept of peer review is one way of doing this.
