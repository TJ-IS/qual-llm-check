# The knowledge management tussle – speech communities and rhetorical strategies in the development of knowledge management

STEFFEN RAUB

HEC Lausanne, Bâtiment des Facultés des Sciences Humaines 1, CH-1015 Lausanne-Dorigny, Switzerland

CHARLES-CLEMENS RÜLING

HEC - Management Studies, University of Geneva, 40, Boulevard du Pont d’Arve, CH-1211 Geneva 4, Switzerland

Knowledge management has become a major trend since the mid-1990s. Different professional communi ties, among them information systems/information technology (IS/IT) and more business managementoriented actors, have created strong conceptual ties with the idea of managing knowledge. This paper studies the structure and development of the knowledge management discourse over an 18-year period with an emphasis on the role of the IS/IT community in shaping this discourse. In order to do so, a content analysis of 434 article abstracts from the ABI/Inform database referring to ‘knowledge management’ was performed. The argument here is based on a theoretical framework derived from recent theorizing about popular management knowledge as fashion and it is assumed that different professional or ‘speech communities’ gathering around a concept such as knowledge management enter into competition for limited organizational resources. The paper’s  ndings show the co-existence of two distinct speech communities involved in the knowledge management debate, focusing on either IS/IT or general management issues and they support the idea that both communities engage in a joint effort at sustaining knowledge management as a fashion  eld.

## Introduction

The role and in uence of information systems/information technology (IS/IT) professionals in organizations and the balance of power between the ‘IT world’ and the management profession has been a topic of interest for some time. The antagonistic elements in this relationship have been analysed by some as a problem of a lack of integration between business managers and IT people. Others suspect business management’s attempts to exert control over the IT function and its experts (see Scarbrough (1993) for a more detailed account). The recent emergence of knowledge management, both in its incarnation as a  eld of scienti c inquiry and a domain of managerial action, provides a new object of study for those who are concerned with a rift between IT and the rest of the world.

The debate on knowledge management is no stranger to belligerent rhetoric. Some observers of the knowledge management scene have even gone so far as to detect a need for IS professionals ‘to up the ante in the KM tussle’ (Hyams, 1998, p. 40). Taking a similar stance means asking whether knowledge management can be classi ed as an ‘IT topic’ or should be seen as belonging to one or several other domains of managerial expertise. If this antagonistic view of ITbusiness management relations were to be pushed a bit further it could even be speculated whether IT professionals and scholars in the IT  eld have exerted suf cient in uence for defending their terrain and substantiating their claim on the knowledge management topic.

This paper uses publications on knowledge management as a starting point for analysing the actors and processes that shape the development of a  eld of inquiry. It will examine the emergence of knowledge management over an extended period of time. Viewing knowledge management as a ‘fashionable’ concept by referring to a number of insights derived from the recent literature on management fashion allows the paper to shed new light on the state of the knowledge management  eld as a whole and the contribution of the IT community within this  eld.

## Understanding knowledge management from a fashion perspective

While there is widespread agreement that knowledge management is concerned with identifying, sharing and leveraging organizational knowledge for competitive advantage (e.g. Probst et al., 1999), there are to date an astonishing variety of perspectives that are subsumed under the knowledge management umbrella. While an exhaustive review would go beyond the limitations of this paper, the most important contributions stem from strategic management, human resources management (HRM) and the domain of IS/IT as well as from the organization theory and organizational behaviour literature.

Strategic management researchers, particularly those of the resource-based view (e.g. Wernerfelt, 1984; Barney, 1991; Conner, 1991) and the core competence approach (Prahalad and Hamel, 1990), have highlighted the importance of knowledge as an organizational resource of strategic signi cance. While strategy research has been occupied with outlining a comprehensive ‘knowledge-based theory of the  rm’ (e.g. Strategic Management Journal, 1996, Volume 17(10)), other disciplines have contributed their views on how to analyse and manage knowledge as an organizational resource. HRM, for instance, focuses on the importance of ‘knowledge workers’ for organizational value creation (e.g. Drucker, 1988; Journal of Management Studies, 1993, Volume 30(6)). IS researchers stress the importance of ‘knowledge management systems’ for supporting the identi cation and distribution of knowledge in organizations (e.g. Alavi and Leidner, 1997). Finally, organization theorists have provided a variety of concepts for analysing the creation, distribution and use of knowledge in organizations (e.g. Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995; Probst et al., 1999).

At the turn of the millennium, there is substantial evidence that knowledge management has become a ‘fashionable’ management concept. For instance, the knowledge management literature has shown an explosive growth curve over the past few years. A recent keyword search on the web site of amazon.com yielded 8003 hits for the keyword ‘knowledge’. In a search carried out in January 2001, the more restrictive keyword ‘knowledge management’ still yielded 172 hits. The EBSCO Business Source Premier database contained an astonishing 1397 article abstracts including the keyword ‘knowledge management’ in the period 1989– 2000. More than 95% of them were concentrated in the 4-year period from 1997 to 2000. Moreover, the variation in publication intensity over time resembles the left half of a bell curve. This is generally seen as a primary characteristic of the early and intermediate stages in the diffusion process of fashionable concepts (cf. Figure 1).

The increased attention that knowledge management has received over recent years is not a purely academic phenomenon. Popular management outlets have contributed their share to knowledge management’s popularity. For instance, Fortune magazine (Harrington, 1999) recently featured a list of the ‘big ideas’ of the twentieth century, in which knowledge management was treated on a par with other management classics such as scienti c management, Ford’s assembly line, Sloan’s concept of a multidivisional organization, total quality management (TQM) and reengineering. Other indicators of a fashion craze include the mushrooming of conferences, symposia and workshops dedicated to knowledge management. These provide a stage for an increasing number of ‘knowledge management stars’ such as Fortune’s Thomas Stewart, Skandia’s Leif Edvinsson or the Japanese knowledge management guru Ikujiro Nonaka.

Last but not least, knowledge management has become big business. The increasing importance of the label knowledge management for the purpose of sales promotion is re ected in the number of IT  rms as well as IT or strategy consultancies offering ‘knowledge management solutions’. These solutions include a large spectrum, ranging from hardware to customized software and related consulting services. Management researchers have sensed this accumulation of trends and, as a result, have portrayed knowledge management as an ideal candidate for the study of managerial discourses (e.g. H. Scarbrough and J. Swan, submitted).

## The management fashion approach

Traditionally, management research has tended to assume that managers use particular tools or techniques in order to improve organizational effectiveness. From such a perspective, changes in the popularity of a tool or technique may be explained by a technique’s perceived impact on organizational effectiveness.

Conventional wisdom suggests that the greater the expected positive impact, the more popular a tool or technique will become.

An example from the knowledge management debate may serve as a case in point. Fortune magazine recently presented a list of the favourite management tools of 1997 based on Bain & Co.’s Management Tools and Techniques Survey. The most revealing features in this article, which was succulently entitled ‘What’s Today’s Special at the Consultants’ Café?’, were the headlines that came with the three charts presented alongside the article text. Headline 1 read ‘These Management Tools Were 1997’s Favorites…’. This was followed by a ‘top ten’ vote including topics from strategic planning to benchmarking, core competencies, re-engineering and TQM. Headline 2 continued ‘But They Go In and Out of Fashion’ and was accompanied by a chart that presented company ‘usage rates’ of the concepts over a 4-year period. Headline 3 summed up the argument: ‘Because Companies Disagree about Their Value’. This was followed by a ‘satis ed/dissatis ed’ vote for each of the top ten concepts (Rigby, 1998).

The arrangement of the charts and the accompanying text suggested a straightforward message: managers use a variety of tools and techniques in order to improve organizational effectiveness. For ‘popularity changes for each of these tools dependent on changes in the degree of user “satisfaction’’’, read ‘the concepts relative effectiveness or instrumentality in responding to current competitive opportunities and threats’.

As illustrated by this example, the diffusion history of knowledge management in the practitioner-oriented literature appears as a story of pioneering companies attaining superior organizational effectiveness by choosing a new way of conceptualizing and managing their knowledge base. While this view of the diffusion and adoption of new management knowledge certainly has its charm, not least from the viewpoint of practitioners seeking to reduce complexity and af rm the rationality of their behaviour, it is argued here that it cannot provide a suf cient understanding of what is going on within the production and consumption of the knowledge management discourse. As pointed out above, knowledge management is not clearly de ned. It signi es different things to different actors and it seems reasonable to assume that different groups of management actors, both practitioners and scholars alike, engage in competition for the resources related to the ownership of this new idea.

For the purpose of this study it is argued that, in many ways, the development of the knowledge management discourse neatly ties into the recent ‘management fashion’ debate. For instance, H. Scarbrough and J. Swan (submitted) argued that knowledge management ‘displays the classic characteristics of a management fashion’ (p. 10), including a bell-shaped pattern of changing popularity (with knowledge management still on the growth side), fashion setting communities with fashion setters and fashion followers and an array of increasingly institutionalized instances of collective selection operating through conferences, showcases and bestsellers. Seen as a fashion, the knowledge management discourse does not stand alone, but relates to a set of predecessors, successors and rival discourses in the management  eld (Blumer, 1968). Both demarcation efforts and presentations of knowledge management as the logical consequence of other concepts (e.g. organizational learning) place it within a particular type of historic continuity in the development of management knowledge.

The traditional paradigm for studying the development of management knowledge has predominantly been one of usage (see, for example, Beyer and Trice, 1982). It has focused on unidirectional transfer from theory to practice, as well as on performance gaps and instrumental rationality as the guiding principles for explaining the application of management knowledge. Conversely, the management fashion approach underlines that the relationship of management theory and practice is far from being a one-way road. Rather, it is seen as consisting of a system in which the producers of knowledge actively seek the adoption of their products. The management fashion approach also challenges the traditional understanding of rationality and progress as the principal legitimate motives for both knowledge development and knowledge application in management theory and practice (Abrahamson, 1996). The quest for rationality and progress is replaced with other motives such as, for example, the exhibition of status differences or the imitation of others as a demonstration of group adherence and social integration. Thus, management fashion provides a new perspective on the status of management knowledge (which is socially constructed, not cumulative), the dynamics of its development over time (cyclical instead of linear) and the ways in which management knowledge is applied (symbolically and strategically instead of instrumentally rational).

The management fashion approach is complemented by other more speci c contributions. These include work on the role of ‘management gurus’ and consultants (e.g. Jackson, 1996; Clark and Salaman, 1998), the role of different ‘carriers of management knowledge’ across countries, industries, etc. (see, for example, the contributions in Alvarez (1998)) and the social mechanisms contributing to the emergence, spread and disappearance of management knowledge over time (e.g. Huczynski, 1993; Kieser, 1997). The debate is far from being restricted to the academic world. In fact, since the mid-1990s the critique of fads, fashions and the role of management gurus has resonated in the business management literature and the popular business press (see, for example, Thackray, 1993; Shapiro, 1995; Donaldson and Hilmer, 1996; Micklethwait and Wooldridge, 1996; Malone, 1997; The Economist, 1997a,b).

## Looking at the knowledge management fashion from an ‘inside’ perspective

Abrahamson (1996) de ned management fashion as ‘a relatively transitory collective belief, disseminated by management fashion setters, that a management technique leads to rational management progress’ (p. 257). In his view, management fashions do not emerge spontaneously. Rather, he saw them as representing ‘cultural commodities deliberately produced by fashion setters in order to be marketed to fashion followers’ (Abrahamson, 1996, p. 263). While management fashion supply depends on a management fashion setting process in which different management fashion setters, for example gurus, mass media, consulting  rms and business schools among others, create, process and disseminate fashion propositions, management fashion demand depends on ‘sociopsychological’ and ‘technoeconomic’ forces competing with each other.

Empirical research putting Abrahamson’s (1996) fashion framework into practice (e.g. Abrahamson and Fairchild, 1999) has tended to adopt an outside analysis of fashion. Its primary interest is in analysing a management fashion by looking at the shape and rhetoric accompanying a fashion throughout its diffusion, i.e. from initial competition with other concepts occupying its ‘niche’ to its eventual replacement. Outside analyses tend to assume the homogeneity of particular management fashion niches. Even though rarely stated explicitly, this assumption underlies all research claiming to study a singular management fashion and this is particularly the case when the underlying data stem from relatively large databases (e.g. ABI/Inform).

In order to complement the outside analysis of management fashions, this paper challenges the assumption of homogeneity and concentrates on the competing discourses that shape a management fashion from within. The approach to analysing the development of fashion discourse used here therefore comes closer to sociological analyses of the fashion  eld focusing on interaction (Blumer, 1969) and the struggle for the power in order to de ne good practice among different groups of actors (Bourdieu, 1984). From this perspective, this paper’s main research interest in analysing management fashion lies in the question of whether there exist different discourses and identi able groups of actors in terms of ‘speech communities’ in the development of a management fashion and whether and how these communities interact.

## Methods and data analysis

The empirical analysis used here is designed for providing an account of the development of the knowledge management discourse over a longer period of time. The paper’s perspective in doing this is threefold. First, the participants involved in the ‘making of knowledge management history’ are examined. Second, an important focus is on the content aspect. The paper analyses which conceptual ideas are linked to the term knowledge management and which shifts in content can be observed over time. Finally, statements that comment directly on the status of knowledge management as a fashionable concept are borne in mind. The entire analysis follows an inductive orientation in the sense that all  nal coding categories were developed from a preliminary, exploratory coding of the data.

The empirical study is based on an analysis of 434 abstracts retrieved from the ABI/Inform database. The search criterion used was the keyword ‘knowledge management’, which could appear in either the title, the keywords or the body text of an abstract. The search period covers the years 1975-1998, with a clear majority of abstracts stemming from the 3-year period between 1996 and 1998 (see Table 1 for the distribution of the article frequencies over time). Building an empirical analysis on article abstracts assumes that, taken together, an article title, article keywords and the article abstract itself provide a relatively comprehensive overview of the topics addressed in the article, as well as a reasonable representation of its overall orientation.

The codebook developed from this preliminary analysis proved adequate for coding the entire sample. No additional variables or categories had to be introduced during the coding process.

Article content is the most important variable in the coding scheme. In this variable each article is coded according to the topics, concepts, tools or ideas that are mentioned in direct relation to knowledge management. The various categories were established on the basis of a preliminary analysis as described above. Each category was coded present or absent. Thus, each article could be coded present in several categories up to a theoretical maximum of 12 categories.

Several articles made explicit statements about the status of knowledge management as a management tool or concept. In particular, statements about the respective authors’ overall evaluation of knowledge management were coded. These could be classi ed as supportive, critical or absent.

Some limitations apply to the results of our empirical analysis. The most important is the choice of abstracts as a basis for coding. Even a well-written abstract does not usually cover all of the important ideas of an article. A more casual approach to abstract writing may introduce an even more signi cant bias. Another limitation is related to the selection of the sample by a keyword search in a speci c database. This procedure may inadvertently exclude (1) relevant journal articles where the keyword knowledge management does not appear in the abstract or (2) articles that are directly related to the key ideas of knowledge management but avoid using the term. In addition, as the ABI/Inform database is limited to periodicals, the analysis does not include important books written about knowledge management during the period under review. Finally, journal articles re ect discourses on knowledge management but may only serve as a vague proxy for evaluating the actual adoption of knowledge management practices in organizations. Our interpretation takes these limitations into account.

## The structure of the knowledge management discourse – distinct speech communities

One of the main focuses of this paper is on the role of the IS/IT community in shaping the knowledge management discourse. Before this issue can be addressed it appears vital to analyse whether separate speech communities (Barley et al., 1988) in and around the knowledge management discourse can indeed be identi ed. This question can be approached from two perspectives: a thematic one and another one that focuses on publication outlets. The thematic perspective involves asking whether certain topics linked to the knowledge management discourse appear in more or less clearly identi able clusters. The focus on publication outlets means that one tries to  nd out how clearly certain publications focus on speci c issues within the overall range of topics linked to knowledge management.

As a  rst approach to this question the 12 content categories from our coding scheme were correlated. Figure 2 shows the statistically signi cant associations (based on Spearman’s r as a non-parametric measure of correlation) among these categories. Even though the magnitude of correlation (as indicated by the line weights) is rather weak for most of the cases, the signs of the correlation coef cients indicate an interesting pattern. On the one hand, a strong correlation is observed between knowledge management tools and IT tools and a weaker one between IT tools and IT systems. These IS/IT-related themes overall show a highly consistent pattern of negative correlations with more managerial issues such as processes, intellectual capital, measurement, strategic implications, human resources implications, organizational implications and learning. The managerial issues in turn appear to be consistently positively correlated amongst each other.

In essence, these results seem to suggest that, from a thematic point of view, knowledge management is discussed in terms of two rather distinct groups of concepts. A group of IS/IT-related topics can be clearly distinguished from a number of topics that are more of a ‘general management’ nature. Thus, whereas the

In order to analyse the stance taken by different groups of publication outlets towards knowledge management the variable journal af liation was recoded into two separate categories. Table 3 displays content variables that show a significant correlation with at least one of the two journal groups. Despite the small absolute value of most of the correlation coef cients, their signs reveal an interesting constellation. IS/IT-oriented journals are positively correlated with mentions of IT systems, IT tools and knowledge management tools and show negative correlations with the remaining content variables. Conversely, the MGT category of journals is positively correlated with the topics of learning, intellectual capital, processes and strategic implications. These journals show negative correlations with IT systems and IT tools. As a result of these two analyses it can be concluded with reasonable con dence that there are indeed two relatively distinct speech communities in terms of two groups of publication outlets that pursue particular thematic approaches in their respective treatments of knowledge management.

The dynamic aspect of the knowledge management discourse can also be analysed beyond this static view. Based on the previous categorization, the overall volume of contributions to the knowledge management debate from the two speech communities over time was mapped. ## Discussion

The data here reveal the existence of two distinct speech communities (Barley et al., 1988) in the knowledge management literature. The correlation analysis of the content variables shows that the issues discussed within knowledge management articles tend to have an af liation with either an IS/IT or a ‘general management’ discourse. Moreover, it has been shown that the respective discourse is signi cantly correlated with particular types of publication outlets. IS/IT-oriented articles are more frequent in journals with a clear IS/IT orientation, whereas there is a higher probability of  nding the more general management-oriented discourse in journals with a general management or other disciplinary af liation.

While at  rst sight this does not seem very surprising - articles in IT journals talk about IT issues and articles in management journals talk about management issues, so what? – there is, in our opinion, more to it.

The  ndings underline the idea that there ‘is’ not only one knowledge management discourse, but at least two and, moreover, that these different discourses are not only situated at an individual (author’s) level, but seem to be related to particular types of journals addressing speci c professional audiences. Until about 1994, the knowledge management discourse appears to have been con ned to the realm of IS/IT. Only since the mid-1990s has there emerged a strengthening of general management-related issues as well as a stable share of all articles that refer to both IS/IT and general management.

These  ndings are in line with other research looking at the assimilation and implementation of loosely de ned sets of ideas among professional communities.

It also supports the hypothesis that IS/IT professionals have stolen a march on general management professionals in actively embracing knowledge management in order to mobilize organizational resources (H. Moreover, the relatively stable association of discourses and publication outlets leads to a discussion of some elements related to the institutionalization of the knowledge management  eld as a whole.

## Struggle for resources and interaction among speech communities

As long as members of different speech communities, IT and human resources managers for example, deal with issues that are of little interest outside their particular domain, any particular con ict would not be expected to emerge from the co-existence of the two knowledge management discourses. However, the coexistence of potentially rival discourses becomes interesting when the discourses relate to an issue, in this case knowledge management, that has risen to general interest across the actors’ particular domains. In this situation, the different communities become rivals for the power to de ne what constitutes a legitimate (knowledge management ) discourse and for access to scarce societal and organizational resources that are typically associated with it. It is expected that competition for knowledge management resources will be re ected in high (hostile) interactions between the speech communities, thereby resulting in the use of antagonistic rhetoric concerning the competing communities’ themes.

Some signs of interaction between the speech communities can be seen in the fact that, 2 years after the strong entry of the general management community into the knowledge management discourse, the share of articles making reference to both content categories rose to 25% and that the proportion of articles in each speech community’s set of journals which used the other side’s discourse was relatively high. However, only an extremely small proportion of all the texts looked at actually contain negative value statements (cf. Table 8).

Abrahamson and Fairchild (1999) argued that negative rhetoric is a typical phenomenon in the competition between different popular management concepts. When this idea is extended to the struggle for resources among different speech communities gathered around a common concept, the question is whether there is a model that explains why, in this case, there seems to be no general sign of con ict even though some isolated sources evoke an ongoing ‘battle’ for ownership of knowledge management (Hyams, 1998).

Abrahamson and Fairchild (1999, pp. 729-30) outlined ‘debunking’, ‘sur ng’ and ‘sustaining’ as the principal rhetoric strategies that can be found throughout the downswing of a management fashion. Debunking involves the rejection of a concept without providing a substitute. Sur ng describes a rejection of an existing concept that goes together with the proposition of a replacement. Sustaining advocates the continued use of a particular technique. While originally conceived for discussing the rhetorical strategies of fashion actors over time, this framework lends itself equally to analysing the strategies used in the struggle for resources among various interpretations of one concept. In the case here, debunking would designate a rhetoric of simple rejection of rival interpretations, whereas sur ng would include rejection of the rival discourse together with promotion of one’s own interpretation. Both strategies would involve the use of negative rhetoric. Contrary to this expectation, the data here reveal no substantial negative rhetoric.

The absence of negative rhetoric leads to consideration of an alternative strategy of interaction among rival speech communities building on a complementary relationship. Such a strategy, which can be termed ‘piggybacking’, would try to bene t from the general interest in a common theme, which, obviously, is also promoted by the rival interpretations, by either underlining the complementarity of the various interpretations or by making no reference to the other discourses at all. Piggybacking leaves the major premises of a competing discourse unchanged but adds new perspectives and/or re nes the processes or conditions of successful implementation. Such a mode appears more adapted than the simple con ict perspective to explaining the interaction of both speech communities with an absence of negative rhetoric on the other’s discourse.

Piggybacking also seems more consistent with positions in the fashion literature that focus on the idea of a type of hidden collusion among the fashion actors and tend to ask why knowledge entrepreneurs should engage in negative discourses at all (e.g. Bourdieu, 1984). It seems reasonable to assume that a large number of in uential trendsetters within each of the respective communities are cognizant of the fact that they are part of a ‘fashion system’ (Blumer, 1969) in which too extensive negative rhetorics could in the long run hurt their credibility as producers of new and better knowledge. A positive ‘synergistic’ or ‘complementary’ discourse is likely to be more in line with their long-term goals.

## Institutionalization of the knowledge management  eld

The data here suggest the emergence of knowledge management as a (relatively heterogeneous)  eld in its own right with its own specialized conferences, journals (e.g. Journal of Knowledge Management), rules (best practices, benchmarking and comparative case studies) and regulatory agencies (knowledge management assessment tools). In this sense, the changing knowledge management discourses re ect an underlying process of institutionalization and the development of a particular, well-de ned technique into an autonomous  eld. Signi cant occurrences within this process appear to be the shift from an IT-based towards a general management-related understanding of knowledge management and the subsequent struggle of IT actors to secure their position (and share) in the knowledge management business. On the market side, the same development appears with the shift from the IT and software industry as the primary source of knowledge management solutions to general consulting and auditing services as the dominant players within the knowledge management market.

A more qualitative reading of the abstract contents (see the Appendix) substantiates this conclusion. The development of the knowledge management discourses represented in the sample abstracts re ects a change of associations of the term knowledge management from its very early origins in the  eld of IT towards a more managerial use and leading to a situation, in which, today, two separate, stable discourses seem to co-exist. On the IS/IT side, this paper’s reading of the development of the knowledge management discourses suggests a shift from knowledge management as a distinct technology entering into the conception of decision support systems towards an understanding of knowledge management as a self-reliant  eld of application and a corresponding view of technology and software

On the management side, the issue of knowledge management has been taken up in the  rst place by actors with an interest in knowledge as a strategic resource. Additional management actors, for example from marketing and HRM, enter the stage relatively late and subsequently try to put forward their particular areas’ linkages with the idea of knowledge (management). The authors would argue that certain journals, in particular those periodicals catering closely to the informational needs of particular managerial audiences, play a crucial role in shaping individual managers’ awareness of and interpretations within the growing  eld of knowledge management. Management periodicals not only passively transmit ideas that have been produced by others, but (have to) engage in the active construction and de nition of the ideas at hand. Individual managers rely on business periodicals as sources of inspiration and legitimization and show a tendency to adapt their justi cation of behaviour to the patterns of argument offered in the business media – as institutional thinking suggests.

The interdependence of journals and their audiences is dynamically reinforced by the fact that, once a particular journal has acquired prominence among the contributions pertaining to a speci c management idea, its de nitional power within the  eld tends to rise and, thus, increases the institutional legitimacy of all managerial action based on the respective journal’s propositions. It is on these grounds that the  ndings here could be framed as indicating a particular form of ‘alliance’ or ‘network’ emerging with the popularization of a new managerial idea. Different management media position themselves in different ways depending on their perceived audiences’ needs and try to acquire definitional power within their respective domains. Managers as consumers of the proposed ideas choose among the available ‘translations’ and through their actions (implicitly) ally themselves with the position of a particular author or journal. Such a framework would assume that some alliances are more stable than others and imply a convergence towards a relatively small number of dominant interpretations over time.

The data here not only con rm the co-existence of different knowledge management discourses across different speech communities and, thus, contradict much of the prominent ‘management fashion literature’ that assumes relative homogeneity of management discourses (e.g. Abrahamson and Fairchild, 1999), but also suggest the lines along which the different interpretations offered in the case of knowledge management converge. Some periodicals (and their respective audiences) consolidate their interpretation of knowledge management in accordance with a set of functionally de ned professional roles (with IS/IT and general management at the opposite extremes).

## Conclusion

This paper has addressed the question of whether the popular discussion of knowledge management comprises single or multiple discourses. A second issue that it has tried to tackle is an understanding of the position of the IS/IT community throughout the development of knowledge management. The  ndings indicate that there is not ‘one’ single knowledge management discourse in the academic and popular management literature. Two types of discourse have been identi ed: one is clearly related to IS/IT issues and the other one is more directed towards issues of general managerial interest. Each of the discourses seems to be related to a particular speech community that has been identi ed through the type of journals in which articles belonging to a particular discourse tend to be published.

Based on the idea that the evolution of the knowledge management discourse(s) can be read as a struggle between different speech communities for limited organizational resources, this paper looked at the rhetorical strategies in the articles associated with the two speech communities. While it was expected that the struggle for resources would lead to negative rhetoric and clear demarcation between competing interpretations, this study did not  nd any substantial support for this hypothesis. This result can be interpreted in line with ideas from the fashion literature, according to which different (competing) fashion actors join in an effort at sustaining the fashion  eld they are engaged in as a whole. The absence of negative rhetoric should therefore not be read as an indicator of the absence of a struggle between different interpretations of the same theme.

In addition to providing a basis for discussing the structure and development of the knowledge management discourse(s) over time, this paper may also serve as an example of how traditional content analysis of article abstracts over time can be used for identifying different speech communities. It is also with respect to its methodological aspect that the main weaknesses of this paper can be seen. A great deal of the  ndings depend on the quality of the database and the article abstracts provided. In this study, it was assumed that the Proquest ABI/Inform database provided a representative sample of journals that are relevant to the  eld of management as a whole. However, in analysing frequency data, it is important not to concentrate on percentages of articles representing a particular interpretation alone. These could in fact primarily re ect the composition of the database. Instead, the analysis should focus on changes in the proportion of articles related to a particular speech community over time.

Another problem is related to the quality of the content categories. In other words, how do we assure that we actually grasp the essence of a particular discourse? Here, this paper has tried to develop content categories inductively from an extensive qualitative reading of the data. A third limitation to the  ndings may eventually be related to the decision to study text rather than practice. In other words, not  nding evidence of open con ict in knowledge management writing does not ‘prove’ that there is actually no open con ict in knowledge management practice. Here, this paper has tried to make the point in the Discussion section that it seems sensible to assume a relative closeness between the (organizational) interests of a given speech community and the publication outlets that are catering directly to this community.

In order to substantiate this paper’s propositions concerning the development of the knowledge management discourse as well as the relationship between the different groups of actors gathering around knowledge management, future research should complement the more ‘macro’-oriented view taken in the present paper with a closer look at actual knowledge management practices. Substantial clari cation concerning the relationship between the different groups of actors that have allied themselves with the idea of knowledge management should be expected from this.

One phenomenon that seems particularly interesting in this context is the growing institutionalization and professionalization of the knowledge management  eld that goes together with the emergence of a group of actors who no longer claim any particular af liation with either IS/IT or general management communities. These genuine knowledge management professionals, who are best exempli ed by their avant-garde, i.e. the knowledge management gurus mentioned in the Introduction, tend to concentrate on integrative rhetorics in order to gain recognition for knowledge management as an issue that lies ‘beyond the eternal struggle of IS/IT and management’. This new group should be expected to grow stronger over time and to develop particular rhetoric and political skills in the competition for knowledge management resources in organizations. Investigating the developing discourses around these genuine knowledge management professionals could provide a challenging avenue for studying the professionalization of a management  eld in situ.

## Appendix: stages in the institutionalization of the knowledge management discourse

As a complement to the empirical analysis presented here, the following sections will provide a qualitative account of the evolution of the knowledge management discourse. Three broad stages in the process are distinguished: the early origins of knowledge management, expansion of the concept and,  nally, its consolidation.

## Origins and the construction of knowledge management as a management task (1985-1995)

Apart from an isolated group of articles referring to knowledge management in the context of political science and public administration back in 1975 (e.g. Henry, 1975), the term knowledge management  rst appeared in the context of arti cial intelligence, information and decision support systems in a small number of journal articles in the early 1980s (e.g. Holsapple and Whinston, 1983; Marchand, 1985). The  rst important shift occurred from the late 1980s to the mid-1990s. Knowledge management was increasingly used not only to designate a particular type of IS integration (e.g. Shen, 1987; Zeleny, 1987), but also to characterize a particular managerial task (e.g. Van Mesdag, 1983; Hildebrand, 1994). This went together with the  rst appearances of the chief knowledge of cer (CKO) as a particular management role (e.g. Penrod and Dolence, 1987; Davenport, 1994). In the mid-1990s, articles mentioning knowledge management spoke for the  rst time of knowledge as a strategic resource (e.g. Birkett, 1994) and operated a shift from knowledge management as technology towards knowledge management as one of the different roles of technology. At the same time, some articles related knowledge management for the  rst time to particular functional areas outside IT and IS: research and development and accounting (e.g. Anonymous, 1995; Birkett, 1995). From about 1995 on, studies linking knowledge management programmes to corporate success as well as the discussion of implementation issues became more frequent. In parallel, more room was given to company examples and the identi cation of ‘leading  rms’ (e.g. Maglitta, 1995).

## Expansion (1996-1997)

The managerial institutionalization of knowledge management continued to be re ected in 1996 with the large-scale creation of chief information of cer positions (e.g. Scheier, 1996). At the same time, a growing fear of IT actors being put on the ‘back seat’ (e.g. Shoesmith, 1996) became visible. On the conceptual level, many contributions fully operationalized the shift from viewing knowledge management as a particular type of technology towards considering knowledge management as a concept in its own right. New technologies and software were increasingly presented as means of supporting knowledge management rather than representing knowledge management in themselves. Industry discussions in this period stemmed mostly from manufacturing and the pharmaceutical industry. An additional issue that became visible in this period was the increasing creation of linkages between knowledge management and other management issues. The most interesting of these linkages concerned the growing presentation of knowledge management as being either a human resources and training issue (e.g. Anonymous, 1996), a strategic concern (e.g. Graham and Pizzo, 1996) or an issue related to the intellectual capital (and, thus, indirectly the stock market value) of an organization. In relation to these issues, an increasing number of contributions drew on comparative case studies and benchmarking of knowledge management practices. A growing number of contributions had an academic origin (for example, the special issue on knowledge management of the European Management Journal, 19XX, Volume 14(4)). In the same period, the  rst contributions that talked about the pitfalls and fallacies of knowledge management and imagined a world ‘beyond knowledge management’ appeared.

The broadening of the issues associated with knowledge management and the (paradoxically) related consolidation of a conceptual core of knowledge management continued in 1997. The knowledge management discussion had by this time reached the banking sector and was for the  rst time related to electronic commerce and data mining on the Internet, as well as to customer retention, marketing and the de nition of core processes (e.g. Brown et al., 1997). The human resources  eld’s contribution to knowledge management took form in particular claims about the (non-computational) characteristics of knowledge (e.g. Allee, 1997). This seemed to point to an underlying con ict between a more IT- and technologydominated view that acquainted knowledge management primarily with different forms of groupware and a human resources view that put forward the complex, self-organizing properties of knowledge. It seemed as if traditional human resources and training actors, who had not been playing a major role until then, had started to claim their share of the knowledge management business (e.g. Stamps, 1997). One effort in this sense might be seen in the link of knowledge management and the organizational learning discourse (e.g. Benson, 1997), which, interestingly enough, only then appeared in the sample literature. Also of interest was the emergence of proper ‘knowledge management assessment tools’ (e.g. Coleman, 1997), implying the existence of increasingly shared criteria of what constituted adequate knowledge management programmes as well as an ongoing effort by consulting and auditing  rms to establish standards and new opportunities for selling knowledge management assessment services.

## Consolidation (1998)

Overall, the 1997 trends continued and even expanded in 1998. Knowledge management appeared to be associated with ever more disciplines (e.g. operations research) (cf. Basu, 1998) and types of businesses (e.g. medium-sized companies) (cf. Restivo, 1998) and it also seemed to stimulate growing academic interest (for example the special issue on knowledge management of the California Management Review, 1998, Volume 40(3). Contents associated with knowledge management now ranged from credibility enhancement (e.g. Trickey, 1998) and the democratization of the corporate environment (e.g. Samuel, 1998) to the need to change an organization’s culture in order to create knowledge management readiness (e.g. Moore, 1998). An increasing amount of articles dealt with the emerging  eld of knowledge management including research into the diffusion of knowledge management programmes in companies (e.g. Anonymous, 1998a), analyses of the growing market for knowledge management products and services (e.g. Stewart, 1998), the knowledge management consulting industry (e.g. Bushko and Raynor, 1998; Mullin, 1998) and new career opportunities in knowledge management (e.g. Trickey, 1998), as well as discussion of knowledge management from a perspective of regulation and legal concerns. Related to this was the re ection on an ongoing ‘battle’ for the ownership of knowledge management (e.g. Hyams, 1998). Attempts to de ne and regulate the knowledge management  eld as well as ‘good knowledge management practice’ became visible through articles mentioning information and knowledge management as a distinct category in a CFO excellence award (e.g. McCafferty, 1998) and the increasing number of reports on industry- and profession-speci c conferences and reports on knowledge management.

The growing autonomy and acceptance of knowledge management throughout the management  eld was instantiated through the use of knowledge management as a distinct category for analysing IT spending (e.g. Eckhouse, 1998) and a number of articles reporting knowledge management practices in organizations enjoying high legitimacy. These included the World Bank (with a \$50 billion (sic!) knowledge management budget) (cf. Anonymous, 1998b), the University of California at Los Angeles (Watson, 1998) and other rather public institutions such as the UK National Health Service (Rowland and Harris, 1998) and Anglian Water Services (Pickard, 1998). In parallel, several articles started engaging in the construction of a historical continuity that has led to today’s knowledge management practices: claims that organizations with ‘knowledge management traditions’ outperformed the stock markets over a 70-year period (e.g. Anonymous, 1998c) or that Lotus Notes software represented a knowledge management platform ‘long before the phrase became a market’ (e.g. McNamara, 1998) exempli ed this tendency. Knowledge management increasingly served to legitimize or revive other management practices. In these cases, the association with knowledge management seemed to be constructed in order to provide legitimacy to otherwise outdated concepts, for example ‘work ow is not dead, but part of knowledge management’ (e.g. Palmer, 1998, p. 10) or ‘mnemonics’ as a knowledge management technique (e.g. Anonymous, 1998d).

An increase in articles that were critical or reported alternatives, failures or new developments replacing knowledge management, particularly concerning the IT- and software-related dimensions of knowledge management, was also seen in 1998. Traditional IS managers were presented as being increasingly marginalized (e.g. Foster, 1998), electronic commerce appeared as a new  eld replacing knowledge management as a growth area for IT  rms (e.g. Dalton, 1998)

and knowledge management was presented as a software industry fad (e.g. Fontana, 1998). A coherent view was that knowledge management did not represent a technology, but a general ‘way of doing business’ (e.g. Angus and Patel, 1998) and a shift of the strategic attributes of knowledge management from providing a competitive advantage to becoming a ‘competitive necessity’ (e.g. Romberg, 1998).
