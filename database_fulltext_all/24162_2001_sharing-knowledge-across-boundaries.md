# Sharing knowledge across boundaries

CLAUDIO U. CIBORRA

London School of Economics, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, UK

RAFAEL ANDREU

IESE Business School, Aveniole Pearson Barcelona, Spain

Practical solutions concerning the strategic use of knowledge and its management depend upon the relevant organizational context. By applying the ‘learning ladder’ model (i.e. a compact way of describing the unfolding of multiple organizational knowledge creation, transformation and transfer processes), three different contexts are explored. First, the single  rm is considered and the governance of its internal knowledge processes performed according to the tenets of the resource-based view of strategy. Second, the boundaries of the single  rm are crossed and the characteristics of the processes of knowledge transfer and production between allied  rms are considered. Finally, emerging features of how knowledge is managed among a large number of interdependent organizations and individuals are explored with reference to the case of open source software. The latter context highlights behaviours that seem, in a sense, at odds with the traditional principles of knowledge management. This is puzzling: conventional strategic and knowledge management frameworks break down precisely when dealing with the case of highly distributed, knowledge intensive businesses.

## Introduction

The problem of managing knowledge needs to be set in fruitful terms before effective organizational and technical solutions can be envisaged. Not only does one have to distinguish between various forms of knowledge, such as tacit or explicit, individual and collective, and how one can move from one type to another. What is relevant is also the speci c interdependence between knowledge and organizational context. Alternative ways of managing knowledge, including different systems for supporting knowledge management, are required in different organizational contexts. For example, consider the design and implementation of a new organizational arrangement which perfectly  ts the needs of a given  rm. If that  rm, in order to access new knowledge or acquire new capital for growth, is forced by market or technology circumstances to enter an alliance with another  rm which has its own knowledge management systems and practices, it may well turn out that its internal knowledge management arrangements and resources are too rigid, ‘closed’ and incompatible. In yet another set of circumstances, the knowledge intensity of the business may oblige the  rm to become a member of an open community of institutions and organizations, in which the very boundaries of the  rm, its internal knowledge sources and  ows and relevant property rights are put into question. It is obvious that knowledge management practices and systems in these three contexts are bound to be different. In particular, in the last case multiple stakeholders, a fragmented, diversi ed infrastructure and the variety of knowledge sources remain in the way of an orderly, fully controlled management policy. In what follows we explore the differences between these three main contexts are explained in greater detail.

(1) The single  rm and its closed processes of knowledge management as inspired by a resource-based view of strategy.

(2) An alliance between a limited number of  rms and the relevant intertwining of previously separated knowledge management processes and systems.

(3) A web arrangement of multiple business, independent research units and institutional entities such as universities, where knowledge does not belong fully to a single stakeholder and boundaries are blurred.

This paper submits that the change of organizational context deeply affects the rules of KM. Speci cally, an inverse relationship seems to appear between the knowledge intensity of the business and the tightness of internal knowledge governance: what is functional and appropriate in the single  rm case becomes inadequate if not dysfunctional in the case of the web of organizations and institutions.

## 2. The learning ladder: a model for the single  rm

Consider  rst the single  rm. In this context this paper focuses on the processes of generation and transfer of knowledge as linked to a variety of internal learning processes. In highly situated ways, people learn by doing (Williamson, 1975; Nelson and Winter, 1982): they learn by using systems and technologies at times they may engage in double-loop or radical learning (Argyris and Schön, 1996) or, more generally, they are busy creating new knowledge by socializing the results of learning and converting explicit into tacit knowledge and vice versa (Nonaka and Takeuchi, 1995).

A previous paper (Andreu and Ciborra, 1996) proposed a ‘compact’ representation of the main learning processes in an economic organization. Such a model has proven to be a useful schema for investigating organizational learning processes and, the authors submit, these can be of help in identifying key aspects of knowledge management in a single  rm. The model, called the learning ladder, is based on the resource-based view of strategy. According to this theory, at the heart of the  rm’s competitive strength is a process that develops distinctive, core capabilities, (Prahalad and Hamel, 1990); i.e. capabilities that differentiate a company strategically and deliver competitive advantage (Barney, 1991; Leonard-Barton, 1992). Core capabilities develop through a series of transformations, by which standard resources, which are available in open markets (where all  rms can acquire them), are used and combined within the organizational context of a  rm in order to produce capabilities, that, in turn, can become the source of competitive advantage if they are rare and dif cult to imitate or substitute. Such transformations are complex, situated learning processes, which can play a strategic role for the  rm, because (1) they imply path dependency and speci city in the resulting core capabilities and (2) consequently, they may be at the root of their inimitability, a crucial characteristic for sustainable competitive advantage. In the context of a single  rm, this paper analyses the major stages of the learning/transformation processes through which the  rm’s core capabilities are generated in detail below.

A  rst transformation consists of the emergence of generic capabilities from standard resources. Two different types of learning take place at this stage: one deals with mastering the use of standard resources and produces what can be called ef cient work practices (Lave and Wenger, 1991). Individuals and groups (or communities of practice) in the  rm learn how to use resources in a given organizational situation. The quest for better work practices may even trigger a search for new resources, that are more appropriate to the practices under development. Alternatively, the introduction and subsequent deployment of new resources (for example, technological innovations) may motivate individuals and groups to ‘take advantage of them’ through new work practices. This results in a learning loop (from resources to work practices) that is called the routinization learning loop, the outcomes of which are repertoires of constrained, routinized and interdependent actions or moves. Learning occurs in an organizational formative<sup>1</sup> context that in uences the corresponding processes and is in turn in uenced by its result (i.e. new working practices become part of the context, thus increasing the knowledge base of the organization). Work practices resulting from this learning loop are concrete, detailed, speci c and operative. From a different perspective, work practices are the  rst step in the  rm’s ‘internalization’ of resources. Mastering the usage of a spreadsheet by an individual or a team in a speci c department is an example of this type of learning.

A second transformation ‘abstracts’ and ‘constructs’ capabilities from existing work practices. Several characteristics connote this learning process, (1) it involves combining emerging work practices and organizational routines, (2) its outcome has a far-reaching potential impact, as capabilities convey what an organization is capable of doing if properly triggered in a variety of situations, (3) capabilities can be easily described in terms of what they do and how they do it, but why they do it is taken for granted, not necessarily well de ned and rarely challenged and (4) capabilities are more abstract than work practices: they are ‘skills without a place’ which can be transferred across the organization (such as, for example, a quality control capability). This is called the capability learning loop. Knowledge management at this level results in a continuously improving set of capabilities – specialized and idiosyncratic ways of using resources for given purposes. These purposes are functionally well de ned and rather stable over time, although how they are attained may change, for example with the emergence of a radically new technology (resource) or a revolutionary new use of an old resource (Penrose, 1959). The driving force for continuous capability improvement is static ef ciency (Ghemawat and Ricart, 1993). Such learning processes tend to occur spontaneously, but the organizational climate and context, the incentive, power and motivational systems, and the technology may affect them deeply. Although ef cient, capabilities lack a sense of why they exist or at least the reasons for their existence are seldom challenged. The know-how needed to prepare ‘professional’ sales forecasts is an example of what we mean by capabilities here. The ‘why’ appears with more clarity as they evolve into core capabilities through a third learning loop (see Figure 1).

Thus, capabilities can evolve into core capabilities, which differentiate a company strategically (Grant, 1991). There are two main factors relevant to the selection of which capabilities have the potential to become core: the competitive environment and the business mission of the  rm. When faced with its competitive environment, a  rm learns whether a given capability has strategic potential. A converse in uence, from core capabilities to capabilities, also exists through the competitive environment as (1) core capabilities of different  rms competing in an industry de ne the ‘standards of excellence’ for that industry – they can elicit which capabilities a  rm should develop in order to compete effectively and (2) it is when confronted with a given environment that capabilities acquire a sense of why they are important, thus revealing more clearly their role and scope. A  rm’s business mission sets priorities in the alignment between capabilities and the current mission. In turn, core capabilities can enable new business missions that, if accepted, may trigger new ‘capabilities–core capabilities’ transformations. These interrelationships are captured by the strategic learning loop, which links capabilities and core capabilities (see Figure 1). Note that the strategic loop also takes place within the  rm’s organizational (formative) context and so it is ‘structured’ by it (Giddens, 1984). In turn, its outcome – core capabilities – can reshape the context itself. This subset of capabilities can be described and understood not only in terms of what they do and how they do it, but also in terms of why: this is in the realm of dynamic ef - ciency. For example, changes in the environment can make a highly ef cient (in the static sense) capability worthless, because it is irrelevant for competing under new circumstances. To continue with the example above, the development of idiosyncratic ways of preparing useful sales forecasts based on the distinct strategic positioning of a  rm is an illustration of a how a core capability might develop. Understanding the market and its functioning in ‘the  rm’s own way resulting from its particular positioning and experience permits the application of forecasting capabilities (based even on standard procedures and algorithms) in a much more effective and focused way, to the point of becoming fundamental for the  rm’s competitive advantage. A case in point is that of Dell Computer: this  rm can signi cantly improve its relationships with suppliers because it knows its clients so well that good, detailed forecasts are the norm, while their competitors cannot.

In sum, the ladder involves three main learning loops. One basic loop routinizes work practices and, indirectly, routines while using resources, a second one combines work practices and organizational routines in order to form capabilities and the third gives meaning to capabilities in the context of the  rm’s competitive environment and business mission, thus allowing the selection and elicitation of core capabilities. The knowledge of which capabilities are distinctive and fundamental to compete is strategic in nature and becomes part of the organizational (formative) context in which all  rm’s activities, including learning, occur. Changing such a formative context may involve a fourth (double-loop) learning process.

The knowledge management implications of the learning ladder for the single  rm case are quite straightforward. While in the routine learning loop the spontaneous unfolding of tacit knowledge takes place, routinization implies a systematic effort at making larger portions of this knowledge explicit for repetitive use. Thus, knowledge management practices can be found as trying to strike a balance between robust, but implicit routines and routines that, through formalization, become standard operating procedures. Information systems can be used in various ways to enable such processes. Groupware applications can enhance the informal transfer of knowledge within speci c communities of practice or teams. On the other hand, workflow applications can deal with the more formalized steps of the tasks being performed. Enterprise resource planning (ERP) applications take care of cross-functional and interdependent procedures. In general, knowledge management can be looked at as an activity dedicated to the transfer of useful knowledge embedded in routines throughout the organization, that is it takes care of the cultivation and diffusion of competencies and their selection and alignment with the prevailing business strategy. Various mechanisms can be deployed to this end, as noted below. For example, Business Process Re-engineering (BPR) and ERP can be harnessed in order to formalize and ‘freeze’ capabilities into standard ways of operating. However, capabilities cannot be put into software alone: organizational and educational processes will also be necessary. Knowledge management related to core capabilities focuses on creating opportunities for identifying the most competitive matches between capabilities and an adequate reading of the market and the environment in general. Knowledge management should be dedicated to creating occasions for the effective mixing of inside and outside knowledge. Intelligence systems can be one important resource in this respect, together with the singling out of individuals and teams who possess ‘boundary spanning’ roles and capabilities. Finally, knowledge management for radical learning consists of staging activities and settings conducive to re ection in action by the key members of the organization. Education and outside consulting expertise can play a decisive role. Attempts have been made to use simulation packages aimed at enhancing systems thinking and learning in action (Senge, 1990). Coming back full circle, knowledge management capabilities of all sorts and supported through the use of different technologies and systems can also become core capabilities.

## 3. Beyond the borders: interorganizational learning ladders

What happens to the learning ladder and related knowledge management policies when two or more  rms adopt a cooperative strategy and engage in some form of alliance (ranging from joint venture to merger)? In a successful two- rm alliance it is likely that the learning ladders of the partners will intertwine, almost like a DNA double helix: in this way knowledge becomes shared across separate organizations and new knowledge is developed in the process. Again, the teachings and models coming from business strategy can be of help in analysing the opportunities and outcomes of the recombination of separate knowledge processes and stocks. Speci cally, the ‘relational’ view of the strategic advantage coming from inter rm links and resources offers interesting insights (Dyer and Singh, 1998). While the early resourcebased view of strategy focuses on the advantage generated by key ‘core’ assets and capabilities, the frequency of alliances, particularly in very dynamic and innovative sectors, suggests that there is another source of competitive advantage stemming from the establishment of inter rm linkages, which in themselves can become a distinct source of relational quasi-rents. As a result, a  rm’s valuable resources do not necessarily lie only within its boundaries; they may extend beyond them. The recombination of separate learning ladders can be used to see how new, distinctive knowledge, routines and competencies are produced and exploited by allied  rms.

Under normal, arm’s-length market relationships, learning ladders of separate  rms do not mix. Knowledge is transferred from one ladder to the other through intermediaries who trade services and products through the market. Standardized goods and services which embed knowledge (Demsetz, 1993) are traded with minimal communication (i.e. the price) across separable technological and functional interfaces. Instead, successful alliances transform standardized relationships into unique ones through various mechanisms, such as investments in relation-speci c assets, recombination of capabilities and routines, developing distinctive know-how, establishing a common experience and joint practices or developing a new jointly spoken language that facilitates cooperation. All these mechanisms favour the non-market transfer and recombination of knowledge and learning (Doz, 1996).

The learning ladder can be used as a tool for identifying such processes better, which has already been pointed out by research and development and innovation literature and highlighting relevant management issues. Thus, interlocking learning ladders show where the potential for knowledge sharing, recombination and transfer lies. Valuable know-how can be attained by mixing/transferring capabilities, by placing resources and routines within new contexts or by letting existing practices be moulded by different capabilities in order to form new routines. To be sure, governance is paramount in securing compatibility, transferability and value generation. Effective governance must look for a smooth interlocking process. Various aspects must be taken care of by asking questions such as is a core competence of one organization compatible with the other  rm’s formative context, portfolio of competencies, routines, systems and resources? Or can it be transferred to the other  rm? Finally, can it lead to new mixes with existing resources and routines leading to new combinations, the generation of different practices and knowledge? These questions can be asked systematically, as shown in Figure 3, and conclusions reached for every relevant cell in the table.

Having veri ed this basic level of interlocking, speci c aspects that may lead to relational rents should be considered. Examples are the uniqueness of the matching (due, for example, to partner scarcity or special bundling of resources), the time required to achieve the fusion of the ladders, institutional factors that may favour a certain type of combination, etc. The interlocking of the ladders provides a map that points out key aspects for the governance of knowledge transfers that can be the source of relational rents. Systems can play a crucial role as well. First, at the infrastructure or sheer resource level issues of compatibility and data transfer emerge immediately. Many ERP or legacy systems may actually block the implementation of alliances because of incompatibility. On the other hand, information technology is a resource that can be creatively reinvented by adopting another organization’s competencies thanks to the alliance. Alternatively, the infrastructure might act as a carrier of a formative context, not only of procedures: this can have a deep and wideranging in uence on the practices and routines of the other  rm. These possibilities should be thought through as much as possible beforehand, since they may impact on the level of actual collaboration between  rms having different systems.

Consider as an example the evolving relationships between suppliers and customers for new product development in the car industry (Ciborra, 1999). Instead of relying on huge internal design departments, most car manufacturers today partially subcontract the design of a new car to outside design  rms or ateliers. Such smaller, specialized  rms seem to offer a working environment more open to creativity and innovation to this highly quali ed workforce. The management of the close relationship between the car manufacturer and the external design atelier consists of  nding a good match between the respective computer-aided design (CAD) platforms (the level of technical resources) and between a more bureaucratic formative context (the large car manufacturer) and the team or project formative context typical of the design  rm. A further challenge is combining the ef ciency capability of the manufacturer with the innovation capability of the design  rm. Relatedly, the creative working routines developed in the use of CAD in the design atelier need to be made to  t with the ef ciency capabilities of the manufacturer and so on. Common computer platforms and applications can be designed only if they are compatible with the multiple balances that have to be struck between the two different learning ladders. For example, the extension of an ERP application from the manufacturer to the design  rm may be dif cult to execute, the idiosyncratic routines in the design  rm being of a different style from the (formal) ones most frequently found in the manufacturer. On the other hand, groupware applications may be better suited for the lower level of formalization typical of the design  rm routines and they can provide useful extra channels of communication between designers and managers at the two sites.

In addition, all the activities deriving from the ladder interlocking process are bound, by their very nature, to be collective in the sense of always involving more than one individual. In such a situation, collective learning and knowledge become paramount. As Andreu and Sieber (2000) pointed out, one important kind of collective knowledge has to do with coordination schemes which are fundamental to putting collec tive action (routines, for example) into actual work. As coordination schemes are often implicit pieces of knowledge, the implication is that their development and transfer must be based on processes that cannot be easily formalized and codi ed. The issue is further complicated by the fact that the routines and capabilities to be coordinated come from different contexts, also often with a strong implicit component. This puts even more pressure on the corresponding coordination schemes. Other collective bits of knowledge and capabilities have to do with how individual decisions or visions are integrated into group decisions. All these issues point out areas where innovation, in the sense of generating new routines and capabilities of a collective nature, will have to be carefully considered. As suggested by the third type of question shown in Figure 3, it is not only a matter of compatibility or transferability, but of how compatible and transferable practices, routines and capabilities coming from different  rms and contexts can be newly combined and coordinated, for example giving rise to new, interdisciplinary teams of such a nature that were impossible to put together in the context of any of the individual  rms alone, simply because the resources, routines or capabilities involved were not all present. This, of course is at the root of synergies that can make the alliance worthwhile in big ways. To be sure, systems can support the processes involved. For example, work- ow-based applications and communication infrastructures can simplify or even make possible part of the coordination (think, for example, how virtual teams’ members can interact when they are geographically dispersed, etc.), but only to the extent that the latter can be made explicit and codi ed in accordance with the functional capabilities of the technology used.

## 4. From the learning ladders to the learning community

A high number and density of alliances characterize knowledge-intensive industries. However, the emerging networks of knowledge-intensive  rms are more complex and intertwined than a cluster comprising few companies, even if these are large companies. These networks have a different shape and require distinct coordination and governance mechanisms. In industries in which know-how is critical, such as telecommunications, software or pharmaceuticals, companies cooperate with private research laboratories, universities, foundations and other  rms at the same time (Powell et al. 1996). In such an emerging pattern of relationships it is dif cult to identify distinct clusters of recombined learning ladders. It is hard to separate the strings and their connections of this entangled, spaghetti-like con guration of resources, practices, routines and capabilities derived from the fusion of knowledge inputs being provided by the various participants. The hard-to-distinguish spillovers or over ows (Callon, 1998) of know how tend to prevail in number, volume and importance over the planned or managed transfer of knowledge (Steinmueller, 1996). Transfer is superseded by spillover through interstices. Just by being immersed in this  ow and breathing its ‘atmosphere’(Marshall, 1930) one can tap into this invisible social knowledge capital that is generated by the interaction between multiple participants. Computer networks such as the Internet or the ubiquitous mobile phones signi cantly facilitate such informal sharing and the rapid, uncontrollable spread of spillovers. Furthermore, ‘boundary objects’ (Star and Ruhleder, 1996), such as standards, systems or applications that are widely exchanged or used by the members of the community, constitute an important ‘knowledge infrastructure’ (Steinmueller, 1996) that further facilitates the ef cient exchange and recombination of knowledge coming simultaneously from multiple sources, thus giv ing rise to new pieces that can become relevant elsewhere.

As for individuals, here learning takes place through the practice of membership in such an evolving community (Brown and Duguid, 1991). However, in such a context it is hard to locate the know-how precisely. Knowledge is simultaneously highly sophisticated (both tacit and explicit) and widely dispersed in the hands and minds of many, and is not easily produced or captured inside the boundaries of one or a few  rms. As an example of this type of work organization consider the development of the Linux operating system and more generally, so-called open source software. Linux is an operating system derived from a Unix kernel that can compete on stability and reliability with current operating system. Apart from the technical differences, Linux is at odds with most of the commercial operating system in the way it has been developed. Huge numbers of programmers coordinating only through the Internet have been able to hack on Linux casually. Software quality has been attained not through hierarchical controls, as would happen in large organizations deploying structured software engineering methodologies, but by ongoing simultaneous, multiple peer reviews over the Internet. Continuous weekly releases of the software were examined and corrected by hundreds of programmers within days (Raymond, 1999). Other relevant organizational aspects are that, in this loosely organized community there are no strict and explicit economic incentives at work, rather there are symbolic ones; for example to be recognized as a smart person in the hacker community. Developers involved in this project perform ‘egoless programming’ (Weinberg, 1971).

From a knowledge management perspective, sophisticated and innovative knowledge sharing mechanisms have also been put in place. For example, spillovers are in fact institutionalized through the ‘copyleft’ practice, by which anyone is given permission to run, modify and redistribute a program. Meeting places have been created for the explicit or informal sharing of knowledge in the form of dedicated portals on the Internet. (Ljunberg, 2000) The formative context that moulds such knowledge management devices seems to be more inspired by the atmosphere of an academic and scienti c community rather than a pro t-oriented business organization.

In sum, the development of Linux illustrates that, in knowledge-intensive environments, where communication, coordination and distribution of competencies exist and where there are serious problems of robustness, reliability and scalability, a novel approach to knowledge management can be used successfully. In particular, the case highlights the following.

(1) Coordination and communication bottlenecks in peer groups of a very large size can be avoided using the Internet and associated tools built in the software environment.

(2) Simultaneous or asynchronous work, which in fact contribute to the development of an integrated piece of knowledge, can take place without the need for strict sequencing or planning.

(3) Opportunism and free riding do not seem to emerge and ef cient coordination may result as a consequence. Instead, symbolic rewards, altruism and a gift economy seem to hold, so that the private property of the  nal product is not an issue either (Raymond, 1999),

(4) Hierarchical organization does not need to come into the picture to replace a team (Alchian and Demsetz, 1972) or a market arrangement (Williamson, 1975). Linux has been developed by a large peer group coordinated by a ‘benevolent dictator’, whose moral authority was recognized by the community and who did not appropriate the results of the cooperative effort.

From a knowledge management perspective what emerges is an organizational form that is able to tap into the brainpower of entire communities; it is not territorial and fragmented and it is not secretive and hostile, nor is it plagued by opportunism. The principles of ‘divide et impera’ and ‘knowledge is power’ do not seem to have a signi cant impact here (Fransman, 1996). There are further peculiar differences. For example, the development organization of Linux is in contrast with the view that sees the  rm as a hierarchically arranged repository of routines (Nelson and Winter, 1982), embedding knowledge and skills: short-term routines take care of operations, while investment routines and higher-order routines modify the lower operating routines over time. The Linux example shows that a productive and knowledge intensive ‘organization’ can literally consist of a knowing community, where one can identify the routines and skills of the members, but these, despite their large number and the feedback processes going on all the time, are not hierarchically arranged.

What are the main characteristics of the situation illustrated by the Linux example that seem to be at the heart of all these results? There are, in the authors’ view, four such basic characteristics. First, the  nal product is a piece of very explicit (codi ed) knowledge (i.e. a piece of software codi ed in a very speci c language) with an internal structure (that of an operating system) that is very well de ned, starting from the initial kernel made explicit by the initiator and later ‘benevolent dictator’ of the whole process. In addition, that  nal product was a ‘digital good’, that was very well suited not only to be transferred over the communication infrastructure, but to actually being tested and redesigned using a standard device such as the personal computer.

Second, stemming from the fact that the  nal product was a piece of well-structured knowledge is the circumstance that the very same structure of the product can be used as a guide for the development process. This is an interesting feature in the sense that the basis for the coordination needed among the knowledge workers participating in the process is de ned by the product structure, which acts as a ‘hanger’ where the different components, which are developed asynchronously by independent contributors, can be inserted with the con dence that they will ‘ t’ as long as the interfaces are respected.

Third, what can be called ‘meta-knowledge’, i.e. knowledge about the functioning and coordination of the knowledge management activities that were used in the process, can also be spelt out explicitly in a language not very dissimilar from the one used to describe the product and, furthermore, it can also be transferred using the same basic communications infrastructure (i.e. the Internet).

Finally, the cultural context is also paramount. The sense of community, the values underlying it, the corresponding reward schemes and behaviour codes and so on are all necessary in order to take advantage of the possibilities created by the three other characteristics highlighted above. That is, all those other characteristics might be present and the results still not be effective if the participants in the process were not moved to actually use them in the appropriate way. On the other hand, however, it is also probably true that the culture part alone would not produce the same results – in that sense, the  rst three characteristics become enablers that are more likely to appear in knowledge-intensive situations. Consequently, an altogether different knowledge management model is being faced, one which is characterized by the possibility of combining know-how from different, dispersed sources in an asynchronous way and enabled by the new communication infrastructures, as long as those pieces of know-how can be encoded in such a way that they can be transferred using those common infrastructures and according to a structural scheme that plays the double role of a skeleton of the resulting new knowledge and also that of a basic coordination scheme for the participants’ activities. The values and culture shared by the participants determine to what extent that possibility eventually becomes reality. This model seems to turn more traditional arrangements upside down. Value in Linux-like processes increases with widespread use and open access. By (openly) adding new features, users increase the value of the infrastructure, which other members of the community can then also, immediately and at almost no cost bene t from (a network externality typical in the ‘new economy’ due to increasing returns to scale on the demand side). The latter characteristic is not unrelated to the fact that the primary ‘product’ (an operating system in the Linux case) is a ‘digital good’ with high ‘production’ costs and very low ‘reproduction’ costs. (i.e. with increasing returns to scale on the supply side) (see Shapiro and Varian, 1999).

Cases similar to that of Linux, the Internet, and the role played by the hacker community are not con ned to the software industry. Similar con gurations and dynamics can be found in other research environments, such as the genome project or nuclear physics. Once again, in contrast to the single  rm and its closed research and development, an anology with ‘industrial districts’ can be observed across the boundaries of the  rm and its research and development activities (Marshall, 1930; Richardson, 1972), where the synergy and atmosphere caused by diffused knowledge made available to the members of the district bene ts everybody. Such knowledge is the by-product of a web of informal and unpredictable knowledge spillovers.

## 5. Conclusions

This paper has used the learning ladder model in order to examine which knowledge management and governance approaches are more appropriate for fostering the development of key capabilities in order to achieve competitive advantages in different organizational settings. While in the case of a single  rm the implications are straightforward and even well known, in the other two cases analysed, namely the interorganizational and community settings, the implications are less obvious and ultimately eye opening.

In the interorganizational setting, although consistent with ideas already set forth in the mergers and alliances literature, the learning ladder model provides a framework from which useful insights can be derived. New sources of distinctive competitive elements emerge if a relational view of strategic advantage is assumed, which conceptualizes them as rooted in unique inter rm linkages. The framework provides a basis for a detailed analysis, taking into account how the components of different learning ladders in various  rms  t or do not  t. The authors suggest that the role that systems, applications and technology can play in the corresponding processes may be better analysed in the context of the proposed framework than more traditional ways, where partnerships usually bring along surprises regarding the incompatibility of resources, skills and systems, as well as cultures. Looking at the intertwined ladders can also help the dif cult task of governance of the partnership from a distinct knowledge management perspective.

Finally, the knowledge community case, for which examples are beginning to exist in knowledge-intensive industries, involves ‘spillover’ processes rather than planned or managed transfers of know-how. This third setting suggests an interesting oxymoron: precisely in knowledge intensive contexts, knowledge creation, deployment and application for the purpose of attaining a sustainable competitive advantage seem to be less rooted in the basic principles that contributed to conceiving knowledge as a fundamental source of advantage in the  rst place. In addition, when ‘digital goods are involved, some principles of the ‘new economy’ may apply, in particular increasing returns to scale both in the demand and supply sides. More research and better models are needed in order to pursue this case further, but the puzzle has at least been de ned.
