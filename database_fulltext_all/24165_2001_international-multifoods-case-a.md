# International Multifoods – Case A

GERARDINE DESANCTIS AND ROBERT M. PRICE

Fuqua School of Business, Duke University, 134 Towerview Drive, Box 90120, Durham, NC 27708-0120, USA

The International Multifoods (IMC) case describes the attempt by a mid-sized company to strategically use information technology for competitive advantage. The managers in this company and its subsidiary, Vending Services of America (VSA), saw an opportunity to leapfrog the competition in the food service distribution industry through development of ‘the most advanced system available in the industry.’ The goal of the Renaissance information system was to ‘leverage VSA’s strength and consumer knowledge in assisting its customers’ and, in the process, bring much needed revenue growth to the IMC parent  rm. The case provides an overview of the IMC and VSA companies, the plans for development of the Renaissance system, and the dif culties that arose as the system was pilot tested and prepared for roll-out.

## Introduction

It was a beautiful summer afternoon in downtown Minneapolis, Minnesota. High above the skyline, in the forty-ninth  oor of the modern Multifoods Tower building, Devendra Mishra was being welcomed aboard as president of the Vending Services of America company (VSA). VSA was a major subsidiary of the diversi ed food company International Multifoods Corporation (IMC) and Tony Luiso, the chief executive of cer of IMC, was leaning over his desk with intensity as he said ‘Devendra, you have the right background for this task. You will probably straighten out VSA’s information technology (IT) problems in 6 months.’ The former president of VSA had recently left under pressure and Devendra had been recruited to revitalize the company and restore its pro tability. After 9 years of continual growth and pro tability, VSA’s business had seemed to hit a wall. Symptoms of serious problems were beginning to surface: costs were increasing and margins declining; there was some erosion in the small independent operator customer base, an indication of customer service problems and inventories were increasing while sales seemed to be stagnating. Within VSA and IMC the consensus was that poor design and implementation of a new centralized management information system (IS) was the root cause of these woes.

Devendra was taking a cut in salary to come to VSA, but he saw it as an opportunity to run a \$1 billion business and his chance to ‘make a mark’ in turning around a large business. He was energetic, ambitious and had been delighted when offered the job.

Devendra came to VSA from Technicolor, Inc., where he enjoyed an excellent reputation as an innovator and leader. There he had started three new business ventures, all of which were successful. Although his distribution experience had largely been in the entertainment industry, at Live Entertainment, Inc., he and the people at IMC felt that this experience, combined with his expertise in market and problem analyses, were just what was needed at VSA. He had been chosen after a national search involving the screening of more than 1200 candidates and careful evaluation of 15 of them. With an MBA from a leading business school, Devendra was a polished, articulate manager who made an excellent impression. He exuded intelligence and con dence. Prior to his entertainment distribution experience, Devendra had been at RCA Inc. There he had met Bob Maddocks, who now was senior vice president of human resources at IMC. At RCA Devendra had been known as an innovative manager who was excellent at working with people and initiating successful change. Maddocks had supported the selection of Devendra as president of VSA. When Luiso offered Devendra the job, he put the central challenge of  xing the stalled new management IS as follows: ‘Devendra, you have implemented systems before. We obviously have not had the management expertise for a project like this.

Before accepting the job Devendra had done his homework on the vending distribution segment of the food service distribution industry. He knew it to be a low-margin business (2–3%) with little value added beyond plain, old-fashioned good customer service. The life blood of the business was service. On the other hand, costs had to be managed very carefully. It was not just a business where pennies counted, it was a business where fractions of pennies counted. A powerful IS which would impose discipline uniformly across the dispersed operations made good sense.

Now he was at the moment of truth. He had to understand how these basic vending distribution principles were working speci cally at VSA and he had to get started getting that IS back on track.

## Corporate and industry overview

## International Multifoods Corporation

IMC was established in the late 1800s as the International Milling Company, Inc., a midwest-based  our milling company distributing  our and related baking products to bakeries, grocery stores, hotels and restaurants. Over many years, bakery product growth and acquisition of a variety of food product, food service product and food service distribution businesses led the company to become one of the leading food product and food service distribution  rms in the USA.

By the late 1980s IMC’s food product businesses included grain, milling, retail consumer food products, agricultural feeds and related agricultural products. The company had signi cant operations in Canada and Venezuela as well as in the USA. IMC also owned Mister Donut, a franchised doughnut chain and the Boston Sea Party restaurant chain. IMC’s food service distribution businesses consisted of VSA and Pueringer. Pueringer was a manufacturer and distributor of food products and ingredients to pizza and some other speciality restaurants.

IMC’s management had decided that the future of the  rm should be in the manufacture and distribution of products to food service operators. The growth and success of VSA was a signi cant factor in reaching this strategic decision and VSA became increasingly critical to IMC’s strategic thrust as the company entered the 1990s. Indeed, by the early 1990s a corporate restructuring was under way and the company was divesting its interest in  rms outside of the food service distribution business while acquiring new  rms to add to its VSA food service distribution company. Still, the company retained diverse interests and, in the early 1990s, IMC was the number 1 producer of bakery mixes in North America and Venezuela, the number

1 producer of consumer  our and retail bakery  our in Canada, the number 1 distributor of food products to the US vending industry and the number 2 distrib utor of food products to independent pizza and Mexican restaurants in the USA.

In the  scal years 1992 and 1993 the company earned \$23 million and \$41 million on sales of \$2.3 billion and \$2.2 billion, respectively. However, in the  scal year ended 28 February 1994, shortly before Devendra arrived at VSA, IMC had incurred an annual loss of \$13.4 million. Much of this loss was attributed to the write down of underperforming assets and the costs associated with closure and reorganization of certain US and Canadian facilities, as well as the disposition of some unpro table assets. Management hoped that these painful changes would enable a pro table future for IMC. However, the company’s lack of consistent performance and ongoing restructuring was producing increasing pressure from stockholders and the  nancial community to take signi cant steps towards improving shareholder value. There was a growing sense that the  rm did not have a suf cient stratgegic focus and management was pondering whether the time had come to focus the company in a speci c industry, either bakery products or speciality food service distribution, which were the two principal businesses remaining at IMC. Food service distribution companies enjoyed higher price-earnings stock multiples than bakery products and the company’s investment banking advisers were strongly recommending that it concentrate fully on this part of the business. The leadership and success of the VSA company would have to be the basic underpinning of such a strategy. Thus, VSA’s performance was critical both operationally and strategically.

## VSA’s challenges

The vending service distribution business had two dominant characteristics: small margins and a heavy reliance on personalized relationships between distributors and vending machine owners. These characteristics had given rise to a fragmented cottage industry of 9000 vend operators with 70% of those operators representing 10% of industry retail sales. These small operators provided personalized service, incurred almost no overhead and had little in the way of fringe bene ts for their employees who were low paid as well.

Although VSA’s sales volume had been growing faster than the vending industry in total, for the  scal year ending 28 February 1994 sales had actually declined, primarily as a result of the loss of a number of smal vending operator accounts. VSA competed in this market by managing in a very decentralized way with each distribution centre operating on an independent basis. However, its margins were coming under increased pressure as its wage and bene t costs began to re ect being a large company and as the costs of the more centralized mode of operating associated with the new IS produced more overhead expenses. The increasing trend in VSA’s  xed and variable costs, as well as information regarding sales and net margins, are presented in Figures 7–13 and Table 1. Overall, VSA had a cost disadvantage of at least 1–2% when compared to low overhead regional competitors. The economies of scale which should have been associated with being a national distributor were not being achieved by VSA due to its inability to exercise its purchasing power seriously and the poor condition of its IS. Fixed costs, such as warehouse equipment and maintenance, were growing and VSA cost centres, such as customer services, IS,  nance, price management, warehousing and transportation, were reporting consistently rising expenditures. The result was high breakeven sales levels for the VSA business.

## Renaissance

By the time Devendra arrived in his new post as president, IMC had invested nearly \$20 million in VSA in order to install ‘the most advanced system available in the industry’. The goal was to ‘leverage VSA’s strength and consumer knowledge in assisting it customers’. The new Renaissance IS would support a central sales order staff with state-of-the-art order entry, inventory control and market data in order to manage better such key variables as product freshness, manufacturers’ promotion and rebate programmes, product turnover trends and customer usage history. Internally, Renaissance would transform VSA from a set of businesses with differing business practices and IS capability into an integrated company, thereby enabling the economies of scale that had proven so elusive in the vending business thus far. Externally, it could provide information to the small independent accounts that would assist them in both placement of their machines and improving product selection. In addition, it would facilitate the integration of new acquisitions and restore growth and excitement for the business.

VSA had not historically applied or used IT as a competitive weapon. Prior to Renaissance, VSA had consistently lagged behind the industry in its IT budget, spending less than 0.2% of revenue per year on technology, compared to the 0.5–1% spent by competing wholesale distributors. IT was not well understood by top managers and information services was viewed as an expense, not a strategic asset. IS resources focused on operations, technical support and continuous hardware upgrades. User participation in systems-related decisions was largely absent and internal systems development projects had been limited in focus. VSA had slipped behind its competition in IT, thereby generating signi cant customer and user frustration. The mission of the Renaissance initiative was therefore to ‘re-engineer our business systems to support our near and long-term success as a competitive, pro table company’.

Renaissance was an elaborate and dif cult project because it would be an integral part of all of VSA’s business processes. The project was orchestrated and managed with the assistance of a major consulting  rm, which advised VSA management with regard to the system design and eventually oversaw its development and implementation. Renaissance included four major system modules: inventory management, sales and marketing, outbound logistics and  nancial management. The plan was to implement these modules with the oversight of a VSA top management steering committee, a project manager, project support staff, technical staff and key managers and management IS representatives from each of the major functional support areas within corporate VSA.

The Renaissance system architecture was highly centralized, with order taking (telesales), warehouse ‘pick’ schedules, billing, inventory planning, procurement and all essential information processing functions occurring centrally in Denver. Each distribution centre would be connected via a 56 kb data communications line to headquarters and there would be workstations for receiving, traf c and warehouse management at each distribution centre. All transactions would  ow into and back out of the central systems where they could be analysed and used to manage the business more effectively and consistently. In this way, customers, suppliers and the VSA operations ‘connecting’ suppliers to customers would all make use of an integrated and homogeneous computer and highspeed communications network. Information describing Renaissance is given in Figures 14 and 15.

The bene ts expected from this system architecture were considerable in terms of reduced operating expenses and improved customer service. For example, by centralizing the order process, VSA could support phone-based ordering with 60–80 people instead of the current workforce of 140 scattered across the 20 distribution centres. Workforce reductions in purchasing, operations and administration were also anticipated. General managers would no longer have to rely so heavily on personalized relationships with customer for managing sales; instead they could focus more on managing warehouse inventory and keeping their district running smoothly. Fewer division genera managers would be needed. Although those who stayed with the company would be compensated at a leve commensurate with their pay in the pre-Renaissance era, new hires at the general manager rank migh receive a lower level of compensation because of the changed nature of their responsibilities. Better information was expected to result in improved pricing control, inventory control and rebate management. In all, the net bene t of Renaissance was projected to be nearly \$25 million over a 5-year period (see Figure 16).

## Implementation plan

Roll-out of the Renaissance system began in early 1994 with initial pilot tests in distribution centres located in Denver and Kansas City. The pilot studies revealed some devastating problems. There were stock outs and mis-shipments of some items. There were also problems in reconciling orders processed between the  rst and second shifts. All this resulted in  nger pointing between sales, the purchasing department and warehouses; they each blamed each other for the problems and they all blamed the Renaissance system. Customers were particularly unhappy. Not only was response time poor, they missed the friendly voice of an order taker at the local distribution centres – a point of contact that they had become accustomed to. Overall, there were disappointing results in manpower savings, overtime requirements,  ll rates, sales revenue and sales margin improvement (see Figure 17).

The project life cycle for Renaissance proceeded in a top-down fashion, with 2 years devoted to systems design, development and testing, followed by a planned roll-out over two 6-month phases in 1994. A major IS consulting  rm served as coordinator of the project, overseeing design and implementation.
