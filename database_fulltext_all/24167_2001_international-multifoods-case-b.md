# International Multifoods – Case B

GERARDINE DESANCTIS AND ROBERT M. PRICE

Fuqua School of Business, Duke University, 134 Towerview Drive, Box 90120, Durham, NC 27708–0120, USA

The International Multifoods company (IMC) confronted a series of crises in the 1990s that are described in this case. The Renaissance information system, which was intended to bring enormous cash  ow to IMC’s subsidiary, Vending Services of America (VSA), was behind schedule and over budget. Furthermore, VSA was experiencing serious managerial problems. IMC had cash  ow problems that were reaching crisis levels due to, among other things, economic volatility in Venezuela where IMC had a major food business. IMC struggled with organization structure issues and was pressed by its investors to reorganize. As Renaissancerelated and other problems brewed within VSA, the CEO of IMC resigned, and an interim CEO had to step in and somehow move the company out of the crises at hand.

## Introduction

This paper covers the period August 1994 to May 1996. In 1994 International Multifoods (IMC) was a midsized food distribution company with operations in the United States, Canada, and Venezuela. The company was headquartered in Minneapolis, Minnesota. One of its major subsidiaries was Vending Services of America (VSA), a speciality foods distribution business operating across the USA and headquartered in Denver, Colorado. VSA operated 20 distribution centres in 1994 through which it served 18 000 accounts, stocked 12 000 items system wide, and was among the largest buyers of Mars, Hershey, Nestlé and Nabisco products.

VSA had the business objective of becoming the low cost and preferred provider in the vend food service distribution industry. Although sales were strong, revenue was not growing and costs were increasing rapidly. VSA hired a new president, Devendra Mishra, who came to the  rm with a strong reputation as an innovator and leader. Devendre took a cut in salary to come to VSA because he saw it as an opportunity to run a \$1 billion business and a chance to ‘make a mark’ in turning around a large business that was struggling  nancially.

Managers at both VSA and IMC viewed their new Renaissance information system as an opportunity to leapfrog the competition and ‘leverage VSA’s strength and consumer knowledge in assisting its customers. They also hoped that Renaissance would help VSA to become one of IMC’s strongest and most pro table subsidiaries.

## Devendre arrives at VSA

The Renaissance system was in the middle of its rollout when Devendre arrived as president of VSA. The implementation of the Renaissance system required that core business activities, such as ordering, procurement and billing, take place at VSA headquarters rather than in the individual distribution centres (see Figure 1). This movement from a decentralized system to a centralized business approach was proving to be overwhelming. The results of the  rst two pilot tests for the Renaissance system had revealed a host of problems. Reviewing the situation, Devendre concluded that there were two alternative courses of action at this point: (1) scratch the Renaissance system altogether and start over or (2) modify the system and/or the implementation plan in order to enhance the chances of success. Option (1) was never seriously considered since the prevailing view was that it was implementation not design or architecture that was the problem. Further, writing off the \$20 million involved in option 1 was unattractive to IMC’s management given the company’s recent  nancial performance.

Devendre saw option (2) as the most viable alternative and he quickly set to work on leading a systems modi cation effort and developing a new roll-out schedule. He started the process of replacing top managers with new people that he believed could lead the repair of the Renaissance system and revitalization of VSA. He also did a careful analysis of the system and pilot test results and, in consultation with key managers and the Renaissance project team, concluded that the key problems with the system were as follows.

(1) The system’s performance was inadequate in terms of customer waiting times and order processing cycles.

(2) Critical components of the system could not be implemented because of system overload.

(3) Greater system capacity in terms of storage and processing capacity was needed.

(4) Data integrity was at 70% accuracy instead of the desired rate of 99.99% accuracy.

(5) The system was overly complex and in need of simpli cation.

Customers were experiencing unacceptable telephone waiting times, order taking errors, stock outs, pricing errors and service unreliability. These system problems were particularly vexing to VSA’s small independent customers who accounted for 65% of VSA’s revenue. A survey of these customers revealed complaints about mis-shipments, stock outs and loss of a telephone customer service representative who they knew on a  rst-name basis. To make matters worse, local brokers<sup>1</sup> were becoming alienated because of the centralization of purchasing inherent in the Renaissance system. There was also dissatisfaction with the new price book (product catalogue), which was customer unfriendly, error prone and confusing.

Devendre informed IMC’s management that ‘signi - cant incremental investment and effort was required to transform the system from being internally focused to being customer driven’ and he set about leading the Renaissance team in making the necessary changes in order to restore successful implementation of the system.

The plan was to ‘re-engineer the Renaissance system and the steps involved included those shown in Table 1. The fundamental architecture was not to be changed. Rather, efforts were directed towards simplifying the system design, improving data integrity and enhancing the system’s basic computing and telecommunications power.

Meanwhile, the loss of sales and the many pressures associated with the Renaissance system were resulting in growing morale problems at VSA. Many division general managers were leaving the company, project team members were frustrated and some top managers, many of whom were newly hired by Devendre, were starting to question everything from the Renaissance system to the management of the company itself.

IMC’s management regarded the Renaissance system as absolutely critical to the success of VSA. No IMC managers were actually on the project team. However, following Devendre’s hiring, IMC’s management kept in closer contact with the project and the developing morale problems at VSA. Bob Maddocks, Senior Vice-President of Human Resources at IMC, visited the VSA Denver of ce twice a month in order to review VSA’s status and offer advice and support to Devendre. IMC’s management was careful not to meddle in the details of the project or actively interfere with Devendre’s operations. Some VSA managers interpreted IMC’s distance as indicating indifference or lack of support. An undercurrent of tension was brewing, with IMC’s management feeling that the VSA people did not want corporate interference and VSA’s management feeling that IMC’s management did not want to get involved with the project.

## Dif culties at IMC

IMC was also confronting other strategic and  nancial struggles during this time. The company had been through a series of strategic repositionings over the course of a decade and had experienced erratic  nancial success over those years. Fiscal year 1994’s operating results were very poor. The company lacked a steady, pro table outlook and pressure to improve the  rm’s  nancial performance was mounting from both the board and the  nancial community. Fiscal year 1995 got under way on 1 March 1994. A critical issue was the search for a consistent, long-term strategy.

IMC’s  nancial advisers suggested that the company separate into two businesses: the distribution business and the food business, which comprised North American Bakery, Canada Consumer Foods and Venezuela Foods (MONACA). Food service distribution businesses enjoyed a higher price-to-earnings ratio. Further, separation of business types would allow each to focus on ‘core competencies’, which would provide both internal value as well as external con dence. Although IMC’s management took this advice seriously, several forces prevented action towards a spin-off.

First, in 1994 IMC was in the process of acquiring the distribution business of the Laprino Company. This business was to be combined with Pueringer – another speciality food distribution company within IMC – in order to form what would be known as Multifoods Specialty Distribution, leading to all the problems inherent in merging two operations. This merger, coupled with the cash-consuming requirements of the Renaissance system at VSA, made it clear that the distribution business was not strong enough to stand alone. The food service distribution had total operating earnings of only \$7 million for  scal year 1994, whereas operating earnings would be \$11.3 million for  scal year 1995.

Complicating IMC’s situation further were the economic problems in Venezuela where the company’s subsidiary MONACA was located. Although MONACA was a solid business, being the second largest food company in the country and in the top ten overall, the country’s economic problems made MONACA’s contribution in dollar terms unpredictable and somewhat volatile. Because of the rate of in ation in Venezuela in the fourth quarter of  sca year 1994 (ending 28 February 1994), it was necessary for IMC to adopt ‘highly in ationary accounting for MONACA. This meant that, among other things, exchange losses due to in ation had to be taken directly to the operating statement rather than simply be accounted for as balance sheet transactions. The effect on MONACA’s  scal year 1994 earnings was modest, since the change was limited to results for the fourth quarter. Even so, MONACA’s operating earnings declined 4.7% from \$25.5 million in  scal year 1993 to \$24.3 million in  scal year 1994. However, the effect in  scal year 1995 was to be an 18% further decline to \$19.9 million. All this was further compounded in June 1994 by the fact that the Venezuelan Government, in spite of raging in ation, froze the of - cial exchange rate at 170 bolivars to the dollar. The government also made all exchange of bolivars into foreign currency subject to government approval. This affected the payment of dividends by MONACA to its parent company IMC.

Meanwhile, IMC’s bakery business and Canadian subsidiary were growing only modestly. US bakery margins were lowered by rising wheat costs, which affected the cost of a primary ingredient, namely  our. Write downs in the meat business and other unusua items brought a \$17.2 million operating loss in the US food service segment in  scal year 1994. Canadian

Foods sales also decreased 3% in that  scal year. In total including write downs and unusual items of \$19.4 million, these businesses had an operating loss of \$9.9 million.

By the end of  scal year 1995, IMC had evolved into four major businesses: food service distribution in the USA, bakery products in North America, consumer food products in Canada and basic foods in Venezuela. Consolidated operating earnings per share (EPS), exclusive of unusual items such as sales of some businesses, declined from \$1.86 EPS in  scal year 1994 to \$1.55 EPS. The right long-term strategy seemed to be elusive. Nevertheless hopes were high for improved results in  scal year 1996.

## Fiscal year 1996: a dif cult year for IMC and VSA

In February 1995 Devendre made an impressive presentation to the IMC board. Reviewing the status of the industry, the company, the Renaissance project and changes that were planned, he communicated a thorough understanding of the business and showed a careful analysis that gave the board con dence in the new president of VSA. However, as  scal year 1996 got under way, an immediate improvement in the Renaissance project or VSA was not evident.

Inside VSA however, the human resource problems were mounting. Management turnover in the distribution centres was increasing. There was a divisive atmosphere brewing as departments and functions feuded over the source of problems and responsibility for change. Many managers felt frustrated and overworked. Despite Devendre’s best attempts, problems with the Renaissance project and VSA as a whole continued. Morale problems grew and Devendre became frustrated and had dif culty in managing and supporting his staff. Many of the new managers that Devendre had brought on board were getting frustrated. Some were on the verge of leaving the company.

VSA’s pre-tax operating earnings for  scal year 1996 were \$10 million and sales declined 1% due to the continuing loss of the small independent vending operator accounts.

There was little solace for IMC’s management as they looked at other parts of the company’s business. In June 1995, because Venezuela’s wild in ation had continued unabated, rather than use the of cial exchange rate of 170 bolivars to the dollar, companies began using exchange rates based on the so-called ‘Brady bond’ rate. IMC began this practice in August 1995 (the start of the third quarter of  scal year 1996). The Brady bond rate was a proxy for what a free market exchange rate would have been. It was 228 in August 1995 and by 28 February 1996 had reached 470. In effect, in 6 months the rate at which bolivars were translated into dollars had gone from 170 to 470. In spite of this MONACA’s dollar-denominated results for  scal year 1996 only declined \$800 000 (4%) to \$19.1 million.

Producing these results in the middle of such extreme economic turmoil indicated a good basic busi ness at MONACA and careful management within government constraints. However, IMC was in no position to ride unperturbed through such uncertainty. People outside the company were viewing MONACA more and more as a liability. On the other hand, i was not at all clear that the company could realize enough money from the sale of MONACA to replace its earnings contribution to IMC’s overall results. Still, rumours persisted that the company would have to dispose of MONACA and the ‘bottom  shers gathered.

Of the two remaining IMC businesses only the Canada consumer segment was a good performer. Its marketing was strong. Robin Hood was a powerful brand name in grain-based consumer foods and Bick’s was well positioned in the condiments segment. North American Bakery had a good market position in bakery mixes but, with marginal competitive differentiation, good returns were dif cult to achieve. The  edgling frozen bakery mix business did not have a critical mass and was operating at a loss.

IMC’s earnings for  scal year 1996, ending on 28 February 1996, were a disappointing \$1.33 per share. Before unusual gains the company’s earnings were \$1.31 per share compared (on the same basis) to \$1.55 in  scal year 1995 and \$1.86 in  scal year 1994. The company appeared to be in reverse gear. As the com pany entered  scal year 1997 on 1 March 1996, the strategy issue had become, to use nuclear energy ter minology, ‘supercritical’. Before the  rst quarter ended, Tony Luiso resigned as chief executive of cer (CEO).

## May 1996: enter Bob Price

With the departure of Tony Luiso and the ongoing  nancial struggles, Bob Price, a member of the IMC board of directors, was drafted in by the board to serve as an interim CEO. Price had been CEO of the computer company Control Data Corporation during the 1980s and he had a strong reputation for corporate leadership in the face of crisis. He had served on numerous corporate boards, lived in the Minneapoli area and knew IMC quite well. Further, he was willing to take on the task on a temporary basis until a new CEO could be recruited.

He was taking over a company that now consisted of  ve pro t centres: Bakery, Canadian Consumer Foods, MONACA in Venezuela, Multifoods Specialty Distribution (MSD) and VSA. It was not to be. IMC and VSA organization charts are shown in Figures 2 and 3.

It was late in the evening on a Thursday night in May that Price agreed to take on the task at IMC. He would start his new post on Monday morning, just 3 days away and he was thinking about the work ahead. Most importantly, he was pondering his priorities. In order to get started in his new post, he needed a plan and he needed it quickly!
