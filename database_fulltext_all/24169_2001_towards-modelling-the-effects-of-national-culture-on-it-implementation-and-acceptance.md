# Towards modelling the effects of national culture on IT implementation and acceptance

JOHN F. VEIGA AND STEVEN FLOYD

University of Connecticut, Unit 4, 2100 Hillside Road, Storrs, CT 06269–1041, USA

KATHLEEN DECHANT

University of Connecticut at Stamford, 1 University Place, Stamford, CT 06901–2315, USA

Two of the most signi cant forces shaping organizations are globalization and the continued, rapid and, some would say, radical changes taking place in information technology (IT). To date, the extant literature has centred on the technology acceptance model (TAM) because it is arguably one of the most widely cited and in uential models used for explaining the acceptance of IT. However, this literature has remained rela tively silent with respect to the role that differences in national culture might play in IT acceptance as globalization continues. In order to begin to address this de cit, this paper offers a series of research propositions that explore the potential impact of differences in national culture on IT implementation and acceptance. Speci cally, the paper explores the effects of culturally induced beliefs – including individualism–collectivism, uncertainty avoidance, long-/short-term orientation and power distance – on the implementation processes that impact on key variables in the TAM. The paper then discusses the potential importance of the revisions it has made for both researchers interested in employing the model for predicting IT acceptance in crosscultural contexts and for managers faced with introducing new IT in a global organization.

## Introduction

Given the pace of information technology (IT) change, issues of IT implementation and employee acceptance of IT have become increasingly important. However, when the additional pressure of globalization is added these issues have the potential for becoming particularly complex and challenging. Although many factors may affect an individual’s acceptance of new IT, a growing but still fragmented body of research suggests that culturally induced beliefs play an important role (e.g. Straub, 1994). Increasingly, therefore, managers in multinational settings are confronted with the need for gaining acceptance for new IT from a workforce comprised of different cultures whose members may resist such efforts for a variety of different reasons.

The idea that culture is a factor in the implementation of new technology is not new (Kedia and Bhagat, 1988). Theoretical arguments for such a link rest on the premise that the beliefs and values shared by a group of people (i.e. their culture) affect their behaviour in a variety of ways that can hasten or retard the implementation of technological change. Indeed, numerous scholars have argued for the in uence of national culture on values and behaviour (England, 1975) and a signi cant body of research has shown that managers in different countries hold fundamentally different values about technology and change (Lodge and Vogel, 1987; Adler, 1991; Shane, 1993; O’Hara-Devereaux and Johnasen, 1994; Straub, 1994; Tan et al., 1995). However, despite its potential in uence on IT acceptance, national culture has not been featured prominently in technology acceptance research (Straub, 1994; Baba et al., 1996).

Given this shortcoming, this paper explores the relationship between national culture and IT acceptance in a volitional use setting by extending and enhancing the technology acceptance model (TAM) (Davis, 1986). This effort builds on earlier empirical work by Straub (1994) who derived an exogenous social presence/ information richness factor (SPIR) and added it as a theoretical antecedent to the TAM. Straub (1994) reasoned that, since Japanese managers had been shown by Hofstede (1991) to be high on uncertainty avoidance relative to managers, that Japanese respondents would rate e-mail low on the SPIR factor because of their tendency to avoid uncertainty in communication. Thus, Straub (1994) proposed that Japanese knowledge workers would be more likely to choose an informationrich, socially present technology over leaner systems such as e-mail. With respect to the TAM, he found that this tendency on the part of the Japanese translated into lower perceptions of usefulness and lower usage of email in comparison to US workers, suggesting that the TAM may be in uenced by cultural differences. Straub (1994) concluded by calling for research that includes culture as a key variable.

Con rming the potential for culture to act as a confounding factor in research, more recent efforts to use the TAM in cross-cultural studies by Straub and colleagues have produced inconsistent results. Rose and Straub (1998) found overall support for the TAM in a study across  ve Middle Eastern countries but, in a more diverse mix of respondents, Straub et al. (1997) found the model to be a poor  t. While it successfully predicted use for airline employees in the USA and Switzerland, it failed to predict use for Japanese employees.

Building on this work, this paper proposes to broaden the TAM by incorporating multiple dimensions of national culture into the model that extend the de nition of external variables as contemplated by Davis (1989). It begins by describing the theoretical underpinnings of the TAM. It then develops theoretical arguments in order to demonstrate how the perceptual and attitudinal constructs in the model are likely to be in uenced by differences in national culture. Speci cally, this paper predicts that the likelihood of technology acceptance will be in uenced by an individual’s culturally induced belief system. The central thesis of the paper is that national culture affects technology acceptance through its impact on certain key variables that are associated with the implementation process. Implementation approaches attuned to these effects are more likely to enhance perceived usefulness, ease of use and attitudes towards use and, hence, to increase technology acceptance. The last section of the paper discusses the potential implications of these arguments for future research and practice.

## The technology acceptance model

In a recent review, Saga and Zmud (1994) identi ed 20 empirical studies aimed at exploring the factors that determine IT acceptance. Among these 20 studies, the TAM originally proposed by Davis (1986, 1989) is arguably one of the most widely cited and in uential. Indeed, numerous studies have employed this model in successfully predicting the intentions of end users in using IT, including spreadsheets (Mathieson, 1991), voice mail and word processors (Adams et al., 1992), data-based management systems (Szajna-Bernadette,

1996) and group support systems (Chin and Gopal, 1995).

Davis (1986) initially grounded his model on the theory of reasoned action proposed by Fishbein and Azjen (1975) who asserted that beliefs in uence attitudes, which in turn lead to intentions that result in behaviour. Accordingly, Davis (1986) reasoned that an individual’s beliefs with regard to ‘perceived usefulness’ and ‘perceived ease of use’ would in uence their attitudes towards use, resulting in an intention to use that in turn resulted in actual use. Davis’s (1989) model is shown in Figure 1.

As shown, perceived usefulness and ease of use were both in uenced by external variables including user support, training, system features and documentation. Moreover, ease of use was hypothesized to in uence perceived usefulness and perceived usefulness was also expected to in uence an individual’s behavioural intention to use directly.

## Towards integrating national culture

One of the most enduring de nitions of culture is that it consists of ‘patterns, explicit and implicit of and for behavior, . . . [that] may, on the one hand, be considered as products of action, [and] on the other, as conditioning elements of future action’ (Kroeber and Kluckhohn, 1952, p. 181, italics added). Culture is thus represented as a series of trace effects or inferred underlying patterns of belief about past and future behaviour that may have no meaning outside of tha pattern (e.g. Deutsch, 1973; Lytle et al., 1995).

In the last 20 years, Hofstede’s (1980, 1991) theory of the speci c patterns in the values and beliefs that constitute national culture have gained signi cant prominence. He de ned culture as a set of shared assumptions that result in a common frame of reference by members of a society or more simply as ‘menta software’ (Hofstede, 1991). One of the most important aspects of Hofstede’s (1980, 1991) work is tha he successfully linked his dimensions of culture to managerial practices. Based on a study of 53 cultures, Hofstede (1980, 1991) initially identi ed four dimensions of cultural variation: power distance, uncertainty avoidance, individualism and masculinity. Later he added long-term orientation (Hofstede and Bond, 1988). Of these  ve dimensions, masculinity has been the most dif cult to conceptualize and validate (Veiga et al., 1993, 1995); therefore, it will be dropped from consideration here. The four remaining dimensions have been conceptually de ned (Hofstede, 1993; Triandis, 1994) as follows.

(1) Individualism. The extent to which people of a country show a propensity to see themselves as self-suf cient individuals (individualist) as compared to a propensity to see themselves primarily as an integral part of a social group (collectivist).

(2) Uncertainty avoidance. The extent to which people of a country show a propensity to maintain the status quo when feeling uneasy or threatened by situations that are uncertain or ambiguous.

(3) Power distance. The extent to which people of a country show a propensity to accept unequal distributions of power, such as between senior managers and rank and  le workers.

(4) Long-term orientation. The extent to which people of a country show a propensity to take a long-term perspective that primarily emphasizes doing things that improve the future as opposed to a short-term perspective that primarily emphasizes the present or the successes of the past.

Berger and Luckmann (1967) and others (Van Maanen and Laurent, 1993) have argued that members of a national society not only contribute to the collective formulation of cultural values and norms, but also experience pressures from society that shape their individual values and assumptions. As a result, there is generally a close alignment between individuals’ beliefs and those of their native culture. Consistent with this view, research has shown that the differing views and assumptions embedded in national culture are re ected in managerial attitudes and beliefs (Lodge and Vogel, 1987) and also in the behaviours and actions by which organizational members discharge their roles (Jackofsky and Slocum, 1988). Managers and workers – socialized from an early age to the value orientation of their respective societies – bring to the workplace perspectives that in uence their behaviours and, in particular, their willingness to accept new IT. Thus, how IT is introduced and the extent to which this introduction is consistent with and reinforcing of cultural values plays a critical role in whether individuals accept the technology.

In addition to the limited evidence for the effects of culture on IT acceptance in Table 1, the idea that the speci c beliefs identi ed in the TAM (perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness) are in uenced by a more general system of beliefs, such as cultural beliefs, can be inferred from several recent studies outside the cross-cultural domain. For example, recent work aimed at integrating individual differences variables (e.g. age, education and gender) into the TAM (Gefen and Straub, 1997; Agarwal and Prasad, 1999) was based on connections between the beliefs produced by a groups’ common socialization experience and their attitudes towards IT. Similarly, Venkatesh and Davis (1996) showed that an individual’s perception of a system’s ease of use was linked to their general ‘computer self-ef cacy’. Moreover, Chau (1996) showed that the concept of perceived usefulness encompasses both near-term and long-term usefulness in the minds of users and that the latter had a positive impact on behavioural intentions to use a technology. In effect, this conceptualization parallels the in uences of Hofstede and Bond’s (1988) dimension of long-term orientation. Similarly, Thompson et al.

(1991) conceptualized perceived usefulness to include ‘near-term job  t’ and ‘long-term consequences of use’. They de ned job  t as the ‘extent to which an individual believes that using a personal computer can enhance his job performance’ and they de ned longterm consequences as ‘outcomes that have a high payoff in the future’ (Thompson et al., 1991, p. 129). The in uence of national culture is not immediately apparent in these de nitions, but the job  t dimension appears biased towards cultures high on individualism that stress the importance of individua achievement. On the other hand, de ning usefulness in terms of long-term consequences could re ect the cultural bias towards long-term orientation.

Together, these studies suggest that perceptions of a technology’s ease of use and usefulness are connected to an individual’s broader system of belief, including culturally sensitive beliefs. Before making such links explicit, it is important to recognize the limitations of this logic for predicting IT acceptance and, hence, the theoretical scope of the model this paper is describing. In particular, the theory described here ignores the effects of organizational- or work group-level in uences on an individual’s perceptions and behaviour. Thus, consistent with recent research (Lucas and Spitler, 1999), this paper recognizes that organizational norms and job requirements may be more important predictors of use in certain settings than individuals’ perceptions of ease of use. However, no theory can account for every factor and parsimony demands that ceteris paribus assumptions are made about these and other potential predictors of IT acceptance. In addition, by setting aside the in uence of formal roles and job requirements, the technological scope of predictions about systems that are volitional in character is limited. This is consistent with most of the research on the TAM, which has focused on e-mail, word processing, spreadsheets and similar systems, where use is relatively discretionary in comparison to transactionsoriented systems.

## Individualism–Collectivism

A primary dimension along which cultures differ is the lens through which individuals in a society view themselves, i.e. personal identity. In societies with strong individualistic tendencies individuals perceive themselves largely as independent actors. Such cultures typically value individual achievement and personal autonomy, thereby enabling members to place personal interests above those of the collective society. Hofstede (1980) argued that cultures high on individualism tend to promote individual decision making over group consensus. In contrast, in societies emphasizing collectivism the group becomes the primary source of an individual’s identity and individuals seek approval, status and support through group af liation. Concerns over group welfare, equality and loyalty are emphasized, as aggregate interests tend to prevail over autonomous, individualistic ones (Hofstede, 1991).

There are several potential effects of individualism on IT acceptance. Expressed as the right to selfdetermination, for example, it may lead to the familiar ‘not invented here syndrome’, thereby creating a proliferation of systems for doing the same work within a single organization. For example, Baba et al. (1996) observed a US  rm operating with a dozen pre-production systems where a comparable Japanese company had only one. Another effect of individualism is the tendency to emphasize an individual’s interests over those of the group. This may lead to a low perceived utility for technologies, such as group support systems, the goal of which is to increase collaboration and integration in the organization. On the other hand, in collectivist societies emphasizing the technology’s effects on the interests of the group may be essential. For example, Ishida (1998) reported that Japanese strawberry farmers became Internet users only after it was explained to them that they could, as a group, make more money by selling fresh strawberries directly to consumers rather than through wholesalers. On the other hand, as Straub (1994) observed, individuals in more collectivist societies such as Japan tend to avoid communication technologies such as e-mail that minimize the ‘social presence’ of the user (Short et al., 1976) even though such technologies may be otherwise more appropriate and ef cient.

More generally, the present authors would argue that, in highly individualistic societies, perceptions about the usefulness of IT are likely to be based on beliefs about how the system affects the individual’s job performance rather than how it affects the performance of the group. Because they believe in personal control and individual achievement, people in individualistic cultures accept task accomplishment as their personal responsibility. Judgements about the usefulness of IT are therefore made based on the extent to which the system is seen to enhance the task performance of individuals. However, in highly collectivist societies the group (i.e. work unit, team, organization or community) rather than individuals is seen as responsible for accomplishing tasks. As a result, judgements about the usefulness of IT are made based on the extent to which the system is seen to enhance the task performance of groups. Thus, the following propositions are proposed.

P1: In individualistic cultures new IT will be more likely viewed as useful when it is perceived as enhancing the performance of individuals.

P2: In collectivist cultures new IT will be more likely viewed as useful when it is perceived as enhancing the performance of groups.

In addition to its implications for the compatibilit of certain technologies with certain cultures (e.g. spreadsheets designed for improving individual decisions in individualist societies and group support systems in collectivist societies), this argument suggests different logics for increasing perceived usefulness in more individualistic versus more collectivist cultures. Thus, in training users to use e-mail systems in collectivist cultures, it may be important to show the bene- ts of use for group-level outcomes, such as enhanced communication within a project group. In individualistic cultures, however, it may be more important to show bene ts for individual productivity. Importantly, if individual productivity improvements are perceived to come at the expense of richer forms of interaction in collectivist societies e-mail may be resisted. In such a setting, users should be trained to use e-mail no only so that their communications are richer but also so that e-mail is not used as an inappropriate substitute for richer forms of media.

If group-level interests are seen purely as the aggregate of individual-level interests, propositions 1 and 2 appear to make a distinction without a difference. In fact, however, outcomes that are good for individuals are often not good for the group as a whole. For example, where individualized performance incentives are used, organizations often reward employees based on their ability to compete with co-workers in a ranking scheme or some other comparative criterion. Thus, in order to maximize outcomes for themselves, workers may attempt to improve their standing by concealing performance-related information from co-workers (Veiga, 1991). In such an environment, implementation tactics will be more successful when individua workers believe that the system will help them compete for rewards. Training should therefore be designed to facilitate beliefs that using the system will give individuals a personal or career advantage. In collectivist cultures, however, attempts to stimulate competition among individuals will be seen as counterproductive. Training and other implementation tactics will be more successful if they increase perceptions that the system improves collaboration among individuals and enhances group-level performance outcomes.

Beyond perceptions of training and support programmes, the individualist/collectivist orientation may also affect an individual’s computer self-ef cacy, i.e. a general belief in one’s ability to use IT effectively (Venkatesh and Davis, 1996). This is important because recent research has shown that increasing objective usability with training and support enhances ease of use perceptions only after direct experience with the system has occurred (Venkatesh and Davis, 1996). At all times and particularly in the early stages of implementation, perceptions of a particular system’s ease of use are grounded in an individual’s general beliefs regarding computer self-ef cacy (Venkatesh and Davis, 1996).

Bandura (1997) suggested that self-ef cacy can be shaped by either direct experience or through observing the experience of others. Hence, in individualistic cultures, computer self-ef cacy is more likely to be shaped by a user’s prior experiences with IT, while in collectivist cultures it is more likely to be shaped by a user’s observations of and interactions with members of the user’s work group. In effect, potential users in collectivist cultures are likely to form consensus judgements about ease of use. That is, they are more likely to base their personal computer self-ef cacy and, hence, their beliefs about the system’s ease of use on their perceptions of the level of computer self-ef cacy within the group (Prussia and Kinicki, 1996). Put differently, because the user’s identity is tied closely to the group’s identity, individual beliefs about computer self-ef cacy and ease of use are likely to be very similar to the group consensus. Thus, the following propositions are proposed.

P3: In individualistic cultures computer self-ef cacy will be based primarily on an individual’s previous experiences with IT.

P4: In collectivist cultures computer self-ef cacy will be based primarily on the previous collective experience of the user’s work group with IT.

Individualistic cultures tend to value and reward high achievers, including those who are adept technology users, thereby accelerating the progress of those in the vanguard. Moreover, the rate of technology adoption (for a society, organization or group) tends to be ‘pulled along’ by individuals in the vanguard who serve as a reference point or role model for other users (Rogers, 1983). Hence, in individualistic cultures, perceived ease of use is more likely to be based on the skill levels of the most successful users. On the other hand, collectivist cultures are less likely to value and reward an individual’s IT  uency. Indeed, the need to prevent loss of face by others may cause skilful individuals to hide their expertise (Imahori and Cupach, 1994). Even assessing individual skill levels may be complicated by the variance-reducing tendencies of th group.

The reference point for users in collectivist cultures thus becomes the collective skill level rather than th most successful user’s skill level. In this situation, potential users’ perceptions of ease of use are likely to regress to the mean, i.e. the average level of dif culty perceived by group members so that the least skilled members play as much a role as the most skilled members in shaping this perception. Such tendencies in collectivist societies suggest that they are much more likely to see technology as dif cult to use. Evidence of this effect may be evident in the lower IT use rate reported in Japan and other Asian cultures (McMillan, 1996), although the availability of the technology must also be considered (Straub, 1994). Finally, given that the least skilled members have the potential for improving the most, targeting them for intensive training could result in the greatest pay-off.

The fact that the perceived usability of a system may be anchored to an individual or group reference point has implications for efforts to in uence it. In particular, there are logical links to the design of training programmes and rewards for IT skill development. Thus, the following hypotheses are proposed.

P5: In individualistic cultures training and rewards for the acquisition of IT skills that encourage the most successful users will be associated with increased perceived ease of use.

P6: In collectivist cultures new IT training that encourages the least skilled users will be associated with increased perceived ease of use.

P7: In collectivist cultures group-level rewards for acquisition of new IT skills will be associated with increased perceived ease of use.

## Uncertainty avoidance

The dimension of uncertainty avoidance concerns how individuals in a society respond to ambiguous and unstructured situations or contexts. In cultures high on uncertainty avoidance individuals tend to experience discomfort and hesitation when faced with situations that violate the status quo and present them with uncertainty. As a result, when faced with such a context they tend to respond apprehensively and with a good deal of caution and scepticism. According to Hofstede (1980), such individuals show a strong preference for greater structure, clear rules and standardized operating procedures. On the other hand, in low uncertainty avoidance cultures there is a greater likelihood that uncertainty and ambiguity will be tolerated and, indeed, to some extent embraced as a positive aspect of change. Members of such societies are relatively more at ease, though not completely at ease, with unde ned or unfamiliar situations and, thus, generally demonstrate a greater tolerance for new and different ideas, approaches and concepts.

The effects of uncertainty avoidance on the TAM may be most clearly discernible on perceived ease of use. Perceived uncertainty and ambiguity in a situation is in uenced strongly by the situation’s novelty (Weick, 1995). Among other things, learning means developing a better sense of a situation’s key parameters, i.e. the factors related to attaining the desired behaviour or performance. In a culture high on uncertainty avoidance individuals are more likely to avoid learning new technologies introduced into their workplace because of the uncertainty and ambiguity involved. Moreover with such constant and rapid change, IT may be considered inherently risky (Hasan and Ditsa, 1999). Evidence for these effects can be found in studies of IT implementation in African (Entsua-Mensah, 1996) and Middle Eastern (Danowitz et al., 1995) countries where uncertainty avoidance is high. Hence, the following proposition is proposed.

P8: In high uncertainty avoidance cultures the rate of individual IT skill acquisition (learning) will be lower, resulting in lower levels of perceived ease of use.

Moreover, in a context where users are higher in uncertainty avoidance it may be dif cult to engage them in attempts to specify system design parameters. A participative design process, particularly when it involves an unfamiliar technology, may be very dif - cult for those who desire avoiding uncertainty (Baba et al., 1996). For example, Abdul-Gadar (1997) found reluctance to participate among managers in Arab countries where uncertainty avoidance and power distance are high. Thus, in addition to skill development, uncertainty avoidance may impede the involvement of users in the design process and this means it may be dif cult or impossible to determine which features of the system might increase users’ perceptions of its ease of use. Even for off-the-shelf systems such as e-mail, there are typically options and/or system features that may or may not be selected by the organization. If users do not provide input on such choices the system is less likely to  t whatever level of skill exists in the user population and this will compound the effects of uncertainty avoidance on learning. Therefore, the following is proposed.

P9: In high uncertainty avoidance cultures users wil be reluctant to participate in specifying features that make the system easy to use so that, when the system is implemented, such users are more likely to perceive that the system is dif cult to use.

In such a context, IT implementation will bene t from an approach that is highly structured and that emphasizes the proven and established nature of the technology. Indeed, the historic portrayal of new IT as risky and uncertain is probably a signi cant factor in explaining the low acceptance of IT in Middle Eastern cultures (Danowitz et al., 1995). When introducing new technologies in high uncertainty avoidance cultures managers may consider implementation tactics that increase perceptions that the new system is reliable and well understood and that not using it constitutes risky behaviour (Hasan and Ditsa, 1999). Such efforts could include organizing visits to other sites where the system is used successfully or developing high-pro le pilot projects internally aimed at demonstrating the system’s reliability.

P10: In high uncertainty avoidance cultures implementation tactics that are designed to increase perceptions that a new system is proven and established will be associated with higher levels of perceived usefulness.

## Long-term versus short-term orientation

The dimension of long-term orientation, which was originally labelled as Confucian dynamism by Hofstede and Bond (1988), refers to a society’s preference to be more future oriented or forward looking. Cultures maintaining a long-term orientation are characterized by values emphasizing the importance of the future and perseverance. Hence, for an organizational change to be embraced there must be a belief in some future pay-off that justi es persevering in the present. In short-term-oriented societies, on the other hand, the perspective tends to be more immediate or quick  x in nature, with an emphasis on present need and/or past success. With respect to acceptance of change, members of societies with short-term orientations are likely to be more inclined to be responsive to change if a need exists, particularly when past successes or traditions are shown to be wanting (Hofstede, 1993). However, implementing change in societies with a long-term orientation requires the recognition that individuals in these societies will tend to be more conservative, embracing the practices and values of the past and, therefore, more reluctant to consider major changes in how things are done.

These observations suggest that values linked to long-term versus short-term orientation are likely to hold signi cant importance for technology acceptance and in particular for perceptions about the technology’s usefulness. Judging the usefulness of a new system involves assessments of the technology’s relationship to work. In short-term-oriented cultures this is likely to mean that perceived usefulness is based on connections between new IT and current tasks and immediate work bene ts. This is consistent with Chau’s (1996)  nding in a US sample (a short-term-oriented culture) that near-term usefulness had a more signi - cant in uence on intention to use than perceived longterm usefulness. However, rather than a  t with the task at hand, perceptions of usefulness in long-termoriented cultures may be based on how the system appears to meet the needs of work in the future, as de ned, for example, by long-term goals or vision statements. In such settings, perceived long-term usefulness may be more important than perceived near-term usefulness.

P11: In short-term-oriented cultures perceived links between new IT and immediate work bene ts will be associated with higher levels of perceived usefulness.

P12: In long-term-oriented cultures perceived links between new IT and future work needs will be associated with higher levels of perceived usefulness.

In addition to looking far ahead, long-term orientation tends to bind people to existing work practices and, more importantly, to the behavioural norms and values implicit in speci c ways of working (Hofstede, 1980, 1991). Thus, even objectively trivial routines such as  lling out paperwork may come to be identi ed with a sense of what is normal and appropriate. In its extreme form this introduces a reluctance to accept anything that creates a signi cant change in work behaviour. Such conservatism is most likely to express itself in how an individual evaluates the usefulness of a technology because usefulness is judged in terms of the system’s effect on work processes and outcomes. New IT almost always involves new ways of doing work and, if ‘new’ is seen as being ‘inappropriate’, then IT is likely to be seen as a poor  t with accepted ways of doing things. If accomplishing work has been traditionally based on social relationships, for example, communication systems such as e-mail that are expected to reduce face-to-face meetings may be perceived as ineffectual. These arguments are consistent with observations from focus groups of Arab businessmen where loyalty to national traditions appeared to be an impediment to the acceptance of IT (Hill et al., 1998). Thus, the following is proposed.

P13: In long-term-oriented cultures perceptions that new IT is a poor  t with valued, traditional work practices will be associated with lower levels of perceived usefulness.

However, it would be a mistake to conclude that a short-term orientation is necessarily consistent with the acceptance of new IT. Research has shown that strategic planning is essential to the long-term development of valuable new systems (Rockart and Scott Morton, 1984; Johnston and Carrico, 1988; Floyd and Wooldridge, 1990) and a short-term orientation tend to focus management’s attention on the immediate future. This reduces the attention given to strategic planning and undermines the effectiveness of the process (Miller and Cardinal, 1994; Hasan and Ditsa, 1999). Poor IT planning leads to the development of new systems that are disconnected from the organiza tion’s goals and strategy and this lack of strategic  t is in turn likely to reduce the system’s perceived utility. Thus, even though short-term results may be more highly valued than long-term bene ts in short-termoriented cultures, connecting new IT to strategy is likely to increase its perceived usefulness. Thus, the following is proposed.

P14: In short-term-oriented cultures strategic IT planning is more likely to be ineffective and the resulting failure to align new IT to organizationa strategy is likely to be associated with lower percep tions of the system’s utility.

## Power distance

The dimension of power distance re ects a culture’s acceptance of social inequality, i.e. the differences between the ‘haves’ and the ‘have nots’. In other words, power distance refers to the extent that those in lower social strata accept as a given the power and status of those in higher strata. In societies where power distance is high, there tends to be an almost universal acceptance of the notion that those with greater status deserve the respect they are afforded without question. Such unquestioning attitudes lead to excessive levels of loyalty and fealty in organizationa contexts and the taking of action only after total sanctioning and support of one’s superior. In high power distance cultures, centralized decision making tends to be the norm rather than the exception (Hofstede, 1980) presenting formidable barriers to introducing change (Hage and Aiken, 1970). Thus, it is not surprising that Shane (1993) observed a linkage between power distance and differences in rates of innovation across cultures. In contrast, societies low in power distance tend to be more egalitarian in nature and members largely (though not completely) tend to view each other as equals. This perception of equality diffuses hierarchical power in organizations and encourages taking action without full sanction from one’s superiors.

Power distance is a potent factor in technology acceptance because the greater the level of power distance in a society the greater the in uence of the social elite, such as senior managers, on attitudes towards change. Put differently, in non-egalitarian cultures, people are more likely to form their attitudes towards new work behaviours based on what they believe are the attitudes of their ‘betters’. Therefore, rather than positing relationships between power distance and perceived usefulness or ease of use, the present authors believe the effects of power distance on IT acceptance are likely to be the result of its direct effects on an individual’s general attitude towards use (see Figure 2). While the TAM argues that attitudes towards use are principally in uenced by perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, the present authors believe that culturally induced beliefs about social status may also play a signi cant role.

When attitudes towards taking some action, such as using a new IT system, depend on the approval of superiors, people may be reluctant to form their own opinions and, instead, wait on implicit and explicit direction from above. Ren (1998) reported on the effect of power distance on a programme for introducing microcomputers into the Ministries of Agriculture in Kenya. Women secretaries in the systems department, because of their ability to type, learned to use the computers  rst. Managers, who were primarily men, soon realized that computers were more than sophisticated typewriters. As a consequence, management instituted a rule that no employee could use any computer, including the desktops, unless they had a computer science degree. As a result, the women who were becoming as knowledgeable and informed as their bosses suddenly found the power distance gap restored and even strengthened. This example illustrates how cultural values that are antagonistic to the changes introduced by new IT may overwhelm and even reverse the implementation process. In particular, when IT challenges the established order in high power distance cultures, it is not likely to be viewed positively by potential users. Thus, in high power distance cultures the attitudes of top management are likely to become a constraint on the positive attitude formation of users as a whole. However, in such a setting, when a new system is implemented using a top-down process, i.e. with strong and widely communicated support from senior managers, middle and lower level managers can be expected to form positive attitudes towards use. The need for top management support may be particularly problematic in the case of new IT because senior managers tend to be older and research has shown that age is negatively associated with positive managerial attitudes towards IT (Floyd, 1988). This effect may introduce a signi cant negative bias towards IT use for organizations in high power distance cultures. Thus, the following is proposed.

P15: In high power distance cultures attitudes towards use are more positive when new IT initiatives are implemented using a top-down approach.

Conversely, in low power distance cultures people feel less constrained by the attitudes of higher status members. In addition, their sense of egalitarianism puts a premium on having an equal say or forming their own opinions and taking action on their own. In such a situation, new technologies that are implemented from the top down or in the extreme by managemen  at risk a negative reaction even before users have had the opportunity of experiencing the system. Indeed, in the absence of power distance, bottom-up initiatives are likely to be supported and even encouraged. In low power distance cultures therefore, it may be important for users to participate actively in the system design and implementation processes, thereby empowering them to decide on system features and functionality. Thus, the following is proposed.

P16: In low power distance cultures attitudes towards use will be enhanced by implementation processes that increase users’ sense of participation in the choice of a new technology.

## Discussion and conclusions

In summary, this paper has proposed a relatively extensive set of relationships between culturally induced beliefs and speci c implementation issues and has further suggested how different implementation approaches in uence the core variables of the TAM. In particular, the paper has argued that the level of individualism/collectivism in uences whether people are more likely to see new technology in the contex of their own tasks or the work of the group as a whole. Uncertainty avoidance affects not only the rate of IT learning, but also the extent that implementation wil bene t from employee participation and from increasing the sense that technology is proven and reliable. Time orientation in uences how new IT should be aligned with current or future work needs, traditional work practices and strategic planning. Finally, the model suggests that power distance plays a powerful role in the utility of more top-down versus bottom-up approaches to implementation. Thus, the model suggests that technology acceptance in a crosscultural setting will be enhanced when managers design the implementation process in order to respond to these cultural differences.

## Implications for research

This paper’s argument has been that the four cultural dimensions play distinct roles in in uencing the TAM. The ‘interaction effects’ from combinations of cultural values in a speci c country are expected to affect IT acceptance in ways that are distinctive to that particular culture. For example, high uncertainty avoidance decreases the pace of individual learning and high power distance increases the in uence of social elites. Thus, people from those countries characterized as high in power distance and uncertainty avoidance, such as Japan, Mexico, Greece, Brazil, Spain, Argentina, France, Belgium and South Africa, are likely to be slower in accepting new IT and require more centralized direction in order to enhance acceptance. Conversely, people from countries relatively low in power distance and uncertainty avoidance, such as the UK, the Republic of Ireland, Canada, Norway, Sweden, The Netherlands, Australia, New Zealand and the USA, are more likely to accept new IT and have a greater need for participation in its development.

A speci c comparison can be made between the USA and Japan. Based on Hofstede’s (1980) original  ndings, Japan scores signi cantly higher on uncertainty avoidance and power distance than the USA, while the USA scores much higher on individualism. Taken in combination, these cultural differences would suggest that the Japanese would be slower to adopt new IT than US managers. The most recent evidence with regard to the rate of introduction of new IT in these countries bears this out. While the Japanese are moving towards a knowledge-intensive service economy, they are not moving as fast as the USA in IT adoption rates. For example, in 1993 Japan had 9.9 personal computers per 100 workers compared to 41.7 in the USA (McMillan, 1996). Most recently, Business Week (1998) reported that many sectors of the Japanese economy have ‘scarcely started the transition to the Information Age [and that] . . . Japan’s consensus-leaning [collectivist], top-down management style [high power distance] is slow to embrace technology that would transfer decision-making to middle managers’ (p. 124).

The fact that country-speci c predictions about technology acceptance require consideration of differing and sometimes con icting cultural in uences raises a number of interesting questions for empirical research. What happens, for example, in societies that are low in power distance but high in uncertainty avoidance? Is the slow pace of individual learning mitigated by the tendency to expect a higher level of involvement in the system’s design? Alternatively, doe fear of the unknown restrain participation in the design process? Similarly, in cases where the effects are reinforcing, whether interaction effects are additive or multiplicative is an interesting and important empirical question.

Since country-speci c effects can be traced to speci c relationships between cultural values and individual attitudes like those described above, empirica cross-cultural research using the TAM should b designed for measuring cultural values at the individua level of analysis and looking for relationships between these and perceived usefulness, ease of use or attitudes towards use. The present authors agree with Straub’s (1994, p. 39) admonition that researchers make no untested assumptions about regional character. However, the present authors would go further than this and recommend that researchers make no untested assumptions about an individual’s beliefs, no matter what their cultural or regional heritage.

## Implications for practice

Although explicit prescriptions should await empirical support for the propositions, the model raises a number of practical questions that could be useful to managers attempting to implement IT in a crosscultural setting. These are summarized in Table 2. As the table suggests, implementing IT in different cultures complicates the design of the implementation process. Among other things, changes in how the bene- ts of IT are framed, how training and reward systems are designed and whether to use a top-down or bottomup implementation process may need to be reconsidered in light of the cultural setting.

Table 2 suggests that, for global companies seeking to implement IT on a worldwide basis, implementation should be managed as a blend of global standard and geographic differences. For example, a  rm seeking to establish a global standard for e-mail should not expect to standardize the implementation process in the same way it standardizes the technology itself. Doing so is likely to yield differential rates of acceptance across different cultures and, in some cases, lead to low usage.

However, designing culturally sensitive implementation processes for standardized technologies is likely to require close coordination between managers in the corporate centre and in regional of ces. Too much centralization may lead to uneven acceptance across regions, but too little may undermine valuable economies of scale and other synergies sought by the global  rm. This situation suggests the need for teams and task forces that draw members from both headquarters and local geographies. Such groups would provide ideal forums for discussing and resolving the kind of questions raised in Table 2.
