# Australian network design practice

G.C. LOW

School of Information Systems, Technology and Management, The University of New South Wales, Sydney, 2052 Australia

C. AITKEN

Andersen, 304 E. 86th Street, Apt. #4B, New York, NY 10028, USA

The network infrastructure in many organizations is now vital to their continuing success and survival. This increased dependence has been fuelled by various factors including new high-speed network technologies and the acceptance of client/server application design techniques. Interestingly, though, there is little published information on network design methodologies that could be used for ensuring that the network design is aligned with the organization’s network goals and that key design tasks are not overlooked. As the  rst stage in the development of a network design methodology framework, this research investigates what issues derived from the literature are seen by network design practitioners as important and what are the current tasks performed by practitioners in network design. Based on this research the outline of a generic network design methodology is presented.

## Introduction

The infusion of information technology (IT) into the economic infrastructure in the past decade has been rapid. The key tools of business are changing as computer and communication technologies open up new business opportunities and affect the heart of many business processes. This infusion has contributed to changes in the dynamics of the business environment, thereby creating new pressures and expectations on the corporate network resource.

Expenditure on telecommunications services in Australia increased by 29% in the 3 years from 1995–1996 to 1998–1999 while expenditure on computer services increased by 26% (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2000a). This increased reliance on telecommunications is also re ected in the large increase in businesses with Internet access (29% versus 56%) between 1997–1998 and 1999–2000 versus the steady increase in computer use (49% in 1993–1994, 63% in 1997–1998 and 76% in 1999–2000) (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2000b).

These statistics suggest that telecommunications have become vital to the success and survival of many organizations. The strategic nature of much of the information being accessed serves to highlight how dependent these organizations have become on their networking infrastructure. Geschickter (1994) stated that ‘network managers need a methodology that maps development solutions to speci c business problems’ (p. 73). He demonstrated how even basic changes in business strategies can require major changes in networking environments. Changes in technology and increasing user demands on the network infrastructure leave many organizations operating with a suboptimal networking solution.

A Dataquest (1997) study of 200 North American organizations found that the network was the second most common cause of mission critical failures, second only to applications. Similarly, Appleby (1996) referred to a data network as a complex information systems (IS) infrastructure and often the most critical infrastructure system for the company; if the ‘ ow’ of information is interrupted in a normal organization, business ceases. In the case of the health care network described, the system is even more critical since ‘the life and death of patients may well hang in the balance’ (Appleby, 1996, p. 30).

Obviously the data networks used by many organizations are critical to the success and competitive advantage of the company. As such a data network’s design and maintenance should be paramount to an organization’s goals. The network designer may use existing application systems development methodologies for the design of data networks. However, these methodologies are not tailored for network design and lack relevant network design principles (e.g. Vittali et al., 1992). On the other hand, the designer can apply design methods for speci c network areas. However, each method covers only a part of the design problem faced in multivendor, multiservice private networks. Furthermore, these methods generally ignore nontechnical issues such as the overall management of the project and the interface with the end-users.

As a  rst step towards the development of an integrated network design methodology and the determination of evaluation criteria for network development methodologies, this research investigated the following research questions.

(1) What issues do practitioners consider important in network design and what is the relative ranking of those issues?

(2) What are the current tasks performed by practitioners for network design? This question allows an assessment of whether the set of issues derived from the literature is complete and whether the practitioners actually carry out the tasks implied by these issues.

Based on this research the outline of a generic network design methodology (NOMAD) is presented.

## Literature review

This section contains a review of the academic literature concerned with network implementation and design. The literature is generally normative based on the particular experience of the writer(s) and fairly narrow in focus. As a broad characterization for instance, Jing et al. (1993) had an orientation towards the ef ciency of network operations, Anderson et al. (1993) addressed effectiveness and performance and Shatz and Wang (1989) placed some emphasis on robustness. In contrast, Keen (1988) suggested that networks should be designed and implemented in order to facilitate business strategies. These are all valuable contributions but individually they do not provide a general structure for considering network design and they do not validate their views by reference to experts and practice in the  eld.

Although the details of proprietary network design methodologies are not available in the public domain, their general principles bear consideration in any generic network design methodology. A number of selected methodologies are summarized below.

Andersen Consulting’s Enterprise Information Architecture provides a framework for the development of IS in general and as such can be used in the design of a network (Ryan and Santucci, 1993). The model was designed in order to be very  exible and to integrate the capability for assessing the impact of change that a new system would have on an organization.

Ghosh (1992) presented an integrated approach to designing networks aimed at reducing the inef ciency of developing several speci c designs in isolation from one another. He recommended specifying integrated design processes, methods and standards that offer structured guidelines for designing an enterprise network.

GTEL (Muhammad and Jasjit, 1989) is a comprehensive and formal process speci cation for network design. The methodology was developed in part to ensure proper documentation, to enhance communications between involved parties and to de ne the responsibilities of those involved. The methodology has ten major areas that support the above processes: general policies, bid proposals, project planning, project implementation, project procurement, schedule control, cost control, contract administration, reviews and reports and close out and transfer. The methodology includes iteration between processes, training of personnel and impact of the project on the organization at a departmental and global level.

IBM’s (1986) Networking Blueprint provides a comprehensive framework for the management of the telecommunications function that is missing from most other methodologies.

Lucent Technologies’ Network Lifecycle Methodology (http://www.lucent-networkcare.com/consulting approach/method/nlm/index.asp.) provides a framework for ‘solving complex network problems’. It has four phases: planning, design, implementation and operations. ‘Each lifecycle function is comprised of a process description, procedure and supporting collateral including too references, templates, sample deliverables and best practice references’.

OPTINET is a methodology for the design of private computer networks (Vittali et al., 1992). It incorporates an object-oriented analysis for describing the network data and applications plus associated tool support for dimensioning and optimizing the network.

Oppenheimer (1999) provided a general network design methodology. However, there are some indications that it is more focused on Transmission Control Protocol/Internal Protocol (TCP/IP)-type networks than general networking.

(1) Overall management. Overall project management is not an issue speci cally supported by many of the network design methodologies surveyed. However, IBM’s (1986) Networking Blueprint includes a number of planning activities speci cally suggesting the following issues, many of which are also suggested by Ghosh’s (1992) and the GTEL (Muhammad and Jasjit, 1989) methodologies.

(i) Resource management planning: disaster recovery, asset protection,  nance and personal. Asset protection has been split into two elements in Table 2: physical asset protection and network security planning.

(ii) Service planning: schedule service and measurement and control. The former issue is addressed by service agreements with suppliers while the latter issue is also addressed by network security planning.

(iii) Customer planning: service agreements with clients/users, implementation/cut over of the network and client programme development. Two issues have been included for addressing client programme development: education and training procedures development and charge-out policy negotiation with clients users. The issue of charge-out policies is raised in both Ghosh’s (1986) and IBM’s (1986) Networking Blueprint methodologies.

(2) Business network alignment. A review of the activities included in the various methodologies suggests that these activities are primarily aimed at ensuring that the network design is aligned with the strategic objectives of the business.

(3) Requirements and logical design. Requirement and logical design is considered analogous to the requirements elicitation and the logical design phases in a traditional application design methodology. The key activities are requirements elicitation and determination of a conceptual or logical design that would suggest a basic telecommunications architecture. This last issue is addressed by the IBM (1986), Ghosh (1989), Oppenheimer (1999) and Lucent Technologies (http://www. lucent-networkcare.com/ consulting/ approach/ method/nlm/index.asp.) methodologies. A review of the methodologies listed in Table 1 suggests a number of issues should be addressed by the requirements elicitation activity including client/user consultation and network and service needs elicitation. Client/User consultation is not necessarily the same as network and service needs elicitation. It may be possible to determine the network and service needs without signi cant client consultation. However, research into user information satisfaction (e.g. Lawrence and Low, 1993) has suggested that the users’ information satisfaction will not be as high as would be the case if the users feel involved (i.e. consulted). Network and service needs elicitation is a generic requirements elicitation issue and can be considered as including issues such as network  exibility and performance, quality of service requirements and traf c behaviour. These issues have been included separately in order to determine the importance the respondents placed on these issues relative to the generic requirements elicitation issue.

(4) Network design. This phase corresponds to the physical design phase of traditional application design methodologies. As such it includes the creation and assessment of speci c network designs (i.e. alternate network design evaluation in Table 2) and the detailed design of the preferred network design. This evaluation is speci cally supported by most network design methodologies surveyed. The Ghosh (1992) methodology speci cally lists a number of factors to consider when comparing alternate network design methodologies while OPTINET suggests selecting the most appropriate design with respect to organisational, technical and  nancial aspects (Vittali et al., 1992). Alternative network design evaluation can be considered as including issues such as the cost of hardware and software. This issue has been included separately in order to determine how important cost is as an issue when selecting the preferred alternate network design. Hardware con guration, network management capability and legal/- regulatory requirements are issues relevant to detailed network design. However, these issues have been included separately in order to determine the importance the respondents placed on these issues in the detailed network design activity. The other main issue raised by the methodologies is select products and services, which has also been included in Table 2.

## Research method

The literature review identi es the need for a complete and integrated network design methodology. This research is the  rst step in developing such a methodology. The two research questions to be investigated are as follows.

(1) What network design issues do practitioners consider important? This question addresses the issues involved in actual design processes. This question is approached from two perspectives.

(i) What issues identi ed in Table 2 are currently important?

(ii) What additional networking issues do practitioners believe are important?

(2) What are the current tasks performed by practitioners in Australia for network design? This question analyses the tasks performed by professionals in designing networks. Each respondent was required to provide a description of his/her network design process before being in uenced by subsequent questions. This question ‘closes the loop’ by seeking to identify what is the ‘state of practice’ so that a preliminary consideration may be undertaken of whether practice addresses network design issues.

## Research instrument and validation

The exploratory nature of this research in uenced the choice of research instruments employed. A combination of a structured interview and questionnaire was employed for each respondent. The questionnaire allowed the collection of quantitative data, while the interview allowed clari cation and the collection of ‘softer’ qualitative data. The combination increased the explanatory power of this work. The questions are listed below.

(1) The number of years of network and telecommunications experience possessed by the respondent and the nature of the respondent’s current position.

(2) Information on the scope of the respondent’s current network implementation. Data was collected about the number of users, type of equipment, protocols and topologies employed on the respondent’s network(s).

The rating scale ranged from very unimportant through to very important. A middle point on the scale was indicated. No other points were identi ed, thereby allowing the respondent to use the scale in its entirety. Respondents were given the opportunity to provide additional items of their own and rate them as described above.

The questionnaire instrument was pilot tested by seven members of faculty and two industry respondents. Consideration and further discussions with regard to the design and content of the instrument were conducted immediately after each pilot test. The reviewers’ comments were considered and minor changes were made in order to improve the readability, clarity, content and structure of the questionnaire instrument. The pilot phase of instrument testing allowed the reliability of the questionnaire to be estab lished while also checking its face validity with people knowledgeable in the  eld.

## Data collection

The respondents that were approached to participate in the research study were required to have experience with network design and/or implementation. The experience could be as any or all of the following: network analyst/designer, network manager/administrator and network implementer. The 41 respondents who participated were drawn from four major organizational categories: commercial organizations (40%), consulting organizations (26%), government organizations (18%) and the military (16%). The respondents within these groups lived and worked in a number of locations, i.e. Sydney, Canberra, Melbourne, Brisbane and Perth. They had substantial networking experience in al cases. The respondents’ breakdown by job category was 21 network analysts/designers, 23 network managers/administrators and 18 network implementers. Note that the respondents could undertake more than one job category, which explains why the total exceeds 41.

## Results and discussion

The results are considered from two perspectives. The  rst subsection considers the survey results of the importance that network practitioners place on the network design issues derived from existing network design methodologies and the literature. During the interview the practitioners were asked to list the actual network design tasks that they perform. These are presented in the second subsection while the support offered by these tasks for the network design issues identi ed in the literature is considered in the third subsection.

## Design issues seen as important by industry

This question is approached from three perspectives. First, the issues included in the questionnaire that are currently important to network design are identi ed. Second, any additional issues raised by the respondents are presented. Finally, the data are examined to see whether signi cant differences can be seen between organizational categories and across the range of experience of the respondents.

## What issues are currently important?

Table 3 presents the ranked median importance ratings for each of the 22 items included in the questionnaire. Documentation was the only issue raised by the respondents that was not included in the questionnaire.

It is encouraging to note that the top issue of client/user consultation is consistent with the IS literature (Avison and Fitzgerald, 1988). It would appear that network designers are aware of the importance of determining the requirements for a network installation accurately and that this requires substantial consultation with the end-user. This is encouraging because it suggests that current designers may not fall victim to the same mistakes of poor/inadequate user requirements that plagued the introduction of new technology during the 1960s and 1970s.

The four lowest rated items warrant examination. The lowest was charge-out policy. Few respondents seemed interested in this issue, thereby perhaps re ecting their technical backgrounds and/or the lack of charging schemes for telecommunication services in their organizations.

The second lowest item was legal/regulatory requirements. This item was commented on by a number of respondents as being a given because, for example, al equipment used in Australia must be Australian Communications Authority approved or most organizations simply will not purchase it. One manager commented that ‘We take Australian Communications Authority approval as a given – without it, we do not purchase’.

The third lowest item was physical asset protection. This is a curious anomaly with the relatively high importance placed on ‘protecting the invested capita base’ mentioned by numerous respondents during the interviews. It appears that network designers and managers are concerned with ensuring their invested capital is protected from becoming obsolete too quickly, yet few seemed interested in physical asset protection (even including items such as insurance). Physical asset protection may be a given from the perspective of the network designers interviewed.

Finally, the fourth lowest item was education and training. This is a very curious result considering the importance placed on identifying user/business requirements accurately and ensuring plenty of consultation with end-users. This again may be in uenced by the technical nature of the practitioners who realize the need for determining the requirements of the network accurately, but have little interest in the less technica aspects such as training.

The 22 issues presented in Table 3 lend themselves to a logical grouping (similar to that presented in Table 2 where the researchers grouped the items into four categories based on logical reasoning and personal experience). Although not statistically signi cant $( \phi > 0 . 0 5 )$ , preliminary factor analysis appears to lend support to that subjective grouping (Table 4). Overall managemen issues are included in factors 1 and 2 with factor 2 also including the business issue of business strategic objectives. Factor 4 covers network design issues while factors 3 and 5 cover requirements and logical design issues. The factor analysis included one additional item in factor 3 that we had placed in overall management. The grouping of disaster recovery with the logical network design factors is understandable and possibly re ects the practitioners’ opinion that this is an issue to be speci - cally addressed at this point in the design process. Certainly robustness requirements will affect the logical design and the design will impact on the level of disaster recovery required. For instance a very robust design will provide for automatic recovery in most situations.

## Effect of category and experience

The responses were dissected according to industry category and experience. Differences between the various industry groups were observed for business strategic objectives and charge-out policy. The military and commercial groups rated business strategic objectives signi cantly less important than either the consulting or government groups $( \phi \ : < \ : 0 . 0 5 )$ . The greater emphasis placed on this item by the government group is rather surprising, but may re ect the greater accountability that has been expected in recent years. The consulting group rated charge-out policy more highly than either the military or commercial groups $( \phi < 0 . 0 5 )$ . The very low importance placed on it by the military group (median 0.6) re ects the nature of the business where cost recovery would generally not be expected to be a major concern.

## Activities performed by industry for network design

Table 5 presents the items given by the respondents pertaining to their network design activities. All responses were unprompted. This question was presented early in the questionnaire before the respondents’ thoughts had been in uenced by the questions or discussion reported above. It was felt necessary to aggregate the responses in order to reduce the bias of poor memory, recent experiences and error. Respondents are more likely to remember only their most recent experiences and may omit a step normally undertaken.

Approximately 10% of the respondents identi ed network design as a ‘top-down’ process. The responses have been grouped into four logical design phases. The four phases are business issues, requirements/logical design, network design and implementation/postimplementation.

The meta-set covers almost all the expected tasks. The only notable omission is a lack of emphasis on documentation. This is consistent with one of the major disadvantages of formal design methodologies reported by the respondents as being substantial documentation overhead (Aitken, 1994). The lack of emphasis on documentation may be the result of it becoming hidden within other activities or tasks but we obtained no evidence of this.

No individual respondent covered all the items in the meta-set. The respondents ranged from covering the majority of items (two respondents in the consulting group) through to a number of government respondents who barely completed 10% of the meta-set items.

A number of interesting tasks were identi ed that did not receive coverage in the literature. One of the consulting group respondents conducted gap analyses of the business requirements versus the existing network infrastructure. Many respondents mentioned eliciting business requirements. Many examined the current network infrastructure. However, no one else explicitly brought these two issues together.

A different consulting group respondent introduced a very interesting approach to prototyping in a network environment. In this particular case, the consultan possessed a demonstration area where commonly speci ed equipment was located. Equipment could be connected to the customers’ proposed speci cation before installation. This allowed the consultant to elicit user requirements better by demonstrating alternative possible solutions and reassure the customer that the proposed design was technically feasible.

The literature makes no reference to this style of prototyping. The effects of this prototyping on the quality of user requirements elicitation could provide interesting future research.

## Practitioner task support for the design issues

Only two of the overall management issues listed in Table 2 were partially addressed by the list of curren practitioner tasks (Table 5). These were  nance management, which was partially addressed by identifying business and technical requirements: budget constraints in the requirements/logical design phase and network security, which was addressed by identifying business and technical requirements: security in the requirements/logical design phase.

The lack of support for the other overall management issues provided by the practitioners’ current network design tasks may re ect the project orientation of many of the respondents. It would appear that, while the practitioners realized the importance of overall management, its tasks may not be second nature to them.

The practitioners also failed to mention the interdependence between the design of application software and the network design. A relatively simple change in the application design can affect the requirements for the network design. Similarly, the networks design (bandwidth and/or reliability) may cause a change in the application design (Low and Looi, 1997).

Other omissions were telecommunications architecture, network management capability and legal regulatory requirements. The former two items were rated in the top nine issues in Table 3. The omission of legal/regulatory requirements is not surprising since the practitioners regarded it as the second least important issue.

## Conclusions

The main purpose of this research study was to identify the theoretical basis of network design as conducted by relatively experienced practitioners. This study is the  rst of its kind in Australia and a thorough examination of the literature failed to identify research addressing this issue. Current and future needs of the network design community were identi ed through interviews and questionnaire-based data collection.

Client/User consultation was regarded as the most important issue in successful network design. This focus on customer/user needs is encouraging. Avison and Fitzgerald (1988) identi ed that one of the main reasons that IS projects failed during the 1960s and 1970s was because of poor user requirements elicitation. These results would seem to indicate that network designers are, at a minimum, conscious of the need for providing solutions to actual business/client problems.

Current network design tasks appear to address most of the issues included in the business issues, requirements/logical design and network design phases. However, they do not address any of the issue included in overall management nor do they cover issues such as telecommunications architecture and network management capability, which are not usually decided upon on an individual project basis.

The desired extent of the generalizability of the results is to data and voice networks within Australia. While the respondents were based in the capital cities, they were also designing networks for use in remote and inhospitable regions. The combination of drawing respondents from four organizational categories and from the  ve ‘major’ business centres in Australia was designed in order to increase the external validity of the results.

The framework for the generic network design methodology (NOMAD or Network Object-oriented Methodology for Analysis and Design) is presented in Table 6. It incorporates the current tasks listed by the practitioners (Table 5) as well as support for those issues derived from the literature (Table 2) that the practitioners rated as important. There is no intention to suggest that the tasks in each phase (e.g. network design) should be performed sequentially. NOMAD speci cally provides support for the overall management issues that the practitioners rated as important considerations (Table 3), but which were omitted in their list of actual tasks performed (Table 5). In order to ensure that these tasks are not overlooked they are speci cally included in the generic network design methodology. In addition, disaster recovery has been included in the requirements/logical design phase as suggested by the factor analysis (Table 4). Three new overall management tasks (plan and manage staf ng, prepare a project plan and manage project risk) have also been included as good IT management practices.

The current paper presents the  rst step in the development of a generic network design methodology that is suitable for the design and implementation of all types of data networks across a range of organizations. The intent is that this methodology will provide a network infrastructure that meets organizational and end-user needs. Given the high cost of many network infrastructure projects, it is also important that the manner in which the project is performed as well as the resultant network infrastructure maximizes end-user satisfaction. Work is currently under way in order to re ne the proposed NOMAD network design methodology and to validate it via a number of case studies, as well as examine the impact of network design critical success factors on user information satisfaction.
