# SMEs: aligning IS and the strategic context

MARGI LEVY

Information Systems Research Unit, Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, UK

PHILIP POWELL

Centre for Information Management, School of Management, University of Bath, Bath BA2 7AY, UK

PHILIP YETTON

Fujitsu Centre, Australian Graduate School of Management, University of New South Wales, Sydney 2052, Australia

Based on multiple-case research, this paper develops a model of strategic information systems (IS) investment in small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). IS investment is modelled as a function of an SME’s strategic context as de ned by its strategic focus, i.e. cost reduction versus value added and its market positioning, i.e. few versus many customers. The paper  rst investigates the ways in which IS may add value to organizations. It then outlines the use of IS in SMEs. This is followed by an analysis of competitiveness in small businesses. The paper develops an analytical model, which is termed the focus-dominance model, analyses case studies of 27  rms and reports that IS investment is strongly in uenced by an SME’s strategic context. Four cases are presented in order to illustrate the four different IS pro les identi ed. Finally, the implications for theory and practice are discussed.

## Introduction

Strategically, information systems (IS) are used in order to either lower the costs of production, coordination and transactions or to add value to the product, process or service. In general, large organizations have reaped the early gains from IS, though the well-publicized failures offer a note of caution. As IS costs fall and their use becomes more commonplace, small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) have begun to exploit the potential of the systems. Much of this exploitation mimics the early cost reduction strategies of large  rms. In addition, there is evidence that some SMEs use IS in order to add value. Yetton et al. (1994) demonstrated that, in one SME case, IS and information technology (IT) could play a critical role in innovation. Yet, the tendency, at least in manufacturing SMEs, is still to view investment in IS as a cost (Levy and Powell, 1997).

Based on multiple-case research, this paper develops a model of IS use in SMEs. The model, which is termed the focus-dominance model, proposes that an SME’s strategic context, as de ned by its strategic focus, i.e. cost reduction versus value added and its market positioning, i.e. few versus many customers, in uences its investment in IS. Twenty-seven case  rms are analysed in order to validate the model.

The paper  rst investigates the ways in which IS contribute to performance in large organizations. It then compares this with the use of IS in SMEs. This is followed by an analysis of the competitive issues facing small businesses. An analytical model combining customer dominance and strategic focus is then developed. Next, the paper outlines the method employed during the case research and develops an analytical model combining customer dominance and strategic focus. The key hypothesis underpinning the model is tested and validated. Four cases are described in order to illustrate the four different IS pro les. Finally, the implications for research and practice are discussed.

## Theoretical framework

IS have long been used for automating transaction processes as a means of cost reduction in order to improve ‘operational ef ciency’ (Ward and Grif ths, 1990). However, the information held on an organization’s systems can provide greater value than merely managing operational transactions (Zuboff, 1988). Indeed, information can be used as a resource for measuring the success of the business (Galliers, 1991). The ‘structuralist’ competitive advantage debate over the use of information in enabling  rms to market new products or provide new services contains similar themes (Porter and Millar, 1985). The need for organizations to use formal and informal information from internal and external sources in order to achieve this objective is recognized (Galliers and Swan, 1997). Earl (1996) acknowledges the efforts that organizations make in order to capture and use a wide range of information in order to develop knowledge strategies.

Many organizations now view investment in information not only as a means of cost reduction but also as a way of adding value. In order to achieve this they need an IS strategy that is an integral part of their business strategy (Galliers, 1987; Earl, 1989). However, it is clear that, for many  rms, an IS strategy is reactive to their business strategy (Galliers, 1991) and the potential advantages of using IS competitively are missed. Strategic alignment linking business strategy and technology is the key issue.

## Small and medium-sized enterprises and information systems

The introduction of IS into SMEs, like the early introduction into large  rms, has tended to be fragmented and based around operational support and transaction processing (Blili and Raymond, 1993; Foong, 1999). Typically, the interest and enthusiasm of owners’ drive IS adoption (Premkumar and Roberts, 1999). Not surprisingly, adoption is often not planned strategically. For example, small manufacturing businesses invest in systems in order to improve production processing without integrating the order processing system or developing stock control systems (Levy and Powell, 1998). In addition, while some SMEs are developing web sites, few have them linked to their transaction processing systems (Poon and Swatman, 1999).

In such businesses IT-based competitive advantage is typically accidental rather than planned. SMEs’ propensity to invest in IS incrementally rather than in a planned fashion is a problem (Hashmi and Cuddy, 1990). While this is typically an inhibitor to the effective use of IS, where incremental investment is tied in with organizational learning, it can be a means of increasing competitiveness (Yetton et al., 1994).

In contrast, some successful small businesses do plan both their growth and the systems required for managing that growth (Levy and Powell, 2000). Planning is particularly important for mature SMEs, as formal systems are likely to be required (Reid, 1999). The likelihood is that successful small businesses will have systems available in advance of their next stage of growth (Churchill and Lewis, 1983).

For most SMEs, their failure to plan the introduction and exploitation of new technology is due to management limitations. These include management having insuf cient time to spend on future business developments and management teams having little experience, skill or interest in exploiting technology (Rothwell and Beesley, 1989). The age and experience of the owner is frequently the most important factor in decisions in IS-based success (Palvia and Palvia, 1999). Limited IS adoption is also driven by a lack of trust of external IS sources, as SMEs do not have in house IS skills (Igbaria et al., 1998) and have limited  nancial resources (Foong, 1999; Premkumar and Roberts, 1999).

Investments in Internet technologies by SMEs appear to follow a similar path to their other IS invest ments. Many SMEs use e-mail for contacting some customers (Premkumar and Roberts, 1999). However, limited customer use still inhibits wholesale e-mail adoption for customer communication (Sillence et al., 1998), in which case there would be limited direct bene ts from Internet use for SMEs, although indirect bene ts such as improving communications ef ciency, presenting a modern image, access to web-based information and identifying new business opportunities are reasons given for developing such systems (Poon and Swatman, 2000). Not surprisingly, SMEs generally are as yet unwilling to develop electronic commerce systems or to change current business models (Keindl, 2000).

Overall, the evidence depicts limited use of management IS in SMEs (Premkumar and Roberts, 1999). Consistent with this, IS provide few bene ts to SMEs in terms of performance (Cragg and King, 1992). Indeed,  rms with more sophisticated IS tend to perform worse than those with more limited systems. This is primarily due to SMEs’ limited IS knowledge and skills, thereby precluding them from taking advantage of the strategic information available from the more sophisticated systems in which they have invested. In manufacturing and trade  rms, SMEs that make strategic IS investments do not obtain strategic bene ts unless the IS is seen as integral to their business strategy (King et al., 2000; Lesjak and Lynn, 2000). Finally,  rms that adopt a low-cost strategy are less likely to use IS strategically (Lesjak and Lynn, 2000).

Considering the above  ndings, IS investment in SMEs is successful when it takes one of two forms. It is either a low-cost investment for providing ef ciency savings or the enabling of a value added strategy. The former is taken by those SMEs for which IS are not cen tral to their business and where the owners’ experience of IS is limited. In this case, IS are likely to be focused on transaction processing systems. The latter is either driven by necessity, as in the case of manufacturing or due to innovative owners who are looking for business growth. IS investment is then likely to be more inno vative with a greater emphasis on management information. Such a contingent model could account for the variation in the  ndings of past research.

## Small and medium-sized enterprises and competitiveness

The above examines IS adoption as a function of a  rm’s strategy, i.e. cost reduction versus value added. A second key inhibitor or enabler of IS use in SMEs is market position. SMEs are driven primarily by customer needs. In many cases, customer power is high (Porter, 1980). Many SMEs are dependent upon a few customers who purchase large quantities of goods and services and these customers are able to in uence price (Reid and Jacobsen, 1988). In addition, the competitive environment in which SMEs operate increases the risk of failure (Storey and Cressy, 1995). Against this, investment in IS increases survival rates (Agarwal, 1998).

For example, in the automotive sector the in uence of the customer extends to ensuring that the SME can demonstrate quality of process and product (Reid and Jacobsen, 1988). This is usually achieved through the introduction of formal, computer-based performance monitoring systems. In addition, information relating to operations (i.e. orders and accounts) and design is often expected to be transmitted electronically (Levy et al., 1999). Sometimes these interactions may be extended in order to develop collaborative advantages.

Successful SMEs cultivate their customers closely by monitoring individual requirements in order to keep their loyalty (Reid and Jacobsen, 1988). This occurs at both a social level and at an operational level. Not only are major customers courted, but so are smaller ones in order to maintain loyalty. Indeed, many SMEs see the size of their order books as a surrogate for success and neglect issues of order pro tability. Advising customers of new products or services is seen as important. Consistent with this, SMEs are valued by their customers because of their ability to respond quickly to customers’ changed requirements.

IS are one of the mechanisms by which SMEs are able to respond to the market. For example, electronic data interchange (EDI) is used for production planning, while designs can be integrated through the application of computer-aided design linked to the customers’ IS (Levy et al., 1999). In general, there is a link between the innovative efforts of an SME and its competitive position (Lefebvre and Lefebvre, 1993).

## Analytical framework

The two dimensions of strategic focus, i.e. cost reduction versus value added and customer dominance, i.e. few versus many customers, de ne the strategic context, thereby creating four competitive scenarios for IS investment in SMEs. They are ef ciency, coordination, collaboration and innovation (Figure 1). This model, which is termed the focus-dominance model, provides an analytical framework for exploring the role of IS in SMEs within the strategic context of the business.

Earl’s (1989) categories of production systems, management support and customer relations can be used for aligning the IS to the business strategy in each quadrant of the focus-dominance model. Word processing systems and accounting packages are used for controlling costs with a focus on ef ciency and are categorized under management support. Customer databases fall within Earl’s (1989) customer relations category. Materials Requirements Planning (MRP) and EDI sys tems form part of Earl’s (1989) category of production, supporting planning and communications with customers. Thus, SMEs with systems restricted to word processing and accounting systems map onto the ef - ciency cell in the focus-dominance model. Those with customer databases map onto the coordination cell and those with MRP and EDI map onto the collaboration cell. In order to complete the model’s IS speci cation, the hypothesis is developed that innovative  rms deploy interactive web sites, auction platforms and other elec tronic business networking systems. In which case, the IS used in each quadrant of the focus-dominance mode would be as presented in Figure 2 and the alignment of business and IS for SMEs in each of the four cells would be as described below.

## Ef ciency quadrant

The focus of IS use in the ef ciency quadrant is for control of the business, primarily  nancial control. IS are concerned with improving the ef ciency of internal processes, such as word processing and accounting spreadsheets. IS are consequently viewed as a cost to the business. This situation typically occurs where there is high customer dominance. This may be either because the business is starting up or because of the nature of the industry.

## Coordination quadrant

In addition to the systems required to keep costs low in the ef ciency quadrant, the primary use of IS in the coordination quadrant is to maintain customer relationships. This is a function of the larger customer base. Customer IS holding basic data are used. These may be supported by a local area network in order to enable communication between different business activities. Internet access is increasingly common with external e-mail being exchanged with customers. The objective of IS is to improve the effectiveness of business processes. Again, IS are viewed as a cost to the business, but a necessity as manual systems cannot cope as customer numbers increase.

## Collaboration quadrant

The third cell is the collaboration quadrant. Here there is an increase in the sophistication of the systems used. SMEs in this quadrant need to communicate and exchange information with major customers in a costef cient manner. Hence, there is more extensive use of systems such as e-mail and EDI. Manufacturing SMEs may be part of an extranet. Often, customers are the driving force behind the introduction of new IS. SMEs begin to see the bene ts of better management information particularly when negotiating terms with their customers. IS strategy is reactive to business strategy as SMEs attempt to capture value from having appropriate information when negotiating with major customers.

## Innovation quadrant

The  nal quadrant is innovation, i.e. the integration of IS with business strategy (Yetton et al., 1994). Here IS are an integral and tightly woven part of the business strategy of the SME. Therefore, IS in uence the direction of business strategy as well as reacting to it. Strategic bene ts are realized only when IS are seen as part of the business strategy.

This examination of each cell in the focusdominance model shows how the business context in uences investment in IS. This can be formally expressed as follows.

H : The adoption of IS by SMEs is a function of the strategic context as de ned by the focus dominance model.

## Method

## Sample

Case research has a long tradition in IS as a method of providing rich, contextual data. This study is unusual in that it combines qualitative and quantitative analysis. The sample is 27 case studies of SMEs. The European Community suggests the following categories for SMEs: micro- rms with fewer than ten employees, small  rms with ten to 99 employees and medium-sized  rms with 100–499 employees (Storey, 1994). The number of employees is thought to be a more appropriate category measure because of the differences in organizationa structure that occur with size (Storey, 1994). The majority of case  rms are small. The case studies were undertaken during the period 1995–1999 in the West Midlands of the UK. This region is characterized by an emphasis on the manufacturing industry, with motor manufacturing being the prime sector. Many SMEs sup port the automotive industry, either directly through the manufacture of components or indirectly through busi ness services and ancillary industries.

The decline of motor manufacturing in the region has led to considerable government effort in the development of business parks and encouragement of internal investment, particularly for IT  rms. In addition, engineering  rms are drawn to the region because of the pool of skills. The case study  rms were not selected by industry, as earlier research has suggested that there is little difference in strategic IS adop tion by SMEs between industries (Levy and Powell, 2000).

## Data

The case material collected is based on Levy and Powell’s (2000) approach for the development of organizational information strategies for SMEs. Each case was conducted over a week during which the owner, senior management team and other employees took part in a number of semi-structured interviews lasting between 1 and 2 hours. History, background, market material and interview outcomes were collected and analysed.

The 27 SMEs used a wide range of IS. These were grouped independently by each of the authors under Earl’s (1989) three categories of production systems, management support and customer relations. All the SMEs had word processing systems and all but one had accounting packages. These are used for controlling costs with a focus on ef ciency and were categorized under management support. Eleven SMEs had customer databases, which located them within Earl’s (1989) customer relations category. Only  ve had MRP and, of these, four had EDI systems. As suggested earlier, these fall within Earl’s (1989) category of production, supporting planning and communications with their customers. Finally, three SMEs had migrated into the electronic business web-based economy. They had multiple systems in both management support and customer relations categories. Not all systems used by the SMEs are included in this classi cation framework. Those that have been excluded, such as e-mail, are discussed later.

The interview data from each SME also provided information on the number and importance of customers. A competitive forces analysis was developed for each of the SMEs, which led to an understanding of whether customer dominance was high or low. This analysis combined with data from a ‘Seven S’ analysis (Waterman et al., 1980) enabled the authors to identify those SMEs that focused on cost reduction and those that focused on value added strategies. The SMEs were classi ed independently by two of the authors who agreed on all 27 assignments to cells in the focus-dominance model.

## Analysis

A two-stage procedure was followed in order to investigate hypothesis 1. First, each SME was assigned to a cell based on its strategic context, as described in the previous section and presented in Figure 1. This classi cation is reported in Figure 3. Next, the classi- cation schema presented in Figure 2 was used to assign each SME to a cell in the focus-dominance model as a function of its IS pro le. This classi cation is presented in Figure 4.

Second, membership of a cell in the focus-dominance model by an SME was compared under the two conditions reported in Figures 3 and 4. If the strategic context is a powerful in uence on IS investment (hypothesis 1) then the assignments will be similar. Otherwise, the two patterns will be independent of each other. The test for this is a proportional equivalence test.

## Results

Hypothesis 1, i.e. that the adoption of IS by SMEs is a function of the strategic context as de ned by the focus-dominance model, is supported. A comparison of the cells to which each SME is assigned in Figures 3 and 4 found matches in 22 out of the 27 cases. A proportional equivalence test was signi cant (u = 0.81 and p < 0.01). More importantly, the strength of the relationship was high with 22 out of 27 SMEs aligning their core IS strategy to their strategic context.

## Discussion

The  ndings strongly support the hypothesis that IS investment is a function of an SME’s strategic context. There are signi cant differences between the IS pro les for the SMEs in each of the four cells in the focusdominance model. For an SME that started in the ef - ciency cell, a shift to the coordination cell as the number of customers increases is associated with the develop ment of customer databases, whereas a shift to the collaboration cell is associated with the adoption of EDI and MRP in order to support an increased level of customer interaction. ## Ef ciency case

Garden Health Care is a private health care provider for the elderly. It provides a home with round-theclock care for frail people. In addition, Garden Health Care supports people living in an integrated sheltered housing development. There is a management team of  ve, each of whom is responsible for a speci c activity in the home and there are 60 other staff. In common with many other health services providers, there is a high turnover among care workers.

There are three main business processes: the delivery of health care, facilities management and  nancial management. The delivery of health care is organization of the day-to-day care of clients. This is managed by the matron whose duties include staff development and training, optimizing bed use and monitoring the quality of care. Data on clients and staff are maintained manually, as are cost information, budgets and income forecasts. Financial information is entered onto the computer by the bookkeeper.

Second, facilities management involves managing catering requirements, plant, gardening and transport. Rooms are also hired out to other organizations such as Social Services. The  nal business process is  nancial management. The managing director/owner sets the annual budget with the management team. Budget performance is reviewed weekly and monthly using information from the managers. The managing director closely monitors and keeps tight control of all expenditure and variances.

The owner’s objectives are to provide quality care and allow clients to maintain their dignity, to maximize  nancial return from the business and to retain control. The key success factors are high bed occupancy to keep costs down and quality of care. The owner is considering expanding the facilities, either on the current site or at other locations.

The market is competitive with a number of other similar services in the vicinity and there are low barriers to entry. Customer power, in the form of the local Social Services Department, which pays for 85% of clients, is high. The primary customer demands quality care and moderate pricing.

The  rm’s major IS contribute to the focus on improving ef ciency. These are an off-the-shelf accounting system for payroll and ledger activities, an invoicing system integrated into the accounting system and spreadsheets used for managing various budgets. The focus of these systems is,  rst, tight control of the  nances as a means of monitoring ef ciency and, second, good communication between management. The management style is formal, but the managing director tours the facilities thereby keeping in touch with day-to-day activities.

## Coordination case

Training Brokerage Co. started in 1989 offering project management consultancy and training, mainly in Hong Kong and East Asia. The  rm has since diversi ed into management training, which was led by a demand from overseas  rms for UK management quali cations. Training Brokerage Co. coordinates and administers courses on behalf of two UK universities in severa Asian regions. In 1997 they had a turnover of 1 million euros and employed ten people in the UK. Their espoused business strategy is to be the biggest provider of in-house management training in the UK. They are also planning to reduce their dependence on the two universities by developing relationships with others.

The  rm has a large number of customers, which are mainly based outside the UK. It views the students or end-users as its customers rather than the two universities. Therefore, customer databases and communication technologies are important to the business. Customer care has been identi ed as a major objective for Training Brokerage Co. This manifests itself in terms of improved marketing as well as support for existing customers undertaking distance-learning courses.

Training Brokerage Co. is proactive towards IS, employing a manager with speci c responsibilities for the systems. The system is networked so that all staff can access the three main databases: marketing, students and corporate clients. The system was developed by their IT personnel using Lotus Notes. E-mail is used to communicate with overseas customers. The  rm has a separate PC-based accounting system while personnel and payroll operations are outsourced. The  rm prides itself on being informal and responsive, with all staff able to undertake all business activities. While the systems are designed for aiding the coordination of business activities, the informality and lack of control mechanisms lead to incomplete data recording and lack of follow-up.

## Collaboration case

Clutch Assembly Co. manufactures tube-based products for the automotive industry. The  rm is family owned with 285 employees and had a turnover of 17 million euros in 1997. Clutch Assembly Co. has grown from a Victorian-era business in tube and wire bending and is now a  rst tier supplier to two major motor manufacturers and a second tier supplier to  ve others.

In the late 1980s the  rm recognized that it was losing competitiveness through creeping inef ciencies and poor management information. The  rm was located over several city centre sites that made it dif - cult to run ef ciently. They took two major decisions as a result of an audit report:  rst, to bring in  nance and production directors in order to improve ef ciency and increase turnover and, second, to relocate to the suburbs in order to enable the  rm to be on one site.

The audit report also highlighted the need for better management information, in particular accounting information, which at that stage was available only after a time lag of several months. The  rm also lacked an effective stock control system. Not only were there pressures to improve internal management IS but, as with other  rms, the major motor manufacturers were insisting on a quality IS that provided production and quality performance measures.

Clutch Assembly Co. has since introduced both accounting and production systems, including MRP. It has EDI links with one major manufacturer for both orders and computer-aided design (CAD). There is currently no link to the MRP system. However, the  rm has introduced these systems in a reactive manner in order to address individual needs. There has been no attempt to develop an IS strategy for looking at the integration of systems. The quality IS is spreadsheet based and not integrated with the production systems. The quality system is a customer requirement in order to remain a preferred supplier. Information has to be re-entered manually onto spreadsheets.

Clutch Assembly Co. is dependent upon its preferred supplier status for its pro tability. The customer demands more integration of processes from design to  nal output. There is a need to improve integration in order to provide better management information in order to ensure that the relationship between Clutch Assembly Co. and its customers provides mutual bene ts.

## Innovation case

Corporate Gift Co. designs and manufactures corporate gifts for a wide range of internationally known  rms. The company also retails a range of gift items such as paperweights. The  rm is based in a converted rural farm and most of its manufacturing is outsourced to South East Asia. IS have enabled the key stakeholders to live and work in a location of their choice and also to manage the relationship with their customers and suppliers effectively.

The business strategy is tightly integrated with the IS strategy and the  rm has invested heavily in IS using Apple Macintosh systems. These systems were initially selected because of their strength in the core business competence of design, but all management systems have been built on this base. Corporate Gift Co. has a policy of open access to information (apart from accounts) to all employees. Management information is driven through the IT system with all staff understanding the need for accuracy.

Only IT-literate staff are employed. The  rm has a web site and is developing its electronic business strategy in order to process customer orders on-line. Most communication with suppliers is already elec tronic. The intention is to integrate order processing with the back of ce transaction processing system. This is seen as a key part of the business strategy in orde to grow the business through identifying new markets. As a response to this change in strategy Corporate Gif Co. also intends to bring some of its manufacturing back in-house The ability to link designs to orders and, thus, to production electronically is seen as a means of keeping costs down and quality up.

## Non-strategic information systems

The development of the focus-dominance model above is concerned with the strategic drivers of an SME’s investment in IS. Not all IS are strategic. To be strategic, the investment must be valuable, unique and dif cult for a competitor to imitate (Feeny and Ives, 1990). For example, Training Brokerage Co.’s use of e-mail is simply a tactical investment. It increases  exibility and is of lower cost than faxes but it does not change how Training Brokerage Co. competes and is easily copied by a competitor. Essentially, their customers are overseas. Therefore, e-mail is the easiest way of contacting them. Taken together, the systems in Training Brokerage Co. are relatively simple accounting, word processing and customer databases.

Similarly, a number of other  rms in the coordina tion cell have implemented local area networks in orde to access their customer databases. In themselves these local area networks are not strategic IS investments. They are complementary IS investments to the strategic investment in customer databases. Intranets and a number of other IS investments also tend to fal into this category. Importantly, higher IS investmen and investment in more complex IS are of themselves not strategic. Instead, only IS investments which are critically contingent on and aligned to the busines strategy should be categorized as strategic IS. Research has tended to treat higher and more complex IS invest ment as a surrogate for strategic IS investment.

## Implications for research

The key  nding from this research and illustrated by the four cases described above is that, in general, SMEs align their IS with their strategic context as de ned by the focus-dominance model (Figure 2). In addition, the analysis distinguishes between strategic IS, which need to be aligned to the strategic context and other IS, such as e-mail, which are adopted for tactical rather than strategic reasons. Tactical IS do not need to be aligned to the strategic context.

The  ve non-aligned SMEs raise questions about the dynamics of alignment. Do SMEs reposition themselves in the focus-dominance model and then realign their IS or do they adopt the required IS before repositioning? Burn (1996, 1997) described how, in Hong Kong, some SMEs adopt IS before strategic repositioning while others align their IS after strategic positioning. Both dynamics appear to be viable.

Three of the  ve non-aligned SMEs have IS which are inadequate for supporting their strategic position. Energy Saving Co. and Seven Stars Printers are located in the coordination cell in the focus-dominance model (see Figure 3), but have IS consistent with the ef - ciency cell (see Figure 4) while Car Sign Design Co is located in the collaboration cell (see Figure 3), but also has IS consistent with the ef ciency cell (see Figure 4). All three would bene t from developing IS for supporting their strategic positioning.

In contrast, Land ll Gas Co. and Landrover Repair Co have developed and implemented IS contingent on their relocation to the coordination cell (see Figure 4) before moving into that strategic context from their current location in the ef ciency cell (see Figure 3). Similarly, others, such as Car Tubes Co., which has implemented CAD as a  rst step in its espoused strategic intent of moving into the collaboration cell to become a preferred supplier to one of the large automobile assemblers, are developing their IS in order to support a strategic repositioning. For example, discussions on future plans for the strategic use of IS suggest that the University Regional Arts Centre and Warwick Training Brokerage are building the IS infrastructures and competencies that are needed in order to reposition themselves in the innovation cell.

The result reported here also informs the existing record on SME investment in IS and organizational performance. Premkumar and Roberts (1999) reported limited use of management IS. This is supported in this research if we interpret those SMEs in the ef - ciency and coordination quadrants which had accounting systems and, in some cases, customer databases as having limited management IS. In this case, 19 out of the 27 SMEs in the sample had limited management IS. In contrast, the SMEs in the collaboration and innovation cells have developed complex IS with which to manage their businesses. For example, advanced performance measurement systems are already in operation in Car Spring Co., Light Assembly Co. and Clutch Assembly Co., which are all in the collaboration cell (see Figures 3 and 4).

In addition, given that the performance of IS investment is contingent on aligning IS and the strategic context, few main effect bene ts would be expected, as reported by Cragg and King (1992). Further, the cost bene ts for low investments in the ef ciency cell are easier to realize than the growth bene ts contingent on the higher cost and more complex IS required for coordination and collaboration. In which case, as Cragg and King (1992) reported,  rms with more sophisticated IS will tend to perform worse than these with more lim ited systems. However, the explanation here is not inad equate management competencies but the contingent nature of IS alignment bene ts and the differential dif- culty of realizing the IS bene ts in different cells.

Finally, the age and experience of the owner a business is frequently the most important factor in IS-based success (Palvia and Palvia, 1999). In order to integrate that  nding with the results reported here simply requires that business dynamism is also a function of age and experience. In this case, the successful owner both leads the strategic repositioning and adopts the appropriate IS as a joint event. Such entrepreneurs are unlikely to be constrained by either a lack of trust in external IS resources (Igbaria et al., 1998) or limited  nancial resources (Foong, 1999; Premkumar and Roberts, 1999).

Taken together, the above arguments show that the major  nding reported here of focused, contingent IS investment by SMEs can be interpreted as consisten with the generally pessimistic literature in this area, such as Cragg and King (1992). However, in contrast to those researchers, we conclude that managers of SMEs do align their IS and strategic contexts with the expectation of collecting signi cant bene ts. Of course, it is possible that the situation changed during the 1990s and a replication of Cragg and King’s (1992) study would now report different  ndings. Against thi explanation, Premkumar and Roberts (1999) recently reported limited IS investment by SMEs. The contingent model developed here combined with the frequency of SMEs in each cell accounts for their  nding. The ef ciency and collaboration cells both specify limited IS investment relative to the collaboration and innovation cells and occur more frequently.

## Implications for managers

This research has developed a powerful analytical model with which managers can frame their IS investment decisions. It both maps the  t between strategic IS investment and the SME’s strategic context and illustrates with the four cases above the realities of the choices to be made. In this section, the strengths and weaknesses of the options in each cell within the focusdominance model are examined in order to help inform managers’ IS strategic choices further.

The ef ciency quadrant’s strength is improved control through simple IS, while its main weaknesses are the internal and operational focuses rather than a customer focus. The  rm’s IS goal is to realize ISbased cost bene ts. The coordination quadrant, which still has a cost focus, uses IS in order to improve communication and customer care, thereby leading to improved operational effectiveness. The weaknesses here are limited integration with business strategy and the need for better IS staff skills in order to work with more complex systems. The primary goal is improved customer relationships. These strengths and weakness are summarized in Figure 5.

The collaboration quadrant focuses on value added IS bene ts. Business strategy is the driver here. The focus is on external relations with major customers. The critical assumption is that better information gives an SME the opportunity for adding value for the customer through collaboration. This emphasis on the joint creation of value added limits customer demand for cost reduction. One major issue is the cost of the systems. This may lead to limited  exibility, as resource constraints limit upgrades and replacement. The other major issue is whether a major customer is tempted to use its power in order to maximize its own shortterm pro ts rather than jointly create value for the partnership (Reid and Jacobsen, 1988; Webster, 1995; Chat eld and Yetton, 2000).

The innovation quadrant demonstrates the strengths of integrated IS and business strategy, which includes both an internal and external perspective to the business. The owner is aware of IS-based opportunities for changes in business processes. The weaknesses seen here are the potential cost to the business of the IS investment, its relationship to the business requirements and the need for  exibility in order to manage continual change.

## Conclusions

The existing research literature on SME investment in IS reports limited investment and weak or negative performance outcomes. In contrast, the  nding reported here show that SMEs’ investment is strongly in uenced by their strategic context. A contingent model of IS investment is developed in which invest ment is a function of the SME’s strategy (cost reduction versus value added) and its market position (few versus many customers). SME behaviour is found to match the model. The discussion shows how SME strategic, contingent behaviour is consistent with the typical null main effect  ndings in the literature. It also highlights the implications of the  ndings for managers.
