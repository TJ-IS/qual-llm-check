# After the goldrush: deconstructing the myths of the dot.com market

DEBRA HOWCROFT

Information Systems Research Centre, University of Salford, Salford M5 4WT, UK

Many would claim that the development of electronic commerce is reshaping almost all industries, even to the extent that we are undergoing a paradigm shift. This enthusiasm represents a speculative bubble, what economists would describe as ‘mania’, as we witness increasing numbers of  rms embracing the need for an on-line presence, despite the absence of both pro ts and sales. The recent turn of events, which is referred to as the ‘dot.com implosion’, indicates that the rapid pro t growth that was initially projected is unlikely to be achieved by many. As the technology markets have crashed along with a number of high-pro le dot.com collapses, numerous investors have almost turned off from funding this sector. This should come as little surprise. What is of interest is why myriad investors were drawn into  nancially supporting these start-ups in the  rst instance. This paper aims to focus on the concept of business-to-consumer commerce and uses mythology for providing some explanation as to why so many investors were lured into participating in the dot.com share bubble.

## Introduction

The Internet has been transformed into one of the most publicized industries of the twentieth century as numerous pundits, academics and research  rms enthuse about the ways in which it has transformed the lives of many. The rhetoric is particularly  orid vis-à-vis business and the development of electronic commerce (e-commerce), which is often claimed to be reshaping almost all industries, almost to the extent that we are witnessing a paradigm shift (Wigand, 1997). Here it is argued that this perspective, which assumes that change is taking place on a fundamental level, is not based on fact, but re ects a speculative bubble, one that has been created by numerous investment analysts and bankers who are keen to build upon rampant speculation about high-technology stocks. Without doubt the development of the Internet and telecommunications industries represents signi cant potential for change, yet it seems that the response of the stock markets in the late 1990s regarding the future rate of return on Internet stocks was based on ‘irrational exuberance’ rather than sound economics (Freeman and Louca, 2001).

In the not too distant past venture capitalists vied with one another for lending money to potential Internet entrepreneurs. This excitement led to an explosion of World Wide Web (WWW) start-ups as it was suggested that we were on the brink of a new economy. Yet, the recent turn of events, which is referred to as the ‘dot.com implosion’, indicates that the rapid pro t growth that was initially projected is unlikely to be achieved by many. In spring 2000 the technology markets crashed along with a number of high-pro le dot.com collapses (such as Boo, ClickMango, Boxman and Etoys, to name but a few). As a consequence of these cascading failures, there has been a signi cant change in the funding climate with countless investors raising their concerns about the potential pro tability of such ventures to the extent they have almost turned off from funding the dot.com sector. This should warrant little surprise. What is of interest is why so many investors were drawn into (heavily)  nancing these start-ups in the  rst instance.

The links between the assumptions that the Internet is driving e-commerce and conversely that e-commerce is driving the use of the Internet are all underpinned by an inherent determinism. This determinism is based upon the premise that technology drives societal change and also that the population is ready to embrace a technological platform that provides a new channel for commercial delivery. Realizing the potential of e-commerce is likely to be less dependent upon the ability to source all technological elements and more upon proactive marketing strategies by  rms that employ e-commerce (Hawkins et al., 2000) since the evolutionary trajectory of the business-to-consumer paradigm is largely driven by commercial rather than technological criteria. Yet often those eager to fund the dot.com start-ups base their judgement on the misguided understanding that technology per se delivers bene ts, including wealth creation. This all takes place within the arena of electronic markets, which is a highly risky environment where employees face strong pressures to perform and succeed (Barrett and Walsham, 1999; Scott, 2000). In the context of what appears to be a booming market, it is understandable that the banking analysts played to their audience, despite their scepticism of the inherently risky, overpriced evaluations.

Without doubt, the rise and fall of the dot.com market represents a remarkable  nancial story, with the investment bankers and brokers who helped create the frenzy emerging as clear winners. The aim of this paper is to try and shed light on why this situation arose, whereby numerous investors and venture capitalists invested large sums of money in young, inexperienced companies only to lose it as reality set in. This will be done via the deconstruction of a number of myths, which are used for explaining some of the rhetoric that adorns this phenomenon. The myths are as follows: the myth of the new economy, the myth of success, the myth of the entrepreneurial geek, the myth of the level playing  eld, the myth of innovation, the myth of the virtual and the myth of the e-consumer pro le. Finally, some conclusions are drawn.

## Myths

This section contains the deconstruction of seven highpro le myths about e-commerce in order to understand some of the rhetoric that  ourishes around this phenomenon. The use of myths in information systems (IS) research is not new and has previously been applied in the IS  eld by, amongst others, Hirschheim and Newman (1991) and Grover and Ramanla (1999). According to Bolman and Deal (1984) myths serve a number of functions, including the provision of explanations and the maintenance of solidarity and cohesion. The function of mythology that is of prime concern here is the creation and perpetuation of myths for the purpose of legitimation and the mediation of contradictions. Here, we see mythology as an important vehicle for establishing a number of dubious justi- cations for participating in the dot.com share market.

## The myth of the new economy

This myth is used for announcing the break with traditional modes of production and the move to the new digital economy that is associated with the rise of information and communication technologies (ICTs), in particular the Internet and e-commerce. This is concerned with the claim that we are moving to a ‘new economy’, which is one that represents a fundamental shift from that of the industrial society to that of post-Fordism/post-modernism. This was epitomized by the largest ever merger in January 2000 when the old economy giant Time Warner was assimilated with the new economy company America OnLine (Wilsdon and Miller, 2001).

Many prophesize that the shift of production and consumption from material objects towards information and services (such as software, telecommunications, entertainment, the Internet, etc.), which is described as the weightless economy (Quah, 1997), will dramatically alter the structure of the world economy. Yet whilst there are increasing numbers of globalized and weightless activities taking place, these do not represent any signi cant threat (Cameron, 1998) and it remains to be seen whether technological advancements will bring about social, cultural and political changes (Freeman and Louca, 2001).

Indeed, with regard to the dot.com rise and demise, we see that cycles of boom, which peak in a speculative frenzy, followed by a slump are as old as capitalism itself. In the words of the liberal American economist Galbraith (1908)

There can be few  elds of human endeavor in which history counts for so little as in the world of  nance. Past experience, to the extent that it is part of memory at all, is dismissed as the primitive refuge of those who do not have the insight to appreciate the incredible wonders of the present (p. 24).

Proclamations to the effect that we are entering into a new age should be examined critically rather than simply accepted without question. Webster (2000) argued that, although change is taking place at rapid speed, this variance is ‘for the most part a matter of the continuity, consolidation and extension of established relations’ (p. 70). He went on to say that history matters and, indeed, repeats itself. During periods of speculative bubbles, one of which is currently being witnessed by the dot.com revolution, but has historically been repeated in the case of the South Sea Bubble, the railway mania of the 1840s and the Wall Street crash (to name but a few), many people believe that a new age has arrived whereby instantaneous wealth creation is accessible by all. (The names of these ‘ages’ come and go and, thus, we have seen the steam age, the space age and we are now in the midst of the information age or digital age: the naming conventions depend upon who is doing the telling, but they all imply a break with the past (cf. Bell, 1973).)

## Deconstructing the myths of the dot.com market

As Webster (2000) pointed out, if we are to appreciate and understand current trends, then capitalism needs to be contextualized. Indeed, there are many super cial resemblances between the bubbles of the 1990s and the 1920s when we previously witnessed over-optimism about new technology (the car and the fridge) (Ferguson, 2001).

Yet during the dot.com boom it seemed that few investment analysts were evaluating these companies in terms of traditional market criteria and conditions based on rational assessments, opting instead for speculation. As the dot.com market hits a slump many of these icons of the new economy are now being evaluated in old economy terms, namely the need for generating a positive cash  ow.

## The myth of success

This myth questions our conventional understanding of both success and failure. Here it is argued that the mainstays of success in a traditional environment, which are fundamentally based on pro t making, are conveniently ignored or swept away in the virtual market-place. Stock market analysts prefer to replace this with empty promises about potential success on a massive scale. And so we see Amazon.com, which is probably the best-known example of a ‘successful’ virtual shop counter and an embodiment of the virtues of a dot.com company, still failing to report any pro ts (although certain elements of their business are achieving pro ts, this is not widespread across the whole company) (Sauer and Burton, 1999). Yet time is limited for such companies that have previously been able to rely on funding from investors, as the ‘dot.com implosion’ means that access to venture capital is far less likely to materialize and that they will now have to consider replenishing  nance through their own business operations.

Amidst the enthusiasm for dot.com stocks in the growing market, some basic rules of the old economy regarding the success and failure of enterprises were suitably forgotten. Success was an outright assumption. This ignored some fundamentals, such as the one that, in the old economy, a large proportion of all new businesses fail within the  rst few years. This, combined with the fact that new Internet companies were largely run by the young and inexperienced operating amongst unknown terrain meant that the failure rate for dot.coms was inevitably going to be higher than average. The paradox of the business-toconsumer market is that many of these companies only exist because of money raised on the capital markets a year or more before. However, it is unlikely that this money would have ever been given had investors known then what they know now about the viability of the business-to-consumer model. But just as these new companies are upheld as representing endless possibilities for success, a type of success that is accessible to all, we witness that this success is often shortlived. Those companies that arise spectacularly overnight then undergo what amounts to a public cremation under a national spotlight when they later fail.

## The myth of the entrepreneurial geek

This myth suggests that much of e-commerce activity minimizes links with physical infrastructures, thereby enabling technologically savvy individuals to enter new and exciting markets and, thus, secure considerable wealth creation in a short time-span. This concept has a strong anti-establishment  avour and is anchored in much of the culture that romanticized the hacker as a counter-cultural hero in the early 1990s (Ross, 1990). The attraction of bringing commercial activity into the electronic arena is witnessed by the enormous entry wave and rapid growth rate of the virtual trader market, yet the economic signi cance of this type of e-commerce has been at an incredibly modest level and business-to-business transactions still make up the largest proportion of e-commerce activity (Hawkins et al., 2000). No doubt these new start-up companies are important innovators and explorers, but their economic signi cance has yet to be seen.

Much of the dot.com publicity focuses on ‘rags to riches’ tales, thereby engendering feelings that largescale success is not only possible, but there for the taking (particularly for the young and technologically competent). The notion that the Internet provides new opportunities for individuals to enter the economy and prosper is somewhat ironic given the concentration and increase of monopolistic corporate power (Freeman and Louca, 2001). Living in a free market society the rhetoric of e-commerce success has mass appeal and is particularly pervasive.

Another element of electronic business folklore is that of the  rst mover advantage, a concept that encourages budding entrepreneurs to climb aboard the dot.com bandwagon sooner rather than later. Yet it remains highly questionable as to whether  rst mover experience will sustain a reasonable market position. As history has revealed, many second movers are able to capitalize on the mistakes of the innovators (cf., for example, technology innovation in Japan). To date it seems unclear as to how many of these highly publicized pioneer traders are acquiring sustainable  rst mover advantages that will represent any long-term signi cance.

For the newly emerging virtual trader  rst impressions reveal that it appears easy to sell a range of goods directly to the consumer, regardless of their ‘shop front’. This includes new economy goods that can be digitized (such as music) or old economy goods that can be purchased without the need to ‘look and feel’ (such as books or wine). The entrepreneur must surely feel encouraged by the suggestion that the eradication of the majority of processes that intervene between buyers and sellers in the conventional market-place will be removed, thus enabling direct (and presumably less costly) connections. Some sources even maintain that the need for human agents will disappear completely (Hess and Kemerer, 1994; Schmitz, 2000). Emerging evidence suggests otherwise as we arrive at the increasing realization that the situation is far more complex than was initially assumed, with variations from industry to industry (Crowston and Wigand, 1999; Grover and Ramanlal, 1999; Schmitz, 2000). For example, disintermediation is far more likely to occur within the travel industry (Lewis and Talalayevsky, 1997) than within the real estate industry, which relies far more heavily on human intervention (Crowston et al., 2001). This focus on ecommerce ‘solutions’ as a substitute for existing forms of business intermediation both over-emphasizes and mis-speci es the development trajectory of virtual trading (Hawkins et al., 2000). The notion that advertising and selling a product on-line will result in a large gain in ef ciency through disintermediation is, quite simply, a fallacy (Pisanias and Willcocks, 1999). It appears unlikely that intermediaries would exist in the conventional market-place if they were super uous and failed to provide some degree of added value. With disintermediation it would seem prudent to unravel the technological capability for supporting the process from the commercial motivation for doing so, rather than simply equating the two together.

Indeed, early indicators show that the shift of the locus of control from the producer to consumers heightens further competition in the electronic market. Hence, the tradition of customer loyalty, which is crucial for many small, specialist businesses and arguably one of their major differentiators (Reid and Jacobsen, 1998), now becomes largely irrelevant. Users are able to search the WWW for alternative suppliers, cheaper products and quicker delivery quickly and easily, which is a bonus for the consumer, but not necessarily for the virtual trader. Consequently, for many of the new Internet start-up companies they end up selling old economy goods to consumers that expect very low prices (since they can use search engines for looking for the best deal) yet they still need to invest in many of the conventional processes such as marketing and delivery.

The assumption that ICTs will produce more open and dynamic market structures and lower barriers to entry seems misguided since it ignores the costs involved in creating and maintaining business relationships, the problems of establishing trust, the accommodating of different operational routines and the establishing of interpersonal relationships that can help resolve dif culties and misunderstandings (Hawkins et al., 2000). Network relationships matter and  rms value stable supplier relationships. It is indeed possible that virtual industry structures could create more rather than fewer barriers to exit and entry, as technological and organizational compatibility factors rival price and competency in partner selection. This adds a further degree of complication rather than facilitating simpli- cation. The most signi cant e-commerce applications to date have been led by major traders who know speci c markets well, wield considerable powerful within them and are able to mould them to re ect market dynamics. Given that the earliest corporate promoters of e-commerce were  rms already involved with electronic data interchange (EDI) and value added network (VAN) implementations, these ‘early adopters’ are already in a strong position for employing the new ICTs to their advantage in order to help strengthen their existing base. This history coupled with their visible market presence and high levels of con dence in their brands means that large established traders are frequently in commanding positions for leading e-commerce developments (Hawkins et al., 2000).

The deconstruction of the myth of the entrepreneurial geek presented here is not intended to imply that there are no opportunities for such people within cyberspace, but enthusiasm for technology often results in ‘bandwagon effects’ that obscure the commercial realities. The point is to highlight the problems of equating the availability of these technological opportunities with a fundamental change in the relationship between actors in the market-place, whether they be established or new.

## The myth of the level playing  eld

This myth suggests that the move to the virtual marketplace enables small  rms to compete on the same terms as larger players as the consumer experience shifts to cyberspace. Yet it must be remembered that the scalability and  exibility of the Internet yields similar advantages for both large  rms and small niche players and so there is little evidence that smaller or more specialized  rms can reap disproportionately large cost savings when adopting an e-commerce approach.

Given that small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) comprise a large proportion of businesses, certainly in the UK, the hyperbole and misrepresentation in the trade and policy literature that suggests huge growth projections (regardless of industry type) in the e-commerce arena is particularly appealing. However, this is very much at odds with research on technological innovations and the possible gains for SMEs. A multiple case research study conducted by Levy et al. (1998) highlighted their limited time for indulging in future business development, including the exploitation of technology. As a consequence, there is a reluctance among SMEs for spending on technology beyond the ‘bare minimum’ that is necessary for basic administration and transaction processing, so that few SMEs have either the motivation or the capacity for introducing systems that would support IS-based innovation.

Sauer and Burton (1999) argued that, based on both empirical and theoretical evidence, ‘online success is neither certain nor easy for retailers’ (p. 388) and they provided some statistics in order to illustrate the point. Whilst studies consistently show that on-line purchasing is on the increase, this is generally from a low base, which amounts to only a small proportion of the population. The Internet shopping study conducted by Ernst & Young reported that the number of US households that shopped on-line had only increased from 7% in 1998 to 10% in 1999. Another 1% of households said they were ‘very interested’ in shopping on-line in the future, whilst 7% said they were ‘somewhat interested’. Despite this limited interest on the part of consumers, we hear a different story from retailers. In sharp contrast we note that the number of retailers selling on-line or intending to sell on-line more than doubled in the same period, from 34 to 76%. This huge gap in consumer interest and retailer provision suggests that the competition for a small number of customers is likely to intensify as opposed to providing a retailing nirvana whereby traditional shopkeepers transform into an international supplier overnight.

This history does not bode well for SMEs. Indeed, the extent of the costs of gaining a signi cant WWW presence raises a number of issues for them, since most of the former do not have strong technological abilities in-house and, thus, will be unable to sustain participation. As noted, there is more to ‘a successful web site than a corporate logo on a home page’ (Sauer and Burton, 1999, p. 296), since it also involves maintenance (a huge resource in terms of traditional IS expenditure), could enhance competition by exposure to the global market-place and, more than likely, will have a considerable impact on their existing business. Most ICT investments are long term and the cost of maintenance and upgrades often far outweigh the initial outlay costs. This is particularly signi cant for smaller enterprises where investment costs in new technology represent a proportionately higher segment of their corporate revenue than those costs for larger organizations. There is little evidence to suggest that SMEs can reap disproportionately large cost savings from ecommerce relative to their larger rivals since, as their business grows, the costs of maintaining and integrating e-commerce sites also scales upwards. The problem that many SMEs now face is that, as increasing numbers of large  rms embrace ecommerce, smaller  rms are compelled to follow suit in order to be able to do business with them, yet the risks incurred are far greater for small  rms.

Consider the following example of the ways in which small and large players operate within the electronic market-place. The UK’s top supermarket chain Tesco exercised a considerable degree of caution before their of cial launch in May 2000 (Barrow, 2000). They spent 5 years testing their on-line delivery service amongst a cluster of ten different shops before they were willing to venture nationwide. This is not to presume that on-line trading will thus become a guaranteed success at Tesco.com; it is just to note their heed in ensuring their business was cost-effective before launching themselves on a grander scale. Compare the logic and experience of this large, established trader with new companies who had no trading presence before the launch of their WWW site. For example, the US business-to-consumer venture Urbanfetch.com delivers a range of products, from sandwiches to CDs, in the London area within the hour. With this there is neither a minimum order level nor a delivery charge. They employ a minimum of 40 delivery people at any one time, yet they only receive a few hundred orders per day, each averaging around £25 (Goodley, 2000). It will be interesting to note the differences (and success) between these two ventures over time.

This myth of the level playing  eld is not intended to deny that Internet commerce could offer some bene t to its adopters, but this depends on a number of preconditions, notably customer participation (Poon, 2000), which is currently less active than initially assumed. The presumption that it is possible to bring almost any commercial activity into the electronic environment raises questions about the appropriateness of this and the circumstances in which it takes place. There are considerable risks entailed in adopting (or failing to adopt) this kind of new environment for different kinds of commercial enterprises, both large and small. It seems that progression towards an e-commerce trading environment is more likely to be evolutionary and incremental as opposed to revolutionary.

## The myth of innovation

This myth is used for illustrating the way in which the Internet was promoted as a technological innovation and how this helped legitimize the euphoria surrounding high-technology shares.

Graham (1999) noted the distinction between the radically new and the merely novel in his discussion of the Internet as a technological innovation. The former relates to innovations that are transformatory in terms of their impact on the character of personal and social life, an example of which would be railways. The latter, whilst being more dif cult to de ne, relates to adaptations and improvements of original inventions, many of which are welcomed, such as the artefacts that are marketed as ‘new models’ in car manufacturing: the merely novel. And so here the question of what is radically new about the increasing automation of transactions conducted over the Internet is asked.

Many commentators have noted that e-commerce is currently viewed as representing a new way of doing business that will revolutionize retailing to the extent that little will be left of the ‘High Street’ (De Kare-Silver, 1998). Yet for the last 30 years or so various aspects of commerce have been conducted with the aid of electronic means, with a large majority of  nancial transactions (certainly in industrialized countries) involving some aspect of ICT. Indeed, e-commerce has been an established part of business vocabulary since the early 1970s,  rst originating with the EDI and VAN concepts in the early 1970s. Twenty years on e-commerce was fairly well established, but was primarily centred on business-to-business and business-to-government transactions in the contexts of supply chain and logistical activities and, thus, received limited public attention. Yet within a decade the vision of e-commerce had altered dramatically, having attained the status of a ubiquitous electronic marketplace that was relevant and, in some instances, essential to all manner of commercial trading activity. Hawkins et al. (2000) argued that the concept of ecommerce encompasses a whole business philosophy that states not just that commerce can be conducted electronically, but that it ought to be conducted in this manner. It has been suggested that those who choose to ignore this new phenomenon are at risk of becoming ‘dinosaurs’, as business becomes increasingly virtual:

The perspectives on electronic commerce presented and the implications to be drawn here are the realisation that the world of economics is essentially borderless, as well as boundaryless, and those who do not see this will create more dif culties for themselves and their organizations than otherwise (Wigand, 1997, p. 14).

Miller and Slater (2000) highlighted the ‘discrepancy between promise and practice’ in their ethnographic study of Internet culture and consumption in Trinidad, whereby there exists an economic inevitability about e-commerce, yet practical experience shows the dif - culties of trying to make a living out of it.

However, technological innovation is not simply about the process of production, since consumption is an equally essential component of the innovation process. Here, we witness a blockage in the innovation process since consumption is limited (Silverstone and Haddon, 1996). For example, when considering Napster, the on-line music community that enables downloading of music via the Internet, we see some of the dif culties that Internet entrepreneurs may face in that it will take a long time to wean consumers away from their CD collections (Leadbetter and Willis, 2001). Consumers as well as producers will have to adapt and innovate. One study alluded to the complexity of opening up the Internet to increasing numbers of consumers by noting that individuals with higher levels of Internet usage are far more likely to make a purchase on-line (Citrin et al., 2000). The  ndings of Kraut et al. (1999) regarding Internet consumption in the home are also of interest here since they are largely at odds with the suggestion that businessto-consumer e-commerce is driving Internet use in households. Their longitudinal study revealed that people primarily use the Internet for interpersonal communication as opposed to the information, entertainment and commercial perspectives that have largely dominated policy debates. If their forecasts for future usage come to fruition, the eventual social consequences of the Internet may well be more important in the domains of work, school and family interactions than in the commercial domains that are so often emphasized.

## The myth of the virtual

The myth of the virtual is not new and was also evidenced in the mid-1990s when virtual technologies were seen as a solution to the decayed state of modern democratic and community life (Robins, 1996). Then it was assumed that these novel electronic forums that constituted the Internet would necessarily imply a renewed sense of community (Talbott, 1995), hence the emergence of popular terms such as electronic communities, virtual communities and on-line communities. Today, in commercial terms, we hear that we no longer need to rely on the traditional infrastructure (material) since everything can be transposed to the virtual.

Various citations regarding impressive on-line sales are speculative and are often based on the assumption that once sales ‘take off’ then the dot.coms will need far less resources than traditional ‘bricks-and-mortar’ retailers. Amazon argues that its ‘success’ is partly due

to it needing no more than one-third of the investment of traditional retailers for the same amount of sales (Hof et al., 2000). This Utopian view ignores the material requirements that persist despite the move to the virtual. For example, when buying a book on-line the paper still has to be manufactured and the book needs to be mass produced and boxed before it is stored in a warehouse until it is ordered and despatched to the consumer. The only difference here, as compared to the last 150 years or so, is the way in which the book is ordered; this and only this uses a different technology. The long-term future of Internet retail trade remains controversial as the costs of warehousing and distribution become more apparent than previously assumed.

When considering business strategies for e-commerce it is also important to consider the commercial transaction structures that operate within the traditional market-place. These involve (1) the management of the key transactions that are related to the choice of product designs and con gurations, (2) the provision of advertising and technical information, (3) the arrangement of supply logistics and (4) the speci cation of ordering and payment systems. These cannot be ignored in the electronic market-place if the business is to remain a viable concern. In order for e-commerce projects to be successful they still need to face the (traditional) problem of reducing the costs of promoting, selling and distributing goods, while achieving higher sales without incurring any additional costs. The ability to do this has not been successfully demonstrated to date.

## The myth of the on-line shopping experience

This myth focuses on the contradictory assumptions that presume that the future of shopping is ‘on-line’ and yet that the participation of women, who are the primary traditional shoppers, is failing to materialize in the virtual market-place.

As increasing numbers of women return to work in a society of increasing pressures and demands, the notion of on-line shopping would seem to have immense appeal, yet in reality this is failing to emerge. The decision as to whether or not to buy a product in the conventional market-place is a complex one and is subject to all types of conventions and pressures (Edgell, 1996). For large numbers of consumers the process of buying a product is in itself a social and cultural experience rather than a necessity that has to be endured in order to purchase the desired product.

Whilst there is a glut of publications attempting to predict the future potential of on-line shopping (CISCO, 1999), its current inability to materialize could be related to the absence of female shoppers (Ody, 1998). Traditional on-line home shopping has its origins in the mail order business where the typical pro le is female, aged 25–44 years and in the low income socioeconomic groups (Baxter, 1998). Yet if we look at the pro le of the typical Internet user we see a very different picture: archetypal Internet users are university-educated, ‘30-something’ males commanding fairly substantial salaries (Ody, 1998). (Whilst over the years the results show slight differences – more women users and more people at either end of the age spectrum – nevertheless they still make the same basic point regarding a stereotypical pro le.) And so we see a gap and a fairly substantial one between the types of generic Internet users and the types of traditional on-line shoppers. Although increasing numbers of women are using the Internet, one study showed that consumers that have been using the WWW for a considerable time (primarily males) were far more open to the idea of on-line purchasing (Bhatnagar et al., 2000). Yet even here consumers had a preference for the Internet as a channel for purchasing goods only in the case of two product categories, namely other WWW services and music products.

A study of an on-line pilot project for a national department store (Sauer and Burton, 1999) revealed that on-line customers may be very different from their traditional customers. Whilst an Internet site provided access to two segments that were traditionally dif cult to attract (younger technologically competent men and older professional men), neither turned out to be a successful long-term prospect in terms of both sales and pro ts. This is in sharp contrast with the store’s key customer segment, i.e. professional women who like to buy medium- to high-price fashion.

This gap between Internet users and on-line shoppers is even wider when we consider women’s access to ICTs within the home. When re ecting on the issues of leisure access to computers, we see that women perceive them primarily as a family resource and, hence, that their needs are often not prioritized (Kirkup and Abbott, 1997). If Internet shopping is to become more popular, then this could further transfer women’s leisure into an arena where there is competition for resources (Green and Adam, 1999).

Clearly women are not a homogeneous group, yet when they use the Internet they prefer to communicate amongst their social network, whereas men are more inclined to engage in downloading activities (Teo and Lim, 2000). For many women, shopping is low on their list of priorities and, therefore, developing an attractive and entertaining on-line ‘shopping mall’ is unlikely to have much appeal for women. It would simply ‘take the fun out of shopping’, which for many women remains a pleasurable and social experience. So whilst at present the potential for women to shop on-line is unknown, unless business-to-consumer  rms can convince women that much is to be gained by doing so, the much-vaunted on-line shopping boom will fail to come to fruition (Ody, 1998).

## Discussion and conclusions

In this paper mythology has been used as a means for deconstructing the somewhat erratic dot.com market, which has been subject to booms and slumps. The generation of a number of myths has helped explain why investors were drawn into initially providing fairly substantial funding for Internet start-up companies, which, under different circumstances, would never have even been considered. Without doubt all myths are rooted in some aspect of reality and change is clearly evident. However, myths are also used for shaping the future, a future that is willing the ecommerce vision and so problems arise when people base their decisions solely upon this mythology.

Having witnessed the rise and fall of the dot.com market, there are still numerous e-commerce proselytizers that are keen to pursue a Utopia of electronic trading. Amidst the pessimism of the business-toconsumer arena some analysts have predicted that one area of commerce, namely business to business, will rise above the carnage and continue to bring together manufacturers and suppliers for the purpose of reducing costs and maximizing ef ciency. However, as SMEs persist in their reticence to trade on-line we see that this area has also witnessed some notable failures (examples include Work24, which is a small business portal established by Scottish Power and the Royal Bank of Scotland at a cost of £30 million and also Barclays B2B.com, which is another venture that has announced that it does not expect to receive revenue from the site this current year). Although the businessto-business arena has not furnished tales of excess, hubris or naïveté as with the dot.coms, nevertheless there has been an equal amount of hyperbole regarding the future of electronic market-places. It appears that only a small proportion of such ventures will survive if current trends continue, namely the very large players or niche providers. Yet again we see fewer and fewer markets wielding ever-more purchasing power. Whilst it is impossible to predict the future, a degree of caution would be well advised in an environment that is still largely inchoate and driven primarily by commercial rather than technological criteria.

So, returning to the mythology, what is its signi - cance? On one level there is nothing new in the linkage created between enabling technologies and the notion of revolution. Over the years various leading-edge technologies have become mythologized and glori ed (Marvin, 1988; Winston, 1998). All have taken centre stage with the claims that a new and exciting age was about to emerge. Just as the telegraph would eliminate wars, the telephone would bring democracy and television would educate the masses, the Internet and with it the new dot.coms are heralded as the technology that has the potential to profoundly affect many industries and the entire retail experience. The notion of transformation must be tempered by noting the hollow predictions about earlier technologies. Much of the discourse about the power of computing technology is blatantly Utopian and framed by a ‘genre of discourse’ (Kling, 1996) with a limited number of themes being explored. Many of these writings shy away from the more dif - cult question of what is happening in reality, preferring instead to opt for speculation about future possibilities (George and King, 1991). Experience should tell us that people often use technologies in far too complex ways for neat predictions to come true as they fail to deliver the promises of market researchers, computer vendors and stock market analysts. On another level, the mythology surrounding e-commerce is signi cant and should not simply be dismissed as we assume just another cycle of booms and slumps. It has political signi cance as the technology becomes colonized in order to strengthen the power of the huge multinationals, which is a far cry from the radical roots of the Internet. As new mergers, acquisitions and sell-offs change the composition of global multimedia conglomerates, they will at the same time enlarge the centralized control over the messages, images, social values and political norms that we are subjected to. Beck (2000) noted that ‘a new gold-digging mood has been unleashing powerful movements of corporate concentration’ (p. 44) as we witness the emergence of an oligopolistic industry with new entrants being submerged by large UK and US companies (Javary and Mansell, 2000). And so it seems likely that the future will not be one of innovative dot.coms, but ‘business as usual’.

## Biographical note

Debra Howcroft is a senior lecturer at the IS Institute and deputy director of the IS Research Centre, University of Salford. She has a mixture of both industrial and academic experience, which includes a number of years as a systems developer. Her research interests broadly lie in the area of the social and organizational aspects of the development, implementation and use of IS. This falls into two broad categories:  rst, concerning the adoption of a critical perspective which opts for a broad approach that considers the social and political factors surrounding technology development and use and, second, combining IS literature with social studies of technology literature as a means for conceptualizing the social context and content of systems development. These two research strands have been evident in much of her work, which applies this combined perspective to a range of IS topics such as participatory design, methodological intervention, IS success and failure, IS evaluation, the role of gender in systems development and the adoption and use of new technologies.
