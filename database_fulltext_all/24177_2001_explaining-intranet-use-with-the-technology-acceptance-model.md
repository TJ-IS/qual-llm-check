# Explaining intranet use with the technology acceptance model

ROBIN P. HORTON, TAMSIN BUCK, PATRICK E. WATERSON AND CHRIS W. CLEGG

Institute of Work Psychology, University of Shef eld, Shef eld S10 2TN, UK

This paper considers the application of the technology acceptance model (TAM) in explaining intranet usage in two organizations. The TAM was originally developed for explaining technology acceptance and has since been successfully applied to a number of technologies. Its suitability for modelling intranet use is considered here. The model is tested in two UK companies from the banking and engineering sectors. Study 1  nds that the TAM may be valuable as a tool for helping with analysing and understanding intranet usage. The second study indicates that its applicability may be variable between intranets and demonstrates that self-report and actual measures of usage are not interchangeable when applying such a model. The  ndings have implications for previous and current research employing the TAM and for intranet research in general.

## Introduction

Simple and effective models have been developed for explaining the uptake and success of many information and communication technology tools. Whilst intranet implementation has accelerated in recent years, little progress has been made towards developing a widely accepted model of intranet uptake and acceptance. This situation is unfortunate as the current feedback from intranet implementation projects uses different measures and produces contrasting reports of success and failure. This paper takes a widely used model of technology acceptance, namely the technology acceptance model (TAM) (Davis, 1989) and applies it to the domain of intranet use in two different companies. In the second study it was possible to include measures of actual usage in the model, a practice that is relatively unusual in this  eld.

De nitions of intranets are not abundant, but frequently vary. However, most include a description of an intranet as a network based on the transport control protocols/internetworking protocols (TCP/IP) belonging to an organization, which are located within its  rewall and accessible only by the organization’s members. This de nition would include e-mail, though in many cases the term intranet is only used to refer to the system of storing and accessing data via World

Wide Web (WWW) browsers. A number of authors have predicted that substantial bene ts will result from intranet development. At one extreme, some describe intranets as having the potential for doing all that previous tools and techniques have failed to, such as business process re-engineering. Drawing on experience from work at Sun, Microsoft and Unisys, Hinrichs (1997) predicted that ‘intranets are going to cut costs, increase productivity, streamline business processes, improve quality . . . improve human resources, strategically align your organizational infrastructure, and help you emerge as a learning organization involved in principle-centred self improvement’ (p. 5).

Some organizations and individuals writing in the knowledge management literature have also anticipated that substantial bene t will be derived from intranet development, whilst others are sceptical with regard to what are effectively technology-driven plans for organizational change. Scarbrough (1999), for example, described a banking organization that aimed to break down barriers between organizational divisions and allow ‘global knowledge management’. In practice, independent intranet development by the bank’s divisions brought about quite the opposite and succeeded in reinforcing the barriers between them. Further issues are raised through studying the interaction between organizational knowledge and intranets (Scott, 1998)

with reports of hidden costs, performance limitations and organizational resistance. Accounts of returns on investment in intranets indicate that the value of intranets to business is convincing, but these studies are largely based upon anecdotal evidence (Ryan and Martin, 1997).

This study is concerned with system usage. Whilst usage is clearly not the same as system success, it is a necessary precursor and, furthermore, it is of interest in its own right. Technology adoption studies frequently adopt ‘usage’ as a measure of success (Szajna, 1993), though others have employed alternative measures of success, an example being ‘user satisfaction’ (Miller and Doyle, 1987; McKeen et al., 1994). The concept of success will have a different meaning to each organization in which an intranet is being developed. However, system usage can be seen as an element of success that will be an important issue to all. The establishment of an appropriate level of system usage is a necessary step towards seeing the bene ts that are expected to be derived from an intranet.

This small array of contrasting accounts is a typical sample of the broader literature. It is therefore unsurprising that many are keen to know whether their intranet is a success. The  rst step in establishing the factors leading to the success of the intranet is to consider the approaches that have been used for measuring the success of other information technologies (ITs).

## Technology acceptance models

One way of examining the uptake and usage of IT is to use models of planned behaviour, one of the most well known of which is the TAM (Davis, 1989). This is an established model of computer usage and has been validated through testing with a number of technologies (Davis et al., 1989, 1992; Davis, 1993; Igbaria, 1993; Igbaria et al., 1994; Lederer et al., 1998; Dishaw and Strong, 1999) and cultures (Straub et al., 1997). Many studies using the TAM (e.g. Anandarajan et al., 2000) have suggested that the theoretical basis for this model lies in the theory of reasoned action (TRA) (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975) and some have noted its links to the diffusion of innovation (Rogers, 1983). The TRA is a model of human behaviour that postulates that reasoning  ows from beliefs and evaluations to the development of an attitude towards performing a behaviour. Attitudes thus bring about an evaluation of intention to perform the behaviour, in turn resulting in its execution. In this case, the relevant behaviour is intranet usage and the beliefs and evaluations are proposed as being made on a number of speci c variables. The original model (Davis, 1989)

proposed these variables as being perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEU), factors that are captured through a small number of short questions. In a study of the use of the WriteOne word processing system, a typical PU item was ‘I would  nd WriteOne useful in the MBA programme’ (Davis, 1989), whereas ‘Learning to operate WriteOne would be easy for me’ (Davis, 1989) was an item on the PEU variable.

The TAM is usually used for explaining the relationship between usage (both self-reported and anticipated future usage) and PU and PEU. Davis et al. (1989) made a comparison between this model and the TRA, in which the TAM included an ‘attitude’ element and an ‘intention’ element. Their study found positive relationships between PU and PEU and attitude, between attitude and behavioural intention and between behavioural intention and usage. They found that none of the other TAM variables had a signi - cant effect on usage over and above intentions which suggests that intentions fully mediated the effects of these other variables on usage (Davis et al., 1989). In order to identify a relationship between TAM variables and usage, whether mediated or not, these direct relationships must be tested in the absence of the mediator and shown to be signi cant. These tests were not presented in Davis et al. (1989). Details of the methodology necessary in these tests are given in Baron and Kenny (1986), a summary of which is presented later in this paper. A subsequent paper (Davis et al., 1992) did test for mediation by intention and found an effect. However, attitude was not tested in that study. Further complications with the inclusion of attitude in the model occur because the calculation of attitude involves the multiplication of beliefs by evaluations of consequences (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975; Davis, 1992). Because the two variables to be multiplied are not measured on ratio scales, such a calculation is often claimed to be invalid. For this reason and to keep the model as simple as possible, attitude was not used in this study.

The work of Davis (1989) has been elaborated on by others who have added further variables to the TAM so as to account for a greater amount of the variance in usage. These amendments may be considered in the light of an argument offered by Warr (1980) in which he described the ‘Christmas tree analogy’, whereby the initial structure of a model becomes lost through the addition of ‘decorations and re nements’ so that it eventually lacks the coherence of a purposeful con guration. Whilst models may provide a better  t with such ‘decorations’, it is no longer possible to observe the value of the original model. For this reason, this paper currently intends to include no further elements in the model as it aims to test the appropriateness of the TAM as a model of intranet use. Successful application of the basic model may pave the way for subsequent addition of more predictor variables.

## Applying the technology acceptance model to intranet use

The TAM has not been tested as a model for explaining intranet use, although there is some research that has indicated that it might be appropriate for such use. Anandarajan et al. (2000), for example, ran an exploratory investigation into the antecedents of Internet usage using the structure of the TAM and TRA. Their study found that usefulness correlated with use, though usability fell just below a signi cant level. No causal relation was shown, the sample size was small (n = 39) and objective measures were not used. Lederer et al. (1998) also found some support for the use of the TAM in modelling WWW usage.

Phelps and Mok (1999) studied elements of the TAM as predictors for intranet satisfaction, but not use. Whilst encouraging, the study was based on a small number of responses and provided few details of sampling characteristics.

In addition to self-reported usage, objective measures of technology usage were employed in a study of e-mail uptake (Szajna, 1996). That study found relationships between usefulness and ease-of-use measures, but indicated an unsteady relationship between intention and use. Intention did not predict actual usage after users received brief exposure to the system and accounted for only 6% of the variance in usage after gaining experience of the system. There are clearly some situations in which the TAM may not be useful for explaining actual system usage. In this situation, the relationship between TAM variables and actual use is only found with experience of the system. This relationship between intention and usage will be considered within the present study.

There is much to differentiate intranet technologies from more simple computer tools such as word processors. Where usefulness judgements of word processors might be made through a focus on the characteristics of the tool in producing a document, intranet usefulness may hinge on judgements about the information contained and retrieved from the intranet as well as on the intranet as a tool itself. The interface to the intranet, the WWW browser, may be considered as a tool in the same way as a word processor package might. However, the concept of an intranet is deeper than this and may include elements of the WWW browser, the material which can be accessed via the browser, the process of accessing such material, the types of information contained in the material, the characteristics of this information (such as its validity)

and even the entire concept of a system for sharing and capturing knowledge in a secure environment. It is not proposed to study the processes by which a user judges usefulness or usability based on these factors here, but note that it has the potential to be more complex than comparable judgements of other ITs. In summary, many questions surround the effect that such differences may have on the suitability of transferring the TAM from modelling traditional IT systems to modelling intranets.

## Research setting

Two studies of the TAM were run in organizations with different information requirements and work organization. The company context and the information requirements of the users in those companies were gauged using qualitative interviews that were carried out as part of larger studies within both companies. The  ndings are important, as it is the differences between the companies that lead to the hypotheses proposing differential applicability of the TAM between them.

The interviews with managers, developers and users in the companies established the following  ndings.

Company 1 is based in the UK banking sector. The company is well established with of ces nationwide, but was undergoing restructuring of its departments and teams. As part of this process the intranet had been recently developed, providing a range of information used on a daily basis, such as customer records, forms and details of social events. The electronic commerce manager stated that ‘We developed the intranet to assist our staff in day to day tasks’.

Company 2 is a large UK engineering company. Work organization was notably different between the two organizations. The methods of work were comparably more structured and prescribed in the banking company. Whilst rigorous and structured processes were followed in the engineering company, individuals had the opportunity for organizing their own work and choosing solutions to problems. As in the  rst study, the intranet had been recently developed, but was intended to be used less frequently, providing large quantities of information that could be used for clarifying ideas or helping answer questions.

With respect to the issues discussed in this section, it is proposed that the application of the TAM will vary between the organizations and the nature of this is described in the hypotheses below.

## Research hypotheses and objectives

The speci c objectives of this paper are as follows.

1. To apply the TAM in two studies of intranet use and to test its suitability for predicting usage.

2. To assess any impact of the participants’ differing information requirements on the applicability of the TAM between the two studies.

3. To assess the importance of using objective recording of intranet usage in place of selfreported measures.

The research aims to test the suitability of the TAM for modelling intranet use through testing four hypotheses, the rationale for which is described below.

Szajna (1996) is an excellent example of one of the few studies that included both self-reported and actual usage measures. She found self-reported usage to be an inappropriate substitute for actual use in the domain of e-mail use. Intention did not predict actual usage after brief exposure to the system and accounted for only 6% of the variance in usage after gaining experience of the system. In contrast, self-reported usage was found to have a stronger relationship with intention after both brief and extended exposure to the system. Such  ndings lead to the belief that similar results may be found in the domain of intranet uptake. Sharma and Yetton (2001) also studied the relationship between the single variable PU and usage. Their metaanalysis found that the relationship between PU and usage is much stronger when using self-reported usage than using actual usage. In light of these papers, the following hypothesis is proposed.

H : There will be a stronger relationship between PU, PEU and self-reported usage than between PU, PEU and actual usage.

PEU has been shown to be a key variable in explaining technology usage in applications of the TAM. The usability of a technology is proposed to be distinct from its usefulness by the TAM, so it suggested that the PEU of an intranet is formed quite separately from the PU of an intranet. Unlike users in company 1, users in company 2 might have had a tendency to access different types of information on each visit. However, in each case they would have gained an impression of the ease of use of the system, which in turn would have affected their use of the intranet. The following hypothesis is proposed.

$\operatorname { H } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { : } }$ PEU will account for a signi cant amount of the variance in intranet uptake in both companies.

PU has been shown to be a key variable in explaining technology usage in all other applications of the TAM, so it is expected that this will also be true in its application to intranet technologies. Whilst the intranets in both companies had been recently introduced, use of the company 1 intranet was more frequent than that of company 2’s intranet. It is expected that regular usage will allow greater familiarization and a more accurate assessment of usefulness. Planned consequential usage is expected to re ect this usefulness assessment in the TAM, so a stronger relationship between usefulness and usage may exist for the intranet with higher use (company 1). The relationships between user reported variables (usefulness and intention to use) and actual usage found by Szajna (1996) only existed after a length of exposure to the system. The following hypothesis is therefore proposed.

$\operatorname { H } _ { 3 } \colon$ PU will account for a signi cant amount of the variance in uptake in both companies, but it will show a stronger effect for uptake of company 1’s intranet.

The TAM proposes that a user’s intention to use a technology mediates the effect of PU and PEU on technology use. The need for information searching arose at various stages of the design work in company 2, the occurrence of which frequently could not be planned. It follows that, whilst a designer may have an attitude towards the likelihood that they will use an intranet for accessing information in such situations, they cannot assess ahead of an unplanned incident of information demand whether they will actually use the intranet for acquiring that information. The ‘intention’ element of the TAM refers to just this sort of assessment and may therefore fail to take a mediating role, indicating that the linear planned nature of the TAM is inappropriate for such intranet use. In contrast, the use of company 1’s intranet arose from a reasonably de ned set of situations of information demand at work. It is hypothesized that because these recur frequently, the user can better assess and report their intention for using an intranet for obtaining information. The following hypothesis is therefore proposed.

$\operatorname { H } _ { 4 } \colon$ The mediating role of intention will only be found in company 1’s intranet.

## Method

## Participants and procedure

## Study 1

This study assessed the application of the TAM in explaining intranet usage in a company based in the UK banking sector. The participants were 386 employees out of 600 who were invited to participate in the study, which is a response rate of 64%. Questionnaires were distributed to these 600 employees who completed and returned the questionnaires to a central collection point within the working day.

## Study 2

The applicability of the TAM to intranet use was considered further in study 2, which assessed its application in a large engineering company based in the UK. This study made a signi cant step forward by extending the concept of intranet use from selfreported usage to objective measures of intranet use. These measures can be made at the time of questionnaire completion and at any time after, thereby introducing the ability for testing the extent to which the TAM variables predict future usage patterns.

Questionnaires were distributed to a sample of 80 engineers and 65 provided completed responses, representing a response rate of 81%.

An objective measure of usage was developed for this sample. Data on intranet  le accessing were collected from the company server and processed into statistics describing the intranet sessions run by each user. This processing was performed using a set of PERL routines that had been developed speci cally for this study by the  rst author. Full details of the sessions that each user had run were calculated (1) prior to the survey, (2) 2 months after the survey and (3) 5 months after the survey.

## Selection of items and measures

The survey was developed from scales that had been used and validated in previous TAM studies (Davis, 1989, 1992). Davis et al. (1992) included PU, PEU and intention scales. The questions were developed and piloted in consultation with both companies so as to ensure that they were appropriate for describing intranet use whilst following the original questions used in the TAM as closely as possible. PU was measured using the measures ‘using the intranet would improve my performance in doing my job’, ‘using the intranet at work would increase my productivity’, ‘using the intranet would enhance my effectiveness in my job’ and ‘I would  nd the intranet useful in my job’. PEU was measured using the measures ‘learning to operate the intranet would be easy for me’, ‘I would  nd it easy to get the intranet to do what I want it to do’, ‘it would be easy for me to become skilful at using the intranet’ and ‘I would  nd the intranet easy to use’. Participants’ intention to use the intranet was measured using ‘I presently intend to use the capability intranet regularly at work’. All responses were scored on a Likert-type scale.

These models and those in earlier papers may appear to provide evidence for the successful application of the TAM. However, on inspection few show a watertight, linear and mediated relationship between the proposed variables. The value beside each line in these diagrams is a b weight and the signi cance refers only to the signi cance of the relationship between the two variables it connects. Mediation cannot be identi ed solely by inspection of these values and this should be kept in mind when considering this and earlier TAM papers. Much higher sample sizes would be required in order to allow a path analysis necessary for controlling for all other relationships in such a diagram. Ensuing a brief glance at a similar diagram in Figure 2 of Davis (1993), a reader may make an interpretation of PU and PEU as explaining system use, with PEU having a fully mediated effect and PU having a direct effect (due to its high b weight even with the proposed mediating variable included). Referring back to Baron and Kenny (1986), as summarized in an earlier section of this paper, it is noted that the existence of PEU in the model is reliant on it having a signi cant effect on usage when tested in the absence of a mediating variable. Signi cant predictor–mediator and mediator–outcome relationships, such as is shown by the lines in Figure 2 of Davis’ (1993) study, are not a substitute for testing this relationship. The calculations performed by Davis et al. (1992) detail all the necessary regression analyses in showing PU and ‘enjoyment’ as explaining usage mediated by behavioural intention.

## Discussion

The objective of this paper was to test the suitability of the TAM in explaining intranet usage. The model was tested in two studies, the second of which tested the model further with the introduction of objective measures of intranet usage.

Study 1 applied the TAM to intranet use in a large UK banking company (company 1). The TAM was successfully applied in this scenario, as in previous applications of the model to other technologies. It was expected that regular usage of this intranet would allow greater familiarization with its usefulness, so planned consequential usage was expected to re ect such a usefulness assessment strongly. PU did indeed have an effect on users’ intention to use the system. However, its effect on self-reported use was not mediated by this intention. PEU was also expected to affect planned usage, which it was found to do, with its effect on usage also being fully mediated by the users’ intention to use the system.

Study 2 applied the TAM in modelling intranet usage in an engineering company (company 2). PEU played a role in explaining both the actual and selfreported usage, whereas PU did not. It should be noted that, whilst the b weights in Figure 2 appear to indicate a signi cant relationship between PU and intention and intention and usage, this does not implicate intention as a mediator between the other two variables. PU itself did not show a signi cant relationship with usage and such a relationship is a prerequisite for intention taking a mediating role between these (Baron and Kenny, 1986). This issue is discussed in greater detail earlier in this paper.

## The role of PU and PEU in explaining uptake: information requirements and situational differences

A comparison between these studies found that, as expected from $\mathrm { H } _ { 2 } ,$ PEU accounted for a signi cant amount of the variance in intranet uptake in both companies. Whilst PU took on a similar role in company 1, the smaller role expected in company 2 (H ) was in fact insigni cant. This paper now discusses the possible causes for this  nding.

A consideration of the context of information gathering in each company took the study some way towards explaining the  nding that PU played a much smaller role in explaining intranet usage in company 1 as compared to company 2. Qualitative interviews carried out as part of larger studies within both companies indicated that the users in each company had very different information requirements. The types of information that were required for carrying out a task in company 1 were much more constrained when compared to company 2. The work of an employee in company 2 often required them to answer new questions and search for novel pieces of information. It is possible that, whilst they could still gain a perception of the ease of use of the system, the novelty of each search may have resulted in variable levels of usefulness. The TAM expects planned consequential usage to re ect an overall PU assessment, so the weak relationship between the two may be explained by the user of the intranet in study 2 being unable to form an overall usefulness assessment.

The process of information acquisition could be categorized as being information ‘retrieval’ from the banking intranet versus information ‘searching’ from the engineering intranet. This distinction was explained by Marchionini (1995) and Deltor (1998) in their discussions of the differences between information seeking and retrieval: information retrieval implies that the object must have been known at some point, whereas information seeking is a process of acquiring and building up knowledge. A usefulness assessment would clearly be more comprehensive for the known objects ‘retrieved’ than for novel objects in a ‘search process.

A further distinction between the companies was the choice of alternative sources of information available to an employee. Employees in company 1 had comparatively less opportunity and choice in terms of methods and mechanisms for searching and locating information relating to their work. In contrast, the information searches run in company 2 may have involved locating experts, retrieving data from databases and examining on-line and off-line material in the form of reports. In other words, one of the key differences between the intranet users in the two companies was that the engineering user in company 2 had much more choice as regards information seeking alternatives, as compared to the user in company 1. Through use of alternative information sources, the user in company 2 would have less opportunity for assessing intranet usefulness. Furthermore, it may prove harder for a user to assess the usefulness of an intranet as it can be useful in different ways. On the one hand, usefulness may describe an intranet providing an answer to a problem. On the other hand, it may provide little useful information, but be a key signpost directing someone to an alternative source of information.

One outcome from the present research is that studies of the applicability of TAM to the intranet and other new technologies need to be more sensitive to the context in which information is being searched and retrieved.

There are further alternative explanations for the minor role which PU was found to have in the study. For example, it is possible that PU does predict usage. However, the scales used in the TAM may have been inappropriate for application to the intranet use in company 2. TAM scales ascertain a general perception of ‘usefulness’ from the user, but an intranet is a complex system in which usefulness may have constituent parts such as the validity, accuracy or uniqueness of information. Perhaps a more accurate evaluation of PU might be gained by asking the user about each of these constituent parts and this is an issue that might be addressed in future studies. With regard to this issue, this paper draws attention to the concept of technology transparency, as described by Lave and Wagner (1991). This concept considers both visible and invisible properties of the technology: invisible in that it is simply a transparent window onto information, but visible in that it is the object seen to be providing access to that information. Measuring the constituent elements of PU or PEU would be no trivial task, as user perceptions and reports of these constructs may be very hard for a ‘transparent’ system such as an intranet when compared to a more opaque word processing tool.

## The role of intention as a mediator in a model of intranet uptake

It was discussed earlier in this paper that, within the working practices in company 2, incidents of information demand were often unplanned or unexpected. If an incident of information demand is unforeseen, users are unlikely to have assessed in advance whether they will use an intranet for locating that information.

The ‘intention’ element of the TAM refers to just this sort of assessment, so it was proposed that the mediating role of intention was only expected to exist in company 2 (H ). Whilst the relationship between PEU and usage measures for company 1 was mediated by intention, the relationship between PU and usage measures was not mediated by intention. The single relationship between PEU and usage in study 2 was indeed not mediated by intention. Why is there such weakness in the role of intention in these studies? The TAM takes a behaviourist approach to modelling usage, as seen in the theory of planned behaviour (TPB), which is an approach which is by nature less suitable to this unplanned information searching process. It is noted that a weak relationship between intention and action occurs elsewhere in the organizational context. An example is employee turnover, for which the literature  nds that strong intentions to leave do not always result in turnover (Mowday et al., 1982).

## The use of objective data in the mode

The issues raised so far could be seen as shortcomings of the TAM, but the suitability of the TAM for modelling either current or future intranet usage is more dubious when the  ndings in this study from the inclusion of objective measures of usage are considered. These may promote an alternative interpretation of the results published both here and in previous studies of the TAM.

The most striking  nding of this study is the poor prediction of actual usage offered by the TAM, as proposed in $\mathrm { H } _ { 1 } .$ The regression analyses found that only PEU shared variance with the actual measured usage. Moreover, no prediction of future short- or long-term usage was shown (measured at time 2 and time 3 in Table 5). These results match the  ndings of Szajna (1996), which supported self-reported usage being an inappropriate substitute for actual use in the domain of e-mail use.

These  ndings also align well with a recent metaanalysis of the relationship between PU and actual and self-reported usage (Sharma and Yetton, 2001), in which PU showed a stronger relationship with selfreported than actual usage. In the second study, in which actual usage data was available, the weak relationship between PU and usage was nonetheless attenuated when self-reported usage was replaced with actual usage data, in agreement with the  ndings of Sharma and Yetton (2001). Their study did not consider the relationship between PEU and actual or self-reported usage. This paper found that the relationship between PEU and usage was not attenuated by the introduction of self-reported usage (see Table 5). This constant relationship between PEU and actual usage may prove valuable to managers or practitioners attempting to promote intranet use.

In addition to a model capable of explaining current usage levels, a model that is able to assess the likelihood of people using these systems in the future might serve the developers of intranets better. It is possible that a model based on the TRA or TPB may never account for future usage. Of primary relevance is the statement by Ajzen (1988) that his prime goal was to understand behaviour and not merely predict it, but here it may begin to be questioned whether Ajzen’s (1988) model was really of planned behaviour if there is no correlation between plans (intention) and actual usage in the future. Indeed, theories of situated action propose that, whilst cognitive science would have us believe our actions are planned, there is evidence that we perform in an ad hoc manner in order to achieve goals (Suchman, 1987). Whilst not predicted by PU or PEU, it was noted that actual usage correlated strongly with self-reported usage (r = 0.59) (Table 5). This was a similar strength of relationship to that between the recordings of usage made over time.

Those concerned about the validity of self-report surveys might raise another explanation for the discrepancies between self-reported and recorded usage. Whilst these surveys are one of the conventional tools used in organizational research (Mitchell, 1985), it is important to consider the validity of self-reported measures of intranet usage. There are two issues that raise concern about this validity. First, questionnaire completion can be biased by a desire to appear in a good light, that is ‘social desirability’. Whilst a minimal effect of social desirability has been found in some TPB studies (Armitage and Conner, 1999), the susceptibility of the intranet users of the companies being studied here was not measured. It may be worth considering that positive (or negative) reports of usability, usefulness and usage may affect the perceptions of or consequences for that individual, so their responses may be biased in a direction seen to in uence this situation most positively. If this is true, then it is assumed that subjects perceive higher usage as appearing favourable, as the mean self-reported usage was higher than the recorded usage. Second, a positive report of intranet usefulness and usability may result in an aligned positively biased recall of usage frequency reported in the same questionnaire and vice versa. Feldman and Lynch (1988) and Harrison et al. (1996) gave details of this type of effect.

## Conclusions

## Summary

This paper has provided insight into the relationship between organizational context and the applicability of the TAM through studying distinctly different organizations. The variables PU, PEU and intention were implicated as being predictive of intranet use. The role of these varied between the organizations studied and the TAM model was found to be more suitable for modelling intranets in organizations with constrained information requirements and a structured work organization. The relationship between the variables in the TAM was strongest when modelled with traditionally gathered self-reported usage data, though a weaker relationship between the variables still existed when modelled with actual usage data. Substitution of actual usage with self-reported data is commonplace in the exploration of any model and is an important issue that warrants attention in similar studies. Despite the complex and transparent nature of intranet technology the TAM offers an outline for modelling its uptake.

## Implications and recommendations for further work

This study carries a number of implications for both future studies of intranet adoption and of TAMs.

The results of applying the TAM to technology usage have been shown to vary, depending on whether actual usage data or self-reported usage data are used. This leads to this paper recommending against the substitution of actual usage data with self-reported data in future studies. Furthermore, interpretation of previous studies of technology adoption and of other models of technology acceptance should be done with attention to the usage data from which their conclusions were derived.

When studying technologies other than intranets, it may not be possible to gather this information. These situations would be aided by exploration of why there is high differentiation between  gures of actual and selfreported usage in future studies. This paper suggests some explanatory variables for this differentiation in the Discussion section; however, studies of these would be likely to be context speci c. For example, the validity of self-report surveys might be moderated by social and physical issues, which are pertinent to the participants in each study. Unless these issues are tackled by future research the use of actual usage data is strongly recommended. With increased availability of off-the-shelf WWW traf c monitoring software it should be possible to gather actual usage data in most cases.

It is common for studies to generalize the  ndings from TAM studies of speci c technologies. This paper advocates that the following  ndings apply speci cally to modelling intranet usage using the TAM, rather than as generalizable  ndings for the TAM.

One aim of this study was to demonstrate the differential applicability of the TAM to intranet usage between organizations’ intranets and this was performed successfully. It was found that the TAM did show a degree of applicability for modelling intranet uptake across both organizations and that the TAM appeared more suitable for modelling intranet use in the organization in which information demand was more consistent in everyday work and in which the work methods were prescribed and structured.

The between-organization variability in TAM applicability also has implications for practitioners building on this area of research on intranet adoption. It should be observed that the conclusions of any studies of the TAM are only valid within the context of that study: variations between technologies, the people of an organization and the suitability of the question scales used for that organization may be key factors. Such an argument of non-generalization could be applied to any case study or experimental work, so with this caveat it seems sensible to consider applying the TAM to further situations. Developers and managers wishing to promote intranet usage may sensibly wish to exploit knowledge of the strong positive relationship between PEU and all usage measures. In doing so it would be valuable to consider each element of the TAM before applying it to a problem, rather than applying the model in its entirety to a real-world scenario. It would be of additional value for future studies to explore its applicability in additional organizations and to study whether modi cations to the TAM allow a more uniform cross-organization applicability.

There would be much value in research considering some of the issues that may be detrimental to the application of the TAM to intranet usage including the predictive validity of planned behaviour models. These issues were discussed earlier in this paper and include the incompatibility between planned behaviour and the ad hoc problem solving sequence followed by some intranet users and the methodological issues in the testing of the model.

Acceptance of technology will continue to be an active area of research as new technologies are continually developed and implemented in organizations. This study provides an insight into the speci c applicability of the TAM to intranet technologies. More generally it is hoped that issues such as model generalizability and usage data collection will inform both the development of future studies and interpretation of the large literature culminating from studies of technology acceptance.
