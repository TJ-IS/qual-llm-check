On behalf of the editorial board, the international advisory board and everyone associated with the journal, the editors would like to extend their sympathies to the families and relatives of those who were killed on 11 September 2001 in the USA.

The events of that day have given rise to an avalanche of opinion, re ection and re-evaluation as commentators consider the past and anticipate a changed future. The importance of information technology (IT) topics has changed. New challenges have been issued for existing technologies. New opportunities have emerged. Less can be taken for granted. So long as we stand up to the challenge and start to think more broadly and more radically then some good may yet emerge.

The crucial question for us all is how fragile and how robust is our increasingly electronic world. What can we do to increase its robustness? Clearly, the fact that even the worst hit companies in the World Trade Centre were able to rebuild their operational infrastructure as quickly as they were is a remarkable tribute to the resilience that a distributed infrastructure can impart. However, how much back-up is required? What if a major corporate were targeted both at its operational headquarters and at its principal alternative site? Maybe back-up tapes in secure safes at remote sites would provide adequate protection. Maybe. Maybe not. Maybe the real risk is the loss of knowledge associated with the loss of employees. How seriously must companies look at that? We must articulate all such questions and seek answers by carefully assessing how well prepared we are.

A particular source of fragility is the increasing tightness of the coupling between units within an organization and across organizations. As companies seek to compete less as company against company and more as supply chain against supply chain, there is enormous pressure to remove the slack resources (inventory, time and people) that currently exist at organizational boundaries. As one company’s systems are integrated with those of another, the costs and implications of having to swap swiftly from an established business partner to a new one become a relevant consideration in architecting integration.

A further issue of interest is how companies will view outsourcing. We talked to a consultant to the  nancial services sector about this. He held a strong view that concerns about disaster recovery planning would encourage more companies to hand over their operations to a specialist provider with the capacity and capabilities for providing fail-safe back-up. Such a concentration of responsibility for our systems also has its risks. Vendors must recognize this and step up to the mark.

Then we encounter the question of how do systems and processes cope with sudden changes in consumer and business behaviour. The airline industry is most obviously affected. How will the models underlying yield management systems respond to a catastrophic decline in demand? What will be the business implications of those responses?

Quite independent of the New York attacks the global airline industry has offered another example of the importance of crisis planning in the recent collapses of Swissair and Ansett (in Australia). The Ansett case is particularly interesting because the Australian domestic market is dominated by Qantas, with limited competition from Virgin Blue. Even to casual observers Ansett’s demise was predictable. Nevertheless, when it came it suddenly left Qantas and Virgin Blue with having to rescue stranded passengers and provide a substantially increased capacity for compensating for Ansett. It would be interesting to know how Qantas and Virgin planned for the non-standard task of rescuing Ansett passengers and what technology issues they faced in taking up the extra workload. More generally, there are interesting studies to be pursued into how companies manage the take-up of business from signi cant competitors who suddenly cease to operate.

Moving from the recent extraordinary events to the day-to-day business of the advancement of knowledge, we have a diverse mix of topics in this issue of the journal. Debra Howcroft sets about deconstructing the dot.com boom. She probes a number of assumptions on which the boom was founded and demonstrates that most were absurd. When next a management fad or technology mania starts to take hold, we should turn back to this article in order to remind ourselves that rarely if ever are there simple ways of revolutionizing the pro tability of an individual company’s business model, let alone that of whole sectors of industry. It should help us to stand up and say as much in the face of what we know to be unfounded hype. In doing so we may help those companies that listen gain a strategic advantage through not making the same mistake as the mass of their competitors.

Our second article is also something rather unusual. While there has been a voluminous literature on group decision support, computer-mediated communication, virtual teams and so forth, facilitation has scarcely been mentioned in this context. David Pauleen and Pak Yoong provide a substantial excursion into this territory. They particularly focus on the special challenge of boundary crossing. As companies increasingly partner with others located in different parts of the world and in different time zones, boundary crossing becomes an important part of virtual team processes. The authors make commonsense points that are often not thought of. For example, they point out that communication within an organizational unit can often be abbreviated because of shared language, shared assumptions and shared understanding. In a virtual team it may therefore be necessary to work much harder at communication. These and other useful points emerge from their extensive discussion of this issue.

Our third and fourth articles are more traditional. Norio Kambayashi and Harry Scarbrough explore cultural differences in the use of IT by factory managers in the UK and Japan. They  nd that cultural differences persist even in the use of advanced technology. They conclude that, rather than diversity being eliminated by globalization, diversity will continue to be a core issue for consideration by the developers of global information systems.

Finally, Robin Horton, Tamsin Buck, Patrick Waterson and Chris Clegg make their contribution to the continuing stream of literature that is developing and re ning the technology assessment model (TAM). Theirs is a challenging piece on multiple fronts. They apply the TAM to intranet adoption and suggest that it may be a more accurate model for situations in which work is structured and information  ow predictable. They also argue strongly from their  ndings that TAM researchers should not employ self-reported measures of use but only measures of actual use. It is a message that should be acted upon wherever possible by all researchers who employ a usage variable, not just those exploring TAM.

Chris Sauer and Leslie Willcocks
