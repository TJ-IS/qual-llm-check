# Exploring the gender question in critical information systems

ALISON ADAM

Information Systems Research Centre, University of Salford, Salford M5 4WT, UK

This paper addresses ways in which theorizing gender may be important in forming an understanding of the topic of emancipation, which is central to the new critical information systems (IS) based on the thinking of Habermas. After brie y discussing some problems with current research on gender and IS the paper argues that appropriate feminist theory may be useful in augmenting our understanding of foundational issues such as emancipation. The development of feminist philosophy and epistemology is brie y introduced. Habermas’ ‘ideal speech situation’ is problematized in relation to feminist writing on male and female communication juxtaposed with recent research in computer-mediated communications. The paper continues by exploring the concept of emancipation through feminist epistemology and it closes with a preliminary consideration of how these concerns may be applied to critical IS.

## Introduction

Gender appears to be a fundamental category for ordering and classifying social relations in the world (Evans, 1994). The  rst thing we are told about a baby when it is born is whether it is a boy or a girl. We are often thrown off guard if someone of the ‘wrong’ gender appears in a given role. We phone Professor Smith and her secretary Mr Brown answers the call. Someone who feels like a man trapped in a woman’s body or a woman trapped in a man’s body may go to extreme lengths to change sex. Leaving aside procreation and potential  eshly pleasures, there is no doubt that gender and ordering the world along gendered lines matters a great deal to us. A considerable feminist literature now exists exploring all aspects of gender relations both in empirical studies and on a theoretical level (e.g. see Code, 2000). Looking to Marx’s 1843 de nition of critical theory as ‘the selfclari cation of the struggles and wishes of the age’ (Marx, 1975, p. 209), feminism would appear to offer excellent credentials as a social theory that can contribute to the critical impulse. For not only may it shed light on the character and basis of changing gender relations, it is a consciously political body of theory as it starts from the base point of women’s subordination. As one of the major vectors in contemporary social theory, it is all the more surprising that feminist thinking has touched the world of information systems (IS) so little, given its conscious turn to the social and political. If feminist writing has hardly impacted on IS in general, it appears to have touched the critical wing of IS barely at all. Although the thinking of Habermas (1972) is notoriously dif cult to apply in a practical setting and there are still relatively few empirical studies in critical IS, his work remains the central vector of critical IS. Nevertheless, some commentators emphasize the potential contribution of other critical thinkers to management and IS research. These alternatives are drawn from within the Frankfurt School, e.g. Horkheimer and Marcuse (Alvesson and Willmott, 1996) and from alternative interdisciplinary computerrelated studies (Spaul, 1997) such as the groundbreaking work of Laurel (1994) on the interface between technology and art and Landow’s (1992) research on textual deconstruction and hypertext.

Given that gender is accepted alongside class and ethnicity and even age, sexuality and religion as an important determinant of social behaviour in research in the social sciences, it rarely appears in the ‘technical’ disciplines as a research topic. It is relatively easy to see how such relegation occurs in subjects such as physics, chemistry and even computer science, where the human subject is largely removed or even expunged into what epistemologists have described as a ‘view from nowhere’ (Nagel, 1986), i.e. a view of the world that emphasizes formal representations from which the human subject may be effectively rendered absent. Prominent authors in IS regard the  eld as rather confused (Checkland and Holwell, 1998) and still to emerge (Mingers and Stowell, 1997). Yet there is general agreement that the discipline is interdisciplinary or hybrid, including ‘not only technology but also management and organization theory, sociology, systems thinking, political science, social psychology, etc.’ (Checkland and Holwell, 1998, p. 9). Given that at least part of the genealogy of IS lies in the social sciences it is not so easy to see why gender as an explanatory category and feminism as an appropriate theoretical body should be so poorly represented.

Exploring brie y why this may have come about, some of the problem seems to lie in the focus of the relatively small body of research on gender and IS that currently exists.

First of all it is interesting to note that feminist research on the actual design and implementation of IS has dwindled from its zenith in the late 1980s and early 1990s when a number of reasonably high-pro le projects were funded and reported in the literature (Bjerknes and Bratteteig, 1987; Green et al., 1993; Green, 1994; Vehviläinen, 1997) to a point where there are now few reports of such projects (Adam, 2000). Although there appears to be just as much interest in gender and information and communication technologies as ever, this implies that the ‘gender equity’- inspired empirical research project in IS seems to have all but disappeared.

It is dif cult to know exactly why this has happened. One problem is that this type of research appears to have lost momentum as research on gender and virtuality and the Internet has overtaken earlier workplace studies. For instance, concerns over of ce automation and detailed studies of gendered segregation along technological lines in the of ce no longer excite the same attention that they formerly did (Webster, 1990). However, if we now take of ce automation as commonplace our concerns over technology and gender should perhaps now move to other newer styles of workplace such as the call centre, where technology is used for monitoring and controlling jobs that are often highly gender segregated (Richardson and Richardson, 2001). However, it should be pointed out that, at the same time, the feminist-inspired gender and IS projects listed above were all but ignored by the IS mainstream. For instance, although the Florence project was reported in a widely cited edited collection (Bjerknes et al., 1987) I have found only one citation of Florence itself in a major IS publication (Hirschheim et al., 1995). Lack of citation is one reason for the failure of gender and IS research getting anywhere near the mainstream and this may have contributed to the apparent atrophy of this paradigm of research. However, another related reason is the discouragement that those who might have taken up this research may receive in favour of more mainstream research, particularly as academic communities throughout the world are subject to ever more measurement and evaluation and where academic tenure is harder and harder to obtain in many countries.

Such research on gender and IS as exists must sail between the Scylla of statistical surveys on potential differences in men’s and women’s behaviours with respect to IS, which tend to force men’s and women’s characteristics into stereotyped straitjackets (Truman and Baroudi, 1994; Gefen and Straub, 1997; Igbaria and Chidambaram, 1997; Venkatesh and Morris, 2000) and the Charybdis of ‘the shrinking pipeline’ of women’s relative absence from the discipline (Camp, 1997; Panteli et al., 1999; Robertson et al., 1999). All this leads to the conclusion that gender as an analytical category in IS continues to be under-theorized (Adam, 2000; Wilson and Howcroft, 2000; Adam and Richardson, 2001; Adam et al., 2001).

A  nal point concerning the relative absence of gender and IS research emanating from within the IS discipline itself lies in the conclusion that this area needs to be informed by a more thoroughgoing theoretical perspective and, in particular, a perspective that places gender centre stage as an analytical category. One place to look for such a perspective is academic feminism, which has been a tremendous academic growth area in the last 20 years or more. As noted above, most ‘technical’ and mainstream disciplines shy away from feminist writing. It is not dif cult to see why. One reason lies in the assumption that feminist writing on gender is con ned to ‘women’s issues’ and, therefore, is not of interest to everyone. However, given that theorization of gender concerns the construction of masculinity as much as femininity, it becomes dif cult to sustain such a position, particularly in view of the wide range of literature on gender that now exists. In addition, but without intending to universalize the experiences of women, feminist writing starts from the base point that women generally stand in an inferior position to men in most walks of life. Whilst analysing and reporting, at the same time much feminist research looks to ways in which such situations might be alleviated. It is therefore a political project. This can be seen as threatening, particularly for areas such as IS, where some authors subscribe to a rhetoric that, as a relatively new discipline, it may have escaped the old discriminatory practices prevalent in longer established domains. For instance, Truman and Baroudi (1994) argued that ‘The prevailing consensus has cast the IS occupation as relatively immune from the serious discrimination patterns found in most other occupations . . .’ (p. 129). This may refer to the way in which new professions are often thought to be more equitable than older disciplines where discrimination may be thought of as more ingrained, but there is very little evidence to support Truman and Baroudi’s (1994) claim either way.

Contemporary feminism eschews a stark and nonanalysed view of oppression in terms of male oppressor and female oppressed, emphasizing instead the many

facets of oppression that clearly affect men as well as women. Feminist writing recognizes that direct oppression of one individual by another is much rarer than the structural oppressions of class, gender, race, religion and poverty, which affect both women and men and where there is no conspiracy on behalf of one gender for oppressing the other. Indeed, recognition and explication of such structural oppressions can at least sow the seeds of emancipation. Here this paper is attempting to present the extremes of the argument in order to  nd reasons why feminist thinking often appears threatening to mainstream writing. Indeed, as suggested above, the stereotypes promulgated by a major strand of gender and IS writing, i.e. statistical gender difference studies, are just as oppressive to men as they are to women. Hence, if we are willing to concede that race and class oppression exist and must similarly be addressed we must, as the contemporary mantra suggests, ‘face our fears’. It would be easy to view the ‘pipeline’ style and statistical research reported above as the acceptable face of feminism, where  gures and statistics can be endlessly analysed, thus postponing the requirement for looking at deeper underlying reasons for women’s absence from technical disciplines. Therefore, it would seem to be in everyone’s interests that gender relations are thoroughly explored and then couched in an appropriate theoretical framework. This is a more radical job but is ultimately more interesting and fruitful and is particularly pertinent for critical IS.

The particular focus of this paper is the concept of emancipation. It is suggested that feminist theory, feminist philosophy and, within that, feminist epistemology may be a fruitful starting place for analysing the problematic concept of emancipation, which has been imported into critical IS through critical management theory and the work of Habermas (1972). Habermas’ (1972) theory of knowledge-constitutive interests is a critique of epistemology that questions the validity of our knowledge of the world. The concept of emancipation is crucially linked to epistemology through the ideal of emancipatory interests overcoming technocracy. Yet feminist writers (Benhabib, 1986, 1992; Fraser, 1995; Meehan, 1995) have criticized Habermas (1972) for his tendency to universalize and also the gender blindness of his work. This paper analyses associated problems through  rst of all considering the ‘ideal speech situation’ of communicative rationality, which has proved an appealing idea in critical IS and then linking this to recent research in computer-mediated communications. Critical IS has appropriated the term ‘emancipation based on its adoption of an anti-positivist position based on Habermas’ (1972) critical theory. Although it might be tempting to regard the concept of emancipation as purely bene cial, this paper argues that a better understanding of the implications of this term can be made through a feminist analysis showing that we need a much more sophisticated understanding of the  ne structure of emancipation. This shows that attempts to universalize the concept are problematic. In particular this paper draws on feminist critiques of Habermas’ (1972) writing (Benhabib, 1986; Meehan, 1995) to argue that, just as feminist writers have criticized Habermas’ (1972) theories as being gender blind, there is a danger that this ‘gender blindness’, with its concomitant problems, may be imported by critical IS in its adherence to Habermas’ (1972) critical theory. In what follows this paper will argue in particular that the concept of ‘emancipation’ is problematic as calls for emancipation may actually mask existing power structures and, hence, reinforce them, offering little scope for emancipation in terms of those who are to be emancipated.

## Feminist philosophy and epistemology – a very short introduction

Space permits no more than the briefest introduction to feminist theory through feminist philosophy, which, this paper has argued, can provide a fruitful perspective from which to analyse contemporary issues in the foundations of IS and, in particular, crucial concepts in critical IS. Thinking broadly of the feminist movement, since the Second World War it has developed two distinctive branches, i.e. continental or what is sometimes termed ‘post-modern’ feminism and Anglo-American feminism (Hekman, 1990). Anglo-American feminism originated from the civil rights movement of the 1960s and 1970s within which the struggle for women’s liberation was prominent. Equal rights, equality, power struggles, inclusion, emancipation and liberation are all terms that inspire this strand of feminism. Hence, the foundations of this strand of feminist thinking belong more to the  ght for women’s equality with men than within post-modern feminism’s challenge to the rational order that men have apparently created. Continental or post-modern feminism rejects attempts at constructing universal theory and unitary truth, emphasizing instead situated knowledge and pluralistic discourse. As one of the major voices in post-modern feminism, Irigaray (1985) issued a radical call for ‘jamming the theoretical machinery’ (p. 78) (quoted in Hekman, 1990, p. 42). Nevertheless, many feminists feel that post-modern feminism’s de-emphasis of materialism draws attention away from real material oppression.

Feminist philosophy is one of the most theoretically developed parts of contemporary feminism and at the same time is one of the most important developments in contemporary philosophy, particularly at the radical end of the discipline. It has two major roles:  rst, to form a critique of mainstream philosophy and, second, to offer alternative theoretical positions, ones which often rest on feminine-inspired attributes such as intuition, collective responsibility and fairness rather than what are often perceived as masculine norms of individual rights and rationality (Tong, 1994). The two largest branches within feminist philosophy itself are feminist epistemology (Tong, 1993) and feminist ethics (Card, 1991). Feminist epistemology challenges traditional epistemology’s propensity to ignore the knowing subject and the contingency of knowledge. Feminist ethics challenge traditional ethical theories, particularly in their tendency for focusing on the individual rational moral agent at the expense of more collectivist approaches towards moral theory. Both feminist epistemology and feminist ethics have a bearing on the critique of Habermas’ (1972, 1984) view of epistemology and ethics. However, for brevity and clarity this paper concentrates on a more general introduction to a feminist critique of critical IS through aspects of feminist epistemology.

Although this paper does not have space for rehearsing the arguments in detail here, it must be emphasized that contemporary feminist writing argues strongly against tendencies for universalizing women’s experiences. For instance, early second-wave ‘women’s liberation’ was later criticized as being a White, Western, middle-class feminism of limited relevance to women of non-White and/or working-class origins.

At a time when new IS writing is beginning to locate itself within critical theory and when IS is developing a distinct critical voice, feminist philosophy as a major contemporary critical theory may offer an alternative critical approach. Second, in following the history of the turn of IS to critical theory it is clear that power, emancipation, participation, democracy and inclusion are concepts that inspire contemporary IS writers and these too are central concepts of feminist theories.

## The critical approach to information systems

The relatively new critical theory school in IS associated with Hirschheim et al. (1995, 1996) (see in particular Hirschheim, 1985) amongst others looks to the social theories of Habermas (1972) in arguing for communicative rationality as an alternative to the instrumental rationality of positivism. As technical knowledge interests have come to dominate society through ‘technocracy’ (i.e. expertise supported through a positivist view of science), critical debate that is free from all social pressure is required. Habermas (1984) described such debate as an ideal speech situation that is free from the distortions of unequal power and relationships. Individuals who gain the necessary cognitive and communicative skills for entering into such discourse acquire communicative competence. Improving mutual understanding and removing obstacles to human communication contributes to communicative rationality, within which the ideal speech situation is the central concept. Habermas’ (1984) characterization of communicative rationality involves the idea that rational consensus should not take place through the power of some individuals to persuade others, but rather because of the inherent cogency of a particular position. A position is cogent insofar as it explains the relationship between some observation and theory. Within such an approach there lies the possibility of freeing individuals from oppressive and unwarranted expressions of power – the emancipatory ideal. If such ideas can be translated into the design and development of real IS then the systems analyst can act as an emancipator rather than someone who imposes a technicist solution on a group of unwilling users.

Critical IS has arisen alongside the introduction of critical theory into management studies. This signals a move away from the stranglehold of scienti c management and an increasing interest in management as a social phenomenon (Alvesson and Willmott, 1992). In addition, the in uence of critical systems theory through the work of Flood and Jackson (1991a,b), Midgley (1992) and Flood and Romm (1996) and others must be noted although the literatures of critical systems theory and critical IS seem to run largely in parallel tracks with relatively few references from one to the other. A notable exception is Jackson (1997) who discussed ways in which critical systems thinking could inform IS and, in particular, critical IS.

## Feminist philosophy and emancipation

The concept of emancipation is a central part of the epistemology of the new critical IS. Yet the power structures that play such a large part in the making and acceptance of knowledge need more detailed analysis than is currently available within the new IS approaches.

Power relations will remain unbalanced if the systems designers cast themselves as the liberators or emancipators with a privileged epistemology based on the new critical IS storming the barricades of ignorance in emancipating the system users who perhaps did not even realize that they were candidates for emancipation. Hence, the will for a generalized non-analysed emancipation is not enough. Indeed, it may serve to reinforce rather than alleviate oppression if it leaves the material conditions that cause the oppression in the  rst place unexplored. Such a version of emancipation leans towards a form of liberalism that is unlikely to achieve its ideals unless we direct attention towards more detailed analysis of the power relations within organizations and the wider society that make emancipation seem so desirable in the  rst place.

Feminist philosophers such as Haraway (1991), Harding (1991) and Assiter (1996) have emphasized pluralism in epistemology. They argued that a viewpoint from an epistemic community committed to emancipatory values gives greater access to knowledge by bringing to light information previously hidden from view rather than viewpoints from other types of community. The implications of this claim in relation to communicative rationality and the ideal speech situation are explored in the remainder of the paper.

## A feminist critique of communicative rationality

Habermas (1984) argued that increased communicative rationality leads to increased opportunity for emancipation, yet many feminist linguists argue that speech situations for women may be far from ideal. For instance Spender’s (1980) research showed that women are interrupted more than men in public discourse. Despite popular conceptions of women’s ‘chattering’, Spender (1980) argued that men contribute more than women in public meetings and that women who take up the same time as men in speaking publicly are seen as ‘overcontributing’. Similarly, Tannen (1992) argued that women and men have different speech styles. Women are often more supportive in conversation and men are often more abrupt.

These concerns have been taken up in the rhetoric surrounding computer-mediated communications, particularly in writing that idealizes the Utopian potential of the Internet for acting as an instrument of communicative rationality for an ideal speech situation (Sproull and Kessler, 1991). Ess (1996) emphasized that non-analysed claims as to what is meant by democracy tend to weaken claims for the Internet’s potential for achieving a democratic polity along the lines that Habermas (1972) outlined in his critical theory. Indeed, it is hard to over-emphasize the power of the rhetoric that computer-mediated communications will spontaneously generate democracy. This has been a vital current in writings on cyberculture and cyberfeminism (Rheingold, 1993; Adam, 1998) and in Winner’s (1997) warnings of the dangers of ‘cyberlibertarianism’. We need to be very wary of crude liberal and even libertarian positions on democracy and freedom as they may disguise something much more unpleasant underneath. The banding together of like-minded individuals and the exclusion of others in Internet communities may serve to reinforce intolerance and prejudice rather than creating Utopian communities (Winner, 1997). This mirrors the position taken in the present paper that an under-theorized notion of emancipation cannot be pressed into service for all groups who might require it.

For instance Herring (1996) pointed to the way in which communications on the Internet magnify stereotypical male and female interactions into  aming and supportive interactions, respectively, rather than somehow smoothing it out and making all voices equally heard. Sexual harassment on the Internet is problematic enough, but extreme versions in the form of ‘cyberstalking’ can result (Burt, 1997; Packard, 2000; Adam, 2001). In the end Ess (1996) realized that democratization via computer-mediated communications along ethical and political dimensions cannot take place without attention to ethical and social aspects in order to include under-represented voices such as those of women and minorities.

This short discussion barely scratches the surface of the subtleties of male and female communicative interaction. Yet unless there is some understanding of its complexities it is hard to see how communicative rationality can be achieved through an ideal speech situation in the world of IS. We must accept that ideal speech situations are not the same for all. More complex analyses are required in order to explore the ways in which concepts such as democracy and emancipation are not equally divided.

## Exploring feminist epistemology and its implications for emancipatory values

When thinking about the potential for feminist philosophy in analysing the  ne structure of the problem of emancipation, feminist epistemology may be particularly pertinent. Assiter’s (1996) arguments are important in this respect. She claimed that the major value of feminist epistemology is in its questioning of the ways in which traditional theories of knowledge regard the business of knowing as independent of social and cultural context, as in the ‘view from nowhere’ described at the beginning of the paper. In contrast, a feminist approach argues for a situated understanding of knowledge. Yet the realist element must not be lost as it is vital to recognize the reality of the oppression of various social groups. A completely relativist approach, which is a danger if we go too far in completely rejecting positivism, has no means of recognizing the reality of oppression and, therefore, the will towards emancipation is fatally undermined. Attempts to denounce positivism in the pursuit of interpretivism that have marked philosophical debate in IS (Fitzgerald and Howcroft, 1998) may well lead us in this direction.

What is the link between emancipatory values and knowledge? Some feminist writers have particularly emphasized the way that taking the viewpoint of subjugated groups in expressing their will towards emancipation seriously may be taken to open up new forms of knowledge. Haraway (1991) and Harding (1991) argued that a standpoint taken from women’s lives and a commitment towards emancipatory values for women leads to more knowledge, adding a dimension to knowledge that is hidden from the viewpoint of a ‘value-free’ abstract science. For instance, witness the way that the discovery of the importance of ‘woman the gatherer’ in addition to ‘man the hunter’ has changed our view of our evolutionary history (Haraway, 1989; Hrdy, 1999). Second, Gilligan’s (1982) research on women’s moral evolution challenged earlier views that women were morally less mature than men, thus changing our views of moral development.

So it seems that writers on feminist epistemology are in agreement with Habermas (1972) that the model for a good science (e.g. in this case IS) should be guided by liberatory, social goals and interests and a commitment to such goals reveals gaps, continuities and partialities in traditional accounts of knowledge. This aspect of revealing the hidden is particularly important in feminist thinking. Repressive values can blinker this. Those who are committed to the emancipation of a particular group may offer us radical insights into the world that may be of value to others, not just those in that chosen group. Knowledge can advance in this way by a window onto new truths. However, in following such a process we cannot claim that emancipatory values always lead to knowledge, rather that the possibilities for new knowledge are opened up.

Haraway (1991) and Harding (1991) argued that those who are in a ‘subaltern’ position, for instance women and excluded from dominant groups are in a better position for articulating emancipatory values that will ultimately lead to knowledge. This echoes older Marxist thinking that looks to the proletariat for a truer picture of the world. Yet the feminist position makes no truth claims, rather it is arguing for the knowledge of oppressed groups to be taken seriously, on a par with the knowledge of other groups. Although in agreement with Habermas (1972) in some aspects this seems to cut across his attempts to universalize. Emancipation as part of everyday relations, he argued, at the same time reaf rms common humanity through universality (Benhabib, 1986). The point about the feminist argument is not that we should lose sight of common humanity, which is the universal aspect that we share, but rather that we must recognize how a pluralism of epistemological understanding contributes to this common humanity.

## Conclusion – how can feminist epistemology serve information systems?

It is not immediately clear how critiques of Habermas (1972, 1984) and, in particular, the problem of emancipation can have a practical bearing on critical IS. Nevertheless, there are ways in which these concepts can offer lessons. First of all they imply that attention to social and cultural context is paramount. This serves to underline the commitment of IS to position itself within the social/cultural arena, in contrast to the more formal stance of traditional computer science. Second, feminist theory’s calls to retain both realism and emancipation are in accord with current approaches to IS, where unbridled relativism seems impossible to maintain alongside calls for emancipation. These two aspects are already part of the rationale of much writing in IS. However, a particular emphasis stems from the third message from feminist epistemology relating to Haraway (1991) and Harding’s (1991) emphasis of the knowledge of subordinate groups in developing their own approaches towards their emancipation. Applying this to IS reveals at least two aspects. Communicative rationality through ideal speech situations as described above may be quite differently construed within a subordinate group. Second, this speci cally challenges the whole notion of the systems analyst as emancipator. It suggests further that managers, systems analysts and those in high positions in the organizational hierarchy cannot easily achieve emancipation of workers lower in the hierarchy. Rather we must look to the subordinate groups themselves for an expression of their own emancipatory values through their own knowledge. This is a much stronger claim than the more liberally inspired ‘user participative’ approaches often found in IS.

One way forward involves a return to the ‘feminist theory’-inspired IS projects described at the beginning of this paper. Bjerknes and Bratteteig’s (1987) Florence project, Vehviläinen’s (1997) study circles and Green et al.’s (1993) libraries project are examples of emancipatory critical feminist projects in the design of IS that may be recast as prototype feminist critical IS projects in order to offer signposts for future work. In the Florence project the dialogue between the computer scientist and nurses revealed a con ict between nurses and doctors over which patient reporting system to use, the nuances of which would have been lost had the proposed IS been developed in conjunction with the more powerful doctors rather than with the nurses. Vehvilainen (1997) offered a feminist methodology for the design of IS that is rooted in the notion of a situated politics of ‘one’s own de nition’. This translates into the practical technique of study circles. Vehviläinen (1997) used this technique in an empirical study set in a large government department in Finland in the 1980s.

## Exploring the gender question in critical IS

Although the rhetoric of user participation was forming in IS development by the time of Vehviläinen’s (1997) research, studies of of ce automation tended to emphasize negative impacts on workers. Vehviläinen (1997) developed a study circle against a tradition of working life democracy in Nordic countries of ten women of ce workers who met regularly in order to support each other in developing knowledge and skills in IS. Green et al. (1993) built on Vehviläinen’s (1997) method in their libraries project where they formed study circles of women library assistants who were consulted on the development of a new library catalogue system. Neither Green et al. (1993) nor Vehviläinen (1997) claimed that the study circle is a perfect method for IS development. Nevertheless, the technique shows some interesting features that marry aspects of feminist epistemology to critical IS. The study circle took the women’s point of view as a standpoint and was therefore very much in the spirit of the ‘subaltern’ standpoint that Haraway (1991) and Harding (1991) argued is important in creating new and liberatory forms of knowledge, which is a major theme in feminist epistemology. This was particularly important for the library assistants in Green et al.’s (1993) study. The library assistants were almost all women and were on much lower grades than the managerial staff, many of whom were men. The study circle ‘broke the bureaucratic deadlock which had threatened to sti e debate concerning new library systems’ (Green et al., 1993, p. 140). It was also the  rst time that large numbers of women library assistants took part in a detailed analysis of information technology systems in this workplace. Although it would be dif- cult to claim this was an ideal speech situation it was clearly much more ideal than previous dialogues where systems had been imposed on the women library workers. The separation of women from male workers was important in these studies, as was the intervention of the feminist academics, who could be tentatively cast as the ‘emancipators’.

The aim of this paper is not to recast these studies in ways that their authors would not recognize. Rather, it emphasizes that we must not lose their valuable lessons, in particular the ways in which these can inform critical IS. We must  nd space to consider them in the mainstream of IS, to take seriously the idea that feminist theory has much to offer the critical approach to IS and to explore this in a more thorough way than can be accomplished within this space and,  nally, to provide further practical demonstrations of how this may be achieved.
