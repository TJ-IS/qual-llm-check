# A theoretical review of management and information systems using a critical communications theory

RICHARD J. VAREY AND TREVOR WOOD-HARPER Information Systems Research Institute, University of Salford, Salford M5 4WT, UK

BOB WOOD

GEMISIS Programme

This paper re ects on the managerialistic orthodoxy of knowledge management in order to show that a critical communications theory is required for addressing real political and ethical shortcomings. This produces an alternative methodological perspective through an intentional synthesis of established methodological views. The paper’s allies in this critical quest include Jürgen Habermas, Werner Ulrich, Stanley Deetz, Geoffrey Vickers, Peter Checkland and their mentors. Information systems and knowledge systems archi tects and engineers and their manager clients conveniently ignore fundamental issues, including politics, power, knowledge and communication. Yet, today the more substantive issues are not technical but ethical. In raising questions about the rhetoric of knowledge management re ections on the instrumentality of much of what is said and done about management and information systems are outlined. The departure point is critical scepticism. This is motivated by concerns for the ethical status of the commercially valuable outcome of (at least) two conjoined simplistic and fundamentally dominatory conventional wisdoms. These stem from two  elds that are managerialistically biased and which share a common basis in a false rationality.

## Introduction

Critical theory makes a substantial contribution to understanding communication by conceptualizing the nature of information as relational, i.e. a property that is not solely of a message. ‘Knowing’ is an active, constructive process (Hosking and Morley, 1991). More (1999) highlighted the general absence of a clear conception of communication in studies of knowledge management. Choo’s (1998) sophisticated model of knowledge management is a stark example of this – that author did not  nd it appropriate (necessary?) to index the term ‘communicate’. Indeed, a communication concept is implicit in most work in this  eld and it is this conception that is a root problem.

The adoption of critical theory provides a means of viewing again the notion of knowledge management in terms of the ethics of human communication and allows the questioning of the centrality of rational decision making as the basis of managing. The provision of information systems/information technology (IS/IT) as a neutral support mechanism is premised on a positivistic ‘scienti c’ rationality, thereby reducing communication to no more than the distribution of information. The epistemological and ontological commitments of those who claim to know (the needs of management) are characterized as alternative ways of knowing. A post-positivist way of knowing in IS research and practice is outlined as a way of producing richer accounts and understandings. The adoption of a critical communications theory brings forth recognition of the inherently political nature of communication and highlights that what is at stake in IS/IT research and practice is the production of information and knowledge and not merely pro cient information distribution.

This conceptual and methodological review produces the conclusion that a multi-method approach is more capable of producing a rich picture of ‘knowledge management’ as a facet of a responsive and responsible management system. The implications of the proposed critical–interpretive–positive way of knowing for addressing managerial concerns of cost, complexity and con ict are highlighted.

## Managing knowledge

When is the social role of an IS debated among the various stakeholders who will have their particular relationships privileged or punished? If the IS is taken to be a representative (of an ‘of cial’ reality) or tool (for unre ective and particular rational activity), then human activities are forced into a mould that distorts human experience, thereby resulting in alienation of the user (see Robb, 1997; Spaul, 1997).

Knowledge is a social process phenomenon that develops collectively from group relationships and patterns of communication. The notion of knowledge has an inherently ethical dimension – knowing what is true and good (proper conduct) has been held to be a virtue throughout Western civilization. When decision making is taken as the control, ‘informing’ will be no more than the rhetoric of unilateralism (Argyris, 1994). This is fostered when knowledge management succumbs to further pursuit of the illusion of control in rationalist science and the ‘omniscient monovocal narrative of scienti c authority’ (Star, 1996, p. 304) shouts down ethical questions. This should not be allowed to prevail – technicist rationalism has failed to account for or to prescribe people’s behaviour.

Social systems exist as a set of interaction rules in the minds of the humans who play the roles of the contrived system of role relationships (Weick, 1979). People construct relationships with each other as a social mirror for viewing themselves (Cooley, 1968). They do not communicate with each other directly, but through the relationships they mutually construct (Pearce, 1976).

The decision-making process is ‘A complicated drama which involves power, in uence, negotiation, game playing, organisational politics, complex social relationships with real people, not merely of ce holders’ (Eden and Sims, 1979, p. 120). The making of a decision is the selection of some interpretation of the world and some set of extrapolations from that interpretation and the use of these summaries as constraints on subsequent action (Weick, 1979). Action is purposive behaviour in following the chosen course of action implied by a number of decisions. Understanding and knowledge are necessary but not suf cient, since there is still the need to choose from available options and the need to commit to a single way of acting. Decision making in social groups is inherently political, being concerned with multiple actors’ interests, valuations (of descriptions) and projects (taking action to avoid threat or create opportunity) (Hosking and Morley, 1991).

Managing is supposed to be decision making in pursuit of declared goals (Simon, 1960). Organizing (i.e. what ‘managers’ do) is a form of political decision making. Actors’ constructions differ from those of others with whom they are interdependent. The political processes of decision making are those within which persons and groups to some degree collectively construct commitments to particular descriptions, relationships and activities. The relational processes of networking and negotiation are processes of power and in uence (Lewis, 1991; Checkland and Holwell, 1998; Choo, 1998). Decision making is a purposive activity – political in resource-driven systems and dialogical in knowledge-based systems. The decision role requires the translation of ‘information’ into knowledge and commitments to courses of rational action. However, note should be taken of Checkland and Holwell’s (1998) realization that goal seeking is merely a special not a general case of the relationship-managing (social) system of the corporation (as detailed by Vickers (1984, 1995)). Information is needed for sense making, but decision making is merely a special case and not a general basis for the actual everyday operation of the corporate social system.

## Information systems and information technology

There appears to be evidence of less willingness in the rather younger (than management) technology-driven  eld of IS for examining epistemological assumptions in the academy than there has been in the management discipline. This has revealed a rather familiar malaise in IS/IT thinking in the recent critical reviews by, for example, Lyytinen (1992), Checkland and Holwell (1998) and Ulrich (2001). The IS  eld deals with technologies for organization and management. The design of systems has been a preoccupation, resulting in a range of prescribed formulaic methods and prescriptive accounts for introducing and operating IT-based decision support, payrolls, personnel records, sales, customer contact and so on.

The  eld has been dominated by engineering and managerial thinking about information processing and decision making. The ‘strategic’ purpose of converging information and communications technologies has been to bring about more ef cient business transaction processing. The management discourse has of course almost universally been that of ‘scienti c management’. For example, Checkland and Holwell’s (1998) review revealed the dominant in uence of Simon’s (1960) notion of rational organization.

The  eld, particularly among practitioners, remains dominated by an instrumental rationality bias that is premised on an assumed need for and attraction to technical control. Some notable contributors to the IS academy, including Weizenbaum (1976), Hirschheim and Klein (1989), Lyytinen (1992), Winograd and Flores (1987) and, in particular, Nodoushani (1999) in the  eld of social systems sciences, have sought to counter the obsessive instrumental pursuit for ‘effective IS that characterizes the computer science  eld. This body of critical work has questioned assumptions about knowledge, information, organization and human behaviour. Social systems have been distinguished from technical systems and concepts of symbolic interaction and action as sense making have been explored. Some contributors have recognized that an IS can be a means of generating discursive examination (for example, in on-line conferencing) and not just a provider of input (i.e. information) into ‘communication’. The application of social theory by those specializing in computer-supported cooperative working is particularly heartening (see, for example, the work of Ciborra (1987, 1992, 1998), Bannon (1990) and Walsham (1993)). Yet, as with the management community, there is a crucial need for a critical analysis.

The convergence of management and information and communication technology (ICT) seems to be coming about through a natural attraction of purposes and applications, but the contemporary conjoining does not necessarily produce a viable solution to the clear problem that is supposed to be addressed. A ‘hard systems’ (functional) conventional wisdom serves up the computer as an electronic version of a bureaucracy. The mechanistic model of rational decision making in the pursuit of goals is intuitively clear-cut, convincing and easily taught (Dahlbom and Mathiassen, 1993; Checkland and Holwell, 1998)! It is also fundamentally and unre ectively dominatory. The ef cient processing and distribution of information is rede ned as ‘communication’ and, thus, appears to be a ‘good thing’, yet hides the dominatory nature of a command and control regime.

Managers readily adopt ICT and become overly expectant of and dependent on computers because of a pervasive particular view of technology that largely excludes ethical considerations. Weizenbaum’s (1976) history of ‘computers’ examined the nature of computers and their place in human affairs. He categorically rejected ‘instrumental reason’ in application to human affairs, prophesying that computers will never emulate human competence in common sense, creative imagination and moral decision making based on compassion and judgement because they are fundamentally data processors. The extravagant prophecies of the early days of computing will never be matched by accomplishments in arti cial intelligence and so on, so we are morally wrong to have what he saw as excessive faith in and dependence on the technology. However hard we wish computers (now ICT) will not give us magical answers to the problems that we or they create.

## Ways of knowing the social world

In applying theories to knowing in our actions, we make assumptions about the world (reality) and about our knowledge. All inquiry is fundamentally rooted in underlying assumptions about what constitutes ‘valid knowledge and which methods of production are appropriate. These are ontological and epistemological (i.e. methodological) in nature, referring to the assumptions about knowledge and how ‘it’ can be obtained.

Burrell and Morgan (1979) laid out a subjectivist–objectivist epistemological dimension and an order–con ict ontological dimension. Using this framework for simplifying the argument, four positions on the nature of social science and the nature of that which is studied (society) can be summarized.

(1) The objectivist position: in applying models and methods from the natural sciences to studying human affairs, the social world is treated as if it is the natural world.

(2) The subjectivist position: in denying the appropriateness of natural science methods for studying the social world, it seeks to understand human life from the subjective experience of persons as they create, modify and interpret their world.

(3) The integrationist position: this emphasizes order, stability, integration, consensus and functional coordination in the social world.

(4) The coercionist position: this emphasizes change, con ict, disintegration and coercion.

Four ‘paradigms’ of inquiry are discerned from this analysis (Figure 1).

The functionalist paradigm is concerned with explaining social order, integration, consensus, need satisfaction and rational choice, i.e. how individual parts of a social system interact to form an integrated whole.

The social relativist paradigm seeks explanation within the frame of reference of the meanings, consciousness and subjectivity of the social actor rather than the observer of social action.

The radical structuralist paradigm focuses on the analysis of structure and economic power relationships, emphasizing the need for transcending the limitations on existing social arrangements.

The neohumanist paradigm seeks radical change and emancipation from distorted communication and power, stressing the role of social forces in understanding change.

These paradigms provide a web of commonsense beliefs and background knowledge (Quine and Ullian, 1970) that serve as ‘theories of action’ (Argyris and Schon, 1974). The functionalist approach has dominated in setting the agenda for what constituted acceptable research, perhaps with a disproportionate in uence on IS (emerging out of computer science and engineering) through management (and other social sciences, e.g. organization) studies. Positivism rules as received wisdom – ‘publish positivism or perish’ (Goles and Hirschheim, 2000)! Ulrich (2001) pointed out the confusion of non-technical rationality with nonrationality (i.e. the belief that rational thinking can only be explained in terms of empirical–analytic science).

Hirschheim and Klein (1989) showed that the four ‘paradigms’ are not clear-cut through their review of IS development practices using generalized story types (genres) (see also Goles and Hirschheim, 2000). They overlapped and their differences were overstated in Burrell and Morgan’s (1979) original presentation. They are archetypes rather than paradigms that do not exist as real entities, i.e. they are ways of performing research (i.e. strategies). It is the properties that are exhibited to a greater or lesser extent in alternative conceptions of and approaches to systems development.

Hirschheim and Klein (1989) identi ed another way of explaining the four alternative conceptions of and approaches to systems development.

(1) Instrumental reasoning: the system developer as an expert Platonic philosopher king following the scienti c method of positivistic means in a ‘mental cage’ of functionalist tenets.

(2) Sense making: the developer as an uncritical anti-positivist catalyst or facilitator of a social construction of reality through social interaction.

(3) Dialectic materialism: the developer as a positivist warrior for social progress or a partisan of workers.

(4) Emancipation through rational discourse: the developer as an emancipator or social therapist, positivist for technical control and anti-positivist for emancipation.

From this three categories of knowledge production based on the underlying ontology and epistemology are considered here: positive, interpretive and critical. These approaches are philosophically distinct (as ideal types), but in the practice of social science these distinctions are not always so clear-cut (Lee, 1989). There is considerable disagreement as to whether these knowledge paradigms are necessarily opposed or whether they can be accommodated within a single study.

Positive researchers generally assume that reality is objectively observable and can be described by measurable properties that are independent of the observer’s instruments. Positivist studies generally attempt to test theory in an attempt to increase the predictive understanding of phenomena. Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991, p. 5) classi ed IS studies as positivist when they included formal propositions, quanti able measures of variables, hypothesis testing and the drawing of inferences about a phenomenon from a sample to a de ned population.

Interpretive researchers assume that reality can only be accessed through social constructions such as language, consciousness and shared meanings. Interpretive studies generally attempt to understand phenomena by observing and describing the meanings that people assign to them and interpretive methods of research in IS are used for producing an understanding of the social context of the IS and the way the IS in uences and is in uenced by the context. Interpretive research does not prede ne dependent and independent variables, but focuses on the full complexity of human sense making as the situation emerges.

Critical researchers (see, for example, Habermas, 1972; Alvesson and Willmott, 1992, 1996; Deetz, 1992, 1995; Calhoun, 1995; Stainton Rogers et al., 1995) have assumed that social reality is historically constituted and that it is produced and reproduced by people and they have worked at systematically uncovering the presuppositions and implications of knowledge claims. The inherent connection of interests, values and knowledge is recognized. Critical researchers recognize that, although people can consciously act to change their social and economic circumstances, their ability to do so is constrained by various forms of social, cultural and political domination. The main task of critical research is social critique, so that restrictive and alienating conditions of the status quo are brought to light. Critical research focuses on the oppositions, con icts and contradictions in contemporary society and seeks emancipation for helping with eliminating the causes of alienation and domination by working against instrumental reason.

## Effort to  esh out a post-positivist way of knowing for information systems research and practice

Now this paper considers the multi-approach route to knowledge and the consequences and implications of how communication is conceived.

A basically divided society of two tribes can be discerned. Some people take a functionalist view of the social world of management. Simon’s (1960) work exempli ed the notion that managing is problem solving through decision making. An IS serves this aim. This is premised on the logic that human behaviour is goal seeking and that problems (to be managed, i.e. solved) are indicated by gaps between performance and goals. This approach has been almost overwhelmingly in uential and continues to form the basis for most explanations of managing and organizing.

Vickers (1965), on the other hand, took a fundamentally different view of human action and rejected the goal-seeking model as being able to match the richness of life. Building on systems ideas, he proposed that personal or collective human regulators choose one of several possible mixes of courses of action. The standards by which these are judged are generated internally by the previous history of the social system and interactions with the environment. Rather than striving for goal attainment, the actions taken are to maintain or elude relationships. Thus, according to Vickers (1965), goal seeking is only an occasional special case of managing relationships. From an interpretivist perspective, ‘organizations’ are networks of communicative exchanges that are facilitated by computer-based IS (that support the exchanges) (Ciborra, 1987).

The technical rationality adopted by managers and IT/IS designers generates a mutually reinforcing view of management and organization that is supported by IS and communication systems as a technology for ef - ciency achieved through the control of information. Yet, those who adhere to the orthodoxy do not generally have to justify their position, whereas non-positivists are largely expected to do so.

The social science problem is one of how to produce the ‘mutual understanding’ form of knowledge of processes that produce the meaningful experience of people (as social beings) living in and connected to a social and cultural context (frame of reference), while recognizing the inherent connection of knowledge with values and interests (politics).

Post-positivism is a movement (a belief about knowledge) that hopes to transcend the limitations of the positivism that is used for scienti cally studying human beings as if they were not social beings. Bhaskar’s (1989) critical realism was formulated in order to transcend the methodological war of an either/or choice of empiricism (objectivism) that favours experience over understanding or rationalism (subjectivism) that favours understanding over experience. Thus, a synthesis of conceptual understanding and experience is sought in a both/and way of inquiring. This accepts that knowledge is produced by the conjunction of experience and reason (i.e. empirically and rationally).

That practical (acceptability to all parties concerned) and instrumental (utility or success) reasons are complementary is the basis for re ective practice. Applied sciences tend to reduce practical rationality to instrumental rationality, thus capturing only the expediency of actions for attaining the chosen ends of those in control of actions. Practical reason is seen as merely utilitarian. Habermas (1984) observed that ‘rationality has less to do with the possession of knowledge than with how speaking and acting subjects acquire and use knowledge’ (p. 8).

This paper proposes methodological sophistication in applying four recursive (inclusive, transcending, ‘building up’ and subsuming) epistemological positions in producing a comprehensive understanding. There is no dichotomy or hierarchy implying superiority in this framework (based on Ulrich’s (2001) discussion of Habermas’ (1984) theory of communicative action).

(1) Philosophical: questions are asked about the assumptions and methods of proof in generating the knowledge (critical).

(2) Critical: values are examined in order to enable ethical judgements, taking account of historical, political, economic and social contexts (critical).

(3) Interpretivist/Constructivist/Humanistic/‘Soft’: reality is a subjective interpretation by human beings in contact with an external reality that produces meanings in social interaction (strategic).

(4) Objectivist/Positivist/Scienti c/‘Hard’: the ‘real world’ is composed on concrete artefacts and relationships that can be objectively observed (instrumental).

This can be visualized in a Venn diagram in order to show the relationships of the three complementary ways of knowing in terms of common properties (Figure 2).

It is not assumed that a single paradigm will reveal all the features of the organizing of social actions (management) in proposing a system of reaching understanding as a critical theorist. Each way of knowing presupposes the other.

Value can be created from the re exive methodological synthesis of moving between paradigms rather than the dogmatic choice of a single approach that closes off other possibilities and thereby limits, distorts or obscures our view of relationships of people, IS, social collectivities and society. Habermas (1972, p. 67), for example, retained an objectivist ontology and accused positivist epistemology of serving to immunize adherents from epistemological self-re ection through its inherent ‘prohibitive function’ by barring debate.

Ulrich (2001) extended this, suggesting that the pressure for explaining rationality in terms of empirical– analytic science immunizes utilitarian rationality against the efforts of practical reason.

Presently accepted research methods may never have been appropriate for the study of IS (Hirschheim, 1985). The presently accepted (and taken for granted) conception of science and derived understandings of IS for managing may not be suf cient for a ‘good life’. Hirschheim (1985, p. 12) was concerned that researchers were no more than ‘tool users’, when they needed to be ‘tool builders’. ‘Scienti c managers’ are also tool users (review your MBA syllabus in order to con rm this).

No single approach to knowing can adequately treat all human problems. Each has strengths and weaknesses. Following the logic of designing an inquiry (see, for example, Robson, 1993) will always lead to the adoption of an approach that is appropriate to the question asked. However, people have other motives for taking a particular approach. We have (and need) several ways of knowing: tradition, authority, belief and intuition, magic and science.

Particular ways dominate as dogmatic orthodoxies that lead to overly one-sided competitive denigration of other ways of thinking (a recent example is Locke et al.’s (2000) attack on Hoffman and Novak’s (1996) model of electronic commerce). Protagonists may well not appreciate the strengths of opposing approaches.

Firestone (1990) suggested that, in practical terms, the walls between paradigms break down and recommended methodological pluralism for maximizing the value produced by bringing together the complementary strengths. Each of the three practical approaches has safeguards for regulating objectivity (not objectivism), with norms for proceeding with a particular form of inquiry, with differing results. Worked together, positivism shows us the exterior of a social system, whilst interpretivism shows us the interior (Firestone, 1990). Critical theory is used for transforming the system into a better reality.

Guba (1990) suggested that the critical theory paradigm is a combination of an essentially realist ontology with a subjectivist epistemology. Goles and Hirschheim (2000) explained Habermas’ (1984) critical theory as encompassing empirical (functionalist) knowledge, hermeneutic (interpretivist) knowledge and critical (radical humanist) knowledge. Other approaches, such as action research, also call forth the assumptions of more than one so-called paradigm. This represented in Figure 3 (see also Johnson and Duberley (2000, p. 153) for a slightly different presentation of Bhaskar’s (1989) synthesis).

Hirschheim (1985) pointed out the post-positivist belief in ‘methodological pluralism’, requiring not one correct method of knowledge production, but many methods to be selected as appropriate for the problem to be studied and the kind of knowledge desired (Polkinghorne, 1983).

## A critical communications theory

Whilst ‘commonsense’ explanations of information, knowledge, communication, learning, organization and management unre ectively centre on instrumental rationality, the analysis here reveals an underlying communications theory for each movement in understanding the management of knowledge (and the knowledge valued for managing). Critical communications theory opens up the knowledge management rhetoric to reveal the damaging mendacity of technical– rational (uncritical) management.

Each of these approaches proffers a particular way of explaining (understanding) information, knowledge, communication and management. Three conceptions of communication are discerned. The technical rationality of positivism provides a transmission explanation of communication. The interpretive approach explains communication as an intersubjective interpretation through interaction. Critical theory requires an ethical communication system (Table 1).

A particular conception of communication is needed for supporting each position: the higher practical level is the critical communications theory that introduces ethical considerations (there may well be a philosophical further level). The IS is then designed for enabling undistorted communication through intersubjective discursive inquiry, resulting in negotiated consensus rather than the pretence of politically neutral knowledge of an object as part of means–ends calculations.

This paper comes to take a critical stance on the question of managers’ motivations for adopting intranet technology for managing knowledge. Prior investment in intranet technology leads managers to seeing knowledge management as a way of exploiting this ‘sunk cost’. Such an investment is invariably premised on the rationality of automation for ef ciency in the pursuit of cost reduction. An intranet is closed, thereby implying that knowledge resides within the organization boundaries, whereas an extranet supports the production of knowledge through interaction in the wider environment. Knowledge is an emergent property of a social system.

What is required is a productive rather than a reproductive conception of communication as the fundamental process by which mutual understanding arises with regard to the subject matter (rather than in the sharing of opinions) – communication is about trans-formation, not in-formation. Entitive ‘message sending’ (telementation, i.e. putting ideas into words (messages) and sending them to the minds of others) (Harris, 1981) is not accepted as the necessary basis for improving the human condition, in spite of the conception of communication as the transportation of information prevailing as the basis for most of what is said and done in ‘management’. This research examined the discom ture of the dominant technicist thinking in search of underlying values and aspirations. It was of course found that people communicating is not merely a technical problem.

Communication is taken to be the social phenomenon that arises at the interfaces of the managing process that is enabled (even enhanced) by IS/ICT. Deetz (1992, 1995, 1999) showed that the traditional concepts of language, experience and communication are  awed as part of an inadequate liberal democracy. Communication is not merely the expression of knowledge, experience and identity. It is the very basis of their social construction (see also Gergen (1999) for an elaboration of this perspective). The adoption of particular concepts of communication and democracy in management practice and in management studies has been politically motivated. Mattelart (1996) showed a historical dimension to how ‘communication’ is imagined. Managerial positivism is a particular utilitarianism that searches for technological solutions and is concerned solely with maximizing resource use and the means for achieving given goals. A theory may be (politically) selected for misdirecting observation, thereby functioning to aid dominant groups. So-called ‘scienti c procedures’ suppress negotiation and avoid their own examination. Certain groups are disfavoured by asymmetrical power relations and dominating ideas. Yet con ict and dissent can generate new and different consensuses. Today, at the dawn of the ‘third millennium’, we are moving once again towards communication as an exchange or ‘connectedness’ (Mattelart, 1996) that produces a third way of knowing (Shotter, 1993) and away from a ‘communication’ that has an instrumental image (Mattelart and Mattelart, 1998).

(1) The management of cost is enacted in the pursuit of productivity in the ef cient allocation of scarce resources: this is an instrumental rationality that is found in the hard systems tradition. The justifying logic is optimization.

(2) The management of complexity is enacted in the pursuit of strategic advantage and competitive success in the effective direction of complex systems: this is a strategic rationality that is found in the hard systems tradition. The justifying logic is leadership (steering) in the face of uncertainty and change.

(3) The management of con ict is enacted in the pursuit of mutual understanding in the social integration of con icting interests: this is a communicative rationality that is found in the critical systems tradition. The justifying logic is a capability for interaction and opportunities for discourse.

(4) Solutions to problems of the past (hierarchical bureaucracy in command and control cultures) are now outmoded. Deetz (1995) urged the adoption of ‘communication’ as both a phenomenon for analysis and a mode of analysis. Ordinary ‘communication’ is explained as discourse: ‘knowledge is fundamentally discursive, at least in the critical sense that claims to knowledge must always remain open to argumentative challenge’ (Ulrich, 2001, p. 74).

Deetz (1992) proposed a stakeholder model of representation in community decision making that inherently provides forums of involvement and stakeholder voice. The common problem is not an inadequate distribution of information, but rather in processes of information and knowledge production and decisions about how to act. In participation in the choice of questions, conceptions, research practices, publication and interpretation there are issues of power at each point and claims of truth and expertise obscure the process.

The search for best practices in achieving ‘good’ communication has diverted attention from the ethical question of participation. ‘Good communication’ effectively controls, diffuses, colonizes and integrates (standardizes) (Deetz, 1992). Communication effectiveness is gauged as reproductive  delity.

However, participation is an unful lled anticipated normative condition in every act of communication. Yet real communication dif culties are found in certain types of practice.

(1) Those that preclude value debate and con ict by asserting a speaker’s opinion over the attempt to reach a mutual understanding of subject matter.

(2) Those that arbitrarily limit access to channels and forums for this purpose.

(3) Those that conceal self-presentation and negotiable truth claims, thus avoiding concept formation as the working out of common meaning.

(4) Those that lead to decisions based on arbitrary authority relationships.

Managerialistic behaviour has the power to preclude certain voices. Ideological domination prevents dialogue and decision making favours some people over others. An unchallenged ‘managerial logic’ can operate, which may not be intentional, but damaging: ‘If we manage in this way what kind of people will we become?’ (Deetz, 1985, p. 257).

When communication is taken to be dominationfree dialogue within the system, interactions are seen as productive rather than content taken as effective. An unethical communication system (Deetz, 1992) has arbitrary restraints on interaction, thereby preventing undistorted expression of interests and the formation of consensus.

In everyday life, manager groups and technical– instrumental reasoning are often (mostly) privileged. Discourse is restricted. Distortion is not a simple mismatch between a  xed interest and a  xed expression of it. Certain forms of reasoning and articulation are dominant: this one-sidedness is a common property of human communication.

The denial of democracy is an everyday occurrence not necessarily done for the purpose of control, for example it may be institutionalized and systemic rather than intentional in everyday work. A web of strategic moves of asymmetrical power-based relationships is enacted through discursive practices. Managed (corporate) rational discourse is needed in order to avoid managerialistic (the traditional ‘corporate’) communication. Indeed, Deetz (1992, 1995, 1999) went further. He proposed participatory democracy as a normative foundation for communication studies. This has clear implications for decision support systems, since the very nature of a decision-making process is to be questioned. Knowledge management must ‘design out’ self referential closed distorted communication systems based on expressive individualism and self-interest advocacy. In its place the decision support system must enable autonomy and collective decision making. The design of IS and communication systems could be a (much more) re ective practice.

## Conclusions

This paper has drawn from a common interest in critical re ection on management, organization, communication, IS, learning and knowledge. The impetus was twofold. The reading of a text on ‘interaction strategies’ for health professionals unnervingly revealed a ‘theory’ of human communication as a technology for objective informing that seems incapable of producing the higher-order ‘connection’ outcomes required in situations of illness and trauma. This is not an isolated example, with a transmissive conception of communication prevailing in the management literature. Prior discussions on the advent of customer relationship management systems, which exhibited an impoverished notion of relationships, have produced in us a sense of the impotence of managerialistic rhetorics that promise that things are and will be better than they are. We have realized that the deployment of relationships understood with an objective and rational understanding of reality – as an essentially sel sh instrument – is far from taking a relational stance to treating collective problems.

Conventional thinking in the IT/IS  eld about communication, information and knowledge was examined. It was observed that the management process is still largely treated as a control system that relies heavily on computers as the control mechanism. This paper has offered a social systems perspective with an exploration of the political dimension of corporate communication systems that considerably broadens concerns for the functioning of ICT in the work environment.

What is proposed is a recursive system for understanding. Relating through communicative action is the basis of con ict management in which argumentative examination of the validity and rationality claims of knowledge is conducted under conditions that preclude systematic distortion of communication. This conclusion has also surfaced recently in organizational learning (for example, Coopey and Burgoyne, 2000) and in business ethics (see French and Muhlfriedel, 1998). This conclusion has profound implications for the design of ICT-based management systems.
