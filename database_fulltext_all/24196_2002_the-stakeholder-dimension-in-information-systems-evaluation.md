# The stakeholder dimension in information systems evaluation

LAURIE MCAULAY AND NEIL DOHERTY

Loughborough University Business School, Ashby Road, Loughborough, Leicestershire LE11 3TU, UK

NATASHA KEVAL

Hewlett Packard, Worton Grange, Reading RG2 OTE, UK

This study used an earlier critique of the cultural sciences for exploring the ideological basis for the evaluation of outsourcing. Questionnaire and interview responses are presented. These incorporate a range of evaluative criteria, which together represent an indicative list of the bene ts and risks of outsourcing. The paper initially suggests that different stakeholders might be impacted on in different ways by bene ts and risks. It is suggested that information technology outsourcing evaluation might thus be partial and not neutral in its impact on stakeholders. However, the differences and similarities between stakeholders suggest a subtle interplay of ideological concerns that does not totally deny the interests of all but the dominant stakeholders. Although the evaluation may be partial this does not deny the potential for those who might be presumed to be disadvantaged for making personal gains. However, in making personal gains, an ideology based on economic interests is furthered, thus occluding the opportunity for autonomous and responsible engagement with the process of evaluation.

## Introduction

Ngwenyama and Lee (1997) suggested that critical social theory allows researchers to ‘assess the ways in which the organizational context and institutional arrangements render sensemaking and communication problematic and politically vulnerable’ (p. 165). This paper begins with this possibility, i.e. that evaluation, as a form of institutionally based sense making, may be ‘problematic’ and ‘politically vulnerable’. In so doing, the paper aligns its interests with the political metaphor of information systems (IS) management (Walsham, 1991). The political metaphor invites us to conceptualize organizations as networks of human beings, actors or agents with diverse interests (Walsham, 1991). This metaphor problematizes the notion of rational choice in matters such as information technology (IT) evaluation, decision making and strategy. It asks questions about the subjective origins of apparently objective matters such as corporate objectives and information (Pfeffer, 1981).

At one level, concern for human agency and subjectivity leads to the concept of the emancipated actor and this is a central issue in critical social theory (Ngwenyama and Lee, 1997). Concern for the emancipated actor in turn begs questions about the possibility of emancipation and seeks to make sources of domination that counteract this possibility explicit.

One view of these questions is that emancipation can be achieved by recognizing and challenging ideology (Macintosh, 1991). Ideology is constituted through ideas that are so commonplace and familiar that they are taken for granted as objective fact or truth (Sargent, 1972, p. 1). Ideology becomes a coherent and widely accepted set of ideas that permeates society. It can be an obstacle to emancipation when it is a form of false consciousness that deludes individuals about the nature of their interests. Critical social theory aims to address this possibility by freeing actors from the self-imposed domination and false consciousness that may result from ideology that is not challenged (Held, 1980; Geuss, 1981; Bottomore, 1984; Best and Kellner, 1991).

The need for challenging ideology and achieving emancipation is consistent with Habermas’ (1972) support for Freudian analysis as an emancipatory science. Emancipation may thereby imply the desirability of surfacing underlying assumptions. Ideologies can be made explicit through this process and can thus be challenged. For instance, within IT outsourcing evaluation, unchallenged belief in the objective nature of measurements may legitimate decisions that are taken about the IS department. However, assumptions of neutrality, as expressed through cost- or pro t-based concepts, may be ideologically founded. Challenging the neutrality of measurement may free us from existing limitations in the practice of evaluation and lead us to exploring new perspectives more fully, such as unforecastability and fakecasting, which work at the level of underlying assumptions and open up the possibility of redescribing the world in ef cacious ways (Singer, 1994). The  rst aim of this paper is emancipatory in that the outsourcing evaluation assumptions that are encountered at a research site are made explicit and are challenged.

The study of ideology has bene ted from Marx’s famous metaphor of the camera obscura. This metaphor suggests that reality is turned upside down by ideology as represented by rhetoric about a public’s view of its condition. Mepham (1994, p. 214) illustrated this by suggesting that a reality that is represented by exploitation and domination may be supported by an ideology that stresses legal equality, freedom and phrases such as ‘a fair day’s wage for a fair day’s work’. In terms of IT evaluation, evaluation has the potential to illustrate the camera obscura metaphor, for instance if it is based on  nancial criteria that are claimed to be neutral and fair in their impact on all stakeholders, but which imply cost reductions that impact on the lives of some stakeholders in unequal ways. For instance, a chief executive of cer may use  nancial rhetoric for supporting an apparently objective decision to outsource that relies upon and exploits the continuing loyalty of IS personnel whose lives are disrupted by the threat of redundancy, whilst arguably improving external impressions about the chief executive of cer’s ability to perform. The second aim of this paper is to explore the metaphor of the camera obscura in relation to evaluation.

The study reported here concerns the outsourcing decision. Smithson and Hirschheim (1998) pre gured some of the issues by showing that there can be ambiguity over the bene ts of outsourcing when the views of different stakeholders are taken into account. In their case study, Smithson and Hirschheim (1998) reported that outsourcing was considered to be good from a senior management perspective because the previous in-house IS facility was perceived to have failed to deliver strategic value, despite independent advice that the service was ‘close to “best of breed” in their data centre operations’ (p. 159). However, business unit managers and staff felt that the outsourced service failed to deliver the previous levels of service. Smithson and Hirschheim (1998) attributed these con icting perspectives to a failure to effect appropriate IS evaluation. This paper takes a complementary line of argument by exploring ways in which decisions might be ideologically driven, with consequences for differences in the impact on different stakeholders and their perspectives. This study thereby extends Farbey et al.’s (1999) concern that stakeholder analysis should be included as an important evaluation theme. The third and  nal aim of this paper is therefore to explore evaluation from the perspectives of different stakeholders.

The structure of the paper is as follows. The next section establishes a theoretical basis for developing a critical interpretation, based on one aspect of Habermas’ (1972) critique of knowledge and human interests. It then presents a study of outsourcing evaluation that develops into a critical interpretation. The paper concludes with an autocritique that subjects the paper to re ection with the intention of opening up further lines of inquiry and argument.

## Communicative action and critical social theory

Communicative action concerns the element of human experience that is based on symbolic interaction (Habermas, 1972, p. 196). People use symbols such as language, numbers, visual arts and physical arts in order to communicate with each other. Habermas (1972, p. 155) drew upon the work of Dilthey in explaining that symbols have ‘intersubjective validity’, that is they are valid when they are shared or ‘common and this sharing takes place within the community, tradition or group within which knowledge emerges. In this respect, knowledge is called objective. The interactions that support this form of objectivity produce knowledge that is ‘practically ef cacious’ (Habermas, 1972, p. 196). In other words, mutual understanding and consensus provide the grounds upon which action is endowed with positive practical consequences.

The notion of a link between positive practical consequences and consensus can be illustrated through Brunsson’s (1985) work on decision making. For Brunsson (1985) decision processes were inextricably linked with action and action could be constructively supported by ideologies that are ‘conclusive, complex and consistent’. The link between Brunsson’s (1985) approach to ideology and Habermas’ (1972) concept of communicative action can be made through the former author’s concept of ‘objective ideologies’, which ‘provide a common basis for discussion and action (Brunsson, 1985, p. 28). Objective ideologies are contrasted with ‘subjective ideologies’, which relate to individual’s sets of ideas and ‘perceived ideologies’, which concern the ideas that individuals have about other individuals’ ideas. These different forms of ideology may be potentially inconsistent (Brunsson, 1985, p. 28), but there are situations where consistency emerges and ideology can thereby become ‘conclusive’. In such situations, ideology may replace the formal decision-making processes that are based on the concept of choice (Brunsson, 1985, p. 93). General agreement about courses of action is already evident before the apparent taking of a decision and actions result from dynamics within the organization as a whole and not necessarily from decisions that can be isolated from other decisions taken within formal processes (Langley et al., 1995). In such cases, ideology is practically ef cacious in Brunsson’s (1985) view, because change management is facilitated and because the necessary precursors to action, which are motivation, commitment, expectation and uncertainty reduction, are enhanced (Brunsson, 1985, p. 95).

From within the more general tradition of writings on ideology, Brunsson’s (1985) claims for ef cacy are somewhat discordant. Ideology is more normall associated with a range of pejorative meanings, the negative sources of which are to be found in an array of notions from fanaticism to false consciousness (Eagleton, 1994). Returning to Habermas (1972), ideology might represent false consciousness, despite any coherence or consistency that might be evident. Critical social theory is not satis ed with coherence and consistency. Instead it seeks to establish when statements ‘express ideologically frozen relations of dependence that can in principle be transformed (Habermas, 1972, p. 310). In order to do this, underlying assumptions must be surfaced and subjected to challenge through a process of re ection. In relation to outsourcing evaluation, for example, consensus might establish that competitive advantage can be gained, even though there is nothing intrinsic to outsourcing that will facilitate this. Generally, the alternatives of insourcing or smart sourcing, which combine insourcing and outsourcing, may be adequate for securing actions leading to the bene ts that are traditionally associated with outsourcing (Lacity et al., 1995; Earl, 1996; King and Malhotra, 2000). For Brunsson (1985) ideology in this example is ef cacious because action and change are supported by belief in the causal relationship between outsourcing and competitive advantage through a process that Brunsson (1985) labelled ‘irrational’. In this respect, Brunsson’s (1985) position is antithetical to Habermas’ (1972) general concern with promoting a rational basis for knowledge and action. Through critical social theory, this paper will seek to question why outsourcing should provide competitive advantage when insourcing and outsourcing fail to do so.

Habermas’ (1972) solution to the negative implications of an ideological foundation to communicative action is a challenging process that incorporates re ection. Re ection seeks to surface unexpressed and implicit assumptions. Ngwenyama and Lee’s (1997) analysis of a sequence of electronic mail messages illustrated this point. They pointed out that an originating text could be taken to be nothing more than what the originator had ‘ostensibly, intentionally, and publicly expressed’ (Ngwenyama and Lee, 1997, p. 162). However, the surface meaning of the originating text is shown to disguise a more important interpretation. The electronic mail’s recipient derives mutual understanding that is contextualized, clari ed and completed through dialogue with other parties that bene t from learning and re ection (Ngwenyama and Lee, 1997, pp. 162–3). Effective communication thereby involves critical interpretation that allows the electronic mail’s recipient to achieve emancipation from distorted communication.

Habermas’ (1972) views on the emancipation that can be achieved through such re ection were inseparable from his views on the emancipated society. According to Habermas (1972) such a society would be based on the realization of autonomy and responsibility at the level of individual human beings. Communication between autonomous and responsible individuals would be achieved through nonauthoritarian and re ective dialogue, which would be free of domination and false consciousness. Habermas (1972) argued that knowledge in such a society would be conjoined with genuine human interest (pp. 286–7 and pp. 314–15). Critical social science may thereby provide an effective basis for evaluation.

In the next section, this paper presents the study together with the surface meaning of its  ndings and a discussion of the possible ideological basis for the responses. This section provides information on the evaluative criteria that questionnaire respondents and interviewees considered to be important in outsourcing. The data were collected four months after the commencement of the outsourcing arrangement and, thus, focus on the ex post evaluation of outsourcing, although re ections on the ex ante evaluation are also discussed later in the paper.

## The study

The study centred on an outsourcing agreement that was stated to be the largest in the industry by one of the interviewees and which involved a multi-billion dollar contract over seven years. One outsourcing partner (the vendor) was chosen to manage the entire IS for the outsourcing company (the client). Both companies operated globally but the study was based in the UK. Details, including the names of the companies, have been suppressed in order to maintain con - dentiality in view of the sensitive issues involved.

The study concerns attitudes towards the bene ts and risks of outsourcing as expressed by different classes of stakeholders. It draws upon the views of actors who were involved in the process of outsourcing. Two approaches were adopted for soliciting views: a questionnaire and semi-structured interviews. The questionnaire built upon a literature review of bene- ts and risks. The respondents were asked to assess the importance of each of 22 bene ts and each of 13 risks using a seven-point Likert scale in which 7 represented the highest importance and 1 the lowest importance. Ten managers were interviewed and the data were analysed in terms of the questionnaire typology, thereby con rming the questionnaire’s list of the bene- ts and risks of outsourcing.

The questionnaire design began with a consideration of the IS outsourcing literature and brought together a range of indicative bene ts and risks. Table 1 shows the  nal list together with indicative sources. This list was not intended to be comprehensive and the purpose of the research was to assess differences in perceptions between stakeholders rather than to assemble a complete list of the bene ts and risks. The questionnaire was pilot tested with eight employees in a company that had adopted outsourcing. In addition to commenting on the list, the respondents also provided advice on improving the response rates. As a result of these comments, the questionnaire was redesigned and also repackaged as a macro-supported Excel spreadsheet with an appropriate GUI interface. This was electronically mailed to 105 managers who were involved in outsourcing. Forty-two usable responses were received, representing three different internal stakeholder groups and providing a 40% response rate.

Three stakeholder groups were de ned as relevant by the piloting exercise: these were non-IS management stakeholders, IS management stakeholders and IS-transitioned employee stakeholders who ful lled management roles in areas such as project management. The non-IS managers relied upon IT within their day-to-day work, the IS managers ful lled a liaison role between the client and vendor companies and the IS-transitioned employees had previously been employed by the client company but had become vendor company employees as part of the outsourcing arrangement. The data were analysed according to this tripartite division of stakeholders.

A feel for the similarities and differences between the three stakeholder groups can be gained by considering the bene ts and risks that were ranked as most and least important by each group. Table 2 shows the three bene ts and risks that were ranked as most important and the three bene ts and risks that were ranked as least important by the IS-transitioned employees in this study. Table 3 shows the equivalent results for the IS management group and Table 4 shows the equivalent results for the non-IS management group.

Analysis of variance showed that the stakeholder groups rated a relatively small number of bene ts and risks as being signi cantly differently and these are presented in Table 5. No statistically signi cant differences were found in the responses of the stakeholders for a number of the bene ts and risks. This  nding, alongside similarities in ranking as shown in Tables 2–4, might point to the bene ts and risks of outsourcing evaluation that are valid. The relatively high scores accorded to items in the list gave some evidence for the external validity of the list, with few scores below 3 and with the lowest means exceeding 3. In other words, the  ndings accord importance to the bene ts and risks, which are more generally discussed within the literature on IT outsourcing and may therefore be generalizable.

Questionnaires might, on the other hand, be viewed as narratives that comply with ‘certain implicit conventions for talking or writing about oneself to others’ (Harré, 1998, p. 131). Questionnaires can thereby be seen as means by which individuals construct themselves in relation to the issue under inquiry and each individual constructs a set of personal attributes that are capable of change as personal, social and broader relations change (Harré, 1998). Questionnaires may therefore present a set of personal beliefs that is mutable. In terms of Brunsson’s (1985) typology, ‘subjective ideology’ is thereby suggested by heterogeneous questionnaire results whilst homogeneity may re ect ‘objective ideology’. The relative homogeneity of the questionnaire responses, adopting the stakeholder groups as a basis for potential difference, may therefore suggest that Tables 2–5 provide an insight into the nature of the ideological foundations of the evaluation of outsourcing. In particular, the basis for the relationship between ‘improve service quality/ service delivery to customers’ and outsourcing may be ideologically driven because of its dominance in the belief systems of all of the stakeholder groups and this possibility is developed further in the next section.

Considering the bene ts and risks that are least important to the interviewees suggests a further argument in favour of an ideological basis for the evaluation. The risk that dominated all three of the stakeholder responses in terms of unimportance was ‘client engaged in a new line of business – requires changes to contract or requires termination’. In other words, all stakeholders saw their business continuing along established lines. Given the uncertain nature of the client’s business (telecommunications), this may have been no more than optimism, which was based on mutual agreement based on positive thinking, which might in turn have been ideological. In other words, this response may re ect no more than a traditional view of affairs that displays little willingness to engage with future potentialities. Of course, it can equally be argued that no plans existed for the client to change the nature of their business.

## Critical interpretation of the study

## Service quality

All of the stakeholder groups listed ‘improve service quality and service delivery to customers’ (service quality) amongst their top three bene ts and this issue also  gured centrally in the interviews. The importance of service quality was reinforced through the symmetry that is evident in the inclusion of ‘vendor failing to provide contracted service to the required level’ amongst the top three risks, since this is a quality concept under the conformance de nition of quality, which is supported by International Standards Organization 9000. Quality therefore  gures highly both in terms of bene ts and risks. Quality has equally been recognized within the general literature of IT evaluation throughout the 1990s. For instance, Farbey et al. (1992) gave ‘being able to carry out either new activities or existing ones at a high quality level (p. 111) as one of  ve bene ts of IS.

Service quality may therefore be taken at a surface level to be a self-evident necessity. However, a critical interpretation of the interviews problematizes the ability of outsourcing for delivering this requirement. In particular, it is dif cult to discern an unambiguous and rational basis for the link between outsourcing and service quality. For instance, there were mixed views about the impact of outsourcing on service quality at the level of service level agreements, which were a vital part of the formal relationship between the outsourcing client and the vendor. At a general level, an IS manager suggested that a drop in performance might be expected to last for 3 or 4 months following the transition to an outsourcing arrangement and that this impact on performance had been experienced. In contrast, a user, who likewise expressed the view that a decline in performance should have been experienced, considered that there had been no change because, to adopt the precise wording of a third interviewee, an IS-transitioned employee, ‘it is business as usual; we are doing the same job, supporting the same applications, reporting to the same manager’.

Service quality is clearly important, but this appears to be a generalized and unsubstantiated importance that may achieve little more than to illuminate the traditional mindset of the business. The senior IS manager who stated that performance against service level agreements had declined as expected expressed the overall consensus as follows.

The service improvement will be long term. The bene t would be professionalism and also the fact that because IT service is [the partner’s] main competence, we bene t from greater knowledge and ability to draw on any solutions. We have a better chance of getting best practice.

A notable aspect of this quotation is the belief that outsourcing will turn out to be a highly successful decision. This optimism might be argued to be ideologically founded in abstract arguments, which evoke the outsourcing partner’s professionalism and knowledge, but which fail to provide speci c supporting illustrations drawn from concrete experience of the  rst four months of the outsourcing agreement. Similarly, two vendor senior managers expressed service quality in terms of the intangibles of trust and partnership, thus furthering general considerations and optimism in the future. Outsourcing may be considered to be a good thing, but this may represent no more than a general act of faith. Interviewee 4, a non-IS manager, provided a summary that links the overall optimism and the lack of evidence to support this world-view.

Since the transition has gone ahead, there have been mixed feelings. On the whole we believe it will be better for the business in terms of service, which is important to us. Without the support of IS I do not think we could get very far. To have the expertise of another  rm to support us in IT is a great bene t. So far though, nothing seems different.

There is therefore a possible mismatch between knowledge and experience and belief in service quality may be little more than an optimistic wish for the future that may be consistent with the metaphor of the camera obscura. This camera obscura metaphor was further suggested at two quite distinct phases of the interview by interviewee 5, a senior transition and service operations manager. Interviewee 5 contradicted an ideology of improved service quality by arguing that cost reductions necessitate a reduction in service quality.

[1] The service level agreements that have been agreed [with the partner] have been agreed at a global level and this is again to bring consistency into [the client company]. The problem has been that the European region has always provided services different to the States. We actually maintain a higher service level [in Europe] than they do in the States. Bringing in a global service level agreement, based on what the States do, means we are now going to drop the standard to provide a consistent service level agreement. [2] So, in some instances, we will have to reset the customer’s expectations from ‘this is what you got before’, which might be at a higher level and ‘now your service will be reduced to be consistent with this level of cost’.

Interviewee 5 argued that service quality must be managed at an ideological level. General expectations about service quality must be re-established if service level agreements are to be negotiated successfully.

In addition to supporting the possibility that the reality of outsourcing might be the reverse of the image of an ideology espousing improved service quality, interviewee 5 also linked service and costs. Reductions in levels of service quality are necessary in order to meet cost expectations and service needs to be understood in the context of costs and other  nancial implications.

## Costs and other  nancial implications

Costs and other  nancial concerns were high on the agenda of the chief executive of cer, whose expectations were repeatedly mentioned by interviewees in terms of the ex ante evaluation. Other case studies have also suggested that  nancial considerations dominated the early thinking about outsourcing (Huber, 1993; Cross, 1995). However,  nancial concerns need to be addressed with care. Financial arguments appear from wider studies to be means by which hegemonic interests can be served whilst subservient interests are undermined, as in the case in which directors’ pay was increased at the time of large-scale redundancies (Barsky et al., 1999) or the case in which the dismantling of retiree health bene ts were justi ed by the need for supporting pro t objectives (Arnold and Oakes, 1998).

Both the chief executive of cer and a single metric, pro t per employee, dominated reference to ex ante evaluation in the interviews. The interviewees explained that the chief executive of cer had communicated the importance of pro t per employee as a way of in uencing the share price at a time when the company’s industry (telecommunications) was undergoing uncertainty and turbulence. Clearly, reducing the number of employees must have a positive impact on pro t per employee, all other things being equal and so the evaluative criteria was unambiguous in its implications for the outsourcing decision, thus reducing sources of debate and securing motivation, commitment, expectation and uncertainty reduction in Brunsson’s (1985) terms. However, pro t per employee can be partial in its impact where the uncertainty and stress that is implicated by potential changes in contract affect some stakeholders more than others. In this case, employees who were transferred to the vendor company initially experienced considerable stress. Attempts were made to mitigate against this impact, but these were only partially successful, as indicated by staff turnover during the period leading up to and following the transition.

A camera obscura perspective on this issue is that economic rationality serves the needs of a dominant class. In blunt terms, cost savings secure the positions of chief executives whilst ensuring the insecurity of lower managers and employees through the threat of redundancy schemes. The chief executive of cer presented outsourcing to employees as a fait accompli and in terms that stressed the importance of corporate image as perceived by capital markets. Whilst from Brunsson’s (1985) perspective it might be argued that acceptance of the decision to outsource is necessary for change and for the success of the outsourcing arrangement, a camera obscura view may suggest an unacceptable abuse of power secured through the manipulation of an ideology in which economic concerns are privileged above other issues. There was wider support within the interviews that those in broader positions of power were prepared to manipulate events for their own personal and economic advantage. Those in positions of power sought to give themselves stability throughout the outsourcing period, for instance, thus reducing the personal stress that was endemic to the uncertainty associated with changing contracts of employment. Interviewee 9 described this exploitation of events as follows.

One most common question was ‘why were there no senior managers outsourced?’ So, you had departments outsourced, but their managers were not. And that was common ground throughout this transition. Managers were aware of what was happening and managed to move themselves to safer environments.

This level of personal interest undermines the possibility of the emergence of a society based upon emancipation and non-authoritarian dialogue and peopled by autonomous and responsible individuals.

The broader implications of economic rationality can be pursued through numerous references in the interviews to issues related to costs including, for example, the possibility that outsourcing can transform  xed costs into variable costs and, thus, improve cost control (Cross, 1995, p. 97). References to cost reductions were as much grounded on general considerations as were claims about service quality. Further, the general considerations associated with cost reduction become problematic at the level of underlying assumptions because the views supporting outsourcing appeared to be based on unchallenged platitudes. In the case of the argument that outsourcing transforms  xed costs into variable costs, interviewee 1, a senior manager for the vendor company, unpicked the underlying assumptions in the following way.

Over time, the idea is that you move to variable costs, where you pay for the service as and when it is required. So there is potential for cost savings to be made. But then again, you cannot be certain when the demand for service will increase; it can  uctuate.

Interviewee 1 implied the correct accounting meaning of variable costs, costs that tend to change in the short term with changes in the volume of activity. Cost reductions therefore imply reductions in the levels of service quantity, which begs questions about the impact on service quality. If greater levels of service quality are provided, costs may increase but the argument provided by interviewee 1 failed to pursue the implications fully.

This argument additionally problematizes alternative views on the relationship between service and costs. Interviewee 5 offered the familiar argument for the cooptimization of costs and service quality levels. Interviewee 5 not only stated that ‘any company always wants the highest level of service and the lowest operational cost’, but also provided a number of assertions that purported to explain how outsourcing can lead to cost reduction.

(1) The outsourcing partner is a major company in the IT industry and, therefore, ‘their buying power should be larger than [the client’s]’.

(2) Capital costs are replaced by leasing costs with a resulting minimization of cost levels.

(3) Slowing response to user demands through the use of service level agreements reduces workload and, thus, reduces costs.

(4) The eradication of ‘cottage industries’ reduces costs.

Each of these four points can be problematized. The  rst two assertions are economic in nature. The  rst is plausible, but the interviewee rested the arguments at a normative and abstract level, which was divorced from speci c examples. Its underpinning assumptions were therefore dif cult to critique. However plausible the contention implied, it is equally plausible that a vendor who is seeking to make pro ts from an outsourcing arrangement will be reluctant to pass on all the savings that may be achievable through ‘buying power’. The second argument implies a highly technical solution that is also dif cult to challenge at the general level at which it is presented. However, in this case, the argument was implausible in terms of the underlying  nancial logic. The substitution of capital costs by leasing costs is normally motivated by a desire to improve return on investment measures and has attracted the interest of accounting regulators for this reason. ‘Off balance sheet  nancing’, of which leasing is a form, came to be seen as a means by which performance measures could be manipulated by reducing the denominator in the calculation of return on investment (Kaplan and Atkinson, 1998; Wilson et al., 2001). The argument for leasing as a form of cost reduction has traditionally been at best a secondary justi cation for leasing. An additional dif culty with this assertion is that leasing can also support insourcing and outsourcing arrangements.

The third and fourth arguments assume changes in the nature of the relationship between the IS department and users. Both mechanisms were available to the client company before the outsourcing arrangement. For instance, in the case of the cottage industries argument, where users develop their own IS because to do so results in a lower user cost than the internal price charged by the IS department, the chief executive of cer had campaigned unsuccessfully for the previous three years to eradicate this corporate-wide activity. The issue of cottage industries was raised throughout the interviews, but no interviewee explained speci cally how the outsourcing arrangement would deal with this issue when directives from the chief executive of cer had failed to do so. In addition, any savings that might be available through outsourcing represent a failure of the internal charging system rather than the bene ts of outsourcing per se since, theoretically, internal charges should be set at levels that motivate economic decisions about internal supply (McAulay and Tomkins, 1992; Kaplan and Atkinson, 1998; Drury, 2000). The assumptions underlying the assertions suggested by interviewee 5 are therefore at best problematic and at worst faulty.

In the absence of a questioning approach to the rhetorical resource of  nancial concerns, the company accepted outsourcing and yet retained continuity of IS support and development by transferring employment contracts from the client to the vendor company. IStransitioned employees continued to carry out their work and, when this study’s interviews took place at 4 months after the start of the outsourcing agreement, the vendor’s impact on service quality was unclear. The major substantive change that was noted by all interviewees was the introduction of an added layer of bureaucracy as new projects were processed through formal communications channels that had been set up between the vendor and the client. This bureaucracy included the need for approving service level agreements formally. Counter-intuitively, this additional layer of bureaucracy had positive impacts on the dayto-day work of IS-transitioned employees.

## Personal consequences

Bureaucracy impacts on the lives of human beings in general but resulted in surprising personal consequences for the actors at the research site. Prior to the outsourcing agreement, the client’s users made demands upon the IS department in ways that one interviewee described as ‘no win’. There was a perception that the IS department provided a free service, despite an internal charging mechanism and this led to unrealistic demands being made for IS services. The lack of impact of the charging system was explained by the culture within the client company, which was dismissive of the ‘wooden dollars’ that measured the levels of charge and yet had a perceived zero sum impact on business costs (McAulay and Tomkins, 1992). The outsourcing arrangement brought in a degree of bureaucracy that created distance between users and IS providers that was absent in the relationship built upon internal charging. As noted by interviewee 5, the need for negotiating service level agreements slowed down responses to users’ needs. These changes gave the IS providers a more stable platform for their day-to-day activities and demands for service also became more realistic.

Acceptance of outsourcing and of the  nancial argument advanced by the chief executive of cer therefore led to unexpected improvements in the personal experiences of some employees. Further personal impacts on IS-transitioned employees are evident in the statements listed in Tables 7 and 8. The bene ts in particular represent a range of interests that are broadly economic in orientation: enhanced career prospects for IS-transitioned employees, job security with the vendor for IS-transitioned employees, the opportunity of a better bene ts package for IS-transitioned employees and better chances of promotion for IStransitioned employees. The interviews thus provided a rich description of the personal consequences of outsourcing.

These comments contrast markedly with the statements that were made about service quality and costs in that the points raised were detailed and substantive; personal consequences might even be at the speci c level of, ‘There were even comments from the  rst week after transition like “Are you allowed to drink coffee because you are now [a contractor]. Should you not now be paying for it”’ (interviewee 9).

Personal interests were also presented in a detailed and speci c way in terms of changes in the relationships between IS providers and users. IS-transitioned employees were more highly respected than when they were employed by the client. IS-transitioned employees had better conditions of employment because their rights under their old contracts had been maintained whilst they were additionally guaranteed two years of employment as part of the transition arrangement. A vendor company that enjoyed an enviable status within the IT industry employed IS-transitioned employees. The IS-transitioned employees were thus in a privileged position within the client company and their jobs were unexpectedly more secure than the job of their previous colleagues because the client company was experiencing extreme uncertainty at the time of this study’s interviews and major redundancies were expected. In addition, the IS managers had been retained by the client in order to act in liaison roles but these roles were described as being largely unde- ned at the time of this study’s interviews and so those managers who did not transition to the vendor company faced considerable uncertainties. Remaining in employment with the client company therefore came to be seen as disadvantageous, where previously there was a perception that the IS-transitioned employees would be most at risk. The general views held at the ex ante stage of evaluation had thus been turned upside down by subsequent experience.

## Autocritique

In this  nal section the paper re ects on both the achievement and failure of critical social theory in providing an emancipatory basis for outsourcing evaluation in this study. A facet of critical social theory is its willingness to re ect upon its own achievements and weaknesses (Ngwenyama and Lee, 1997). A paradigm that seeks emancipation would not be consistent if it simply provided a further ideological basis for human endeavour. This paper therefore offers re ections in this  nal section rather than conclusions.

It has been argued that the issues of service quality and costs raise more questions than answers in terms of a justi cation for outsourcing. This paper shows that a metric such as pro t per employee has implications for the sense of security of employees and, therefore, may be partial in its impact on different stakeholder groups. The unsurprising  nding that  nancial issues impact on service quality is noted, but the precise details of this impact were not evident in the mindset that emerged from the interviews, even though costs and service quality were presented as important justi cations for outsourcing in the ex post evaluation. The criteria of costs and service quality are therefore problematic from a perspective that is consistent with Habermas’ (1972) approach to critical social theory and its questioning of underlying assumptions. Knowledge in Habermas’ (1972) terms should be based on a consensus that has been challenged through a process of re ection. This study discovered that the criteria that were implicated in the evaluation of outsourcing at the research site represent concepts that have super cial plausibility, but the assumptions of which are either ambiguous, theoretically implausible or dif cult to substantiate.

The paper has illustrated degrees of agreement and disagreement amongst different stakeholders about the factors that might in uence the evaluation of outsourcing. In this way, the paper has discussed the possible ideological basis for outsourcing evaluation, but has also shown that ideology cannot be simplistically disengaged from stakeholder interests in the way implied by the metaphor of the camera obscura. The paper thereby illustrates both the importance of alerting ourselves to the partial nature of economic measures, such as pro t per employee, but also shows that it is possible that partiality can be attuned to the needs of individual stakeholder groups where, for instance, IS-transitioned employees unexpectedly bene t from improved conditions of employment. The paper does not see stakeholders naively saturated by the discourse of a dominant mode of thinking to the point at which they cease to see the impact on their own lives. Instead, the paper sees the application of the dominant mode of thinking to the construction of positive meaning in the lives of stakeholders during times of change. Ideology thus has a negative face: the enslavement of individuals to economic modes of thinking at the research site. However, it also has a positive face: the ability to adapt and retain a positive view of experience. The  rst of these two faces is consistent with Habermas’ (1972) ‘rationality’ whilst the second is consistent with Brunsson’s (1985) ‘irrationality’. Perhaps the employment opportunities of IT specialists explain this complex interpretation. Certainly, as a means of self-empowerment, those employees who became disenchanted with outsourcing simply changed employment. The ability to change employment accords those in putative lower positions a power that is not envisaged by the metaphor of the camera obscura.

Viewed in this way, the interview responses take on a subtle meaning. This meaning rests not only upon the debatable notion that wealth creation is advantageous, but also shows that those who might be thought to be disadvantaged can secure personal gains in both economic terms and through improvements in personal image. In the case of the IS-transitioned employees, career bene ts represent wealth considerations that support personal interests. The IS-transitioned employees might have downplayed the importance of  nancial concerns for their employers through their questionnaire responses, but by asserting the importance of  nancial concerns for their personal interests through interviews they laid down a personal stake in the maintenance of the importance of wealth creation more broadly. Their personal re ections on the bene- ts of being IS-transitioned employees reinforced  nancial issues and wealth as an ideology and, thus, supported that which at a corporate level they may seek to challenge at a personal level. Personal interest and ideology are mutually supporting within a subtle relationship that fails to question the signi cance of the arguably greatest challenge to personal emancipation, i.e. wealth creation.

What does this insight contribute to our understandings about evaluation? The most substantial contribution of this paper is intended to illuminate Lyytinen’s (1992, pp. 164–5) ‘ rst front’ of IS research as in uenced by Habermas’ (1972) critical social theory. Adopting critical social theory in this way has previously contributed to our understanding of the ways in which mutual understanding and consensus drive IS experience. Within this tradition, emancipation has previously received relatively little attention amongst IS researchers (Lyytinen, 1992, p. 165). The implication of emancipation for outsourcing evaluation is the possibility that evaluation might in future be founded upon dialogue that is re ective and responsible, genuinely consensual and free from domination. However, the domination of the chief executive of cer’s metric of pro t per employee in the ex ante evaluation, the differences between the stakeholder groups in terms of the importance that was attached to the criterion of costs in the ex post evaluation of outsourcing, the problematic construction of signi - cant criteria by the interviewees and the signi cance of personal interest throughout the outsourcing process undermine the notion of autonomous and responsible dialogue. These facets of organizational experience at the research site would need to be reshaped before critical social science could make an impact on the practice of outsourcing evaluation.
