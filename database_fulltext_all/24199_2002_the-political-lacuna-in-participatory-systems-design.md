# The political lacuna in participatory systems design

KALA SARAVANAMUTHU

School of Accounting, Finance and Entrepreneurship, University of New England, Armidale, NSW 2351, Australia

This paper critiques three prominent information systems (IS) contributions to the development of social (work) systems, namely ETHICS, UTOPIA and Multiple Perspectives. They are based on the sociotechnical Quality of Working Life and generally rely on systems design for improving working conditions by allowing employees to appreciate a holistic work experience, instead of being treated as a cog in the pro t-making machinery of capitalism. However, this paper argues that ETHICS and Multiple Perspectives in fact shy away from the ideo-political differences that cause workplace con ict in the  rst place. Their pluralist means of resolving workplace con icts through discussion and negotiation fail to problematize the colonizing or hegemonic in uence of economic rationalism in obscuring the feasibility of alternative modes of workplace relations. They play down the asymmetrical power relations that perpetuate the exploitation of weaker stakeholders in real-life employment negotiations. Hence, political quietism in social IS approaches fail to acknowledge the ideological causes of con ict, that is the pursuit of pro t at any cost, as opposed to a more sustainable and ethical approach to workplace relations. This paper presents a study of an organizational accounting IS system and uses it for illustrating the ideological roots of differences in employee–employer needs and priorities and revealing how re exive decision makers are alienated when they attempt to distance themselves from the myopic ethos of economic rationalism. Despite these criticisms, the paper concludes on a relatively positive note. It argues that, since the Brundtland Commission report Our Common Future was released in 1987, there has been increased awareness that organizations cannot continue to exploit our resources (and ourselves) blatantly in the name of pro t maximization. This process of rede- nition opens the door for more realistic and politically challenging systems approaches.

## Introduction

The last two decades have witnessed the widespread inclusion of ‘microelectronic-based technology’ in factory, clerical and management work (Rosenbrock, 1981; Zuboff, 1988; Harrington, 1991). Burnes et al. (1988) compared its effect on the transformation of work practices with the earlier mechanization of tasks (in the Industrial Revolution) and concluded that technology has been used for obscuring the continued destruction of human potential, which is effected by deskilling, cheapening and displacing labour (following Braverman, 1974). It also implies that the manner in which technology has been perceived has reinforced the hegemony of ef ciency (following Gramsci, 1971) in the work practices of the 1980s.

In the name of ‘progress’ a futuristic fatalism is nurtured which generates an acceptance of the inevitability of technological developments and a reluctance to question the way it is used, even when this is consistently destructive of jobs and skills. In part, the problem stems from a tendency to treat new technology as a self-contained, uniform phenomenon, isolated from other societal and organisational in uences and having an inexorable momentum of its own . . . what is needed are studies of new technology within the context of political, socio-cultural and organisational developments; its treatment not as an isolated or uniform phenomenon, but as something that varies depending on its particular form and on the nature of the organisation, industry and society in which it is used; and  nally as a view of new technology and the social organisation of production linked to historical change (Burnes et al., 1988, p. 3).

This paper will revisit this debate (see also Pettigrew, 1975; Clegg, 1993; Knights and Murray, 1994; Farbey et al., 1998; Waring and Wainwright, 2000) by attempting to ascertain whether more recent formulations of participatory information systems (IS) approaches have overcome this problem by conceptualizing technology as a social construction that is shaped by societal struggle over ideology. (Here, social con ict is explained using Marx’s (1970) analysis of how value is created in the production process. The production relation between capital and labour is politicized by virtue of it being a competitive–interdependent one. In order to earn a living, employees sell their labour power to capital in return for wages. Labour expects a holistic work experience, whereby its mental, physical, spiritual and emotional needs are all met. However, capital which has a legal claim on surplus (the difference between wages and the exchange value of the products and services produced) increasingly exploits labour’s physical and mental capacities only. Capital is motivated by the need for maximizing returns on all its resource inputs and, consequently, treats labour like any other insentient commodity it exploits in order to reach its goal. Labour’s capacity for resisting comodi cation of its multidimensional potential is increased when it is able to withhold its skills, thus jeopardizing the accumulation pro ts. Therefore, capital has to seek labour’s consent to participate. The underlying ideological contradictions are obscured in several ways, including using technology (including IS and accounting information) for portraying the exploitative relationship as the only workable means of ensuring job security and increased pro ts (Hunt, 1990; Thompson, 1990; Tinker, 1998).) Wilson and Howcroft (2000) argued that existing theoretical and methodological IS evalua tion approaches consistently neglect ‘the social and political issues that are inherent in this process’ (p. 2). They pointed to a couple of ‘notable exceptions’ (Wilson and Howcroft, 2000, p. 2), namely Smithson and Hirschheim (1998) and Walsham (1999). This paper will also examine Burnes et al.’s (1988) generalization about the hegemonic role of technology in the context of the 1980s’ and 1990s’ transition of IS approaches: the formulation of Mumford’s (1983) ETHICS (an acronym for Effective Technical and Human Implementation of Computer-based Systems) and Bodker et al.’s (1987) UTOPIA (an acronym in the Scandinavian languages for Training, Technology and Products from the Quality of Work Perspective) will be contrasted against Linstone’s (1999) (following Linstone, 1984) Multiple Perspectives.

ETHICS, UTOPIA and Multiple Perspectives are essentially based on Trist’s (1981) socio-technical Quality of Working Life, which seeks to reduce workplace antagonism. They generally rely on technologically integrated work systems for allowing employees to enjoy a holistic work experience instead of being treated as a cog in the pro t-making machinery of capitalism. However, this paper will argue that the 1990s’ Multiple Perspectives, like the 1980s’ ETHICS, continues in fact to shy away from the ideo-political differences that cause workplace con ict in the  rst place. Both these approaches rely on pluralist notions of discussion and negotiation for identifying goals and priorities regardless of the fundamental ideological differences that exist between organizational stakeholders who are locked into a competitive–interdependent relationship (following Burawoy, 1985). The determination of these goals is crucial for the design of their con ict-free systems.

Even when these models do acknowledge antagonism, it is con ned to ‘healthy’ disagreement, which is the code for perpetuating the hegemony of ef ciency. On the other hand, UTOPIA only considers the needs of employers because of demarcation disputes between newspaper unions representing competing professions, namely journalists and graphic workers. Their professional controversy points to the centrality of ideological differences in workplace relations: both groups feared that the hegemony of economic rationalism would prevail even in a controlled programme such as UTOPIA and that computerization would result in job losses for either the graphic workers or journalists.

However, the time is ripe for changing societal attitudes. Spearheaded by the Brundtland Commission’s report on the need for sustainable management practices (Our Common Future, which was released in 1987), the community is increasingly aware of the need for revamping business practices that exploit natural and human resources in the name of pro t maximization. However, genuine change has to be preceded by a rede nition of ‘successful performance’ so that business outcomes are in the interest of all stakeholders, not just the shareholders (Elkington, 1999; Economic and Social Research Council, 2000; Sorensen and Lundh-Snis, 2001). The process of rede- nition opens the door for more realistic and politically challenging systems approaches involving a range of stakeholders (see also Earl, 1989; Clegg, 1993; Sauer, 1993). Conversely, apolitical IS approaches represent lost opportunities for reforming work practices. Their failure to pick up the sustainability gauntlet could see their participatory rhetoric camou aging business-as-usual practices.

This paper is structured as follows. The next section will critique the 1980s’ models, namely Mumford’s (1983) ETHICS and Bodker et al.’s (1987) UTOPIA, in the context of the political economy of work. These criticisms are borne out in the third section, which provides real-life illustrations of the socio-economic tensions that dominate workplace struggles. It will also serve as platform for evaluating Multiple Perspectives’ (Linstone, 1999) capacity for meeting the sustainability challenges of the twenty- rst century in the fourth section. The  nal section details the paper’s conclusions.

## The labour process and participatory systems design: ETHICS and UTOPIA

Braverman’s (1974) seminal work on the transforma tion of work under monopoly capitalism rekindled interest in Marx’s (1970) relatively obscure labour process writings. He showed that management of the

labour process in the twentieth century relied more heavily on ideological control (as compared to the coercive control of the previous era) as workers became more skilled and gained greater leverage over their work. Ideological manipulation of workplace values was used for securing workers’ consent to participate in an unjust production process, which continued to allocate the lion’s share of the fruits of worker effort to capital. In other words, ideologically manipulated consent obscures and perpetuates the fundamental contradiction between the needs of capital and labour, that is pro t maximization versus a holistic work experience, respectively.

Social relations of production re ect the underlying ideological struggle between capital and labour (Thompson, 1993). In this context, an IS is a social construction that re ects the norms and power structures of the previous period: ‘instead of simply producing social relations, (technology) is produced by the social relations represented by capital’ (Braverman, 1974, p. 20, emphasis in the original) (Adam and O’Doherty, 2000; Avital and Vandenbosch, 2000). Despite the evolution of (technologically integrated) work practices over time, the struggle is an ongoing and open-ended one because the contradictions between the two socia classes continue to exist (following Thompson, 1990; Psoinos et al., 2000).

It has been demonstrated that accounting, which is a calculative technology and a major component of any IS, shapes and is shaped by workplace politics (Miller and O’Leary, 1993, 1994; Arnold, 1998, 1999). Despite accounting being a contested technology, critical studies have long revealed accounting’s partisan role in entrenching and legitimizing the logic of capital in workplace culture and IS (Previts and Merino, 1979; Tinker et al., 1982; Tinker, 1985; Munro, 1995). Further, Kunda (1992) revealed how top management apply partisan information and dramaturgical techniques in order to elicit consent (to the alienating notions of ef ciency) from ‘empowered’ employees. Thus, despite the display of employee consensus to organizational priorities, there is a growing sense of frustration and disenchantment among those who realize that they are ensnared in a cycle of self-exploitation. Their resistance usually manifests as (higher levels of) employee stress, absenteeism and undetectable (but) recurring acts of sabotage that impact negatively on the real productivity without seriously jeopardizing employment security (Burawoy, 1985; Dunkerely and Thomas, 1998; Uddin and Hopper, 1998). Conventional (accounting) information does not make the cost of lost productivity visible, partly because it does not acknowledge the existence of contradictions in workplace relations (following Williams et al., 1989, 1994).

Boland (1999) referred to accounting as a representative craft because it frames people’s perceptions of organizational performance through its categorization of organizational activities into elements that constitute the balance sheet and income statement. Similarly, IS is an intellectual technology (Lee, 1999, following Curley and Pyburn, 1982; Markus et al., 2000) that shapes workplace relations:

in the process of its instantiation into one or another information system, a given set of information technology becomes subject to the shaping in uence of the intellects of its implementers and users, who can end up creating an information system that the inventors of the supporting information technology never had in mind . . . [Further], once instantiated, [information technology] can react with and extend the intellects of its implementers and users (Lee, 1999, p. 8).

This dialectical role of information technology (IT) also has a political dimension: its systems’ dissemination of information in uences the balance of power in the struggle for control over the labour process (following Edwards, 1979). In this respect, Liu et al. (2001) regarded IS as means whereby ‘signs are created, processed and consumed’ (p. 229), thereby in uencing human behaviour. It in turn raises complicated philosophical and legal issues over the delegation of human tasks to technology (see also Bannister and Remenyi, 2000). However, this inherently social process of integrating technology into a work environment (Corbitt, 2000; Marginson et al., 2000; Serafeimidis and Smithson, 2000; Hareton, 2001/2002) has been reduced to its technical form, namely automate, informate (following Zuboff, 1988) and transform (following the Massachusetts Institute of Technology’s 1990s research team in Scott Morton (1991)). Automating a task refers to the situation where technology is used for replacing humans ‘following the principles of the substitution of human labour and cost ef ciency’ (Brooke, 2000, p. 23) and informating a process involves enhancing human effort by ‘helping individuals to add value through application of intellectual skills’ (Brooke, 2000, p. 23), while transforming processes refer to the use of technology for more far-reaching structural changes within an organization (following Cash et al., 1994), to the extent that the ‘very nature and purpose of the business itself requires reevaluation’ (Brooke, 2000, p. 24). The Massachusetts Institute of Technology regard a move from automate to informate to transform as being a (progressive) trait of a successful organization (Cline and Guynes, 2001), despite the limited number of analyses on the social effects of higher levels of technological integration (Brooke, 2000).

The 1980 participatory systems approaches reviewed in this section (Mumford, 1983; Bodker et al., 1987) have been innovated in the (socio-technical) spirit of enhancing human potential. Thus, they may be regarded as falling within the ambit of ‘informate’, whereas Linstone’s (1999) Multiple Perspectives could be seen as straddling the ‘informate–transform’ phases of organizational change. Despite the backdrop of technical jargon used for describing the impact of IT, it is argued below that the 1980 participatory systems share the same partisan traits as accounting (a calculative technology).

ETHICS was heralded as a ‘problem solving methodology’ that would ‘assist in the introduction of organizational systems incorporating new technology’ (Mumford, 1983, p. 64). She argued that Frederick Taylor’s in uence on systems design resulted in a focus on

structural elements in the change situation and used machines to replace people and to monitor more strictly the work of those who remained. Yet because people are an integral part of all work systems, and people have values, attitudes and psychological needs, a neglect of their interests can throw a change situation into a state of disequilibrium so that instead of the change producing the desired increase in ef - ciency the new system operates more inef ciently than was anticipated and gains may not outweigh the costs of innovation (Mumford, 1983, p. 12).

Thus, the ETHICS method, which consists of ‘a set of logical, sequential analytical steps which should be taken when a new computer-based work system is being designed’ (Mumford, 1983, p. 37), aims to legitimize the needs of systems users. It includes the following steps.

(1) Diagnosing business and social needs and problems, focusing on both short- and long-term ef ciency and job satisfaction.

(2) Setting ef ciency and social objectives.

(3) Developing a number of alternative design strategies that will  t ef ciency and social objectives.

(4) Choosing the strategy which best achieves both sets of objectives.

(5) Designing this in detail.

(6) Implementing the new system.

(7) Evaluating it once it is operational (Mumford, 1983, p. 39).

Therefore, by involving the users in the diagnosis of information speci cations and prescription of systems characteristics, Mumford (1983) hoped to ‘design-in the joint goals of job satisfaction and ef ciency gains. She assumed that these aspects of work would reinforce each other: an ‘ef cient, well run department reduces frustration and increases job satisfaction. Similarly high job satisfaction generally requires feelings of competence, responsibility and pride in work (Mumford, 1983, p. 76). Because Mumford (1983) believed that a ‘condition for the participative approach . . . is that no one must suffer and as many employees as possible must gain from the change’ (p. 30), she did not give adequate consideration to employee alienation underpinning their participation in employment activities. Her stand was tantamount to asserting that, despite securing ef ciency gains by ideologically manipulating workers (through capital’s use of employee task knowledge that has been codi ed into rules and procedures), worker satisfaction would emanate from being empowered to formulate speci c job satisfaction objectives.

ETHICS relies on employee cooperation for creating a well-functioning and compatible social system that in turn buttresses its technical framework. In essence, its workplace harmony assumes an apolitical workforce that subjugates its social needs in return for continued employment. However, real industrial democracy would see the establishment of job satisfaction objectives that could jeopardize the central tenet of capitalism, i.e. the prioritization of property rights over labour’s entitlements. Therefore, genuine worker choice of task objectives is not likely to occur without a signi cant paradigm shift in managing the labour process because task changes are not con ned to the shop  oor (or of ce). Changes in task objectives impact on the bigger picture of how society’s wealth and power are distributed (following Allen, 1975; Burawoy, 1985). ETHICS does not cater for such a paradigm shift because it is restricted to matching personal and departmental needs. Mumford (1983) recommended that ‘individual or group’s job needs and the requirements of the job’ (p.77) be matched on three levels.

(1) Personality (that is, knowledge and psychological needs).

(2) Competency and ef ciency in the work role (which is related to the task needs).

(3) Employee values (that is ethical needs of workers regarding how they expect to be treated).

Mumford (1983) also relied on job enrichment, where an ‘employee is able to use a number of different skills, some of which are quite complex and require judgement to be exercised, choices made and decisions taken’ in order to ‘improve both job satisfaction and ef ciency in many situations (p. 59). Further, group autonomy (or a ‘self-managing group that is multiskilled’) was recommended as a way of incorporating ‘developmental aspects of work into non-managerial jobs’ (Mumford, 1983, p. 60). However, labour process literature has revealed the dialectical nature of multi-skilling and self-managing groups outcomes, namely they serve to improve as well as exploit labour (Burawoy, 1985; Knights et al., 1985).

Mumford (1983) was not unaware of the ideological tensions in the workplace and its alienating effect in securing worker consent for participating in an unjust system. Mumford (1983) admitted that management may use employee participation as a ‘manipulative tool’ for producing an ‘interested and committed workforce and therefore assisting in the avoidance of industrial relations problems’ instead of making the workplace ‘truly democratic’ (p. 23). She also recognized Gouldner’s (1958) categories of ‘punishment centred, representative or mock’ bureaucracy. (Punishment-centred bureaucracy is where management responds to deviations from rules and procedures with punishments, whereas the representative approach is more democratic in nature. Mock occurs when neither management nor workers accept the existing rules and regulations.) However, she only regarded his representative concept as relevant to ETHICS because her

procedures are jointly developed by management and workers to meet a group of shared and mutually agreed objectives . . . [thereby acknowledging that] ethics and moral philosophy of a company, together with the kind of legislation that management formulates, and employee perceptions of the legitimacy of this, must have an in uence on job satisfaction (Mumford, 1983, p. 42).

Consequently, although the ETHICS systems design is well intended, it remains unrealistically apolitical in according ‘ef ciency and job satisfaction equal weight (Mumford, 1983, p. 30). Everyone, from the users to system analysts, is on its learning curve (apolitically) acquiring ‘diagnostic’, ‘design’ and technical skills (Mumford, 1983, p. 91). However, when con ict over choice of ef ciency and satisfaction objectives arose, Mumford (1983) urged the IS design group to resolve it through ‘goodwill by all parties’ (p. 35).

resolve the dif culty by replacing that objective [at the operational level] with another, less divisive one. Sometimes con ict of this kind cannot be resolved and the Design Group has to live with it. But ETHICS has the advantage of bringing the con ict out into the open where it can be discussed and a solution sought (Mumford, 1983, pp. 88–9).

(Con ict could occur at stage 11 of the ETHICS method that is used for ascertaining the organizational options (or different ways in which to organize departments so as to achieve ef ciency and job satisfaction). The design group is advised that it should identify the key tasks that should be carried out in order to achieve the key objectives, but the manipulatory aspects of designing a system to accommodate the tension is not suf ciently explored. It has more to do with politics than learning.)

The  nal crunch occurs at the cost evaluation stage because ETHICS relies on accounting to select the ‘best socio-technical solution’ (see Figure 1 for a diagram from Mumford (1983, p. 58)). A critical review of the history of accounting (Previts and Merino, 1979) reveals the partisan nature of accounting information. Accounting unproblematically privileges and entrenches (whilst obscuring) the ideology of ef ciency over social needs in the organizational culture (Tinker, 1985, 1988). This reinforces the argument for bringing the politics of ascertaining technically ‘integrated solutions’ to the fore.

UTOPIA (Bodker et al., 1987), on the other hand, puts forward a trade union alternative to a computerintegrated system of producing text and images in the newspaper industry. Traditionally, technology has been introduced using ‘turn-key’ systems where a complete system is purchased and implemented. There are several shortcomings to this approach: they standardize the organization of work, the levels of training provided by suppliers are generally inadequate and the ‘distance (Bodker et al., 1987, p. 255) between the systems developers and users makes it dif cult for local unions to in uence the subsequent transformation of work. Unions are con ned to demanding reforms in legislation and agreements (between management and the supplier  rm). The UTOPIA project was intended to produce an ‘example’ illustrating that it is feasible to have a trade union-oriented system that would complement human creativity ‘under certain favourable conditions’ (Bodker et al., 1987, p. 257). Technology would therefore be

an extension of the accumulated knowledge of tools and materials within the domain . . . The intention is not to automate parts of the work process, but to build computer-based tools by which the craftsmen can still apply and develop original skills (Bodker et al., 1987, p. 261).

The success of this ‘organized experiment’ in integrating technology into work depended heavily on support from management and unions in the page layout area (Bodker et al., 1987, p. 260). However, the fundamental UTOPIA inquiry of how work should be organized when new technology is introduced raised so much controversy between graphic workers and journalists that the project team had to abandon the hands-on aspect of the experiment aspect, which would have involved these (competing) professions jointly seeking ways of working together in the interest of industrial democracy. Union demarcation disputes were driven by fear of loss of jobs that (usually) accompanied job redesign.

The con icts have their roots in the fact that newspaper management introduces new technology to save man power. Journalists, graphic workers, and administrative staff confront each other in the struggle over decreasing number of jobs . . . Journalists are afraid of losing their control over the lay-out if the graphic workstations are placed in the composition room and are operated by the graphic workers. Alternatively, they naturally see a possibility for creating new jobs for themselves if the subeditors obtain the right to operate the workstations. The graphic workers not only risk losing jobs, but they also risk losing page make-up, a corner-stone in graphic work, if the journalists obtain the right to operate the graphic workstations (Bodker et al., 1987, p. 274).

Hence, UTOPIA became a study of how the employer newspaper exploits technology, how the technology functions and how work would be organized ‘if, in fact, it is put to use in the daily production of the newspaper’ (Bodker et al., 1987, p. 260). Bodker et al.’s (1987, p. 278) admission that the UTOPIA aim of designing work systems prioritizing industrial democracy was unlikely to succeed in the 1980s’ corporate climate because of lack of resources that would allow skilled workers, trade unionists, computer and social scientists to work together over a long period of time developing tools in the interest of the end-users was a reiteration of the need for a paradigm shift, a point raised earlier in the critique of the ETHICS method.

## Paradigm paralysis

Both the ETHICS and UTOPIA approaches have been directly or indirectly affected by the paralysing logic of cost-ef ciency. In the case of the former, it affects the  nal choice of socio-technical solutions (see Figure 1), whilst the UTOPIA experiment itself was undermined by worker fear of the repercussions of the hegemony of economic rationalism. perspective of the impact of technology on work practices. However, in the lead up to the twenty- rst century, there was increasing recognition that there was a need for reprioritizing collective goals in the interest of sustainable development. It involved changing how work is viewed and organized (following Elkington, 1999; Economic and Social Research Council, 2000). Such changes will admittedly be slow in coming because they affect the existing distribution of power and wealth: this section illustrates the socioeconomic consequences of the unsustainable logic of pro t growth (which is embedded in accounting IS). In the context of this stalemate, the catalyst for sustainable change has to come, ironically, from within the knowledge-creation machinations of capitalism itself, that is from disciplines that frame the way in which people view the reality ‘out there’. Against the backdrop of the politics of sustainability, the next section examines whether Linstone’s (1999) Multiple Perspectives, which gained signi cant prominence in the 1990s, is capable of facilitating a paradigmatic shift in how problems are framed and solved.

Saul (1997) showed why the myopic economic focus of corporate (accounting) information dissuades management from taking responsibility for the impact of their decisions on society and the environment.

our concept of assets and liabilities, of goods versus expenses, has a negative effect on the realities of growth . . . Investment in training . . . cannot be treated as an asset. Yet the illusion of growth through the sale of golf balls remains  rmly in place (Saul, 1997, pp. 155–7).

This dilemma also emerged in a longitudinal study that was carried out in an Australian  rm in the automotive and parts manufacturing industry. This industry was regarded as one of the forerunners in globalizing production of component parts, so much so that the ef ciency of plants anywhere in the world has been evaluated against standardized (international) labour productivity benchmarks regardless of differences in geographical location, production methods and cultural differences.

Under the  rm’s programme of continual improvement its budgetary system was oriented towards keeping up with the ever-tightening international productivity benchmark. The economic focus inherent in the construction of budgetary feedback signi cantly in uenced management behaviour because it was a means of evaluating departmental and subordinate management performance. The economic orientation is depicted in Figure 2 as Senge’s (1995) ‘reinforcing behaviour’ loop, which prioritized reducing machine downtime as a means of maximizing output. Minimizing downtime also meant that labour power had to be used in an inhumane fashion, where workers were expected to repeat the same monotonous task at an increasing pace. However, workers in Department C were highly skilled workers, who could bring production to a halt by withholding their labour temporarily, by calling in sick without prior notice to management. The problem of absenteeism was rife in the industry: it was an indicator of worker resistance to work intensi cation practices that accompanied the industry’s adoption of lean manufacturing techniques that embody the ideology of ef ciency (Womack et al., 1990).

Subsequently, the manager of Department C faced a dilemma: confronted with intense competition for his products, he had to keep production  owing smoothly. As the  rm had embraced just-in-time production and inventory methods, any disruption to supply caused by absent skilled workers (who could not be replaced by casual workers) would jeopardize the  nal assembly of the product. He therefore used his meagre budget funds for training his staff, thereby providing skills as well as respite for his workers. This meant that he had to divert funds from proper maintenance and repair of equipment: this is the  rst balancing behaviour loop in Figure 2. This set off a chain reaction of other unintended consequences, such as the need to carry more stock than necessary and the production of defective units that had to be reworked later, etc., which are captured in the second balancing behaviour loop. These social costs (with related consequences for economic performance) were not made visible by the accounting IS. The study also considered the behaviour of the managers of two other departments. They too coped with the failure of the management control systems to re ect the politics of managing by making other non-sustainable decisions, such as carrying excess stock and manipulating the data collection process. In this study, the environmental achievements were not considered due to nonavailability of company data (for details see Saravanamuthu, 2000; Saravanamuthu and Tinker, in press).

The  rm’s IS did not support the political, valuebased decisions that management has to make on a recurring basis in prioritizing the competitive– interdependent needs of workers and capital. At present, management has to risk sabotaging the control system in order to override its economic bias before implementing responsible labour strategies.

However, the increased awareness of ‘sustainable development’ alternatives could herald changes for the better. However, sustainability has been portrayed as a politically correct (and pro table) way of conducting business. The monetary premium attached to sustainability credentials could also be its downfall because it could be used for camou aging the perpetuation of traditional business practices. It is not surprising that capital market monitors such as the Dow Jones have been at the forefront of this trend, shaping public expectation of business conduct by formulating ‘alternative’ indicators of stock performance, for example the Dow Jones Sustainability Index. It is based on a basket of companies that have embraced social and environmental policies and feedback systems in addition to the economic ones. The index was innovated in 1999. It has outperformed the conventional index in its  rst year of operation, thereby re ecting the premium investors place on sustainable practices. Despite the ambiguity surrounding the meaning of sustainability and how it is to be achieved, it is currently exploited for connoting ‘win–win’ outcomes for all stakeholders.

Here at Globe ’90, one of the biggest and possibly most successful environmental trade showconvention ever held, sustainable development was deployed 4279 times by 500 speakers during more than a hundred seminars over  ve days.

For most part, nobody seems to care what the words really mean, or whether they even have any real meaning. But everybody at Globe 90, whether they were selling products or services or ideas, sought to capitalise on the words . . . leading business executives use it, politicians can’t form sentences without inserting it, bureaucrats and regulators are creating new careers on it, consultants are making a killing off it, and many people will get rich off it. Have we reached the point where sustainable development has become a hazardous concept? (Corcoran, 1990).

Further, the World Business Council for Sustainable Development, which boasts a membership of 120 major companies, uses ‘sustainability’ as a vehicle for projecting an image of responsible corporate citizenship, even as multinational corporations continue to reap economic rewards at an unhealthy pace on the global stage. It coined the term ‘eco-ef ciency’ in its submission to the 1992 Rio Earth Summit Conference, essentially reducing the relationship between nature and business to yet another cost-ef ciency compromise: it refers to the ‘merits and importance of pursuing the goal of combining resource ef ciency and  nancial performance . . . to add more value for money with less impact’ (B. Stigson, 1999, ‘Eco-ef ciency: The WBCSD Perspective’, speech given in Sydney, Australia, 19 March and available in the World Business Council for Sustainable Development Speech library at http://www.wbcsd.ch/Speech/s71.htm, p. 1).

Corporations such as NIKE and GAP, which have been in the public spotlight for associating with subcontractors that operate sweatshop operations in developing countries, also exploit the sustainability euphoria to their advantage. In order to maintain their market share, they have drawn up internal social policies for ensuring non-employment of child labour and the provision of basic (humane) working conditions in their global (subcontracted) production operations. While such public demonstration of social concerns may make customers feel less squeamish about purchasing the company’s products, it does not ensure that these policies will be adhered to (in the face of pressures to attain the opportunity cost of capital). Consequently, NIKE has sought to have its operations audited by an accounting  rm for compliance with its social policies (British Broadcasting Corporation (2000) ‘NIKE and GAP – No Sweat’, documentary Panorama  lm broadcast on SBS Television, Australia, 6 March 2001). Accounting audits are traditionally restricted to the expression of an opinion on the fairness of the economic representation of annual accounts. Far too often,  nancial audits have failed to provide adequate protection for investors, workers, creditors and communities, who are dependent on companies for their livelihood. Accounting’s  nancial policing track record does not augur well for its ability to provide reassurances in what is essentially a grey area of corporate social responsibility.

Therefore, the catalyst for sustainable change has to come from knowledge creation disciplines that frame the complex and politically divisive reality. There is little doubt that the hard sciences directly shape the collective (sustainability) consciousness through the impact of their technical innovations on our daily lives. Scientists have accepted this challenge, but have wisely expressed reservation over the political and economic in uences on the direction of their investigations.

Scienti c, technological, and health capabilities – if supported by the necessary worldwide political will and international cooperation, and mobilized by appropriate social and economic policies – can produce substantial progress over the next two decades toward a sustainable human future (World’s Scienti c Academies, 2000, p. 5).

Political will and international cooperation is contingent on how we view the world and it is very much shaped by social science disciplines such as accounting and IS. They play a major role in transforming complex reality into forms that help us  nd our place in the global socio-economic relationship. Here it is suggested that the ability of these disciplines for rising to this challenge depends on their acknowledging and incorporating the politics of production and management into their respective frameworks (following Burawoy, 1985; Brooke, 2000; Wilson and Howcroft, 2000; Saravanamuthu and Tinker, in press). So, how does Multiple Perspectives (Linstone, 1999) measure up on this score?

## Multiple Perspectives: a catalyst for sustainable development?

Linstone (1999) proposed a three-dimensional way of viewing a socio-technical system (or TOP), namely through technical (T), organizational (O) and persona (P) perspectives.

Using the technical perspective, we may see the organization as a hierarchical structure, model it using system dynamics, and apply decision analysis or other tools of management science. The organizational perspective sees the same organisation as a powerful or weak unit; a living system  ghting competitors, cohesive or divided . . . The personal perspective may see the same organization as job security, an opportunity to exert power . . . Each perspective yields insights about the system not obtainable with others . . . (Linstone, 1999, p. 33, emphasis in the original).

On the face of $\mathbf { i t } ,$ Linstone’s (1999) holistic approach (see Table 1) appears to provide a ‘truth content relative to overall goals and objectives’ because of its capacity ‘to “sweep in” new components’ (p. 51). In other words, Linstone (1999) extended Feyerabrand’s (1978) assertion that ‘the only principle that does not inhibit progress is: anything goes’ (quoted in Linstone, 1999, p. 275) to argue that TOP perspectives ‘make us see the limitations of any one type’ of outlook (p. 275). Linstone (1999) believed that this approach would enable Multiple Perspectives to step outside the ‘circle of accepted corpus of theory’ (p. 275).

However, herein lies Multiple Perspectives’ shortcoming, one that could be exploited for perpetuating the unsustainable status quo well into the twenty- rst century. While it is undeniable that the three perspectives provide greater coverage of views, it still fails to reveal the underlying ideological differences that cause social (and environmental) damage. In other words, it fails to extricate itself from the colonizing effects of the ruling ideology of ef ciency on the life world (following Habermas, 1984, 1987) or the hegemony of the logic of capital (following Gramsci, 1971, 1995) because it does not develop the political overtones of the three perspectives. Its TOP perspectives are socio-institutional manifestations of the underlying politics of production and management referred to earlier. When workplace con ict comes to a head, it is usually resolved (politically) to suit the demands of those who control the asymmetrical distribution of global wealth and power (following Greider, 1992, 1997, 1998). Such politicization is absent from the historical illustrations that Linstone (1999) provided (in Table 9.1, pp. 264–5). For instance, he analysed the Union Carbide disaster in Bhopal, India in TOP terms.

Multiple Perspectives has to be politicized if it is to remain true to its stated aim of ‘continuing to advance the frontiers of the T perspective and expanding its realm to develop insights on sociotechnical systems’ (Linstone, 1999, p. 243). In order to become a catalyst for sustainable business behaviour, it has to openly restate its TOP perspectives in value terms. The social–environmental–economic designations will require organizations to place their cards on the table instead of disguising the pro t agenda in sustainable rhetoric.

It is not before time that business comes clean on what it means by ‘adding value’ (Hilton et al., 2000). Sustainability require that IS theorists take a stand on the taken-for-granted trade-off between matters  nancial and social, a compromise that has been camou- aged in ‘feel good’ messages about improved productivity and customer satisfaction in the behavioural literature. However, in allowing market terminology to permeate into managerial accountability, the age-old link between the generation of higher surplus value and worsening work conditions is conveniently obscured (Munro and Hatherley, 1993). Accountability to the ruthless market regime ‘distances’ the traditional capital–labour struggle from underlying intensi cation of work and deskilling of labour (Munro, 1995). Taylor (1995) argued that it is more likely to be a question of achieving a suf ciently large margin between cost and price in order to absorb the cost of poor quality product without jeopardizing pro t levels. It is estimated that US motor vehicle makers spend close to US\$9 billion on  xing quality problems on vehicles. The quality of vehicles produced had improved, but not suf ciently to cause a dent in warranties, resulting in the increased cost of a vehicle to the customer. Therefore, public (or market) interest arguments may contain spurious and even manipulative managerialist claims.

A twenty- rst century systems approach should be based on a value commitment about the purpose of collective business endeavour and it should seek to ensure that quality of life is improved through fair employment practices. Quality of life includes consideration for the health of our environment and biodiversity. The sustainable orientation requires that social, environmental and economic needs are made visible in the (value-adding) chain of priorities. It ensures that management are not hindered from acting on their allegiances to non-equity owning stakeholders. With the aid of the sustainability momentum, the corpor-ate ethos should extend beyond the bottom line to social corporate responsibility. It should no longer be acceptable to exploit all resources under corporate control in order to yield maximum pro t. The norm will be one of growing, but progressing in a manner where corporate citizens take responsibility for the assets they control, as well as the common assets (such as air, water and soil) that they do not control (but on which their activities leave their mark).

## Conclusions

This paper applied a labour process perspective (Braverman, 1974; Burawoy, 1985; Thompson, 1990, 1993) for arguing that political quietism in the participatory systems literature results in an unrealistic depiction of employment relations, thus casting doubt over its ability for introducing genuine workplace democracy (in the spirit of Trist’s (1981) Quality of Working Life initiative). Participatory IS approaches may instead become tools of ideological manipulation, as they sidestep workplace con ict and (implicitly) legitimize the logic of ef ciency.

ETHICS and Multiple Perspectives are premised on the notion that dialogue–interaction will improve the quality of working life without displacing the privilege accorded to pro t maximization. Their pluralist notions of resolving workplace con icts through discussion and negotiation fail to problematize the colonizing (following Habermas, 1984, 1987) or hegemonic (following Gramsci, 1971) in uence of economic rationalism that in turn denies and delegitimizes the viability of alternative modes of social interaction. However, in the rea world negotiations are grounded in the competitive– interdependent relations between employer and employees (Braverman, 1974; Burawoy, 1985). The asymmetrical power relations in the workplace usually result in exploitation of weaker stakeholders (following Allen, 1975). The (subsequent) alienating fear experienced by employees was found to be so signi cant in the UTOPIA experiment that it restricted the scope of the study. Hence, the failure of the UTOPIA project to capture the working dynamics of participatory (capital–labour) dynamics is in itself an indictment of the highly unreasonable and unrealistic pluralist presumptions that sweep aside workplace antagonism with unproblematic notions of cooperation and negotiation. ETHICS and Multiple Perspectives set themselves up for criticism because their core presumptions about workplace relations suppress the fundamental differences between capital and labour and, hence, do not acknowledge the open-ended nature of workplace antagonism. They also do not recognize the central role of ideology in a competitive–interdependent relationship, i.e. why employees either consent to exploit themselves or resist attempts to subjugate their needs (following Thompson, 1990, 1993).

Burnes et al. (1988) advocated linking technological advancement to historical change as a means of appreciating its political, social and cultural context. Therefore, despite these criticisms of the IS participatory literature, the paper concludes on a relatively positive note because of growing community awareness of the need for changing our relationship with

each other and nature. Greater acknowledgement of the socio-economic costs of the relentless pursuit of a higher rate of ef ciency means that the pendulum is swinging towards socio-ecologically conscionable organizational practices (at least in the public forum). It is politically correct to be seen to concerned about the effect of business activity on the wider community and the environment. However, the continued existence of ideological contradictions between capital and labour means that the public rhetoric is not translated into concrete action within the con nes of the organizational decision making process. Here, it is suggested that participatory IS approaches, which have to function within inequitable structural constraints, may nevertheless be able to make meaningful contributions to a sustainable future by incorporating the underlying politics of workplace management into their systems approaches. An IS approach should allow the public debate (and social consciousness) to in ltrate and shape organizational priorities and decision outcomes, instead of perpetuating the inequitable status quo by not problematizing the underlying cause of workplace con ict.
