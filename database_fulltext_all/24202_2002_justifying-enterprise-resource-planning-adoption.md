# Justifying enterprise resource planning adoption

DAVE OLIVER

Faculty of Informatics and Communication, Central Queensland University, Rockhampton, Queensland 4702, Australia

CELIA ROMM

Information Systems and Management, Wayne State University, Detroit, MI 48202, USA

This paper presents a critical approach to the way organizations justify adopting enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems. An earlier critical theory provides a framework for exploring the themes of communication, rationality and domination. Technology, process and organization are forms of domination that may appear in the context of ERP adoption. The literature on the formal and informal (qualitative) justi cation of information technology investment is reviewed and assessed. The signi cance of ERP systems is examined and also motives for their adoption. The data used for the study are electronically mediated justi ca tions of ERP adoption that were presented by universities. A content analysis is applied to these data. The paper concludes that, despite reservations about the sensitivity of domination and emancipation to identi - cation and measurement, there is some evidence to suggest people are considered to occupy a subservient role to technology, process and organization.

## Introduction

There is no single theoretical perspective that embraces all information systems (IS) research (Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991). Many differing approaches have been taken, depending on the subject of the study and the inclinations of the researchers. However, critical theory approaches appear to be comparatively rare. Critical theory may aim to locate IS issues within a more general social theory, such as the themes of domination in society explored in Marxism and the theories of the so-called Frankfurt School, within which Jürgen Habermas is prominent. Taking a more positive stance, critical theory seeks to be liberating and emancipatory by identifying inhibitors to human potential in a social context. In a more general sense, a critique of accepted social norms within the prevailing conventional wisdom may constitute a weaker version of critical theory. In this paper, a critical theory approach drawing on the ideas of Habermas (1971, 1984) is employed in a study of how enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems are legitimated. The way IS are legitimated provides an insight into the social norms within which IS are situated, which is of interest to social theorists. This study also provides insights into the vision managers have for reshaping their organizations through information technology (IT).

In order to maximize the prospects for their effective deployment, management will endeavour to create a climate that will encourage acceptance and even enthusiasm for the adoption of new IS. Consequently, there are various constituencies that need to be in uenced. Most immediate are those who work closely with existing IS, who will need to adapt to new work patterns. Others whose perceptions need to be cultivated as the organization changes its focus to the introduction of the new systems are employees who are affected by peripheral impacts and stakeholders such as shareholders or customers. The need for generating favourable attitudes and behaviours towards the new systems within these constituencies is one reason why rationalizations are put forward. Another reason for this explicative behaviour in the context of ERP adoption is to promote the institution as a conspicuous consumer of advanced technology, which, as this study indicates, is perceived to be of some value to organizations and to broadcast requirements to potential suppliers.

There has been considerable attention in the literature to the actual justi cations used by decision makers and also to developing new techniques for evaluating IS/IT investments. However, it has been argued that how change is received is as important as the nature of the change itself. As King and McAulay (1997) observed, the need for gaining commitment to a course of action may be more important than the application of a particular evaluation technique. This suggests that there are issues of change management to be addressed, which are distinct from the management of change. This is similar to the situation in theatre where, prior to the staging of a performance, a whole raft of preliminary activities must be undertaken, such as printing the programme and advertising in order to create an eventual audience. There do not appear to have been any studies on how new IS are justi ed to their constituents, those upon whom the success of the new systems hinges to a considerable extent and whose working lives may be transformed as a result.

## Critical theory

An analysis that follows a critical theoretic approach may acquire this classi cation on the basis of adopting a particular ideological perspective, for example socialist or feminist. A critical approach with an orientation of this nature challenges the prevailing ideology, for example a Marxist analysis of a phenomenon in the West or a paternalist analysis in a matriarchal society, such as that on the Indonesian island described in Williams (1998). A critical theory approach typically identi es issues of domination. The emancipatory interest, which is how release from domination is to be achieved, forms another dimension to critical approaches. For Habermas, emancipation worked via self-re ection by ‘emancipating people from the illusions and ideologies that distort their perceptions of themselves’ (McClure, 1991, p. 37). However, as Lyytinen and Klein (1985) observed in their study of how the ideas of Habermas could be applied to IS research, emancipation is a complex issue. Taking a broader interpretation, a critical approach may simply seek to illuminate aspects of situations beyond the super cially obvious, to shed new light or to adopt a fresh perspective on a familiar context.

IS de ne a territory within organizations inhabited by processes, technology (hardware, software and databases) and people (Laudon and Laudon, 1984; Khazanchi and Munkvold, 2000). Therefore, within the scope of critical theory as it might be applied to IS are issues that relate to the domination of technology or process over people. We might also want to include organizations themselves as a third dimension (since IS exist within organizations). Deetz (1992) advised us to be aware that, from a critical theory perspective, organizations can never be considered politically neutral, as in contemporary practice managerial groups are clearly privileged in decision making. We may also wish to make generalizations applicable to society as a whole, as organizations exist within society.

Habermas (1971) suggested that technology can exert a form of domination and, as technology is inherent in IS, we need to be conscious of potentially adverse effects. Marcuse (1970) also argued that technology might exert a type of control over people themselves, contrary to the apparent purpose of the application of technology as a tool or adjunct for produc tive activities. In a context of technological domination, managers and workers alike become servants of the needs of technical systems of production (Schroyer, 1975). However, technical systems of production must be legitimated by the societies in which they operate, otherwise they would fall into disuse as a result of their failure to meet socially constructed expectations. Even if they do exert a dominating role, that dominance has to be tacitly accepted by social actors in order to not only continue but to develop and thrive. Hill (1988) argued that new technologies diffuse through a society and transform it, more because the technologies align with people’s desires than because of the singular power of repressive interests for enforcing ownership and participation. Hill (1988) further argued that the technological context, once created, limits and focuses the future social developments that are likely to follow and, thus, creates a particular shape of demand for subsequent technologies. From this perspective ERP systems can be seen as an evolutionary development from earlier types of ITs that possessed a lesser degree of technological sophistication.

A study of the justi cations used by organizations for adopting ERP systems can provide insight into the values that underlie this course of action. Some justi- cations appear to have a purposeful intent and appear designed to persuade the uncommitted. Others are more self-re ective as the organization acquires an understanding of current realities through committee reports on IS/IT issues and examines future prospects. The communication of the decision to adopt an ERP system assumes importance since it prepares the ground for the implementation process. In an organizational context, communication is the process through which organizational members acquire an understanding of ERP adoption. Mumby (1988) observed that communication is the basis for social formation, transformation and legitimation. Mills (1940) regarded the languages of situations as given, as valuable data to be interpreted and related to the conditions in which they were generated. The justi cations for introducing new systems may reveal the values associated with each of the dimensions of organization, process, technology and people and their relative importance. Of course, if in justifying a course of action an organization chooses to conceal some of its motives, not all values will be revealed. Critical theory in the context of IS should enable us to move beyond an understanding of what is being done to why it is being done. This is important both from the perspective of increasing our understanding of the motivations of individual organizations and the broader society they comprise. Critical theory may also attempt to contrast the compatibility of desired outcomes with the stated aims and intentions of the organization within the broader social context.

Only the most senior people in an organization possess the necessary authority for instigating a course of action that has such extensive  nancial, technical and organizational rami cations. On the other hand, the implementation of an ERP project requires the acceptance, compliance and even commitment of a broad range of people.

Implementing any integrated ERP solution is not so much a technological exercise but an ‘organizational revolution’. Extensive preparation before implementation is the key to success (Bingi et al., 1999, p. 9).

Consequently the power relations operating in this context are complex and, as Foucault (1979) indicated, should not be thought of as unidirectional.

It appears to be the case in today’s society that individuals are less responsive to direct commands than was once the case. Most would agree with the assertion of Axley (2000), who stated that

We live in a time when neither ‘Because I said so . . . nor simple positional authority carry the kind of motivating weight they used to among organizational members (p. 20).

Thus, organizations and in particular those that have some democratic tendencies tend to feel that organizational decisions require some form of explanation in order to achieve legitimacy.

Generally accepted norms induce organizations to provide explanations that are viewed as rational in order to be perceived as legitimate (Meyer and Rowan, 1977). Whilst the social actor does not actively participate in the decision-making process, he or she is invited to examine but not to challenge the justi cations put forward. In today’s society, with its relatively welleducated workforce, legitimacy is mainly achieved via rationality concepts. This tendency is emphasized for public institutions where objective measures of ef - ciency tend not to operate and, thus, the creation of perceptions of ef ciency becomes extremely important. This would appear to be particularly true of educational bodies such as universities, which are both public institutions and purveyors of rationality (Langley, 1989).

Habermas (1971, 1984, 1987) wrote extensively on the subjects of communication and rationality. These two themes are at the heart of this study. A conclusion formed by Habermas (1971) of present society is that it is preoccupied with issues of instrumental rationality. Habermas (1971) made the judgement that issues of instrumental rationality have subsumed those of practical and affective reason. In other words society has tended to concentrate its attention on issues that relate to the technical performance of social components as opposed to those of overall purpose and direction. One explanation he advanced for this tendency, which was initially identi ed, according to Habermas (1984), by Max Weber, is that it is easier to reach agreement on technical issues than those that relate to more subjective criteria, such as values and feelings. The rationale for the adoption of a new type of IS, such as an ERP system, would appear to be predominantly an issue of instrumental rationality, though other rationality complexes may also come to the surface.

In common with other types of investment activity the adoption of an ERP system is a purposive intervention by an organization for bringing about a new state of affairs that is judged to be superior to the current state. In presenting a rationale for this course of action, the organization makes statements about the existing and desired states of affairs. A third party may assess the validity claims embedded in these statements on the basis of their truth, rightness, appropriateness and intelligibility. These are the four components of communicative competence de ned by Habermas (1984). However, the placement of justi cations on electronic media does not present a context in which these claims may be challenged, so that a discursive resolution of the issue, as envisaged by Habermas (1984), may be reached. The justi cations are to legitimate, persuade, convince and encourage acceptance, not to open dialogue. Statements concerning ERP adoption can express two types of rational relation to the world. From one perspective a university makes statements about that which exists and in the other direction statements about the proposed course of action, i.e. ERP adoption. These relations are rational because they are open to objective appraisal (Habermas, 1984). However, the purpose of this research was not to assess whether the statements are grounded in reality, which can only be achieved from knowledge of each case. However, the extent to which these statements cohere does provide additional insight into the emergence of the ERP wave of adoption. What organizations value or consider important in the context of ERP adoption can be shown as well as an indication of the relative importance of the more general dimensions of society, organization, process, technology and people.

## Formal assessment as a justi cation for information systems/information technology investments

A number of studies have examined how IT investments are evaluated. A general assumption is that investment in IT systems should be justi ed using commonly understood  nancial appraisal techniques that are applicable to any sphere of investment. Accordingly, many have concentrated on the role and extent of formal justi cations in IS investment. Hochstrasser and Grif ths (1991) reported that 84% of companies did not employ formal analysis. Farbey et al. (1992) studied 16 IT investments, only nine of which were approved in a formal manner. Bacon (1992) received 80 responses from a survey of 203 large organizations and reported that only 40% of all projects in all the companies were subjected to some type of formal analysis. More recently Ballantine et al. (1999) reported that 62% of the Times top 1000 organizations evaluate IT projects and that most of these companies report dif culties in quantifying the costs and bene ts. These  ndings suggest that a solely  nancial rationale does not provide an adequate explanation of why organizations invest in IS/IT systems.

Some sources offer an explanation for the relatively minor role played by formal analysis in many investment decisions. Steiner (1979) stated that the likelihood of quantitative criteria being employed diminishes as the importance of an issue rises and the higher up the organization hierarchy a decision is made. Similarly Symons (1990), Hochstrasser and Grif ths (1991) and Powell (1992) reported that, the greater the expense and strategic importance of an IS, the weaker the role of formal evaluation methods. Whilst this  nding is seemingly paradoxical, it may re ect the dif culty in conducting a formal analysis in certain contexts. It appears that the more extensive the scope of the IS/IT investment, the more complex is the task of conducting a formal analysis and the less convincing are its conclusions. Here it is noted that ERP systems are broad in scope, strategic and important.

## Qualitative justi cations for information systems/information technology investment

Stone (1991) argued that senior management has a preference for qualitative information and that, while quantitative techniques offer precision, they do not offer the descriptive accuracy provided by natural language. Isenberg (1988) suggested that managers use both rational analysis and instinctive judgement in determining a course of action. The variety of inputs to a decision situation can make it dif cult for decision makers to articulate the process whereby a decision was reached (Mintzberg et al., 1976; Keats, 1991). This dif culty tends to increase the value of published justi cations, where the rationale for if not the process leading to an investment decision has been recorded.

Two of the three most important bene ts identi ed by Sheppard (1990) for investing in IT were related to better information provision. These were improved management information and easy retrieval of information. The third was reduction of costs, a generic bene t that is applicable to all types of investment. Other reasons for IT investment given by Sheppard (1990) were subjectively evaluated quality improvements, image with the client and replacing older technology with newer technology. Jurison (1996) claimed that IT investments are increasingly made to provide bene ts, the value of which is dif cult to determine. These are intangible bene ts such as improved quality, an increased variety of products or services and better responsiveness to customer needs. Ward (1994) suggested that there are three principal forces that encourage IT investment in key operational systems: (1) the domains of ERP systems, which are improving the performance of existing activities, (2) integration of data and systems and (3) avoiding business disadvantage or allowing a business risk to become critical. An objective held by organizations is to increase their control over internal operations (Hall, 1977). A prerequisite for effective control is good quality information. The provision of a better information channel may underlie the desire for the integration of data and systems.

In summary these objectives relate to processes (better performance, lower costs and better information), technology (modernization and integration) and organization (increased service and quality and risk avoidance). Although people are integral to organizations, the literature does not suggest they are used as a justi cation for IS/IT investment, except as customers of the organizations that use IS/IT systems.

## Investment drivers for enterprise resource planning systems

Of particular recent interest has been the widespread adoption of ERP systems by large and medium-sized organizations, which is described as the ‘ERP revolution’ (Ross, 1999). This has resulted in the replacement of a considerable quantity of disparate IS with one single ERP system in many organizations. ERP systems are large and complex integrated software packages that support standard business activities. The scope of ERP systems, aligned with their numerous con guration alternatives and breadth of organizational impact, make the task of implementing them considerable, extending in many cases over several years. The  nancial impact is signi cant:

By early 2000 the ERP revolution generated over \$20 billion in revenues annually for suppliers and an additional \$20 billion for consulting  rms (Willcocks and Sykes, 2000, p. 32).

The majority of the Fortune top 500  rms have now adopted ERP systems and, as the high end of the market becomes saturated, ERP systems are  ltering down to medium-sized organizations such as universities and to regions beyond those initially penetrated in Europe and North America (Kumar and Van Hillegersberg, 2000).

There are numerous reports addressing the reasons why ERP systems have been adopted, but many of these are based on informed opinion rather than extracted from a research study. Sumner (1999) regarded how investment in ERP systems is justi ed as a research question. Some survey research has been undertaken into the reasons why ERP systems have been adopted. Ross (1999) surveyed 15 American companies through 40 telephone interviews. Ross (1999) and Markus et al. (2000) both identi ed integration of data as achieving better information for decision making. Ross (1999) also cited integration of systems expressed as a need for a common platform. Apart from facilitating the provision of information for decision making, the integration of both data and systems simpli es systems management. Integration was a major theme reported by Alvarez (2000) in a study made of an ERP implementation. Boudreau and Robey (1999) and Jenson and Johnson (1999) also reported the move to integrated systems, but mainly as a consequence of replacing legacy systems exhibiting various symptoms of inadequacy.

## Research approach

March (1988) asserted that there are two approaches to building theories that attempt to describe the behaviour of social institutions. These are prescriptive theories, which describe optimal behaviour and behavioural theories, which describe actual behaviour. However, there is another possible level of analysis. Pfeffer (1981) made a further distinction in approaches to behavioural analysis and theory building. One involves analysing and predicting substantive outcomes and the other is the analysis and prediction of how organizational activities are perceived, interpreted and legitimated. A critical theory approach is possible from this perspective.

The intent of this research was to explore the actors own view of their social world and, therefore, imposing externally de ned categories on the phenomenon was inappropriate. Rather than projecting a predetermined system of meanings onto the textual data (Gadamer, 1993), an approach that seeks to be open to the possibilities afforded by the text is to be preferred. The data for this research, i.e. the communications issued by organizations relating to ERP adoption, were considered too subjective and ambiguous to be analysed using positivist research techniques. However, Sarker and Lee (2000) appeared to have adopted this approach successfully in their research. Following Geertz (1983), Langley (1989) and Remenyi et al. (1998) a qualitative research approach, which incorporated the richness of the data examined, seemed most appropriate.

The concepts under scrutiny in this study; the rationalizations used for justifying the adoption of ERP systems, were human constructs. However, they are grounded in the objective reality of the application of speci c technologies in a particular organizational context. Some tacit knowledge is acknowledged from the necessary capacity on the part of the researcher to sense which important concepts and what consensus of evidence emerges from the  eld of enquiry. Some initial focus rather than a hypothesis was therefore inevitable, but should not have directed or constrained the research (Guba and Lincoln, 1989). For this study the broad groupings of organizations, processes, technology and people seemed a suitable starting point.

There is a strong case study tradition in the academic  eld of management IS (Kling and Scacchi, 1982; Markus, 1983; Kling and Iacono, 1984; Benbasat et al., 1987; Lee, 1989; Weill and Olson, 1989; Alvarez, 2000; Sarker and Lee, 2000). The case study methodology, which is an umbrella term, does not impose any speci c research technique, but rather de nes a context (Bell, 1993). Schramm (1971) indicated that the purpose of a case study is to illuminate a decision or a set of decisions, which conforms to the direction of this research. As this study focused on a speci c event, i.e. the adoption of an ERP system within an organization, the case study approach was considered suitable. Moreover, as ERP systems are software packages, there is here a situation where the same class of product is being adopted by a number of organizations in the same industry. These commonalities opened up the possibility of adopting a multiple case study research approach. A multiple case study provides greater potential for theoretical generalization than does a single case study.

Universities were chosen as cases for the study for a number of reasons. Firstly, universities are substantial and experienced users of IT and a signi cant number have emerged as purchasers of ERP systems. Secondly, Rands (1992) and Weill and Olson (1989) argued that the requirements for software acquisition vary considerably across different industries. Not only does the demand for software vary across industries, so to does the source of supply. Universities are a speci c vertical market targeted by ERP vendors, which conveniently identi es stability on the supply side as well as the demand side. Restriction to a particular industry and market segment prevents industry and market effects that might be uneven across industry sectors from distorting an analysis. Finally, following the direction given by Eisenhart (1989) that the process of interest must be transparently observable, universities emerge as suitable candidates. Universities have formal approaches to action and, consequently, their activities are habitually documented. In addition, many universities have placed material relevant to this research programme in an accessible form on their World Wide Web (WWW) sites. This varied collection of documents included requests for proposal, publicity statements and committee reports relating to the ERP project.

It was impractical in this and many other studies, given the large volume of data, to study more than a limited number of cases. The technique of maximum variation sampling advocates the use of diverse cases for aiding theory development (Eisenhart, 1989; Guba and Lincoln, 1989). Theoretical saturation is said to occur when incremental improvement to theory development is minimal. Eisenhart (1989) and Yin (1994) suggested that in practice somewhere between four and ten cases is likely to provide a suf cient empirical grounding. From this broad sample the researcher attempts to construct a consensus of evidence from successive data points. Data for this study were obtained from a sample of eight universities (six from the USA and two from Australia) taken from the population of universities that have adopted ERP systems. From this population only those universities that made their rationale for adopting ERP systems accessible in an electronic form were considered.

These justi cations are expressed in written communications using electronic media such as e-mail and the WWW. There are a number of techniques available to researchers for analysing data of this type. Critical theory is non-prescriptive with respect to research approach or methodology and is weak as regards empirical grounding. In this study, content analysis (Krippendorf, 1980) was used for placing statements from these cases in conceptual categories. The content analysis proceeded by coding documents that addressed the topic of replacing the university’s existing IS with an ERP system. The task of coding the documents required inferential judgement in assessing to which conceptual category particular statements should be assigned. A statement was deemed to form a rationale if it expressed negative opinion about the characteristics of existing systems or expressed objectives sought or located in the replacement (ERP) systems. These evaluative expressions justify a course of action by appealing to the standards applicable in a particular cultural setting (Habermas,

1984). The language, which forms the conceptual categories and their relative importance, constitutes descriptive data that can be discussed from a critical theory perspective.

Once the data has been coded into conceptual categories by the researcher, the software can compute frequencies. The quantitative indicators available from a content analysis are insensitive and shallow in providing insight (Krippendorf, 1980). Although it is agreed quantitative indicators possess these inadequacies, they are of some value in assessing the relative importance of different conceptual categories. In addition, they are obtainable with relatively little effort using software packages such as Nvivo. Whilst such quantitative measures are useful it is important not to lose sight of the fact that language itself is a very expressive medium. Consequently, the researcher should avoid submerging potentially interesting data in a morass of quantitative analysis. The expressiveness of the language analysed can, if it is included, add richness to a research report. In addition, as language is capable of multiple interpretations, the researcher may be advised to provide suf cient examples of the data being analysed in the research report in order to convince the reader that any conclusions reached are the correct ones. These enrich the account, assist in allaying suspicions of ambiguity that can arise with qualitative data and enable the reader to con rm the veracity of the analysis (Geertz, 1983; Langley, 1989; Willcocks and Smith, 1995).

## Integration

Integration was used as a rationale in a number of different ways and, as a consequence, integration is also discussed below under the section on organization. Integration was the most important rationale in terms of the frequency of reference in each of the technology and organization dimensions and was the most important single rationale if these were combined.

The historical development of IS,  rstly from discrete mainframe-based application systems to distributed solutions, has resulted in a plethora of systems within many organizations. Inef ciencies appear on two main dimensions. Multiple discrete mainframe applications with their different design strategies frequently have different operating commands, user interfaces, databases and maintenance requirements. This creates a number of problems: additional training overheads because of the different operating styles, potential inconsistencies in the data since each application has its own database and differing maintenance requirements. The problems caused by multiple databases were evident from the following.

Our data environment is characterized by redundant data entry, cumbersome tools for data access, questionable data integrity, and unnecessary restrictions on access to information (University of Colorado, 1998, p. 3, emphasis added).

Data integration is an important component of the technical rationale of integration. The other aspect of the technical dimension of integration is the situation where different sections of the same organization have different applications with broadly similar functionality. Autonomous software acquisition by sections within the organization that are driven by an attempt to overcome inadequacies in centralized legacy systems has resulted in shadow systems that have some degree of duplicate functionality.

Shadow systems have developed resulting in ‘increased information systems investment, duplicate data entry, inconsistent reporting results or output, and wasted time’. In fact . . . almost every department that was reviewed had a shadow system to track either human resource or  nance data (University of Colorado, 1998, p. 8).

Universities, in common with other organizations that may be motivated to reduce this complexity in the infrastructure of their IS, are likely to be receptive to the idea of integrated systems, such as ERP systems. The integration of different applications into a single software product simpli es the management of IT and increases usability through a common look and feel.

## Dissatisfaction

Dissatisfaction with existing administrative systems was a common theme expressed by all universities and accounted for 9% of all justi catory statements. The source of this dissatisfaction was a sense that these systems were reaching, were at or had exceeded their ‘use by date’. These systems were variously described as ageing, outdated, unworkable, costly, inadequate, inef cient, outmoded, expensive, poorly coordinated, in exible, disparate, limited, old, idiosyncratic, redundant, cumbersome and technologically inferior. Strongly evocative expressions of existing systems acting as ‘a roadblock’ and ‘limping along’ resulting in ‘cries of despair’ in relation to their performance re ected a powerful, indeed almost desperate, desire for change.

Many of these systems were 20 or more years old, itself remarkable in an era of such rapid change in IS/IT. The depth of expression regarding these legacy systems suggested that emotions regarding them had been long suppressed. The ERP route was doubly fortuitous for those universities who had adopted ERP systems before the year 2000. They were able to address the issues of dissatisfaction and the speci c problem of year 2000 compliance in one hit. Other universities were less fortunate. They had to adapt these outdated and unworkable systems for year 2000 before disposing of them.

## Additional factors

Additional rationales featured in this domain, but space and relative signi cance did not merit a full discussion of all of these here. Among these was the rationale of modernization, which is in part the converse of dissatisfaction, although factors such as WWW enabling represented an extension of capability. Service and quality enhancements represented further justi cations. However, the maintenance issue deserves discussion.

A concern over maintenance costs and dif culties associated with performing modi cations to older systems was an issue addressed by  ve of the eight universities. Three of these  ve universities used the provision of vendor-supplied maintenance as a rationale for ERP adoption. Neither of the two Australian universities included any mention of maintenance problems with existing systems, although it cannot be concluded that these universities did not experience maintenance problems or perceived advantages in maintenance from purchasing a package. The University of Minnesota described their existing systems as being ‘costly and time consuming to maintain’ and California State University stated that the adoption of an ERP system would provide ‘a path to contain and control the costs of maintaining administrative software’. The adoption of the ERP system was justi ed on the basis that it removes a current maintenance problem and puts in place a new system, in which the onus to maintain is shifted to the vendor.

## Process improvement and standardization

Process improvement accounted for nearly 15% of all justi catory statements. The idea of process improvement is part of the ideology of business process re-engineering, which gained considerable attention during the 1990s. Business process re-engineering proposes a radical rethink and reconstitution of established working practices. It proposes this revision should reconstitute organizations around processes as opposed to functions:

These current systems, built originally to support an organizational view, should be systematically replaced with systems supporting a process view (University of Michigan, 1995, p. 13).

Organizing on the basis of process cuts across established functional hierarchies and tends to broaden rather than narrow the functions a worker can perform. Whether this process-driven approach will be compatible with the strongly hierarchical structure and culture prevalent in most universities remains to be seen. The objective of implementing improved processes forms an important part of the rationale for ERP adoption. Some of the universities had conducted a detailed analysis of their policies, processes and procedures prior to ERP adoption and formed an awareness of potential ef ciency improvements, but others had not and were content to assume their procedures contained inef ciencies yet to be identi ed and used ERP adoption as a pretext for reviewing and changing them:

We must conform our processes to the systems we are purchasing, and not the other way round (Central Queensland University, 1999b, p. 1).

The universities in general appeared to regard many of their own practices as outdated and confused and were convinced that any new system would represent an improvement. They also used the rationale of adopting best practices for justifying the acquisition of ERP systems. ‘Best practices’ is a seductive concept and by claiming to embody them ERP systems are able to present themselves as inherently superior. This basis for adoption contains the implicit assurance to adopters of ERP systems that they are acting rationally by opting for the best. Most universities used the justi- cation that they are adopting best practices, though what exactly this epithet means was not well de ned.

A further rationale is standardization. Many of the universities had dispersed operations on different campuses where different groups of people were engaged in the same activities in different locations. It was perceived by senior managers that university policies were subject to different interpretations and implementations at these different locations.

There is no consistency in the application of university policies and procedures across the four campuses. In some instances, different campus interpretations of university policy cause large differences in the time spent to process a transaction (University of Colorado, 1998, p. 9).

Perhaps use of exaggeration can be seen here. Can it be the case that there was no consistency whatsoever between campuses? The desire to enforce consistency emerged as a motivation for the adoption of an ERP system. An ERP system not only enables an organizational standard but also can enforce adherence.

## Organization

The organization dimension includes the vision of integration, business considerations (including formal assessment, risk and vendor evaluation), service and quality, mandate for the decision and enhancements to image.

## Vision of integration

Integration was presented as a vision or ideal and comprised the greatest percentage of references in the organization domain. The vision of integration encompassed notions of togetherness, teamwork and synergy, which expressed a hope or wish for a future of ERPenabled harmony for the organization.

The system should deliver a seamless  ow of data, eliminating the walls that prevent people, departments and systems from communicating with each other (University of New South Wales, 1998, p. 18).

When our goal is to create synergy, we need to think about how to integrate our systems. Even if they are all working individually, it’s not as good as if they are working together (California State University, 1999, p. 1)

These statements appealed more to the emotions rather than making a rational technical justi cation for the system. Perhaps it is the case that ERP adopters are themselves as much seduced by the rhetoric as those they are attempting to in uence.

## Business considerations

A formal assessment of ERP adoption was in most cases conspicuous by its absence. Only one fully- edged business case was presented. However, it is dif cult to draw the conclusion in this survey that the other universities did not prepare a formal assessment, as there may have been con dentiality reasons for not revealing such information on the Internet. Two universities alluded to the existence of a business case and two more described their ERP investment as signi cant.

Half of the universities referred to similar projects occurring at other universities and most discussed why alternative approaches to ERP adoption were less favoured. Other business considerations such as an assessment of the reputation and  nancial stability of the ERP vendor were also evident. Most of the universities reported that they were working with external consultants and some stressed the level of commitment at senior levels to the ERP project.

The fact that half of the universities made statements anticipating an improvement in external percep tions as a consequence of adopting an ERP system indicates that improvement in image is a factor in ERP adoption. The following statements from the University of Minnesota and the University of New South Wales supported this analysis.

To improve external perception in the wider community of the University of Minnesota as innovative, cost-effective, and ef cient (University of Minnesota, 2000, p. 1).

Enhance the view that the university is an information technologically advanced and open institution (University of New South Wales, 1998, p. 10).

Although the 1% frequency of reference did not suggest this is a particularly important issue for universities adopting ERP systems, in this case the frequency of reference may not have been a good indicator because of the self-re exive nature of this factor. Publishing information relating to ERP adoption on the Internet is itself an image raising activity.

## Discussion

Whilst Habermas (1971, 1984, 1987) was principally concerned with broad social movements, the importance of technical rationality can be observed in social institutions such as universities. The rationales that accompany the introduction of new administrative systems present an opportunity for examining the underlying values held by an organization with respect to each of the dimensions of organization, process, technology and people. The multiple case study approach enabled some generalized observations to be made. Although this study was limited to universities, the cultural values they displayed are likely to resonate with those of the society in which they are located.

The importance of integration as a justi cation for ERP adoption emerged strongly in this study. Ross (1999), Alvarez (2000) and Markus et al. (2000) also identi ed integration as an important issue in ERP adoption. While integration has clear technical dimensions in terms of databases and systems, it also appears to  nd congruence with general organizational aspirations in a modi ed form and is used in both contexts as a rationale for ERP adoption. Whilst it was evident in these documents that integration was regarded as unreservedly bene cial, it is worth re ecting, as did Hanseth et al. (2001), that integration may impede adaptability in an organization. This is because, when systems are integrated, the whole system moves as one, so change in one part of the system requires change in the whole system.

A further dif culty that the integration move may encounter is in regard to shadow systems. If shadow systems which have functionality that is not embedded in the adopted ERP system are eliminated, some degradation in organizational performance may occur in the short term as shadow systems are forced out. In the medium term it is likely that new shadow systems, which are complementary to the ERP solution, will emerge.

Although integrated ERP systems are presented as offering greater  exibility than earlier systems, they may have dif culty in measuring up to these high expectations. There will continue to be problems in those areas where data has not been collected and stored. The required functionality has to be made available from the software vendor in the  rst instance. Secondly, there will inevitably be some inertia in recon- guring such large and complex systems to new requirements. In addition, historical data, which may not have been amenable to porting to the newer ERP system, may be even further out of reach than with current systems.

The maintenance of existing systems is viewed as costly and problematic whereas vendor-supplied maintenance is seen to contain a secure prospect of future enhancement at a known cost. In practice, much systems maintenance re ects changing requirements and the responsiveness of ERP systems in dealing with these is so far unknown. The onus for maintenance falls on the ERP vendor so there is no certainty that the nature and timing of upgrades will be advantageous to the user. Moreover, failure to keep pace with vendor-supplied upgrades can result in a loss of vendor support. The in exibility of legacy systems is replaced in ERP systems by a different type of in exibility. The cost of maintenance becomes more predictable, but it may result in unwanted and untimely change. ERP systems are as complex as the systems they replace and will probably require as much technical expertise as the older systems in order to be managed properly. A new ‘cadre of acolytes’ will inevitably surround them, with the capacity to control them much as before.

Technologically orientated societies seem destined to experience recurrent bouts of dissatisfaction as technological development, whilst it creates new opportunities, generates a continual need to change and update in order to maintain currency with continually revised norms. This creates stresses of change and adaptation in organizations, which may cause con icts with more fundamental goals and directions. ITs are currently very in uential in this respect because of their rapid rate of change and, therefore, pose questions of conformity to new technological ideals more often.

The adoption of best practices embedded in an ERP package represents a move towards a type of conformity dictated by the ERP package. The move to best practices is a move to common practices and standardization across the higher education industry. Not only does an ERP system apply an organizational standard it also incorporates by de nition an industry standard on the way a university conducts transactions in all spheres covered by the ERP system. The concepts of integration and standardization both re ect organizational aspirations relating to conformity. A tightly meshed organization with a singular outlook is presented as an ideal. Whilst standardization is presented as desirable for an organization, there may be instances in practice where the rigidity of conformity imposed by an ERP system excessively restricts and inhibits local  exibility. This demonstrably corporate outlook may seem surprising to those who may assume that liberal, divergent attitudes dominate university life.

The investment decision, in so far as it is re ected in these rationales, illustrates the complexity of the investment context. There are many underlying themes: integration, dissatisfaction, improved information access, maintenance, best practices, modernization, image, similar projects and so on, which make a formal assessment of the investment context extremely complex. However, in spite of this complexity, since a formal assessment was presented by one of the eight universities, it does appear to be possible to perform one. As indicated by Vitale and Johnston (1997), a formal assessment of an IS/IT investment is frequently dispensed within the Australian university context. The research conducted in commercial organizations is similarly ambivalent. While, as the literature indicates, a formal assessment of an IS/IT investment is generally only one aspect of the investment decision, it is perhaps surprising in an era when technical rationality is apparently dominant that a formal assessment is not conducted more routinely

The notion that a university believes it can enhance its image by adopting a particular IT illustrates the importance of ITs in cultural congruence. Compliance with both accepted and emerging norms of technical rationality becomes a way of obtaining legitimacy and legitimating planned activities. Universities use technical rationality to legitimate the new ERP system and in turn anticipate that legitimacy will be conferred on them by the society in which they exist.

As observed by Iacono and Kling (2001), the issue of why so many organizations at similar points in time attempt to implement the same technology, in this case ERP systems, is largely unexplained. The reference to similar projects occurring at other universities and the involvement of consultants suggests that imitation, which is consistent with the concept of mimetic isomorphism (Oliver, 1991), has an in uence in this ‘ERP revolution’. This may be a partial explanation of why so many universities are attempting to implement this technology at the same time.

The references to people are much less abundant than those that relate to technical, procedural or organizational matters. Clearly there are opportunities for some staff to acquire marketable skills in the process of installing and using new technology. Whether ERP systems enhance job satisfaction compared to the systems that they replace is a research question. These rationales propose this will be the case. The ability of a university to teach and research more effectively as an indirect result of installing these systems is also postulated. This suggests the adoption of ERP systems may to some degree be made to attain broad reaching goals beyond the immediate impact zone of the system itself. Issues relating to the nature of work and the attainment of strategic institutional goals are important in every institutional context, not just universities.

## Summary and conclusion

Previous studies into the motives for IS/IT investment and ERP adoption were explored and these revealed a variety of motives for business systems investment, though none re ected a strong orientation towards the needs of people. The approach taken in this study was to use the justi cations published by organizations for the adoption of an ERP system as a basis from which to explore issues of domination by technology, organization and processes. The issue of domination is a sensitive one. It is dif cult to measure precisely and may not be detected by all individuals with the same degree of sensitivity. The same may be said for emancipation. What emancipation might imply is an open question. These justi cations for ERP adoption provide an indication of the prevailing values applying to the IS used for core business processes in universities. Communication and rationality play a highly visible role in ERP adoption. There is some evidence in these justi cations to suggest that technology exerts some dominance. The rhetoric of escape from the legacy system syndrome suggests this. In addition, the need to conform to the package suggests that both organization and people occupy a strongly subservient position to ‘the system’. The desire for more integration is expressed in very inclusive terms. However, greater integration and standardization are likely to result in restrictions on individual or departmental initiative. Greater integration is linked to better information, which may be motivated by a desire for  rmer control from the apex of the organization. Some indications of a focus on the needs of people were discerned here in terms of the rationale of greater job satisfaction and empowerment for employees and better service and quality provided to customers. However whether these systems (or indeed any IT system) can provide staff with greater work-related ful lment requires a different type of investigation.
