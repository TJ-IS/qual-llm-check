# What drives the configuration of information technology projects? Exploratory research in 10 organizations

ANDREW MARTIN

Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry CV4 7AL, UK

The development of high quality technical systems is an ongoing important element of information systems success. This paper investigates the current practice of information technology project configuration from a management viewpoint. It is based on a series of semi-structured interviews across a range of medium to large organizations in the UK, including both users and providers of IT services. It finds that project requirements, strategic IT policies, risk management, pragmatic considerations, the managed exploitation of experience and the managed adoption of new technologies drive individual project configuration. The paper proposes a new integrated model that explicitly identifies the drivers of project configuration management. It illustrates the model using a published case study and generates directions for further research and implications for practitioners.

## Introduction

Beyond 2000, the introduction of the Euro and the dot.com boom and bust there is continued demand for IT-enabled information systems (IS) that support business applications such as electronic business, customer relationship management, enterprise resource planning (ERP) and a multitude of custom applications. Managing such projects is one of the core ongoing roles of the IT function (Feeny and Willcocks, 1999) and there is a natural motivation for research that seeks to improve the quality and success rates of all aspects of this activity. Markus et al. (2000) argue that one of the most enduring research topics in the field of IS<sup>1</sup> is that of system success. Researchers of IS success and failure separate the quality of the technical system development from the delivery of organizational benefits (DeLone and McLean, 1992; Ballantine et al., 1996; Seddon, 1997). Although it is generally considered that organizational issues are more frequently problematical than technical issues (Ewusi-Mensah and Przasnyski, 1994), technical system quality remains an important element of success (Lyytinen and Hirshheim, 1987; Ballantine et al., 1996). A number of risk factors are present in the development of quality technical systems including technology, human resources, systems development methodology and project management (Ballantine et al., 1996).

This paper focuses on the management of technical application development, particularly the drivers of the initial configuration of a project’s technical environment. By configuration it is meant the choice of the key elements of the project’s technical architecture, resourcing and development methodology.<sup>2</sup> Turbulent business environments, new and fast-changing technologies, evolving methodologies and new sourcing options, together with the constraints imposed by legacy systems and skills availability, make the technical configuration of a project environment a significant task for the project team. Where the project itself is complex, such as multiple-systems integration for electronic commerce applications or global ERP implementation, the task becomes particular challenging. The configuration strongly shapes the project and it is reasonable to expect the technical configuration to be an important influence on successful technical development. Yet, although the topic attracts some interest from both software engineering and management communities, there is relatively little integrated theory that is focused on the project configuration task and the project configuration process appears to be something of a ‘black art’. How are project configuration decisions made and what influences them?

This research therefore seeks to contribute to the literature by identifying the broad elements and drivers of project configuration decisions from a management perspective. It is not supposed that project configuration is a deterministic process, nor that there is a single optimal solution for each project: rather it is assumed that technical architects, project managers and business managers make informed decisions about project configuration. It is important that these key decision makers recognize the factors that might reasonably or unreasonably influence their decisions and trade-offs and have language for reasoning about and procedures for dealing with their potentially conflicting influences. The paper explicitly identifies a number of key drivers of the current practice of project configuration, integrating insights from a number of organizations, as well as the limited existing literature. It applies the framework to a published case study in order to illustrate its external validity. It begins to address the gap in the literature for the benefit of other researchers, students and practitioners. Further work will move towards the identification of best practice in project configuration and will help organizations to benchmark their knowledge and procedures with a view to increasing the rate of successful project development.

## Theoretical basis and literature

Whilst there is considerable research into related aspects of IT projects there are relatively few studies that explicitly identify a holistic set of influencing issues of the process of configuring specific projects. However, a few recent papers do touch on the topic. Subramanian and Lacity (1997) discuss the successes and failures of client–server architecture, mentioning issues of sourcing and methodology, but did not focus on the configuration decision. Ross (1999) describe a broad SAP implementation, which as a case study drew out a number of useful issues, but implicitly. This case is returned to later. Whilst Sawyer (2000) focuses on software development team culture, he helpfully emphasizes that project choices are determined not just by individual project factors but also by more strategic organizational and industry forces. Wexelblat and Srinivasan (1999) identify the drivers of IT strategic planning, namely the mission and vision of the organization, ‘customer pull’, architecture and standards, earlier plans, technology push and external factors including commercial products available in the market, legislation and organizational oversight. Pressman (1997, Chapter 10), looking at the operational level, suggest that product configuration requires trade-offs concerning a number of issues of project cost, time, risk, profitability, technology, resources, facilities available, politics, environmental interfaces and legal considerations. The current work seeks to develop, integrate and make more explicit this process and these factors at both operational and strategic levels.

Following an initial literature review three topics were considered to form the key management decision variables of project configuration to be studied in order to draw out the factors that influence them.

(1) IT architecture. By IT architecture it is meant the planned, integrated choice of computing (hardware and software), communications, data and applications elements that together enable the project’s functionality to be built (Earl, 1989;

Gunton, 1989; Ho, 1996; Wexelblat and Srinivasan, 1999). What governs the choice between many potential architectures for a given project?

(2) Resources and skills. Resources are a critical element of the software engineering project (Sommerville, 1996, pp. 567–88; Pressman, 1997, pp. 61–8). Whilst Sommerville (1996) points out that ‘the people working in a software organization are its greatest assets, representing intellectual capital’ (p. 568), Lacity and Hirschheim (1993), Willcocks and Lacity (1998) and Broadbent et al. (1999) are concerned with organizations where the primary task is not software development. These organizations have a clear choice today about whether to resource internally or externally and about whether software is custom designed (bespoke) or acquired as a package. What drives management’s choice of how to resource a particular project or projects in general?

(3) Methodologies and processes. Project process and methodology are equally critical to software development (Sommerville, 1996, pp. 7–18; Pressman, 1997, pp. 22–56). Methodology has been an ongoing focus of IS research: the literature reports up to 1000 published methodologies in existence (Fitzgerald, 1996), but also questions whether a methodology is or indeed should be used (Fitzgerald, 1996, 1997, 1998; Fitzgerald et al., 1999; Russo and Stolterman, 2000; Truex et al., 2000). What determines management’s choices about which if any methodology should be adopted for a particular project?

Lyytinen et al. (1996) present a useful theoretical framework that helps to position this work (Figure 1). Their model segments software development activity into three levels or ‘environments’: the ‘system environment’, where the software system operates, the ‘project environment’ in which the development takes place and the ‘management environment’ which shapes software management activities. They then segment each level into structure, task, technology and actors, according to Leavitt’s (1964) model of organizational change. They explain how the task of the higher level is to use actors, structure and technology for managing the lower level environment, in particular in terms of risks. These same variables and their interactions can also classify the risks themselves.

The derivation of the framework of Lyytinen et al. (1996) is a very general one, meaning that it can be used for structuring management research into IT development environments in general, not necessarily just research into risk management. Using this model as a reference point, the project configuration process discussed in this paper includes activities at the project environment and the management environment levels. It includes a significant concern with Lyytinen et al.’s (1996) ‘risk-based management process’ but does not focus solely on the risk management driver. Issues of architecture map on to the technology variable, issues of resources map mainly on to the actor variable and issues of methodology map on to the development process. The task is taken as a given for this work at the system, project and management levels, i.e. the requirements are taken as given (although changes to requirements is recognized as an issue), as are the need for developing a computer-based system in order to address the requirements and the need for the management of this task. Issues of organizational structure were not addressed by this work.

This study did not seek to identify a detailed or universal design logic for designing the architecture, resources and methodology elements. Instead, it took a step back and adopted a management perspective that sought to identify the integrated set of broad factors, both strategic and operational, that influence the current practice of configuring the architecture, resources and methodology elements of individual IT projects. The three project configuration elements were taken as given: the drivers were to be ascertained.

## Research methodology

Researchers in IS are encouraged to choose appropriate methodologies contingent on the nature of the task (Galliers, 1991a; Lee, 1999). These include qualitative and quantitative, positivistic, interpretive and critical approaches (Klein and Myers, 1999). Due to the apparent lack of literature this work was considered exploratory/ theory building in nature and adopted a multiple case study approach considered fit for this purpose by Galliers (1991a) and Yin (1994). In particular semi-structured, one-to-one interviews were used, since these are openended enough to allow exploration as well as factfinding, but short enough to allow a good range of views to be collected in a reasonable space of time. Recognizing the dependence on individual’s views and their analysis, this work treated the case study approach as interpretive (Yin, 1994; Walsham, 1995) rather than ‘scientific’ as categorized by Galliers (1991a). Further reflections on this approach follow later in the paper.

Nine interviews were held in person and one shorter interview was held by telephone, which was feasible once the main issues were established. The organization contacts were identified primarily via executive alumni from the researcher’s institution as well as personal contacts. The interviewees are listed and their industry sector shown in Table 1. Industry representativeness was not a major goal at this stage and, therefore, the findings cannot be strongly generalized, although no issues arose that were apparently industry specific. There is a larger company bias intentionally, since smaller companies are less likely to have IT departments with such development responsibility. There is also a less intentional private company bias.

Interviews were held from late 2000 to early 2001 and were of a mean duration of 1.5 hours. In each case the contacts were managers within the IT function, mostly with responsibility either for project management, IT strategy or IS strategy. The anonymity of individual and company names was assured and so these are not quoted here. One manager represented two companies (companies C and D), having recently changed employer, and two managers from company H were interviewed separately. These interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed by the researcher within 36 hours. Each transcription was returned to the relevant interviewee who was given the opportunity to comment on the validity of the transcript and provide clarifications where requested or required.

The interviews were structured around the elements of architecture, resources and methodology, at strategic and operational levels, as described above. The questions used for guiding the interview are given in the Appendix and included a mix of closed and open questions. Following an overview of the organization and IT function, the interviewees were asked to discuss one or more particular recent projects. They were asked to state the particular choices of the technical architecture, resourcing and methodology adopted for each project and to say what factors influenced these choices. Discussion of individual projects was used for linking to issues of IT strategy as played out in the ‘management environment’. The IT strategy process and its links with the project environment were also examined. Further reflection about the drivers was stimulated (1) by more detailed discussion, (2) by asking which factors were the most influential, (3) by asking for examples that were exceptions to the normal process, when IT strategy changed and why, (4) by clarifying and challenging the policy and (5) by inviting direct criticism by the interviewees. The approach followed Walsham’s (1995) advice of maintaining a balance between excessive passivity and over-direction.

Following the interviews, the transcripts were analysed in order to elicit the key drivers of project configuration decisions. The analysis process was one of reflective interpretation, iteratively mapping the interview transcripts and notes against candidate frameworks, the theory and literature (Walsham, 1995), as well as discussions with research colleagues. The aim was to develop a coherent interpretation that was consistent with a consensus of the views expressed by the interviewees. The findings reflect the author’s interpretation, having been fully involved in all stages of the data collection and analysis.

## Findings: the drivers of project configuration

Rather than labouring repeatedly through each of the project configuration elements of technical architecture, resources and methodology for each of the six drivers identified, the findings are presented according to the drivers that form the main interest of the paper in the form of cross-case analysis (Yin, 1994). Nevertheless, each section covers the elements of architecture, resources and methodology where appropriate, reflecting arguments and examples that were given by the interviewees. Direct quotations from the interviewees are presented in support of the interpretation, together with further references to literature.

## Project configuration is dictated by project requirements

It almost goes without saying that project configuration is strongly driven by the need for the project to satisfy the functional and non-functional requirements within its time scale and budget. This is fundamental to the nature of a project and is an acid test of a successful configuration.

What is achievable in a given time frame and cost envelope inevitably will drive a project more than anything else. . . . The bottom line is that the client’s requirements will override all (company B).

Time scales seemed to be a particularly critical driver. The interviewees frequently mentioned the strong constraint of business or client deadlines for both project-level and strategic architectural decisions, even if these were not always considered to be realistic.

The time scales we are given to make these decisions now are unbelievably short. One of the business values is ‘high velocity’ . . . Any project that takes longer than 12 months has already failed (company H).

In addition, a little more cynically,

Most clients still do not know what they want their IT to do for them – but they want it faster than they used to! (company E).

The importance of commercial time constraints was also reflected by the use of variants of rapid application development as a significant development methodology by half of the interviewees.

The interviews focused more strongly on how other factors shape the configuration but it is taken that it must satisfy the requirements.

## Project configuration is strategically guided

IS academics have for some time been reacting against the lack of business and customer responsiveness that has been historically exhibited by the IT department (Galliers and Sutherland, 1991; Earl, 1992; Galliers et al., 1996; Ward and Griffiths, 1996). The strategic IT architecture makes an important link between strategic objectives and individual project configurations (Earl, 1989; Scott-Morton, 1991). Wexelblat and Srinivasan (1999) argue that part of the role of the corporate IS architecture is to constrain the technical scope of individual projects in order to achieve economies of scale, interoperability and a more manageable set of technologies.

The interviewees viewed the strategic IT architecture as a strong mechanism for embedding business objectives and benefits into the systems development environment. The key strategic principles articulated strongly by the interviewees were as follows.

(1) Integration across applications and across global business units:

Integration of systems is key in terms of competitive advantage (company J).

(2) Flexibility to respond quickly to unanticipated and emergent business requirements:

. . . to improve flexibility and agility of systems to support multiple business configurations (company D).

(3) Standardization to reduce support costs and maintenance workloads:

Given our business processes are so disparate and badly defined anyway, from a business process point of view let us just take the application as it stands. We will remould based around the software and wherever possible make our business work the way that the Oracle application works . . . Oracle is our only solution . . . if it is not Oracle, it is nothing (company H).

The architecture offers hardware, software, communications, application and data standards or patterns that are used for ‘solutioning’ problems within the project requirements. The interviewee organizations were particularly concerned with using strategy for reducing the risk of exposure to unknown or non-compliant technologies.

Applications should be made to fit the standard architecture unless this is unworkable (company G).

The resourcing of project configuration is also strategically guided. In particular, the IT user organizations did not see their primary role as software development.

Fundamentally, H does not want to be in the business of writing business systems – that is not the way we make our money (company H).

I cannot envisage a large business application without a package (company A).

Several organizations employed relatively few development staff, but considerable numbers of support, implementation and strategic planning staff. Company I reported employing only IT strategists, having adopted a total outsourcing model. Only companies J and H of the IT user organizations either considered IT as a strategic resource or felt large enough to justify an internal centre of expertise. Moreover, company J has since announced its intention to enter a large outsourcing agreement. In contrast, maintaining IT skills is clearly seen as strategic for IT provider companies.

The pressure is on E and our competitors to be seen to have the skills and experience – though sometimes this needs to be bought in. . . . It is relatively easy to access people with appropriate technical or other skills from our internal knowledge base/intranet. This is a core competence of E – being able to bring resources to bear to meet agreed customer requirements (company E).

Expressed most simply, companies that have an internal staff pool will use this pool as much as possible and will use external consultants or contractors only if suitable internal skills are not available or where strategic sourcing partnerships dictate.

The key driver is to use our own resources, which are costed and need to be utilized if at all possible (company B).

This policy was almost identical across the sectors of IT provider and IT user companies.

Two organizations indicated that they employed a strategically preferred project development methodology unless there were compelling reasons not to do so. Other organizations employed a range of methodologies, choosing from them according to the application.

## Project configuration is technology enabled

It is to be expected that the ‘top-down’ strategic influence is balanced by ‘bottom-up’ technology-led pressures (Earl, 1989). It is clear from the whole history of IS that new and more powerful technology continually drives opportunities for IS applications (Somogyi and Galliers, 1987).

Requirements may be such that new technology or a new approach offers a potentially cost-effective way of solutioning with potentially significant returns over a long period (company B).

The way that the new technology driver is brought to bear, i.e. the new technology adoption process, is sometimes less than transparent, systematic or controlled. Star (1992) worried that ‘The number and density of information technologies are growing; yet it is increasingly unclear who is controlling them, and toward what ends’ (p. 500). For most organizations technology is an external factor, the influence of which is exerted through relationships and communications with technology vendor companies, implementation consultants and IT industry analysts. New technologies are proffered as the latest ‘silver bullet’ whilst support is withdrawn for older technologies, thereby producing a two-pronged ‘carrot and stick’ driver. More than one company commented on how this pressure is mediated or perhaps even amplified by IT staff.

The biggest pressure is from companies themselves to make solutions more complex and more leading edge than they need to be (company B).

Companies J and A reported clear tensions in respect of how to manage technology innovation, both strategically and operationally.

We do wrestle with whether to look at investment in new technology as an infrastructure investment or whether we take it forward on a project-by-project basis (company J).

We made incremental decisions, with some strategic experiments. It just happened . . . it would never have gone beyond a debate unless there was a suitable requirement to experiment on (company A).

Technologies such as Computer Aided Software Engineering (CASE) tools and new development approaches might also be expected to influence methodology and resourcing, but discussion with the interviewees did not emphasize these issues.

## Project configuration is risk managed

The determinants of IT project risk are fairly well documented (McFarlan, 1982; Willcocks and Griffiths, 1994; Lyytinen et al., 1996; Yetton et al., 2000). Moreover, its importance is becoming more clearly recognized and risk management has become an explicit driver of project configuration decisions for mature IT organizations. The role of IT architecture in managing risk exposure was mentioned above and several of the interviewees expressed clear resistance to the pressure for using new technologies for their own sake.

If the existing platform supplier fits the requirements you go with them – you do not want to risk going somewhere else. Certainly there are huge cost benefits in sticking with what you have got (company A).

More than one interviewee explicitly labelled their company as ‘behind the leading edge’ (companies A and J) and their recognition of this is a sign of management maturity, if not technical leadership. The interviewees consistently reported using experienced external resources for managing large or innovative projects such as ERP implementations: this can be interpreted as risk management, acknowledging their lack of experience (cf. Subramanian and Lacity, 1997).

. . . we got Gartner group in to help with package selection . . . for facilitation and to apply their current industry knowledge (company A).

This does not mean that only unambitious low-risk projects were attempted by these organizations and some interviewees noted the danger of ‘not challenging existing strategy enough’ (company A). Martin and Chan (1996) observe that, paradoxically, projects that were recognized as risky up front had a greater success rate. The interviewees acknowledged that where the project is large, fast, bespoke or using newer technologies the risk is particularly great, but they were prepared to identify and manage this risk. Company H cited a high-risk 90-day project and claimed to have made ‘full risk and contingency plans for any eventuality you can think of’.

One IT provider recognized risk management as a key element of their competitive offering.

One of the biggest issues of systems integration projects is – the customer is looking for someone to take risks on. If you focus purely on lowest cost, you are looking for someone who will take on the most risk, which may not necessarily be what you are after. Clearly there is a balance to be struck: price is one factor, risk is probably a bigger factor, particularly on a larger project (company B).

Such an IT provider needs to be able to demonstrate due diligence, capability, risk awareness and mature project management in order to win contracts: this helps to explain the role of a formal methodology and architecture. The methodology and company’s experience can be used not only to manage the project, but also to estimate and negotiate contracts up front (Harris et al., 1999). The use of proven templates and an established approach to problems of a certain type greatly increases confidence in their estimate and their ability to control project costs, the time scale and quality. This research therefore found that risk management is not only a prerequisite for successful project configuration, but it is a marketable competency.

## Project configuration is learning intensive

Curtis et al. (1988) suggest that application experience is particularly important in the process of programme design (and by extension project configuration). They also suggest that the knowledge required for successfully building most large complex systems is thinly spread across staff. Wexelblat and Srinivasan (1999, p. 267) note that current plans are always dependent upon earlier plans, implying a dependence upon experience. Company B strongly agreed.

It is important to appreciate that build/implement is not a stand-alone exercise – it will have been shaped and guided by other work (company B).

Stam and Molleman (1999) call for a longer term ‘learning organization’ approach to IT staff development in order to match supply and demand for skills. Lyytinen et al. (1996) note that, by using a learning loop between the project environment and the management environment, ‘managers become more competent and experienced, the organization integrates new experiences and schemes into its systems of interpretation, and new methods, codifications of procedures, and decision making rules are adopted’ (p. 279), but they do not develop the theme nor include the learning process explicitly in their model.

The maintenance and exploitation of the intellectual capital of experience and methodology and the internal skills pool is becoming recognized as a critical success factor, particularly by IT providers. Typically these organizations have invested significantly in knowledge management systems for locating expertise and project managers are skilled at using such knowledge for forming project teams to meet the dynamic requirements of their client contracts. Such a resource not only helps to retain the learning as insurance against staff movement and turnover, it is necessary for managing the huge quantity of knowledge that is generated. In the literature Harris et al. (1999), for example, report that ‘IBM is continuing to seek out key ideas from the best working designs and solutions, and to “harvest” these ideas and store them back into the asset base’. The methodology itself, together with architectural templates, becomes part of the company’s differentiating intellectual capital.

The requirement for a maintained, documented architecture is also important to larger internal IT units.

Business IS directors and strategists review the road map to ensure the research is in line with their requirements and understand architectures against a technology evolution framework (company J).

Standardization gives us the ability to retain the knowledge base for global implementation (company F).

One company was struggling in respect of managing the learning process, perhaps due to its ambiguous strategic view of IT:

Undoubtedly we make similar mistakes repeatedly (company H).

Wexelblat and Srinivasan (1999) also contribute a warning that, though crucial, learning from the past is not a guarantee of success in the future. The learning process should be an explicit element of a theory of project configuration.

## Project configuration is pragmatically resolved

This paper has noted how strategy both guides and constrains project configuration quite strongly. However, at the operational project level the pre-eminent concern of both IT provider and IT user organizations is to achieve the specific deliverables. Technical architects and project managers must reconcile the need for delivering to specification, within time and budget, with strategic guidelines for architecture, resources and methodology (Meredith and Mantel, 2000). Tensions that must be resolved inevitably result and, at the operational level, pragmatic decisions dominate this resolution:

You always end up with pragmatic solutions, compromises, workarounds (company B).

Four specific examples reported by the interviewees concerning resources and architectural issues follow. First, one of the tensions facing an IT organization or department is to manage the demands of their human resources for training and experience in new tech-nologies. This leads to a pressure within IT providers and internal IT units alike to offer staff work with leading edge technologies, as recognized in the discussion about the technology driver above. This pressure is potentially in conflict with delivering low-risk solutions to clients. To put it bluntly, management wish to exploit experience, whilst staff wish to develop experience (Sommerville, 1996, pp. 568 and 575).

Internal staff would love to be using the latest technologies, contractors love to use the latest technologies. But . . . management just want to get the product out of the door (company A).

There is a trade-off between skill requirements and education needs (company B).

This tension is resolved at the project level by negotiation between project managers, line managers and staff (Curtis et al., 1988). External resources are used for supplementing skills shortages within the constraints of strategy.

You make use of contractors as well – where you require specific detailed skills in a particular technology – though it may be an expensive way of doing it, quite often it is the only solution (company B).

Second, architectural compromises sometimes make very practical sense.

My role is to see that standards are applied, but if they are not we go through due process. If this is an off-the-shelf product, fits this particular business hook line and sinker, what is the point in forcing that supplier to change their database engine from Ingres and their hardware from RS6000 to Oracle or Sun, on which they have no experience. Often there are one or even multiple deviations from standards . . . (company I).

In practice there will always be exceptional cases that do not fit the strategic plan. As the third and fourth examples, the interviewees reported two surprising exceptions to the strategic rule of ‘package where possible’ that demanded a pragmatic response. Third, company A chose to develop a bespoke application for a relatively standard application, because, although packages existed with the required functionality, they contained much more functionality than was required, yet were not commercially available in smaller modules. In this case business economics and an incrementalist development plan prohibited purchase of the whole package. Fourth, a package was desired in company H, but the businessdriven project time scale of 90 days was too short even to configure a package: instead, again, an essential subset of the package was developed by bespoke methods!

There is evidence in the literature that developers are also very pragmatic about the way and the degree to which they apply methodologies (Fitzgerald, 1997).

A final pragmatic influence of architectural decisions that was observed by the interviewees was organizational politics.

SAP was not necessarily the best choice but was the most politically simple to get through (company D).

## Project configuration is significantly constrained

Before the findings are brought together, a more general observation is made about the project configuration process. The project configuration process can be seen as making choices within constraints which, once identified, are often very strongly binding, leaving relatively few, relatively clear options. More than one interviewee described certain architectural choices as ‘no brainer’ decisions (companies J and A) and

It is very rare in any development/build project to have a free hand, green field (company B).

A lot of decisions are made by what you have around you (company H).

Time scale always is the most critical – it binds an awful lot of other issues – you just do not have the luxury of detailed design or specification or build to do things the way you would like (company B).

Thus, it can be noted that, although the project configuration decision process is not trivial, its complexity in terms of the sheer number of options is much reduced. This implies that identifying the constraints is an important strategic management task.

Resources are constrained by strategic considerations as well as the supply of employees/contractors available.

The desire to re-use or make use of other implementations, skills, resources will constrain things notably (company B).

Likewise, the choice of system development methodology may be constrained, either by the time scale required by the business, by client specification or, as company J indicated, by the technology adopted.

The methodology has been dictated by the technology that we have decided to purchase (company J).

This raises the question of whether managers have any control over project configuration. As mentioned in the Introduction, it is affirmed that managers do exercise choices, but that these choices are strongly influenced by the constraints. It may be that many organizations have little control over the drivers themselves, particularly the technology. In practice, the constraints are effective at simplifying the configuration decision and the mature technical architect is well aware of the importance of identifying constraints and using prior experience for developing feasible project configuration proposals.

The first job of the project manager is to determine what kind of project he is working on and fit to an appropriate combination of templates (company B).

## Discussion: towards a model of the drivers of project configuration

This research confirmed the importance of several drivers of the process of IT project configuration. Existing literature recognizes each driver, but so far has not brought them together explicitly with respect to the project configuration process. This paper’s main contribution is therefore to address this gap: it does so now by using the findings above to propose a model (Figure 2) that represents the drivers of IT project configuration decisions, showing their source and point of impact with key elements of the project configuration process.

The model shows its links with Lyytinen et al.’s (1996) more general framework by retaining the management environment and project environment levels and including the concepts of risk management, development process (methodology), actors (resources) and architecture (technology). However, this model is focused particularly on the particular process of IT project configuration and, therefore, it excludes the system environment level. It includes a reference to the influence of external drivers such as technology developments and to legal and regulatory constraints. The latter, although not emerging significantly from these cases, recognizes the point made by Pressman (1987) and Wexelblat and Srinivasan (1999) as mentioned earlier and acknowledges that this study did not encourage an external perspective from the interviewees: it is therefore indicated by a dotted box, the influence of which is likely to be felt at both strategic and operational levels. ## Illustration of the model: the Dow Corning case

It is instructive to revisit Ross’s (1999) case study in the light of the proposed model. The case provides a relatively rich and detailed published account of a system development programme. The case describes a major SAP implementation in Dow Corning Corporation over the period 1994–1997. Its focus is primarily on the organizational implementation project, but includes aspects of project configuration and development.

Dow Corning’s principal business was the development, manufacture and marketing of silicone materials and polycrystalline silicon-based products. In 1994 it had sales of \$2.2 billion but faced increased competition and needed to respond faster to customer needs. Further, it faced a financial crisis, a result of claims, actual and anticipated, over the safety of silicone gel-filled breast implants of which Dow Corning had been the largest manufacturer. The context is therefore a business in turbulence under pressure to deliver change fast in order to achieve short-term cash flow and long-term competitiveness. In terms of IT, Ross (1999) described how the company had evolved from a state of the art mainframecentric environment to a distributed processing approach between the 1970s and 1990s. This had resulted in disparate systems that were responsive to local demands, but were unable to support global business operations that were seen as essential for the future of the company. Project PRIDE, launched in 1994/1995, was a businessled re-engineering project aimed at achieving global transaction processes supporting customer focus, product delivery, financial management and personnel management.

Dow Corning management chose to implement a standard global enterprise resource planning system, SAP/R3, through an initiative that was strongly led by business requirements as well as supported by senior business managers: ‘Project PRIDE was driving IT requirements’ (Ross, 1999, p. 261). Lacefield (vice-president and executive director of business processes and IT and a member of the operating committee) ‘wanted to ensure that investment of IS resources was clearly aligned with the business process priorities’ (Ross, 1999, p. 259). Although the rationale for choosing SAP itself is not discussed in the case, the make versus buy decision was clearly rationalized through the need for standardization: ‘The team was instructed to design workflows around the capabilities of the software rather than solicit requirements based on current Dow Corning practices’ (Ross, 1999, p. 259). In terms of IT strategy the company was ‘getting away from doing a lot of programming to doing systems integration’ (Ross, 1999, p. 261).

The Dow Corning implementation adopted a very high-risk approach. The technology was a relatively new release of SAP. In terms of resourcing they decided that ‘despite the fact that Dow Corning had no prior SAP/R3 expertise, the team was to receive minimal support from consultants’ (Ross, 1999, p. 260). In terms of methodology ‘It became obvious [to Reed, project manager] very quickly that the only way to do a project is to do it as fast as you can’ (Ross, 1999, p. 264). Not surprisingly, this approach proved to be problematical for Dow Corning management: ‘the team . . . became overwhelmed by the complexity of the system’ (Ross, 1999, p. 261) and ‘the team struggled constantly to define its methodology and establish a project plan’ (Ross, 1999, p. 261). There is evidence of management resolving issues decisively and pragmatically with a view to risk management, although reactively at the system development stage rather than proactively at the project configuration stage. For example, in response to the loss of momentum coming to grips with the complexity, Lacefield ‘reorganized the team . . . brought in a new project manager’ (Ross, 1999, p. 261) and set a tight deadline for implementation. Finally, although the initial experience of the project team was a serious weakness, the case reports that, following pilot implementation, ‘Lacefield worked with global team management to formalize as much of that learning as possible and restructure roles as appropriate’ (Ross, 1999, p. 264).

It is neither claimed that the case is a perfect illustration of project configuration, nor that it firmly validates the proposed model. However, it does serve as a relevant published example that lends a degree of face validity to the model, since it demonstrates management thinking along the lines suggested by this research. Dow Corning’s approach shows how the model highlights areas for management consideration rather than prescribing a solution or decision-making formula.

## Implications for research and practice

This work is exploratory and its main contribution is to act as a focus for further work in the area. There is an opportunity and a need for further validation, development and application of this model in a number of directions.

(1) An extension of the population to include greater numbers of organizations and/or interviewees, to elicit perceptions of the relative importance of the drivers associated with project configuration and to enable broad sectoral or international comparisons. Correlation with the success or otherwise of project configuration practice would help to identify best practice and causal relationships. Which drivers warrant the greatest attention, given a set of requirements: strategic direction, technology innovation, experience and learning, risk management or good pragmatic management?

(2) Theories of learning and knowledge management, risk management and the technology adoption process can be applied in order to develop measures of the maturity of organizations with respect to IT project configuration. For example, knowledge management in IT units can be compared with theory and with other professions such as law, medicine, construction and consulting. Further, knowledge management practice can be compared between IT provider and IT user organizations. These measures can be deployed for identifying and diffusing good practice.

(3) Attention to issues of organizational structure and dynamics may add further drivers to the model, as hinted by Lyytinen et al.’s (1996) model. Further, although the interviewees did not raise examples, legal, regulatory and other business environment factors may influence project configuration at both the management and project levels and should be considered explicitly in further research.

(4) Advanced interpretive techniques and in-depth case studies could be used for gaining further insights into the decision logic as well as the process of project configuration. In particular, several discussions with the interviewees revolved around relatively standard projects, yet innovative bespoke development was also occurring in many organizations. For such niche/innovative projects there remains a need for ‘first principles’ logic in order to design an appropriate architecture, since established templates may not be appropriate. Innovative, exceptional or deviant projects would be of particular interest and potential explanatory power.

(5) Research into good practice of the delicate balance between strategic planning and operational experimentation of new technologies would be of interest to organizations such as company J.

In addition, there is already clear applicable value from the model for practitioners.

(1) They can use the model as an awareness framework that can be applied to particular project situations and to encourage informed and reasoned debate of project configuration conflicts and trade-offs.

(2) Organizations can benchmark their own concepts, practices and procedures of project configuration against the insights and practices presented in this paper. The cases demonstrate evidence of different levels of maturity in project configuration practice, although exploration of this topic was beyond the scope of this paper. They can consider more clearly what makes good practice and how to move their organizational structures, roles, processes and technologies in order to improve their practice.

(3) They can address issues of the process of project configuration such as the complex relationship between strategy and project and issues of managing the diffusion of new technology into the organization, its strategy and its individual projects. Attention to the management of learning, knowledge and experience of project configuration is likely to be fruitful in the knowledge management era.

(4) Further insights may be gained by mapping each of the factors in the model on to the specific agencies that mediate them with respect to the particular organization. This process would help to ground the analysis and further illuminate the strengths of each influencing factor and how they are brought to bear. For example, technology suppliers, industry research organizations, technology consultants and internal staff may all be involved to differing degrees in communicating new technologies. The organization may be able to review its strategic relationships, information search and filtering practices and internal political structures in order to optimize its efficiency and effectiveness in the processes of technology adoption, learning and improvement.

It is not suggested that these activities do not already occur, but that a clearer understanding and articulation of an organization’s current practice in the light of the model is likely to stimulate it to improved, more holistic and more consistent practice.

## Critical reflections

This section will briefly reflect on three aspects of the work. The first two concern methodology, while the third revisits the three project configuration elements.

First, the research approach could be coined ‘multiple mini case’. It trades off depth for breadth, having consulted nine organizations but with data sources restricted to single-person interviews. It cannot claim the label of a full case study (Yin, 1994), yet it fitted quite closely with the case study approach and Walsham (1995) argues that interviews are the primary data source for the outside observer. It was seeking to answer the questions of ‘how’ project configuration decisions are made and ‘what’ are the influencing factors. This approach seems justified for exploratory research, since a series of interactive interviews allowed ideas to be developed and compared over a period of time and a variety of organizations from a grounding in the initial literature base.

It was too early to commit to a formal and inflexible questionnaire, requiring open-ended discussion more than closed questions. The number of cases was in part dictated by contacts’ availability and the researcher’s time, but also was capped at the point when it was felt that the researcher had appreciated the essential issues. Perhaps such a multiple, mini case approach warrants a greater profile in the research methodology literature.

Second, the style of supporting argument through quotations may be questioned, but can be used successfully as an element of high-quality case study research (Lacity and Willcocks, 1998). In this case it is accepted that there is room ideally for greater auditability of the interpretation process and that high reliability cannot be claimed. However, it is felt that convincing evidence is presented to support the findings and that the resulting model fits the stories told by interviewees rather well. The presentation of the evidence, together with the application of the model to a published case, contributes significantly to building external validity. The value of the work can be judged by the persuasiveness of its findings (Walsham, 1995; Weick, 1995) and complementary work by other researchers.

Third, in retrospect, although issues of technical architecture, resources and methodology were all addressed, as it turned out the technical issues appeared to dominate the thinking. This may be due to the interest of the author or perhaps happens to be the richest or most natural focus. However, it is felt that the balance used was sufficient for drawing out an interesting variety of drivers of project configuration.

## Summary and conclusions

This paper presents a focused, integrated and current view of the drivers of IT project configuration from an analysis of the views of practising IT managers. It serves as a useful connection between the practice of project configuration and more general IS management, IS development and project management theory. It finds that IT organizations are implementing sophisticated applications, often using powerful, standardized, mature packaged software. IT project configuration is a learning, risk-managed, strategically directed and constrained pragmatic process that is concerned with specifying the technical architecture, resourcing plan and methodology for an IT project that will meet project requirements and satisfy environmental constraints, adopting new technologies as appropriate. Strategic guidelines and practical constraints reduce the potential number of feasible configurations significantly. Strategic management and maintenance of architecture and staff knowledge and managed adoption of new technology are likely to be critical issues for success in a fast-changing business and technological environment. Risk management is not only a prerequisite for successful project configuration, but it is a marketable skill.

The paper has integrated the insights of practitioners with those from the literature in order to present an explicit and holistic new model of the practice of IT project configuration from a management perspective. For now the contribution is mainly towards increasing awareness of the drivers and addressing the limited literature that focuses on the issue of project configuration. The model proposes a reference point for the management, research, evaluation or improvement of practice in the topic of IT project configuration. The paper has also identified a number of directions for further research, including comparative perceptions of the relative importance of the different drivers and their contribution towards successful project configuration, the development of measures of good practice in project configuration, case studies of innovative bespoke project configurations and further development and validation of the theoretical model.
