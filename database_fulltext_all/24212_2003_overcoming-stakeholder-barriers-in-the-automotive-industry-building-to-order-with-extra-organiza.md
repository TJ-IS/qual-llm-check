# Overcoming stakeholder barriers in the automotive industry: building to order with extra-organizational systems

MICKEY HOWARD, RICHARD VIDGEN and PHILIP POWELL Centre for Information Management, School of Management, University of Bath, Bath BA2 7AY, UK

The automotive industry must significantly change its outdated and unprofitable ‘sell-from-stock’ business model to a streamlined ‘build-to-order’ model. Extra-organizational systems (EOS) enable multiple firms to share industry-level systems linked by electronic portals and hubs. Successful EOS implementation requires a coordinated effort among various stakeholders including customers, dealers, vehicle manufacturers, first- and second-tier suppliers and logistics firms. However, each of these stakeholders erects different but significant structural, managerial, user and technical barriers to EOS. This paper analyses these stakeholder barriers and prescribes how they must be managed in order to implement EOS successfully. Three recommendations raise concern over (1) the differentiation between EOS and inter-organizationa systems, (2) industry dynamics, particularly the role of suppliers and customers and (3) supply topology, i.e. the importance of World Wide Web services and standards in creating electronic markets.

## Introduction

Automotive manufacture is a \$17 trillion industry, producing 56 million new cars per annum worldwide and 1.6 million in the UK (Crain, 2000). However, the industry is facing a period of significant change driven by poor profitability, excess finished stock and over-capacity (Pemberton, 2000). Customers are becoming more price conscious and less patient, demanding vehicles built to individual specifications and delivered in shorter lead times (Williams, 2000). Vehicle manufacturers can no longer rely on selling cars from existing stock, no matter how leanly produced and are shifting their business models from mass production and sell from stock towards build to order (Holweg and Pil, 2001). This in turn increases the reliance in the supply chain on integrated information systems (IS) for efficient order execution, business-to-business responsiveness and information sharing.

Communicating effectively at both a local and global level increases the reliance on IS and yet specialized vertical systems, chimneys (Merton, 1968) and silo mentality (Bannister, 2001) persist in an industry wary of the Internet and struggling to come to terms with the digital economy. To satisfy the demands of consumers, players in the auto industry need to move from chains of limited interaction supported by partial interorganizational systems (IOS) to shared collaborative industry supply networks built on business-to-business electronic marketplaces and supported by extraorganizational systems (EOS). In order to survive firms must identify not only the barriers to change, such as information technology standards and lack of sharing, that exist at an organizational level, but also the influence of powerful stakeholders at an industry level. This paper establishes who or what really counts in twentyfirst century automotive supply networks in the UK by developing a structured analysis of IS-related barriers to change and the industry stakeholders that can constitute or impede this change.

This paper is structured as follows. First, the move from supply chains to EOS is outlined. Then the research method appropriate to EOS investigation is discussed and the research protocols outlined. Third, in order to understand stakeholders and IS-related barriers, two frameworks are developed. These are then used for analysing the data derived from the case research. Finally, the implications of the outcomes for management and for research are presented.

## From supply chains and IOS to networks and EOS

The problems associated with a diversity of stakeholders involved in systems and the evolving nature of the World Wide Web (WWW) is common in IS development. Lane and Koronios (2000) argued that IS development approaches have evolved over time from humble beginnings to addressing the problems associated with developing IS across broader organizational scope (intra-, inter- and extra-organizational). This research looks beyond distinctions of boundary, scope or technological enablement in order to differentiate EOS from IOS using an industry (automotive manufacture) as the unit of analysis.

IOS focus on organization-to-organization chains involving bilateral arrangements between two firms (n = 2). A control host or stakeholder governs interaction between ‘lower level participants’ and the ‘node’ (Barret and Konsynski, 1982). EOS transcend conventional supply hierarchies and encompass multiple stakeholders (n > 2) including many small enterprises and individual customers. Characterized by interactivity, dynamic updating, hypertextuality and global presence, these systems are more pervasive than the client server networks of the 1990s and require the consideration of a broader range of internal and external stakeholders. Whereas IOS tend to focus on organization-toorganization chains, EOS involve electronic marketplaces and collaborative networks of supply that create value for the end customer. The bilateral inter-organizational arrangements typical of IOS research in the 1980s require updating in order to reflect shared electronic markets and Internet-based IS. This research defines EOS as ‘multiple firms sharing industry level systems linked by collaborative, electronic portals and hubs’.

Firms worldwide are increasingly setting up businessto-business electronic marketplaces for the procurement of supplies and product development, either privately or as a consortium of competitors within an industry. Baldi and Borgman (2001) argued that two types of exchange have taken over from traditional, bespoke electronic data interchange (EDI)-based systems in the auto industry: consortium trade exchanges and private trade exchanges.

The consortium trade exchange represents a network in the current supply chain structure, consisting of firms that wish to establish trade communication links with suppliers of products and services. One of the largest consortium trade exchanges is Covisint, the members of which include Ford, General Motors, Daimler Chrysler, Renault Nissan and Peugeot Citroen. Business-tobusiness electronic marketplaces represent a central information hub or ‘industry trade exchange’ and, while technically feasible, require the collaboration of a critical mass of industry players in order to be successful (Barratt and Rosdahl, 2002). However, suppliers are sceptical of the ‘win–win’ promises of consortia organizers because they are not being offered shares in the venture. Covisint has evolved from a collaboration between vehicle manufacturers into an exclusive procurement club in order ‘to extract the lowest price from suppliers and solicit electronic requests for quotes, parts, and services’ (Kisiel, 2002, p. 3). Suppliers fear their profit margins will be slashed through Internet auctions and their products turned into commodities. Supplier chief executives claim that ‘All Covisint does is look for low bids. It’s just a bid-trolling process’ (Sedgwick et al., 2002, p. 35). While the consortium trade exchange offers all the functional requirements needed in terms of connectivity and system standardization, it does not overcome the reluctance of suppliers to collaborate. On-line bidding for parts focuses on price, ignoring issues such as quality, plant proximity, technical support and investment in research and development. This has little impact on commodity components (e.g. batteries), but seriously affects the future development of complex vehicle modules (e.g. driver consoles). Only if benefits exist for both buyer and supplier will sufficient firms subscribe to the Covisint exchange and enable it to become a profitable business model.

The alternative is the proliferation of further consortiabased exchanges at different levels of the supply network. For example, first- and second-tier suppliers have responded to Covisint by launching their own consortium exchanges, including SupplyOn (Bosch and others) and Rubbernetwork (Goodyear and others). Some vehicle manufacturers have also refused to join Covisint, opting to continue developing their private trade exchanges, such as Electronic Supply Network (Volkswagen), which focuses on collaborative product development, electronic procurement, logistics and knowledge sharing (Howard et al., 2002). However, the proliferation of electronic exchanges, whether consortia or private, threatens to increase the already burgeoning number of IT standards and protocols across the industry.

Inter-firm communication and information exchange plays a key role in developing holistic value stream optimization in supply relationships (Owen, 1997). The value stream concept evolved from lean manufacturing philosophy during the mid-1990s and concerns tactical and strategic cost-related benefits that ‘extend beyond the single company, and stretch from customer needs right back to raw material sources’ (Hines et al., 2002, p. 2). Value streams also possess the intrinsic capability for distorting information flows, consuming embedded costs and inhibiting the compression of customer order lead times. Thus, inter-firm collaboration is limited by a failure to focus on the exchange of information between organizations, a failure to adopt a total supply view and the inability to manage a network of supply relationships rather than linear chains (Cox and Lamming, 1997). Increasingly, organizations from a range of sectors view value streams as potential sources of competitive advantage and have attempted to foster long-term relationships founded on mutual benefit rather than competition and adversarialism (Owen, 1997). However, structural barriers related to beliefs about other partners are often widespread, leading organizations to suspect that firms calling for the sharing of information have ulterior motives, particularly where there is an imbalance of power within the relationship. For instance, Webster (1995), in her classic description of the pitfalls of collaboration using early IOS in the auto industry, described how EDI is used for preserving the power of dominant stakeholders such as vehicle manufacturers, locking suppliers into procurement relationships and keeping competitors out of them.

In order to achieve build to order automotive partners require EOS that replace the bilateral arrangements that currently exist between two firms. In order to develop EOS the barriers erected by different stakeholders must be managed, shifting from chains of limited interaction and supply hierarchy (e.g. EDI) towards systems that encompass multiple stakeholders (e.g. industry trade exchanges). This constitutes a transformation in thinking for all partners, from old-world industry strategies driven by adversarial relationships and production push towards a collaborative mindset driven by information sharing and customer demand (Lamming, 1993; Holweg and Pil, 2001).

## Research method

Any move from sell from stock to build to order so as to create value for the end customer requires industry transformation and the implementation of EOS, whereby participants collaborate in network fashion in order to create value across the entire industry. Thus, the unit of analysis for this research is the industry. However, investigating the implementation of EOS and build to order requires analysis of the barriers to change and so the concept of industry-level stakeholders must be addressed in order to understand the power dynamics. As in any transformation some stakeholders may gain and some may lose: the political dimension must be considered.

The research aim necessitates an exploratory case approach in order to understand who and what counts and a systematic analysis of the barriers to building to order. Case studies are applicable where control over behavioural events is not needed and there is a focus on contemporary events and multiple levels of analysis. They allow reality to be captured in detail and many variables to be analysed.

This paper builds on inter-organizational collaboration and supply relationships (Sako, 1992; Lamming, 1993) using multiple case study research methods (Eisenhardt, 1989; Yin, 1994). The research was undertaken as part of the 3DayCar programme launched in 1999, which aims to understand the current practice, relationships and technology between key partners in the UK auto industry. Three academic centres conduct research with 25 participating sponsors drawn from vehicle manufacturers, suppliers, dealers, logistics firms, trade associations, IT consultants and financial institutions. The objective of the 3DayCar programme is to develop a framework in which a vehicle can be built and delivered to customer specification in minimal lead times, with 3 days as the ultimate goal.

The research design here develops a framework of barriers and stakeholders and then applies it to the automotive industry. The research method was operationalized through semi-structured interviews, event mapping and review sessions.

## Data collection

Primary data were collected through a series of semistructured interviews with volume vehicle manufacturers and their partners in the UK (Table 1). The Appendix outlines the research instrument, which focuses on defining current IS, describing problems (e.g. integration, lead times and standardization) and seeking to understand where the industry intends to proceed with IT systems. Sixteen meetings comprising one to three management staff were held with firms across the supply chain, half of which provided subsequent access to their manufacturing or technical facilities. Where possible two researchers attended each event in order to divide the task of investigation from data capture and to cross-check for completeness and understanding. Tape recording was considered inappropriate on account of the exploratory (and, therefore, possibly sensitive) nature of the questions and the commercial confidentiality of the subject.

## Mapping events

During the meetings a series of process maps were developed in collaboration with the site staff that reflect the flows of information and products across the organization and the technical infrastructure used to support it. This technique is derived from ‘value stream mapping’ (Rother and Shook, 1998), which exposes waste in the system in terms of inventory or delay. The results of the mapping events were amalgamated in order to provide a whole-system generic view of the interactions between customers, dealers, vehicle manufacturers, suppliers and logistics firms (Figure 1). A key finding centred on the discovery that, of the 40 days it took to deliver a car in the UK, 85% of the delay in the order pipeline was derived from IS, order entry, order processing and scheduling and not vehicle manufacturing.

## Review sessions

The initial results were shared with participating sponsors via a preliminary report and presented at both a private 3DayCar sponsor conference and academic conferences (Howard, 2000; Howard et al., 2001). This allowed for reflection on the initial results and the opportunity for triangulation provided by the views of other IS researchers. The key feedback that resulted in modification of the research was the view that, despite having little interaction in the current order fulfilment process, the role of customers should be emphasized as very significant stakeholders whose car buying habits could define the future of the industry.

## Data analysis

Having arranged the cases into industry groups, i.e. customers, dealers, vehicle manufacturers, suppliers and logistics firms, frameworks were required in order to organize and understand the primary data. Two frameworks were developed: one for assessing the barriers to IS-related change and the second for understanding auto industry stakeholder salience. These provided a structured method of planning for IS-related change in the UK auto industry, with implications for management and for research.

## Stakeholder salience

Stakeholder salience theory (Mitchell et al., 1997) identifies that the most important or noticeable stakeholders possess one or more of three relationship attributes: power, legitimacy and urgency. Mitchell et al. (1997) defined a group or party to the relationship as having power to the extent it has access to coercive, utilitarian or normative means for imposing its will in the relationship. Legitimacy is a desirable social good and is more over-arching than individual self-perception and is shared amongst groups, communities or cultures. Urgency is based on time sensitivity and criticality: the importance of the claim or the relationship to the stakeholder. Combining these attributes generates eight types of stakeholder: dormant, discretionary, demanding, dominant, dependent, dangerous, definitive and non-stakeholder (Figure 2).

(1) Dormant stakeholders possess the power to impose their will on a firm but, by not having a legitimate relationship or an urgent claim, their power remains unused. They have little interaction with the firm, but this will increase should they acquire a second attribute.

(2) Discretionary stakeholders possess legitimacy, but have no power for influencing the firm and no urgent claims. There is no pressure to engage in a relationship with such a stakeholder.

(3) Demanding stakeholders exist where the sole stakeholder relationship attribute is urgency: those with urgent claims, but having neither legitimacy nor power.

![](/api/attachments/MDRAHJGF/fulltext/images/5b01a672b52544f1cea2187a5015a5bc6b83cd81de3d4cb9417d89efb6976267.jpg)  
Figure 2 Stakeholder typology (adapted from Mitchell et al., 1997)

(4) Dominant stakeholders are both powerful and legitimate. Their influence in the relationship is assured, since by possessing power and legitimacy they form the ‘dominant coalition’.

(5) Dependent stakeholders are characterized by a lack of power, but have urgent and legitimate claims. These stakeholders depend on others to carry out their will. Power in this relationship is not reciprocal and is advocated through the values of others.

(6) Dangerous stakeholders possess urgency and power but not legitimacy and may be coercive or ‘dangerous’. The use of coercive power often accompanies illegitimate status.

(7) Definitive stakeholders possess power legitimacy and urgency. Any stakeholder can become ‘definitive’ by acquiring the missing attribute(s).

(8) Non-stakeholders possess none of the attributes and, thus, do not have any type of relationship with the group, organization or industry.

Stakeholder salience analysis is conducted at an industry level, for instance first-tier suppliers’ and vehicle manufacturers. IS-related industry transformation consists not just of inter- or intra-organizational change, but requires an extra-organizational or meta-view of the relationships between all groups in the supply chain. This fits with the current image of networking and boundary spanning in the twenty-first century extending beyond the traditional boundaries of the firm and connecting with ‘any group or individual who can affect, or is affected by, the achievement of a corporation’s purpose’ (Freeman, 1984, p. vi). Industry-level stakeholder salience therefore reflects current thinking in managing inter-organizational relationships where the firm is considered an unsatisfactory unit of analysis and it is necessary to take an industry or total supply chain view (Cox and Lamming, 1997). Stakeholders form an integral part of IS planning in industry and part of the ‘sociology of technology’: IS are not simply a technical– rational process of solving problems, but involve the economic and political processes in articulating interests, building alliances and struggling over outcomes (Webster, 1995).

Two levels of analysis are required for understanding change in the auto industry. The industry-level framework examines industry stakeholder salience. The organizational-level framework examines IS-related barriers: structural, managerial, user and technical. When applied together both frameworks address structure and process at an organizational and industry level. This enables cross-referencing on the difficulty and importance of developing relationships in collaborative supply networks. This paper argues that, by understanding the organizational barriers to change in the context of industry stakeholders, a successful development of EOS strategies and business-to-business electronic marketplaces is made more likely.

## Current stakeholders in the UK auto industry

This section introduces five automobile industry stakeholders, namely customers, dealers, vehicle manufacturers, component suppliers and logistics firms, in the context of the IS-related processes.

## Customers

While customers are becoming more demanding in terms of the quality of service, orders for customized vehicles in the UK can take 50–60 days to be built and delivered. Car buying can be one of the least enjoyable experiences because of the tension between the salesperson who has to sell stock and the customer who wants to make their own choice (Whiteman et al., 2001). In an age of ‘instant gratification’ where personal computers (PCs), photographic development and spectacles have all experienced drastic lead time reductions in order to remain competitive, it seems reasonable to expect commitment to the delivery date and preferred specification of new vehicles. Research in Europe has revealed that some customers want to take delivery of the car they order within 1 or 2 weeks, otherwise they approach an alternative dealership. Increasingly, customers are operating a ‘virtual build to order’ where, by searching the Internet, they find the car that matches their personal requirements somewhere within the distribution system. Despite the proliferation of ubiquitous ‘customer service centres in the UK, 25% of customers do not get the exact specification they want (Williams, 2000).

## Dealers

In the UK, the lack of integration between dealer management systems and dealer communication systems causes high levels of hand keying and information duplication, with identical data such as vehicle description and owner details hand keyed into both systems. Changing the vehicle delivery process from a manual to an electronic system requires significant levels of planning and system integration, as there are approximately 20 hard copy documents per vehicle. The dealers interviewed do not always trust the information they receive to be reliable:

We do not confirm the delivery date to the customer until we have the vehicle on the forecourt: so much can go wrong (sales director, dealer 1).

The manager of one dealership stated that he had heard of 13 new software improvements currently being prepared by the vehicle manufacturer, but had not been consulted about any during the development process. He felt that ‘IT specialists’ at the manufacturer create new software without working with end users. This extends to the use of the Internet by dealers for new car sales: their lack of involvement in the vehicle manufacturers going on-line makes dealers uncertain of the future and provides a gap for new entrants, particularly brokers, for entering the market.

Significant conflict exists in vehicle retailing at present between the traditional ‘territorial sales’ approach encouraged by the vehicle manufacturers and the ‘empowered customer’ approach adopted by new entrant IT specialists. New entrants, offering customers the facility for trawling for a quote from a number of dealers, are undermining the current system based on local sales territories. Vehicle manufacturers of premium vehicles have begun to incorporate configuration tools into their WWW sites, recognizing the power of enabling customers to self-configure and price their own vehicle specifications on the Internet (e.g. www.lexus.com). Despite the imminent threat of losing market share to importers, the traditional boundaries between manufacturer to dealer and dealer to dealer remain, where system ownership and resistance to sharing is obscuring the potential benefits of technological solutions. As one dealer put it

Technology is the easy bit, 90% of our problems are process related (IT manager, dealer 2).

## Vehicle manufacturers

Batch processing is the major IT barrier to a 3-day car: large numbers of customer orders are processed prior to production at a fixed time every 24 hours. The current configuration of manufacturers’ systems typically result in individual mainframe systems updating overnight, processing batches of orders in time-intensive cycles that add 4–5 days to the order lead times. As the information flow through batch processing systems is largely nonsequenced, it is possible for the output of one process to miss the start of the next window, adding further time to the process.

Legacy systems were originally built for a different world of IT capability, for specific tasks and in a situation where technology was associated with control. Systems today are still driven by inbound logistics and production push rather than by order demand. Changes to the IT infrastructure have been achieved by vehicle manufacturers ‘bolting on’ additional systems alongside existing mainframe architecture. Although PC-based client–server architecture offered a powerful industry standard on which to base new systems, few of the old systems had ever been fully engineered out of the business and switched off, resulting in complex, overlapping systems. All of the manufacturers interviewed faced an expensive and ongoing burden of replacement and repair of an ageing systems network. The total lead time required for planning, piloting and ‘rolling out’ systems across several continents can be 10 years. Once committed to an operational IT strategy they have little choice but to complete it, regardless of changes to the external environment that may occur. These are typically central management systems, popular amongst vehicle manufacturers due to their ease of maintenance and the purchasing advantage gained through economies of scale.

The map of the order fulfilment process (Figure 1) illustrates the chimney or silo mentality that persists in the auto industry. Here, functions, operating systems and database storage systems are linked by flows of information. IT tends to be designed to meet the specific objectives of the different players in the supply chain within an organization rather than driven by a customer order fulfilment philosophy and, therefore, inhibits information flow. A mentality exists that focuses on the needs of specific parts of the process without considering what effects may result in other areas. Delay is compounded by the series of batch systems operating such that, once an order has entered the system, it is invisible to the rest of the firm and to other supply chain partners until it reaches manufacture. One IT manager commented on this approach to system design:

It is the interfaces that are the problem, not the systems (systems manager, vehicle manufacturer 3).

## Suppliers

Suppliers to several assembly lines perceived the major IT barrier as a lack of adherence to EDI standards by vehicle manufacturers in terms of protocol (the language used during transmission) and format (the label layout or visual interface). Currently, vehicle manufacturers make EDI format changes up to three times a year. Suppliers already receive messages in approximately a dozen different formats, all of which must be converted to a common standard before they can be processed internally. This causes delay and disruption to the system, such as in the event of a system malfunction.

The first- and second-tier suppliers interviewed were concerned about the significant costs of IT administration caused by the undisciplined approach by vehicle manufacturers and the implications of adopting new technology on an unregulated, individual basis. Converting electronic data to hard copy documentation is a lengthy process for suppliers faced with delivering vehicle parts to depots in transit to other destinations. Typically seven duplicate copies of documentation per part is required, specifying carrier, warehouse, depots and final destination. Smaller, less powerful tier 2 and tier 3 suppliers felt particularly affected:

Once you get past tier 1, there are not any system standards (systems manager, second tier supplier 2).

The WWW has rejuvenated EDI by allowing the same data to be used in a different manner: using the existing telecom infrastructure with any customer in real time. Web-EDI is powered by industry standards delivered in eXtensible Markup Language or ‘XML’ (Vidgen and Goodwin, 2000) and represents a significant opportunity for tier 2 and 3 suppliers, a high proportion of whom are not connected to first-tier suppliers and rely on a fax, telephone or post. However, despite recent innovations, uncertainty exists over what technology they should invest in. Suppliers have taken a rather haphazard approach to IS strategy where, according to the manufacturer they are supplying, they adopt a mix of systems.

There was also concern among suppliers who had adopted web-enabled systems that they do not offer sufficient reliability or security for conducting transactions between businesses. The low stock levels typically held at most plants cannot buffer a total system failure, whether caused by the Internet or otherwise. The suppliers questioned whether the Internet is ready for supporting mission-critical operations, where delay in delivering a message can stop a vehicle assembly line. They were unsure how they will fit into the manufacturing portal Covisint and concerned that it is the vehicle manufacturers, who currently hold the most power in the supply chain, that stand to benefit most. The suppliers were afraid that once they are committed to new technology, it may be used against them to turn their components into commodities and enforce unilateral price cuts.

All Covisint does is look for low bids. Its just a bid-trolling process (Sedgwick et al., 2002, p. 35).

## Logistics firms

Lack of information from the vehicle manufacturer, both in terms of frequency and accuracy, is the greatest operational constraint to logistics firms achieving efficiency in transportation. While inbound logistics systems for materials and components are more developed than outbound vehicle distribution, this contradicts the value of the goods carried. Despite having their own systems, the lack of contractual commitment given to outbound carriers promotes short termism, which hinders longterm investment and the development of new IT facilities. A key issue is poor data on projected volumes where logistics firms can be given different information from component suppliers on required volumes. Outbound logistics carriers particularly complained of inadequate real-time forward data for delivery planning and load consolidation and the lack of a universal open-access data system that is integrated into the manufacturers’ system. An IT manager at a logistics firm commented on the current situation in the UK:

Twenty years ago [logistics] companies worked for dealers. Working for 1000 dealers was easier and more profitable than working with manufacturers today (systems manager, logistics 1).

There is often a lack of integration and coordination of information where most supply chain logistics systems are still organized in a way that reflects the mass production origins of the industry. Proposals by logistics carriers exist for current systems to be reconfigured in order to provide instantaneous updates in order to route changes and to allow for greater flexibility of use by all supply chain partners. It is suggested that wide area networks and extranets can provide a high accessibility of information, ease of transfer and eliminate re-keying of data and time lost on updates. One logistics firm interviewed proposed that there is a need for a reliable information sharing ‘pool’ with manufacturers, which incorporates genuine multi-franchise delivery using an electronic portal or hub. If all systems have access to each other, this enables the maximization of transport through combining overall volumes and using cross docking. However, this approach needs vehicle manufacturers to appreciate what is required: cooperation and trust in the development of new processes and communicating with reliable information.

The outcome of reviewing the IS-related processes reveals that current infrastructure reflects its ‘oldeconomy’ origins (Tapscott, 1996): centralized systems and an emphasis on purchasing advantage gained through economies of scale. This infrastructure was originally built around specific vehicle manufacturers’ functions or departments and serving hierarchical reporting structures where the use of technology was often associated with control (Howard, 2000). The delay illustrated in Figure 1 is less about the physical ‘metal bashing’ and more related to the inefficiencies in the information flows that accompany the supply of materials and production processes. In order to reduce order lead times significantly a transformation is required that embraces the concepts of time-based competition (Stalk and Hout, 1990; Jayaram et al., 1999), transparency (Lamming et al., 2000) and real-time information flow (Womack and Jones, 1996).

The auto industry operates complex IS involving multiple series of batch processing and supplier-tosupplier code conversions before a customer order can be released into vehicle production. These systems have become embedded into the daily routines of the firm, acting as an inhibitor to time compression, customer order fulfilment and organizational change. However, technical barriers are not the only factor inhibiting change, other forces also act, such as social dynamics and the reluctance of industry players to collaborate or share information (Kanter, 1994). Thus, the identification of a broad range of inhibitors or barriers needs to be considered in order to promote a successful transition from long lead times, production push and the erosion of profits through discounted sales towards responsive production and customer pull (Womack and Jones, 1996). Although this paper considers individual firms with regard to the impact of technology in achieving an industry-level transformation, it is also necessary to take account of the wider context of inter-organizational relationships and boundary spanning activities occurring industry wide (Oliver, 1990; Holland, 1995; Kumar and Van Dissel, 1996).

## Understanding barriers to IS-related change

The cases above suggest an industry divided by functional barriers and lacking integration and connectivity. They also illustrate the difficulty of being able to distinguish between product and technology and process and organizational issues. This section argues that a more complex relationship exists between IT barriers and organizational change in the auto industry than simply the concern over the integration of new technology. A barriers approach to change is helpful during IS planning, since the most significant factors affecting implementation are often those that already exist in the firm and have become institutionalized or embedded in daily routines.

Information barriers have been presented in the literature at individual, organizational, industry and international levels. Examples can be found in health care (Kyu and Michelman, 1990), government (Heeks and Davies, 1999; Bannister, 2001), the automotive industry (Wastell, 2001), cross-industry studies (Wilson, 1991;

Li and Williams, 1999) and nationally (Damsgaard and Lyytinen, 1998). Barriers research can be traced to early IOS literature (Barret and Konsynski, 1982) that considers the interchange of information to form the basis of all organizational activity. In their assessment of the impact of inter-organizational information sharing systems, Barret and Konsynski (1982) described ‘factors of concern’ over how such a strategy might be introduced and the effects on organizational structure, the users and the IS department. Markus (1984) described a variety of factors that hinder system implementation: the limitation of technology, the resistance to change on behalf of the individual and the interaction between individuals and the technology.

Kirveennummi et al. (1998) developed the notion of formal and non-formal structural factors that affect the change process. Their ‘barriers information framework classifies the categories based on Kwon and Zmud (1987) as structural, managerial, user and technical. However, the results of the research typically provide only a list of factors, with little underlying data or explicit method of theory generation. This research argues that, in order to understand the inhibitors to successful IOS adoption, the level of barrier must be extended from intra-organizational (the firm) to interorganizational (the industry). Any classification of barriers or factors must also be linked to the type of barrier and its impact on the stakeholder during change (Table 2). For instance, a stakeholder may become disaffected or simply overwhelmed by IS-related change that is too difficult or exerts excessive demands on resources.

Structural barriers include both the formal and non-formal barriers that affect the change process (Kwon and Zmud, 1987). Understanding how information and decision processes flow through organizations requires a thorough knowledge of structure (Mintzberg, 1979). Formal barriers are those that depend on formal organizational structure, such as organizational hierarchy, which can lead to varying degrees of inflexibility, resistance to change and slow reactions. Deep hierarchy indicates vertical communication and centralized decision making and such organizations often find it difficult to see the need for change. The size of an organization can indicate complexity leading to centralization and functional specialization (Kwon and Zmud, 1987). Non-formal barriers can originate from culture and values such as a closed environment and organizational inertia. If people protect and institutionalize these routines they can become blinkered to change and tethered by traditional, outmoded mindsets. Structural barriers therefore consist of all those factors that emanate from organizational structure, culture and policy (Kirveennummi et al., 1998).

Managerial barriers originate from top management where the chief executive officer (CEO) can be the most powerful driving force or hindrance to change. Schein (1994) found in his study of the role of the CEO and the management of IT change that, while all were driven by the state of affairs in their industry, only a small number had transformation visions that went substantially beyond what other firms were doing. Barriers to change emerged where visions of IT empowerment had been abused by control-orientated CEOs attempting to monitor subordinates’ performance. Management’s role in an organization is twofold, involving task-orientated processes and team support. The task-orientated role includes taking initiatives and sharing information while the team support role includes maintaining team harmony and controlling internal communication. Managers may feel threatened by the loss of power heralded by the computerization of production processes and the creation of automated factories (Zuboff, 1989).

User barriers include both internal and external factors as explanations for individual resistance to or acceptance of IT (Martinko et al., 1996). Internal factors can be negative prior experiences with IT, lack of felt need and individual prejudices. External factors include the influence of co-workers and supervisors, technology and management. In order to handle change successfully the learning process must be understood and support the transfer of the learning process into practice, motivation, imagination and creativity (Argyris, 1990; Senge, 1990). Difficulties in conceptualizing change may cause a fear of failure and loss of power and control. People often resist the social changes during IS-related change more than the technical change itself (Kirveennummi et al., 1998).

Technical barriers are studied from a practical viewpoint: to fit IS into a specific role in the organization. New technology requires compatibility with existing norms and user needs and compatibility with other IS. Factors such as IT legacy result in poor visibility and connectivity, where supply chain partners remain unassimilated in collaborative networks. The emergence of web-based IOS has encouraged the growth of middleware and other translator products that link web-based, customer facing systems with operational or back-office systems. However, the commonest technical barrier to IS change is problems that hinder usability, for instance a lack of IT standards, where if a process is too complex resistance occurs.

## Data analysis: identifying the barriers to EOS

The barriers framework (Table 3) provides an analysis of the barriers to IS-related change in the auto industry, serving as an introduction to the problems faced by the UK car industry in planning for the adoption of the customer-focused, build-to-order production philosophy. This section provides insight into the difficulty posed by IS-related barriers to change and the importance of industry stakeholders.

Customers are less willing to accept cars from stock and are increasingly making unfavourable comparisons with other industries. Many dislike the car buying experience and are frustrated when dealers or dealer websites cannot provide order availability because their systems are not linked with production. The managerial and user-related barriers imply customers are becoming disaffected with the current process.

Dealers suffer from poor visibility of orders, an unwillingness to share information and a lack of integration between systems. The structural and technical nature of the problem suggests they are unassimilated into the customer order fulfilment process.

Vehicle manufacturers are overwhelmed by the extent of structural, managerial, user and technical barriers. The barriers framework highlights the legacy-orientated and centralized nature of manufacturers’ systems where current IT solidifies the formal aspects of organizational structure and becomes associated with control at the managerial and departmental levels.

Suppliers are concerned over where they fit into business-to-business electronic trade exchanges such as Covisint and the lack of EDI standards for formats and protocols. Traditional EDI systems are not seen as facilitating vertical disintegration or collaboration, rather for vehicle manufacturers gaining competitive advantage by locking suppliers into its systems and locking competitors out of them (Webster, 1995). Firsttier suppliers are experimenting with web-EDI and have launched their own business-to-business portals, but are hindered by the structural advantage of the vehicle manufacturers. Second- and third-tier suppliers are uncertain over what technology to adopt in the future and lack the resources for coordinating or initiating change. They are resistant and blinkered by the managerial and technical barriers to change.

Logistics carriers are the least integrated into manufacturers’ systems and suffer from a lack of direct planning, lack of contractual commitment and ad hoc sharing of information. They are held back or tethered by the lack of contractual commitment, poor IS links and quality of advance information received from the vehicle manufacturers.

## Industry stakeholders

Barriers look at the members of the supply network, while the industry stakeholder salience framework presents a holistic, outward-looking perspective and defines the motivation driving industry relationships by the attributes of power, legitimacy and urgency (Table 4). While the significance of power in supply chain relationships and the urgency to increase customer response times during build to order are evident, legitimacy requires further clarification. Legitimacy is viewed from a limited economic/consumerist perspective that draws a boundary around the auto supply chain and excludes government, trade unions and environmentalist action groups. This research prioritizes legitimacy in terms of ‘saving the industry’ and returning it to profitability before considering other concerns. However, this limited perspective should be placed in a broader context of social and environmental issues (Miemczyk, 2002).

Customers possess comparatively little power over monopolistic vehicle manufacturer practices, but they have a legitimate claim to good service. However, despite the apparent urgency over transactions with the dealer, many do not receive their preferred vehicle specification and order-to-delivery lead times of 40 days or more is common. The Internet enables customers to become better informed and they are becoming demanding stakeholders, liable to switch support from a dealer or brand in the event of locating a product or service representing better value or attractiveness.

Dealers are controlled by central policies such as pricing strategies determined at the vehicle manufacturer. They are dependent stakeholders because they have legitimacy and urgency, but do not have power. They possess legitimacy in their aim to provide excellent customer service. Urgency is based on the survival instinct to ‘close the deal’ with the customer and encourage repeat business. However, this depends on effective collaboration with the manufacturer in terms of their ability to locate or build the exact specification of vehicle required and commit to a delivery date.

Vehicle manufacturers control major information processes such as production sequencing and scheduling. They justify the centralized and hierarchical systems because of the complex and geographically diverse nature of assembling the product. Their interest in business-to-business electronic marketplaces, such as the launch of Covisint, has also consolidated their power. First-tier suppliers are becoming more powerful by adopting and developing many of the competencies once held exclusively by vehicle manufacturers. Vehicle module assembly is becoming outsourced from production as designs become more complex, requiring more supplier engineering skills and therefore increasing value adding activity. However, despite increasing levels of outsourcing and responsibility over the delivery of modules, first-tiers suppliers are worried by price cuts and the use of Internet auctions. Some first-tier suppliers are developing their own consortium trade exchange, SupplyOn, which enables on-line collaborative development capability and greater bargaining power with vehicle manufacturers. Table 4 shows that first-tier suppliers possess power and urgency, but lack legitimacy and, therefore, are classified as dangerous stakeholders.

Second- and third-tier suppliers lack power in the supply chain and are dependent on vehicle manufacturers for subsidizing EDI links and co-managing component tendering and development. They possess attributes of high legitimacy and urgency where the design of components is critical for the integrity of product architecture and one of the big fears for all manufacturers is stopping the assembly line through problems of component quality, delivery or information reliability.

Logistics carriers lack power and are selected accord ing to price not service. Logistics firms’ IS are not linked to the vehicle manufacturer, resulting in low switching costs. Outward logistics carriers are provided with minimum information, often on a day-to-day basis. Their low legitimacy is due to short-term contractual agreements and where all activities are governed by legislation, for example driver hours, routes, emission and safety. However, logistics firms are driven by the urgent responsibility of maintaining the flow of raw materials and components to suppliers and vehicle manufacturers. Outward logistics is very close to the customer, which means a fast, reliable service using in-house IT is essential. Logistics carriers are demanding stakeholders who require better quality information and contractual agreements from vehicle manufacturers.

The above highlights a critical issue in IS planning: the development of EOS by manufacturers must recognize the importance of collaboration, visibility and reliable information flow and where industry structure can no longer be described in terms of supply chains. In order to continue to attract new members and remain competitive the founders of Covisint must consider first-tier suppliers or ‘half-tier original equipment manufacturers’ whose CEOs already consider themselves to be part of a supply network (Harland, 1996; Cox and Lamming, 1997). While barriers such as legacy systems, lack of standards and functional mindsets within the firm are important (Table 3), they do not represent a complete picture for effective IS planning during industry transformation. The complex nature of the relationships between major players in the auto industry requires a dynamic, outward-facing approach that reflects the motivation of multiple industry stakeholders (Table 4). ## Implications for industry, management and research

Analysis reveals that, while vehicle manufacturers are overwhelmed by the extent of structural, managerial, user and technical barriers, they remain dominant stakeholders because of their power over other groups in the supply chain. While first-tier supplier stakeholders emerge as dangerous stakeholders and are experimenting with web EDI and business-to-business portals such as SupplyOn, they are hindered by the structural advantage of vehicle manufacturers. However, if first-tier suppliers gain legitimacy by developing sufficient leverage for dictating terms on component price or introduce a vehicle model of their own they will become ‘definitive’ and represent a serious threat to vehicle manufacturers. Customers are also expected to become definitive stakeholders, gaining bargaining power over dealers from web-based self-configuration tools and Internet brokers and legitimacy from the increasing competitiveness of the market.

Customers are the drivers and recipients of change, but they are currently both disaffected and demanding and, thus, their characteristic or role in the change process is elusive. Until the system can provide new vehicles in terms of ‘what they want, when they want it’ customers will continue to defect from dealers to using on-line brokers such as Jamjar.com and Virgin.com. Conversely, dealers are unassimilated and dependent and their role as identity seeking indicates a desire for greater integration into future plans for the customer order fulfilment process. The recent removal of block exemption by the European Commission suggests that dealerships face even tougher trading from increased competition. Thus, in the face of legislative and technological change, dealers must embrace new web-enabled concepts (e.g. on-line customer relationship management, vehicle pricing and tracking) and redefine or abandon current territorial boundaries in favour of optimizing customer service.

Vehicle manufacturers are overwhelmed by the task ahead, but remain dominant. They wish to perpetuate their hold over the industry and are characterized here as control seeking. As the most powerful stakeholder they need to take charge of a situation that is rapidly deteriorating into a mess of competing, overlapping networks. A key priority is the establishment of European IT standards similar to those developed by independent bodies in North America (Automotive Industry Action Group) and Australia (Button Programme). Vehicle manufacturers must recognize that the transition from sell from stock towards build to order is a complex process, the evolution of which involves several intermediate stages, such as a ‘locate-to-order’ system between dealers, before the development of full upstream visibility into production.

First-tier suppliers are hindered by the structural advantage of vehicle manufacturers, but are definitive stakeholders who are in a position to lead or take a proactive role in change. First-tier suppliers have already established successful electronic portals that capitalize on the development of core skills and knowledge across supply partnerships. However, a solution must be found between suppliers and vehicle manufacturers where Internet auctions can distinguish between commodity components and high value-added assemblies. Secondand third-tier suppliers are more limited in their plans to adopt new IS and are dependent on vehicle manufacturers: they are playing a reactive role and will require assistance from other partners in order to become fully integrated. While logistics firms are currently tethered by short-term contracts and dependent on manufacturers sharing information, their in-house IT capability, skills in crossdocking and potential for load sharing with other firms means they are eager to become more closely involved in the transformation of the industry.

## Research implications

While the barriers framework (Table 3) works as a device for structuring basic understanding, as with any framework classification it presents ambiguities. For example, structural barriers are defined as formal (organizational structure and policies) and informal (culture). There is overlap between, for example, the user barriers and the cultural aspects of structure. Further, many of the issues will be associated with more than one type of barrier. For instance, with EDI standards there is a technical issue concerning traditional EDI and dedicated computer-to-computer connections versus Internet data standards based on XML. This technical issue also has structural implications for the industry players, as it will affect the formal communication channels between parties. If open data standards are used in business-to-business portals there are management policy aspects to consider, such as the exclusion of smaller suppliers through business-to-business marketplaces (Baldi and Borgman, 2001).

This paper identifies three shortcomings of the barriers approach: the potential for misclassification, a lack of concern for motives, interests and power and the insular nature of the model. The stakeholder salience framework overcomes the second and third problems, while the first problem is one inherent in any classificatory scheme. This suggests that care must be taken when categorizing aspects of the situation as a single type of barrier, particularly where it perpetuates a strong but ultimately artificial divide between social and technical barriers.

Restricting the analysis of IS-related change to frameworks places considerable emphasis on framework categorization, where the danger exists that it may distort or lose some richness of data. Some difficulty was also experienced in narrowing the response to fit the binary nature of the stakeholder salience framework, i.e. low/ high. An improvement over Mitchell et al.’s (1997) original Venn diagram typology could include tabular format with a low/medium/high scale. This raises the methodological debate over how the researchers’ lens may reflect a view that is both falsifiable and generalizable and whether it is ever possible to resolve this using these methods.

## Summary

Four issues emerge from this research resulting in several recommendations. First, the auto industry has to move from a sell-from-stock to a build-to-order business model. If the industry does not then others will and the UK auto industry will enter a terminal decline.

Second, industry transformation is socially, politically and technically complex. From a supply perspective it requires networks rather than chains and from an IS perspective it requires EOS rather than IOS. It also requires genuine collaboration, information sharing and much greater transparency of operations using electronic marketplaces and exchanges. Without this there is no chance of a 3-day car.

Third, the stakeholders in the auto industry are faced with different barriers to change (intra-organizational) and have different interests and more or less to gain/lose (inter-organizational). Hence, the unit of analysis for this work is the industry, not the individual firm, but to understand industry dynamics requires examination of the barriers to change within firms.

Finally, the analysis of barriers and stakeholder salience leads to the following recommendations/ findings for the auto industry.

(1) EOS is not the same as IOS: EOS does not mean connecting enterprise resource planning chains together, but requires an ‘intelligent hub’ at the core. The introduction of electronic hubs in the auto industry remains in a transitional phase and has in some cases simply ‘speeded up the mess’ (Howard et al., 2002). Other industries have introduced electronic hubs more recently, with greater commitment from supply partners and with resultant benefits, for instance the aerospace hub ‘Exostar’.

(2) Industry dynamics: vehicle manufacturers need to on their guard against first-tier suppliers usurping their long-held position and where the barriers to entry into the industry are diminishing. In addition, customers are becoming increasingly suspicious of high vehicle prices in the UK and dissatisfied with long delivery lead times. The Internet enables them to bypass the UK and to search for better service across Europe.

(3) Supply topology: technology, web services and standards are essential in creating electronic hubs and electronic markets. The move from EDI to Internet-based standards requires collaboration from the industry, but who will set them: the government, the industry or a third party service provider? The auto industry needs to embrace build to order at all levels, but is held back by the chimney mentality and functional mindsets. Genuine commitment from all partners is needed in order to overcome the barriers to industry transformation.

## Appendix: current IT systems in the UK auto industry

This document defines the scope of the study into current IT systems within the UK auto industry for the purposes of further investigation by the 3DayCar team. The objective is to detail the current generic IT systems and identify inhibitors to progress towards the 3-day car. The study is aimed at dealers, vehicle manufacturers, component suppliers and logistics carriers.
