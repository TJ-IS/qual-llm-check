# Writing case studies in information systems research

HEICO VAN DER BLONK

Department of Organization Studies, Faculty of Management and Organization, University of Groningen, PO Box 800, 9700 AV Groningen, The Netherlands

Case study research can be reported in different ways. This paper explores the various ways in which researchers may choose to write down their case studies and then introduces a subsequent typology of writing case studies. The typology is based on a 2 × 2 matrix, resulting in four forms of writing case studies: chronology, play, biography and voices. The four forms are characterized and discussed and some methodological issues concerning the writing of case studies are highlighted.

## Introduction

We know of most case study research through the written accounts of those cases. The researcher writes these as an account or a reflection of the research. It is therefore noticeable that the ways or the forms in which case study research can be written and reported is a rather neglected issue in many case study accounts as well as in much case study methodology literature. The information systems literature clearly lacks ideas about the various forms in which case studies can be reported and how this can be understood. Consequently, most cases are reported in a cultivated style of writing that values limited metaphor, simplicity and a formal precision (Stake, 1995; Van Maanen, 1995). This paper is concerned with a larger variety of forms of case study accounts that a researcher could choose from in writing his or her case study. This variety of forms also includes the widely used causal form, but includes other less orthodox forms as well.

Engaging in case study research involves formulating research questions, gathering data, analysing the case and being reflective about the position of the researcher with regard to the case, among many other things (Yin, 1994; Stake, 1995). Certainly in information systems research case study researchers have to position and legitimate themselves next to, for example, survey research. This generates an image that case study research is a distinctly identifiable research method. However, case study research in itself is very diverse and it is difficult to view it as one uniform method of research. Case studies can be designed and conducted in various ways and, moreover, data gathering and analysis can be done in various ways (Platt, 1992; Stake, 1995).

The issue of writing case studies is approached here primarily as a methodological issue and not so much as a paradigmatic theme. Although the paradigm of the researcher from which the research has been performed and the form in which the research is written are certainly related, this relation cannot be viewed as unambiguous and deterministic. Therefore, a discussion about forms of writing should not be a disguise for a debate on paradigms because that would conceal the relevance of writing in case study research and the choices open to the researcher with regard to writing case studies. The paradigm debate is discussed elsewhere (Hirschheim and Klein, 1989; Orlikowski and Baroudi, 1991) and is not touched upon further in this article.

The aim of this paper is to introduce a typology of several forms in which case studies can be written. However, in order to describe this typology of forms, some observations on the nature of the activity of writing itself is first included in order to highlight the importance of writing as part of research. Subsequently, some important thoughts on writing as part of research are presented by briefly reviewing the methodological literature as well as literature from the social sciences. Next, two dimensions are introduced that form the basis for a matrix of four forms of writing case studies. Each form will subsequently be characterized, described and illustrated with some tentative examples from the literature. The paper will end with a discussion of how researchers could face the choice of what form of writing to choose for their case study accounts.

## About writing

Writing is an often undervalued and neglected part of research within the social sciences, which far too often is viewed as ‘the final stage’ of research in which everything read, thought over or discovered is simply summed up and written down in a commonly used foreign language.

Writing, however, is an integral if not the most important part of research because it is a way (perhaps the most dominant way) of expressing and communicating thoughts (Richardson, 1994; Wolcott, 1995) or, as Stake (1995) put it, ‘. . . the final writing, for me, is more than aggregation of sections but a shaping of them into a narrative that makes the case comprehensible. It sometimes takes on a story quality. And that is a story I come to understand as I am writing it, not really before’ (p. 124). It follows that writing cannot be seen as a neutral process in which one’s thoughts become explicated in words on paper, but is in itself a powerful and forming (and yet very limiting) process of selecting, formulating, editing and rewriting texts that are co-determined by the rules of language, institutions, fashion, an imagined audience, style preferences and the talents of the author. Written text (writings) is a way of presenting ideas and facts and, thus, possesses subtle structures of manipulation, power, prejudice and presumptions. Writing is a process of ordering and a process in which actors define order in their worlds (Hassard, 1993).

Writing case studies is a purposeful act or, to put it differently, cases are written with a purpose, even though what exactly the purpose is does not always seem clear. The purpose is of course closely linked to the goal of the research project of which the case study is a part, but other purposes might also be present in the description itself. The purpose of case study accounts may be to test a certain theory, to illuminate a certain remarkable happening or to describe a phenomenon or process that is not yet theoretically understood. Alternatively, the purpose may just be to structure the chronology of events.

Although writing seems a standard practice for many of us, guided by several conventions and procedures (such as usual text structures, compositional conventions, principles of communication and, of course, language), writing can in fact be a very personal activity, a praxis that is neatly connected to preferences and ways of relating to the world. Instead of seeing writing as a sterile phase at the end of the research it rather needs to be seen as an active struggle by the researcher with his or her material and what it is that needs to be said. Instead of writing down thoughts ‘[w]riting is a great way to discover what we are thinking, as well as to discover gaps in our thinking (Wolcott, 1990, p. 21). A nice example of this can be found in Turkle (1995) where the researcher reflected on her manner of writing: ‘In the late 1960s, I studied history and political theory in Paris. In my academic program, all foreigners had to take a French composition class. Over the year, the format of this class never varied. A subject was set, everyone had one week to turn in an outline and two more to write the composition. Then the three-week cycle would begin again with the assignment of a new topic. The format of the composition never varied. Each one had to be written in three parts, with each of these parts further divided into three parts. Although I knew many of my classmates took to this style easily, for me this was a completely alien way of writing. My way had been to read, think, and make notes on little pieces of paper. I would spread these notes out in my room, across my bed, desk and floor. Then I would immerse myself in their contents, move them around into patterns, scribble in their margins, associate to new patterns, write small bits of text, and frequently rewrite sections. Now, under pressure from the new rules, I developed a technique for getting by. I did my reading and thinking, wrote my notes and bits of text, spread them out in my room, and let my composition emerge – but I did all this in the first week, periodically adjusting the emerging composition so that it would grow with the right number of divisions and subdivisions. After a week of hectic activity, I extracted an outline in three parts. I turned in that outline and put the completed composition in my desk drawer, where it waited two weeks for its turn to be handed to the instructor’ (p. 50).

Writing is a word with a double meaning: it refers both to the end product as well as to the process by which it comes about. It is difficult to see them apart from each other, although the emphasis of this paper is on the various forms of writing rather than on the process of writing itself (see Becker (1981) and Wolcott (1995) for interesting discussions on the process of writing). Having collected and analysed the empirical research material the writing of a case can be done in different ways. The time of translation of a rich and complex experience (which a case study often is) into a certain number of pages of written text is a sometimes limiting but sometimes revealing process. Several authors have introduced classifications of and systematical distinctions between writings.

Pettigrew (1990), for example, distinguished between several varieties of ways in which research outputs from longitudinal (comparative) case study research may be reported. One form of output is what Pettigrew (1990) called analytical chronology. In such an output the case is described and the story is told across several levels of analysis. The aim throughout such a description is to interpret and explain explicitly the specific questions that guide the case study, both in terms of the features of the inner context as well as changes in the outer context. Analytical chronologies are prepared for clarifying sequences across levels of analysis, suggesting causal linkages between levels and establishing early analytical themes. The disadvantage of such chronologies is that they do not provide an understanding of how and why those linkages exist and, thus, the need arose for a more thematical approach in which events and facts through time are grouped together. Another form of research output, the interpretive theoretical case, moves the analysis and writing beyond analytical chronology. It is aimed at interpreting the narrative and also linking emerging conceptual and theoretical ideas that are inductively derived from the case both to stronger analytical themes within the case and to wider theoretical debates in the literature. Other forms that were mentioned by Pettigrew (1990) are diagnostic case reports and cross-case analysis. Diagnostic case reports are produced in order to interact with the organization in which the research was conducted. Besides analytical chronology a diagnostic case contains a listing and analysis of current problems or concerns as perceived by the researcher(s). These reports can be used for feedback to the research site, which can be an agreement in order to gain access. The goal of cross-case analysis is to link theoretical and empirical findings across cases and to relevant literature.

These different ways in which research outputs can be presented refer to several issues a case study researcher is confronted with when writing case studies. The first is the choice of whether to present the case in a more distant way (such as analytical chronology) or a rather immersed way by explicitly including interpretations as well. The second issue is the (perceived) audience for whom the case study is written down, i.e. for interaction with the organization studied or for an academic audience. The third issue refers to the purpose of the description such as feedback to an organization or comparison of several cases.

Whereas Pettigrew (1990) was concerned with the different formats of research outputs, Van Maanen (1988, 1995) focused on the art of writing itself as a crucial element of writing ethnographies in social and cultural studies: ‘it is the written report that must represent the culture, not the fieldwork itself . . . Writing is office work or deskwork, not fieldwork’ (Van Maanen, 1988, p. 4). He viewed the process of writing as far from neutral and as being influenced by traditions and institutional and disciplinary forces. He further referred to ‘narrative and rhetorical conventions’ that influence writing, such as preferences of expression, the use of metaphor and ways of phrasing and organizing text. Van Maanen (1988) described the various styles of writing as ‘tales’, thereby drawing attention to the idea that fieldwork accounts often have a story-like character: ‘. . . writing is what writers do, and it stands at least one-off from what is written about’ (p. 8). Van Maanen (1988) came to a ‘loose categorization’ of styles of writing or ways of working and telling.

He first described realist tales as tales that ‘provide a rather direct, matter-of-fact portrait of a studied culture, unclouded by much concern for how the fieldworker produced such a portrait’ (Van Maanen, 1988, p. 7). Realist tales are the most common form of ethnographic writing and are characterized by four main conventions. First, the author is almost absent from the finished text.

Second, realist tales focus heavily on regularity and often-observed activities and behaviour. Third, much effort is put into making sure that the material presented is really the informants’ view and not that of the fieldworkers. Finally, the researcher is the final (and just) interpreter and presenter.

Second, Van Maanen (1988) mentioned confessional tales that ‘focus far more on the field worker than on the culture studied’ (p. 7). The confessional tale is mostly characterized by its personalized style, in which the author’s voice is not disembodied from the text, but where the text is intimately connected to the researcher. This is portrayed, for example, when researchers report about fieldwork in all its incompleteness and fallacy instead of usual (and realist) accounts of unstained research. In addition, it is stressed in the account that the point of view is the author’s point of view. Finally, regardless of the confession about the imperfections of the fieldwork, in the end the resulting research is more or less adequate. A nice example in the information systems literature is Schultze (2000).

The third and last style of writing is referred to as impressionist tales, in which, analogous to impressionist paintings, attempts are made at capturing a certain impression or moment in time (Van Maanen, 1988, p. 101). The researcher sketches an account that consists of metaphors, words, phrasings and experiences during the fieldwork. An impressionist tale represents the doing of the fieldwork rather than the doer or the done, while the fieldworker is a student learning about the culture studied, i.e. the account suggests the role of a learning process on the part of the researcher. Reflective and mediated themes may develop during the account in directions determined by the fieldworker. The quality of an impressionist tale is constituted by its believability instead of its accuracy and the narrative must be kept rolling for the sake of continuity. It is important that the tale is transparent and concrete, so that the reader can form his or her own judgements about it. The account provides the idea that fieldwork is a long social process of coming to terms with a culture, and important aspects of the tale are interest (does it attract?), coherence (does it hang together?) and fidelity (does it seem true?). The characters in the story must be given a name, a face, motives and lines to speak. It is a vital aspect that the fieldworker gives voices to the natives.

Van Maanen (1988) also drew attention to various issues in writing case studies. First, accounts of fieldwork may involve several characters or voices that interact. In addition, the style of writing may vary from distant to more involved and literary.

Unlike Van Maanen (1988), Yin (1994, p. 133) saw written case studies more as storage of information rather than as stories. Therefore, he was not so much concerned with styles of writing but with the importance of composition and structuring in writing case studies. By their very nature case study accounts need to be composed rather than just filled out according to a predefined format. Yin (1994) distinguished six alternative illustrative structures. The linear–analytic approach is the first and standard approach, which follows the outline of a problem statement: a literature review, methodology, empirical findings and conclusions and implications. A comparative structure is used when one case is described several times in order to compare the various descriptions of the explanations. The chronological structure is the third type, which generally covers events over time ordered in subsequent periods or phases. The fourth type is the theory-building structure, which is structured in such a way as to make a theoretical argument. In the fifth structure, a suspense structure, the outcome of a case study is presented up front while subsequent parts of the description are devoted to the explanation of this outcome. Finally, an unsequenced structure is used when no particular order or sequence is seen as important, i.e. the parts of the description could easily be rearranged without the descriptive value being lost.

## A typology of writing case studies

As discussed in the previous section the differences in writing case studies that one could observe have to do with several issues of writing as an activity and with the writer as an actor. Researchers as writers may have several roles to play, may write for different audiences or may tell different tales. Moreover, this article tries to concentrate on the forms of written case study accounts for which two dimensions may help us to understand the many differences that could be observed. This section deals with the two dimensions that are used for generating a matrix of four distinct forms of writing case studies (see Figure 1).

These forms will subsequently be discussed in relation to the issues brought forward in the previous section. However, first the two dimensions will be described.

## Dimension 1: reduction–complexity

Case studies are often conducted in order to gain a rich understanding of a phenomenon and, in information systems research, the intensive nature, the richness of a case study description and the complexity of the phenomenon are frequently stressed in case study reports. However, the dilemma of the researcher regarding how to represent an empirical case stands in opposition to the richness and complexity of it or, as Wolcott (1990) put it, ‘The major problem we face . . . is not to get data, but to get rid of it!’ (p. 18). Any description inherently means a loss of the richness of the empirical material because not everything can be described and any description is a selection.

This dimension refers to the level of complexity that the researcher wants to portray in his or her account of the case study. It is split between attempting to understand the case through reduction and attempting to understand the complexity of the case. The first position (reduction) aims to reduce the richness and variety of the case into a predefined format (such as chronology or a storyline) so that a certain pattern or insight is generated. In order to construct the case study account the richness and complexity is negotiated, simplified or left out so that a clear storyline is created that aims at a particular outcome or insight.

The other position (complexity) tries to understand or explain the complexity and variety of the case study explicitly by, for example, including contradictory material, chaotic situations, impressions, extreme opinions or emotions. The structure of such forms of writing may turn out to be more ambiguous or left (partially) open to the reader. In addition, this form is usually more extensive as compared to reduced accounts, thereby resulting in longer texts or even books. Such a complex form of writing is particularly suitable in cases when contradiction, diversity and variety are at the heart of understanding the focal phenomenon of the research.

## Dimension 2: monologue–multilogue

In the previous section it was also mentioned that case studies may include several characters with their own positions, opinions and views that might be included in case study descriptions in order to enhance the understanding of particular issues. This may present a dilemma for a researcher regarding whether to describe the case from a distant point of view (the viewpoint of the researcher) or to include different characters and viewpoints in the account. The second dimension refers to this dilemma by distinguishing between monovocality and multivocality in the case study description.

First, in the monologue position the case is reflected as a uniform story that is to be told in one way: the author’s way. The author here is the powerful (though often invisible) voice that tells the case story. The intention of how the story is told may not always be clear, although often the intention is explicated, as in research studies in which careful analysis may be mentioned. However, the fact that other forms of telling the same story might be possible is often not acknowledged. To put it differently, in the monologue position the way of telling might be made transparent, but the way of telling is not contrasted with other possible ways.

In the other position, which is termed the multilogue position, the case is represented as an interaction of multiple voices, possibly including the author’s voice as one of them. Here, the description is less distanced and gives the reader a feeling that he or she is interacting with different people with different positions or opinions. It gives the reader a closer feeling to the empirical material, such as interviews with people, documents or written reports, telephone conversations or recorded observations. Of course the researcher is omnipresent in the description, but the (powerful) voice of the researcher gives room for different voices.

## Forms of writing case studies

In this section the four forms of writing case studies will be briefly characterized and illustrated with examples of empirical research in information systems. The chosen names for the forms of writing case studies need to be seen as ideal types based on the two dimensions described. Various mixes between the four forms are possible and distinctions between the four types cannot just be based on their labels. For example, a biography can also be (partly) written as a chronological account or the author of a multiple-voice account can also be absent from the text, thereby appearing as the director of a (non-objective) play. The main characteristics and distinctions between the four forms are depicted in Table 1 and will be described further for each form afterwards.

## Chronology

A chronology is probably the most common way of reporting case studies in information systems research. Case descriptions often start with some historical context or antecedent conditions before the chronology of the case unfolds from the beginning until the end. Facts and events are introduced and structured along an (imaginary) time line, so that a causality of how events at time 1 preceded or led to events at time 2 is produced.

The structure of such accounts often follows the form of a classical historical account that consists of the following elements (De Buck et al., 1992).

(1) Exposition. This part mentions the nature of the material and the sources used for the research. Relevant conditions are described as well as the factors that are going to be important in the entanglement. An exposition can be synchronical (i.e. a ‘slice’ of a particular moment in time) or diachronical (i.e. an overview of several opinions of a historical period).

(2) Entanglement or plot. In this phase the factors introduced in the exposition are developed further and circumstances change so that the narrative enrols. The narrative may be split up into several levels of analysis, which is best seen as an analytical device for structuring the material.

(3) Outcome. At each level of analysis the narrative may end in an outcome, i.e. outcomes may be different at different levels of analysis. The narrative ends with a conclusion that presents a summary of the narrative and locates it in the historical or theoretical framework used in the historical narrative.

Researchers construct their narratives roughly along guidelines like these of the historical narrative. This leads to the observation that researchers bring with them a perspective or a central conception that lies behind the narrative and which is needed to select the facts and give them meaning (Lorenz, 1990). Although this perspective may be questioned, many researchers would contend that the facts are the facts. Facts can be seen as the building blocks of reality, even though the researcher remains the architect of the narrative. Such central conceptions may even determine or construct chronology.

Chronological narratives of case studies are often presented as accounts of reality, as if separated from the author yet carefully observed and formulated by the researcher. Many examples may be encountered in the literature, but as a good example the case of the evolution of airline reservation systems, as reported in Copeland and McKenney (1988), may be thought of. The case description starts with ‘the experimental years’ and the ‘humble beginnings’ and progresses through ‘technological innovations’ and ‘learning by doing’ to a situation of ‘monopolistic power’.

## Play

A less common way of writing case studies is referred to here by the metaphor of a play. It reflects the important yet mostly invisible position of the researcher as the director of the play or as the author of the text. The case description seems to leave room for the different voices of the actors who are brought to the stage, but the contents of the voices are predetermined by the author so as to create a story that appears to have multiple perspectives interacting towards one outcome or the climax of the case. However, the multivocality is highly manipulated and predetermined (in contrast to the voices approach) by the author in order to tell the drama story. The story itself is not fictional but built on facts that are used as a kind of raw material for creating a dramatic story. The case study writing unfolds because the author brings in or removes characters in the story or lets the characters have lines to speak or ideas to generate.

Newman and Robey’s (1992) social process model provides an example of a play. That model appears to explore the interaction between systems analysts and users, but the description is preoccupied with the outcome, i.e. the level of success of the project. Both the analysts and users (as if they were actors on a stage) are drawn apart in order to create a story of conflict and opposition that is used for the climax or the outcome. The process described in the case develops in several directions, but it progresses towards one eventual outcome. Another example is Isabella (1990) who described four stages in the interpretation of key events by managers and where the description contains many quotes confirming and illustrating the message of the description. Moreover, Barley’s (1986) case study concerned the introduction of computerized scanners in two different radiology departments of hospitals. The description reads as a play in which various actors have roles to play and use scripts in their interaction. Besides the various roles of the actors, the process (or play) progresses through several subsequent phases and produces an outcome.

## Biography

Biographers write about lives and that is all that biography is actually about (bios means life and graphein means to write) (Smith, 1994). Biography is some form of historical description, as definitions in the main English dictionaries suggest and is obviously concerned with the lives of persons or individuals. However, it also includes much interpretation because no two biographies are the same and many biographies may exist about the same person, as is, for example, the case with Charles Darwin, Virginia Woolf or Karl Marx.

Smith (1994) reinforced the interpretive nature of biographies even more strongly: ‘For the reader, determining what one learns [from a biography] becomes an exercise in critical judgment . . . readers must do their own constructing, reconstructing, and evaluation’ (p. 288). The metaphor of a biography as a form of writing case studies is also recognizable in information systems research where, for example, the researcher describes the ‘life’ of an information systems project (Paul, 1994).

Several types of biographies can be distinguished, based on the underlying dimension of the ‘degree of intrusion of the author in the manuscript’ (Smith, 1994, p. 292).

(1) Objective biography. A factual collation, with minimal biographer interpretation. Impossible in an absolute sense.

(2) Scholarly–Historical biography. The emphasis is on facts and chronological organization, but with increased attention paid to the historical background and character development.

(3) Artistic–Scholarly biography. Whilst retaining the factual research, the author takes the role of the creative, imaginative artist who presents details in a lively manner.

(4) Narrative biography. Scenes and conversations may be fictionalized based on letters and documents.

(5) Fictional biography. Almost a historical novel, with minimal attention to original research and primary resources.

These various types of biography reflect the choices available to the researcher for describing the case selected or a person’s life. In addition, in describing case studies of information systems a choice needs to be made as to whether to present a set of ‘facts’ or to what extent it should be turned into a story.

## Voices

In this form of writing case studies the focus is on interaction between multiple voices. The empirical case material is not approached as a neutral collection of facts but as an ongoing outcome of the interaction between the diverse actors and events that are presented by the writer of the account. Space is given to the different voices that mutually generate and shape the case story. The account in this sense becomes the result of a complex construction of influences and interpretations, of which the author’s own voice is not the least important one. The voice of the researcher should also be clearly defined and be made recognizable. The author is a facilitator of the various voices and is therefore very much present in the description.

Weil (1996) provided a striking example of the voices approach. In writing up her research on adult learning processes she attempted to create a multitextured narrative that would do justice to the journey of the research, instead of just presenting an outcome of the research. She also strongly opposed conventional ways of academic writing, what she termed ‘anaesthetized accounts’, in which the author is not present. She described her approach as ‘opening up multiple realities’ by ‘remaining alert to different voices of others and of herself’. Weil (1996) used four voices in her account, which consisted of detailed excerpts and appendices: her own voice as a researcher, her own voice as a reflexive voice, which engages in dialogue with the first, field records (such as time- and context-specific documentation) and the voice of the participants in her research project.

Other researchers have also followed a voices approach in their case study descriptions. A classical example is the way in which Whyte (1943) described street gangs in his book Street Corner Society: The Social Structure of an Italian Slum. Various gang members and related people tell their opinions and interpretations of events. Law (1994) let people formulate and tell their own stories of themselves and one another – layer upon layer of stories. Then he formulated and told stories of them. Stories are often more than stories: they are clues to patterns. Law (1994) arrived at the idea that, although matters are contingent, there are patterns in that contingency. Latour (1996) used a similar technique: in his book a young engineer is describing his research project and his sociotechnical initiation. His professor offers a running commentary. The (invisible) author adds verbatim accounts of real-life interviews along with genuine documents and several voices chime in.

The voices approach compares to what Van Maanen (1988) referred to as jointly told tales where informants are given space by the author in which to tell their views. This form of writing respects the authority of the informants and tries to bridge the gap between the different meaning systems of the researcher and researched. However, this striving for equal validity is not the same as equal power because it is the researcher who takes care of the editing and publishing, not the informant (Van Maanen, 1988 (referring to Tyler, 1986)).

## Conclusion

Several authors have written about the variety of styles and forms of writing case studies. In this paper an attempt has been made to present systematically the various forms of case study writings that researchers can use for reporting their case studies. Attention has been paid to several elements of case study writing that influence the format of the case study description. First of all it is important how the researcher wants to represent the case and what (research) aim the description needs to serve. Second, it is important how a researcher perceives his or her own role and position, not just in the research but also in the writing of the research. Third, a researcher needs to choose whether to write a linear, multilinear or non-linear account of the case.

All too often researchers do not seem to be addressing this opportunity for choice in writing case studies or are not fully aware of these choices. In this paper the need for researchers to be reflective about these issues is stressed because, after all, writing is a way of selecting, choosing and forming and, thus, an important part of research.

## Biographical note

Heico Van der Blonk is an assistant professor of organization studies at the Faculty of Management and Organization of the University of Groningen. He holds a bachelor’s degree in information systems analysis and a master’s degree in organizational sociology. He worked with the Department of Information Systems at the Free University Amsterdam to complete his PhD on long-term change processes around information systems in organizations. His current research is focused on the uses of and social practices around technology in general and information technology in organizations in particular.
