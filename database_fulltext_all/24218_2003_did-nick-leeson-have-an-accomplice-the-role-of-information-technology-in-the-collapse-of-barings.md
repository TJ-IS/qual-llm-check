# Did Nick Leeson have an accomplice? The role of information technology in the collapse of Barings Bank

HELGA DRUMMOND

The Management School, University of Liverpool, Liverpool L69 3BX, UK

This paper analyses how Nick Leeson exploited information technology (IT) in order to trade without authority and thereby causing Barings Bank to collapse. What emerges suggests that, not only can IT systems enable malfeasance on a large scale, but that very little specialist knowledge is required in order to turn the computer into an accomplice. The most disturbing feature of the Barings Bank debacle is the contrast between the mundane act of suppressing a control mechanism and the consequences that flowed from this act. It is argued that organizational security ultimately lies in knowing the limits of one’s information.

## Introduction

The five eight’s account showed a shocking disparity between what they thought Leeson had been doing and the truth (Gapper and Denton, 1996, p. 28).

Every year organizations lose billions of pounds through malfeasance. Electronic transmission increases the risk because of the speed with which records can be set up, manipulated and stored. This point was graphically demonstrated in February 1995 when Barings Bank suddenly collapsed. The unauthorized trading of a relatively junior employee named Nick Leeson destroyed Barings Bank. For 3 years Leeson deceived the bank, reporting huge but fictitious profits whilst concealing mounting and, ultimately, catastrophic losses. Much has been written about why Barings Bank failed to detect the true nature of Leeson’s activities until it was too late save the bank (e.g. Fay, 1996; Gapper and Denton, 1996; Ross, 1997; Stein, 2000). Yet comparatively little attention has been paid to the role played by information technology (IT) systems in enabling Leeson to trade without authority.

The issue is important because the system of surveil lance did not fail but worked exactly as it was intended to. Yet malfeasance on a large scale went undetected for nearly 3 years. Moreover, Barings Bank was destroyed not by the malevolent genius of a computer ‘hacker’ or a technically literate insider. It took just a few strokes on a computer keyboard and a piece of routine form filling to set the stage for Barings Bank’s destruction. In other words, what happened in Barings Bank could happen in almost any organization.

More specifically, this paper focuses upon a computerized error account that Leeson used for hiding his unauthorized trades. Analysis seeks to answer three questions as follows.

(1) How was Leeson able to evade organizational controls in order to establish the account?

(2) Why did the existence of the error account go unnoticed until Barings Bank was doomed?

(3) What are the implications of the answers to questions (1) and (2) for practice?

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. First, the theoretical context is sketched. This is followed by a brief account of the background to events and a more detailed description of the circumstances concerning the establishment of the error account. Analysis then considers how employees can defeat systems of surveillance. The paper ends with recommendations for practice.

## Theoretical context

Malfeasance is basically a form of opportunism. In theory, opportunism in organizations is impossible because organizations employ bureaucratic techniques of surveillance, including IT systems, in order to ensure that employees do not deviate from their contractual obligations (e.g. Ouchi, 1980). In practice, perfect control is virtually impossible to achieve. This is because organizations rely upon employees as agents for executing their directives. Such dependence invariably confers some autonomy, that is the option of acting differently (e.g. Giddens, 1979; Wrong, 1979). Giddens (1979), for example, stated that ‘However wide the asymmetrical distribution of resources involved, all power relations manifest autonomy and dependence in both directions’ (p. 149).

In theory, the scope for exercising autonomy in organizations is extremely restricted, particularly at the lower echelons as employees are allocated sufficient power for enabling them to do their jobs, no more and no less.

Yet empirical evidence suggests otherwise. The organizational literature contains many examples of how employees can acquire power above and beyond their role prescription and how such power can be used for destabilizing the organization (e.g. Sterba, 1978; Drummond, 1991).

Mechanic’s (1962) seminal analysis suggested that informal power arises because an organization’s dependency upon its employees gives employees access to resources, namely people, instrumentality and information, that can be used for unintended purposes. Mechanic (1962) suggested that informal power becomes enhanced when a lower participant controls something of importance to the organization. For example, Sterba (1978) noted how the Chinese civil service was dominated for 1300 years by low-ranking clerks who used their control over information systems to ‘Confound, frustrate, inveigle and even intimidate their more prestigious superiors’ (p. 70).

Sterba (1978) did not explain how the clerks achieved such control in the first place and why their ‘more prestigious superiors’ were unable to penetrate it. The issue is all the more perplexing because the principal information system was handwriting and the records were of the most basic manual kind. In other words, extreme penetration was achieved even though no abstruse knowledge was involved.

Resources alone cannot explain informal power because resources are not in themselves power. They are only the media through which power may be exercised (Giddens, 1979; Clegg, 1989). As Clegg (1989) pointed out, virtually anything can be turned into a resource. Moreover, Mechanic (1962) assumed that a clear and definite distinction exists between formal and informal power. Whilst this may be true in theory, in practice the distinction may be problematic because power is an inherently dynamic entity. For example, Dalton (1959) argued that

Multiple relations, with continuous interaction and change, become too dynamic to be handled entirely inside such conceptual walls as ‘form–informal’ . . . . Exclusive reliance on this couplet ignores the wholeconfused middle ground where there are ‘mixtures’ and where new formal and informal actions are obscurely initiated (p. 222).

Giddens (1979) made a similar point using different language. Specifically, power in organizations is embodied in structure as depicted, for example, in job descriptions and organization charts. Whilst structure can be depicted as a static entity, according to Giddens (1979) the ‘living, breathing’ organization is far from static. To be more precise, Giddens (1979) argued that, by their actions, organizational actors are constantly creating and re-creating structure. In this view, structural reviews and adjustments may have less to do with changing the organization and more to do with formalizing changes that have already occurred (e.g. Drummond, 2001).

The distinction between formal and informal power also presupposes that organizational existence is either devoid of ambiguity or that ambiguity is a temporary state of affairs that can easily be corrected. Hatch (1999) built upon Gidden’s (1979) structuration theory by suggesting that organizations are inherently ambiguous and that ambiguity presents the ‘empty space’ whereby actors can insert their own ideas about what should be done. For example, organizations cannot foresee every contingency and, therefore, employees sometimes have to use their initiative in deciding what should be done.

When employees make decisions of their own volition in effect they create and re-create structure – with potentially important consequences for the organization.

Clearly there are limits on the freedoms that employees enjoy in exercising discretion. Moreover, when it comes to IT systems organizations tend to be designed with very clear rules and restrictions in order to prevent employees abusing power (e.g. Laudon and Laudon, 1999). Yet Hatch (1999) argued that the existence of structure does not guarantee security because it is misleading to imagine actors as working within stipulated boundaries. Hatch (1999) argued that actors are forever testing boundaries in order to see what they can get away with and boundaries are apt to stretch much further than management might envisage. Besides, rules cannot enforce control. Only people can do that (Weick, 1979). Indeed, Weick went so far as to argue that, instead of seeing people as bound by rules, it may be more accurate to imagine them as forever constructing their own rules of organization.

Testing boundaries and constructing rules is not necessarily bad for an organization because organizations require flexibility as well as control. Indeed, there is nothing like a ‘work to rule’ for causing chaos. Yet an organization’s need for flexibility can enable opportunism.

## Information technology systems control

IT professionals recognize that employees pose a bigger threat to system security than outsiders do because they generally have greater access to an organization’s hardware and software.

IT professionals seek to prevent opportunism by limiting access, usually through a system of passwords and monitoring. Yet IT professionals also recognize that too many controls can be cumbersome and may discourage employees from using the system and lead to organizational inefficiency. In addition, it is difficult if not impossible for organizations to monitor every aspect of a system’s usage.

## Did Nick Leeson have an accomplice?

For that reason risk analysis is sometimes used for striking a balance between an organization’s need for control and an organization’s need for flexibility. Generally speaking more sensitive information is subject to greater protection and monitoring than information that is perceived as routine and low level. This was precisely the situation that prevailed in Barings Bank. This paper will now consider what went wrong.

## The background to events

The collapse of Barings Bank was the subject of two official inquiries by the London and Singaporean authorities, respectively (Board of Banking Supervision, 1995; Ministry for Finance, Singapore, 1995). In addition, there followed a UK parliamentary inquiry undertaken by the Treasury Committee the proceedings of which are reported verbatim. The account that follows is based mainly upon those three sources. The researcher also had access to transcripts of the evidence given in proceedings against Barings Bank’s former directors in the High Court. The case was also the subject of several books including Gapper and Denton’s (1996) lucid and authoritative account, which is based in part upon transcripts of recorded telephone conversations between various Barings Bank staff. There is also Leeson’s (1996) own gripping account of events.

A case should be replicable in principle. The account that follows is based upon documents that are in the public domain. Moreover, it is also a case where the substantive facts are not in dispute. In order to save repeated referencing the materials for the case study are mainly drawn from the Board of Banking Supervision’s (1995) report. The author has also included quotations from Gapper and Denton (1996) in order to convey a sense of the atmosphere in which the events unfolded.

Leeson was seconded to Singapore in March 1992. His main task was to manage the back office. In investment banking the front office is where trades are conducted. The back office is where settlement takes place. Settlement involves the processing of documentation connected with trades, including the recording of contract prices. In 1992 the Singapore Monetary Exchange (SIMEX) was a relative newcomer to the financial markets. In order to attract business SIMEX’s rules and charges were more favourable than those of some other exchanges in the Far East. Another of Leeson’s duties involved executing trades on behalf of colleagues based in Japan acting for customer’s wishing to take advantage of the facilities offered by SIMEX (Board of Banking Supervision, 1995; Ministry for Finance, Singapore, 1995).

The task of executing business is relatively straightforward. Leeson was simply required to take telephone orders for buying or selling financial contracts at a given price. Yet Leeson appeared to have an almost magical ability for obtaining prices below market rates. Indeed, he became so successful that Barings Bank began charging customers a premium for his services. By around September 1992 Leeson’s colleagues in Japan had stopped specifying prices, as they felt confident about allowing Leeson to use his discretion.

In fact Leeson was deliberately under-pricing business in order to ingratiate himself with colleagues in Japan and hopefully fulfil his ambition of becoming a trader in his own right. He hid the resultant losses in a secret account, account number 88888, which became known as the ‘five eights’ account.

By September 1992 the losses had become too great for them to be kept hidden indefinitely, prompting Leeson to try and trade his way back into profit. Since Leeson had no authority for risking Barings Bank’s capital, he led Barings Bank to understand that he had discovered an opportunity for generating ‘risk-free’ profits from arbitrage. For example, if apples cost 10p in London and 11p in Dublin, the arbitrageur buys a quantity of apples in London and immediately sells them on in Dublin. Arbitrage does not involve predicting market movements as the arbitrageur merely exploits price anomalies between markets. Provided a contract to buy is matched with a contract to sell the only risk is one of market movement in the time it takes for contracts to be executed. Barings Bank therefore deemed arbitrage to be an activity that was ‘essentially without risk’ – like ‘shooting fish in a barrel’ (Gapper and Denton, 1996).

In practice, arbitrage is more difficult than it sounds, particularly in the financial markets where price differentials are both wafer thin and fleeting. However, Leeson’s situation was apparently different. According to Leeson his extraordinary profits from arbitrage reflected his unique position on the information curve, which was enabled through trading options on behalf of Barings Bank’s customers – a form of ‘insider trading’ in other words (Board of Banking Supervision, 1995).

An option gives another party the right but not the obligation to buy or sell a given quantity at some date in the future in return for payment of a premium, for example to buy 1000 apples at 10p each in 9 months time. If at the end of 9 months the price of apples has fallen to 9p, the option expires worthless. However, if the price of apples has risen to 11p, the option is exercised. Options trading is extremely risky because the party granting the option is obliged to supply the apples at the contracted price regardless of how far the market may have moved in the meantime. If the price of apples rises to 20p or more for example, the loss may far outstrip the premium received for granting the option (Gapper and Denton, 1996).

Options are mainly bought as insurance against currency fluctuations. Leeson was forbidden to expose

Barings Bank to such risk but he could buy and sell for Barings Bank’s customers. According to Leeson, whenever options were bought or sold in significant quantities there was a domino effect in the market. As Barings Bank understood it, Leeson’s knowledge of customers’ orders enabled him to anticipate the emergence of price differentials and to act before markets moved.

Leeson certainly seemed to have stumbled upon a ‘pot of gold’. For instance, the reported profits for 1994 outstripped those of the entire organization by 133%. On 17 January 1995 an earthquake struck Japan, prompting a wave of selling in the financial markets. Thereafter Leeson’s profits soared. For instance, the daily income report for one particular day, 24 January 1995, cited Leeson’s profits as £3.265 million. This was more than double any month’s reported profit during 1994 (Board of Banking Supervision, 1995).

As Barings Bank understood it, Leeson’s options trading was mainly for a so-called ‘customer X’ whose identity was known only to Leeson. By February 1995 rumours were circulating in the financial markets of the Far East about Leeson’s mystery customer. Customer X was clearly pursuing a highly aggressive trading strategy, writing thousands of options seemingly in an effort to corner the market for volatility but incurring enormous exposure to risk. What if that customer defaulted? During the next fortnight the rumours grew louder and more specific until reputable investment houses were warning their customers to be careful about using Barings Bank as a counter party (Board of Banking Supervision, 1995; Ministry for Finance, Singapore, 1995) Then on 23 February 1995 Leeson mysteriously disappeared.

## Information technology as an accomplice

This paper now backtracks and examines the history of account 88888. Leeson made a lot of mistakes in learning how to execute business. For example, in the noise and tumult of the trading pit a contract to buy might easily be wrongly executed as a contract to sell. It is normal practice in investment banking that, when a mistake occurs, the customer is made good and the bank stands the loss, which is booked to an error account.

Barings Bank’s financial reporting system (known as CONTAC) would send details of account 999905 (the original error account) to London and details of 36 other accounts every day. On 3 July 1992 Gordon Bowser, Leeson’s line manager in London, telephoned Leeson from London to say he did not want all the details of account 99905 because they were causing reconciliation problems. Specifically, the small errors had become so numerous that they were undermining the First Futures IT system. Leeson agreed to set up another account for holding minor errors:

Bowser: ‘Can you set up another Error Account and keep them all in Singapore? We don’t want to be bothered with all these tiny errors you’re clocking up. There’s over fifty a day, and so there are a hundred entries needed to reverse them. The auditors will start asking questions.’

I put the phone down and turned to Risselle.

‘Can you create another file?’ I asked her. ‘We need another Error Account.

‘Sure,’ she said, clearing her screen and tapping keys. ‘It’s all ready. What number do you want to give it?’ (Leeson, 1996, p. 53).

Leeson recounted that he asked the clerk to suggest a lucky number. The clerk told Leeson that eight is a very lucky Chinese number. Leeson replied

‘How many numbers does it have to be?’ ‘Five.’

‘ . . . Let’s give it all the luck it can handle. Let’s call it 88888’ (Leeson, 1996, p. 53).

Leeson then instructed a software engineer to amend the reporting software to exclude the five eights account from the daily feed to London.

Approximately three weeks later Bowser telephoned Leeson and reversed the instruction as the IT system had been upgraded to handle small errors. Leeson duly complied and small trading errors were once again reported to London. In consequence the five eights account was now a dormant account. However, it was still lodged in the computer.

Leeson began to use this account for concealing the losses made on apparently profitable trades. When these trades were fed into the SIMEX computer system Leeson originally designated them as house trades. In other words, the designation of the account suggested that the transactions (and accompanying market risk) were booked to Barings Bank.

On 17 July Leeson filled in a form redesignating the account as client trades. This was significant because it suggested that any exposure to risk was a client rather than Barings Bank. By late August 1992 account 88888 contained so many trades that it became a reportable account under SIMEX rules. Accordingly, on 26 August 1992 Leeson faxed a BC4 form to SIMEX, which identified the account as ‘Baring Securities London – Error Account’ and described it as a related house account. That same day, Leeson faxed an amended BC4 form to supersede the previous submission. It was identical to the previous form except that the reference to the account being an ‘error account’ was removed.

Thereafter the volumes of the transactions booked to the five eights account rose exponentially. For example, by 20 January 1995 there were almost 7000 contracts hidden in the account. However, by 26 January that number had risen to over 16 000. By 24 February 1995 there were over 61 000 contracts concealed in the account. SIMEX did not become suspicious at the volume and value of the transactions listed in the account because it appeared to be a bona fide customer account.

The stage was now set for Leeson to sell options without authority and to use the premium received to cover his losses. Options trading is not only risky, but it requires a mathematician to calculate the premium necessary for offsetting the risk. Lacking the necessary mathematical sophistication, Leeson sold too cheaply and so became trapped in an escalatory spiral, writing more and more options in increasingly desperate efforts to recoup his losses. Since the premium received was booked as pure profit, the more money Leeson lost the more profitable his trading apparently became whilst in reality Barings Bank was exposed to huge risk.

## An opportunity lost

Yet Barings Bank financial controllers in London knew about the five eights account, albeit indirectly. Although the listings of contracts were not transmitted to London, a file was posted to London that made reference to account 88888, but was either not noticed or not appreciated (Board of Banking Supervision, 1995).

The system worked as follows. Information was received from Singapore to be entered on to the so-called First Futures system in London. First Futures first checked whether it recognized the account number by making reference to a master file, which contained a list of all known account numbers. Since the number 88888 did not appear on the master list, the data was rejected by the First Futures system. In consequence, the information was not downloaded into the First Futures system. There was therefore nothing in the ledger referring to the five eights account.

The information concerning account 88888 was left in a suspense file. Moreover, the system did not detail which accounts had been skipped during the downloading process. The head of IT at Barings Bank said that there were two ‘margin processing’ screens available to users of the First Futures system. The first screen listed the entire margin balances loaded from the CONTAC system in Singapore. The second screen listed the accounts recognized by First Futures as being valid London accounts, for which the margin balances were loaded into the First Futures database. If the two screens had been compared it would have been clear which accounts were being rejected by First Futures as irrelevant (Board of Banking Supervision, 1995).

It is a matter of record that this comparison was never made. Moreover, there were no system-based reconciliations or control totals for ensuring that records received in

London were examined. Unreconciled accounts would be held in suspense to await allocation. The settlement staffs in London were responsible for clearing out suspense files. Barings Bank head of settlements said that she never saw account 88888 and only discovered the account in the margin file after Barings Bank had collapsed along with a suite of other accounts that did not belong to Barings Bank. She had no idea how long it had been there. Had all the accounts from Singapore been printed off questions might have been asked. However, the head of settlements did not regard it as her or her department’s responsibility to undertake the task (Board of Banking Supervision, 1995).

Shortly after Leeson disappeared his colleagues discovered the printout of the five eights account lying on his desk. It contained thousands of contracts. Moreover, every one of these was losing money:

The loss was escalating by the second. The futures in the five eight’s account were so volatile that each point the index changed made £100 000 difference to the amount that Barings owed . . . As the Nikkei fell again, the loss spiralled upwards at an almost dizzying speed (Gapper and Denton, 1996, p. 27).

Leeson’s colleagues quickly realized that it was indeed a customer account, only the customer was none other than Barings Bank.

## Analysis

IT is defined as the systematic gathering and communication of information. The reporting of errors formed part of the system of communication that enabled Barings Bank’s controllers in London to mount surveillance over its employees in Singapore. Electronic transmission facilitated control by reducing the gap in time and space. The system of registering accounts was part of SIMEX’s system of monitoring trading in order to ensure that firms like Barings Bank complied with the rules.

Apart from a minor ‘glitch’ when the number of errors suddenly increased, the system worked perfectly in that it enabled Barings Bank’s controllers to monitor erroneous trades. Monitoring was important because error accounts can be abused. For example, Barings Bank was risk averse as a matter of policy, preferring to earn commission for trading on behalf of customers instead of engaging in the much more lucrative but also riskier business of putting its own capital at risk, which is known as house trading. In practice, however, traders might hang on to errors for a few days hoping for the market to move in their favour – a form of house trading (Gapper and Denton, 1996). The system for reporting errors enabled Barings Bank to ensure that such practices did not get out of hand.

Authority exists where one party has an acknowledged right to command and another party perceives an obligation to obey. Authority differs from power in that power is the ability to achieve something regardless of whether one has authority (e.g. Wrong, 1979). Leeson clearly had authority to set up a new error account and, initially, to withhold details of the account from London. By exercising his authority Leeson effectively re-formed the organizational structure in a manner that subsequently enabled him to hide his unauthorized trading from London.

Leeson had no authority whatsoever to use the five eights account as he did. However, Leeson’s position within the organization enabled him to abuse the system.

In Leeson’s hands the seemingly mundane trappings of bureaucratic surveillance became a powerful resource as it enabled Leeson to record thousands of unauthorized trades. Leeson also used his knowledge of procedure and his access to forms for redesignating the account on SIMEX’s records. In short, by manipulating organizational controls Leeson effectively evaded them. As the weeks and months passed without Leeson being challenged it became clear that the boundaries could be stretched a long way.

## Lack of segregation

Barings Bank broke an important precondition of computer security in that Leeson’s roles were completely unsegregated, that is he controlled both trading and the subsequent processing of trades – known as settlement. Leeson therefore had the opportunity for operating the five eights account and changing the designation of accounts without anyone in management being aware of what he was doing.

Significantly no-one in Barings Bank decided that Leeson’s roles should be unsegregated. What happened is an example of how organizations exist in a constant state of flux and transformation (e.g. Benson, 1977; Giddens, 1979; Drummond, 2001). Specifically, Leeson’s official role was in settlement. He became a de facto trader when colleagues stopped specifying prices. Indeed, Leeson had been trading for approximately 6 months before London learned of it. Even then confusion persisted for months as to Leeson’s precise role in the organization (Gapper and Denton, 1996). Ultimately, Leeson continued in both roles, not because he was given a mandate to do so, but, subtly different, because no-one stopped him.

## The form to SIMEX

The system of using forms for redesignating accounts was a programmed decision. Organizations use programmed, that is automatic, decisions in order to avoid the need for constant referral. This explains why SIMEX accepted the form redesignating the five eights account without question and why the change failed to attract attention.

## The suspense file

Meanwhile, computer-generated information that could have led to Leeson’s exposure in time to save the bank lay in a suspense file. It is unclear why the suspense file was not audited. A plausible possibility as to why this line of defence failed is that the suspense file was not regarded as important because it functioned as a dustbin for all the ‘rubbish’ generated by the computer, including accounts that apparently had nothing to do with Barings Bank. What may have occurred is an example of the ‘paradox of consequences’, whereby organizations become deflected by concentrating upon the things that make sense. It is like the drunk who searches for the car keys not where they dropped them, but under the lamppost because the light is good (Watzlawick et al., 1974).

## Discussion

The most striking feature of the present study is the contrast between the mundane acts whereby the stage was set and the catastrophic consequences that followed. Before considering the implications for practice it is important to stress that there were other factors in Barings Bank’s downfall besides IT, that is Leeson was protected by a series of managerial and communications failures whereby no effective investigation was made of other emerging signs of malfeasance, despite Barings Bank being well aware that Leeson’s roles were completely unsegregated.

That said, IT played a significant role in Barings Bank’s downfall and there are lessons to be learned from the debacle. Recall an important line of defence in ensuring that IT security rests heavily upon controlling access. In turn controlling access relies upon being able to draw and maintain a clear distinction between authorized and unauthorized personnel. The present study shows how hairline cracks can develop in controls as authorized personnel may use their authority for illicit purposes.

The present study also highlights the subtly shifting tides of an organization whereby one state of affairs gives way to another as Leeson’s activities shaded from execution into trading. The point is that profound change may occur almost gradually, almost imperceptibly, thereby undermining basic security arrangements in a manner that is difficult to predict, far less control.

The present study also shows mundanity is an illusion. Organizations may regard strategy, people, markets and finance as being of pivotal importance. Yet so too are the routine day-to-day aspects of an organization, including the mechanics of IT systems. More specifically, the present study highlights the importance of IT systems and their related routines in defining organizational reality.

Although reality can be perceived as something ‘out there’ waiting to be discovered, it can also be regarded as something that organizations largely create for themselves by the manner in which they scan the environment. Scanning refers to the information that organizations regard as important and the information that gets filtered out of the decision-making process (Weick, 1979; see also Starbuck, 1983). In this view what organizations call reality is really a myth, that is a theory of the world, a partial mapping of reality usually partly true and therefore partly false. A myth provides the interpretation of reality upon which an organization should act (Hedberg and Jonsonn, 1977; see also Drummond, 2001). In other words, a myth represents the received view of reality, the dominant idea about what should be done. In Barings Bank’s case the ruling myth was that Leeson had discovered a bubble of risk-free profits that he was exploiting to the full. The myth was sustained by the computer printouts showing reported profits, whereas information that would have destroyed the myth, namely the options contracts stored in the five eights account, was suppressed and therefore not part of what Barings Bank called ‘reality’.

Empirical evidence suggests that, once individuals and organizations decide upon an explanation for something, when contradictory information emerges we tend to rationalize the information to fit our preconceived views (Watzlawick, 1976; Weick, 1995). The rumours that were circulating in the financial markets contradicted the computer printouts of Leeson’s reported profits. Instead of investigating the rumours Barings Bank made the mistake of rationalizing them to fit their expectations, that is they assumed that, since SIMEX published trading positions whereas the Japanese exchanges did not, the market was seeing only half of the equation. In other words Barings Bank assumed that the market was seeing contracts to ‘buy’ unaware that they were matched by equal and opposite contracts to ‘sell’. In fact, Leeson’s position were not matched but completely open: hence the rumours.

Strategies can be seen as bridging the gap between myth and reality by ordering feedback information into categories provided by the myths and filtering out ‘signal that are inconsistent with the world views that the myths represent’ (Hedberg and Jonsonn, 1977, p. 96). The filtering function is double-edged because it prevents organizations from overreacting to minor fluctuations, but they can mean that organizations are slow to react where reorientation is required as the gap between myth and reality widens. In Barings Bank’s case the short-term strategy was to reap as much profit as possible before competitors awoke to the opportunities and, in the medium term, to try replicate Leeson’s methods in other emerging markets.

A myth becomes discredited when it can no longer account for reality. The problem for organizations is that, by the time a myth is discredited, it may be too late to change direction. The alarm was triggered by Leeson’s sudden disappearance because here was something that could not be rationalized to fit expectations. By then, however, Barings Bank was doomed.

## Implications for practice

The present study suggests that IT professionals have a duty to remind managers of the limits of what IT can achieve in monitoring and controlling organizations. Although IT professionals can design security measures for preventing abuse, malfeasance is like water. It erodes control structures by seeping into hairline cracks. To mix metaphors formal controls over access are probably best seen as imaginary Maginot lines.

If malfeasance is impossible to prevent the issue becomes how long it takes to detect it. One place to look for signs of malfeasance is in the routine ‘taken for granted’ aspects of IT systems and to apply imagination. What might the system enable a potential miscreant to do? Another possibility lies in the boxes of computer printouts marked ‘garbage’ gathering dust in office corners. The word garbage refers to nonsensical information and is usually regarded as a sign of a program error. Yet, as the present study shows, that which makes ‘no sense’ may be the outcroppings of malfeasance.

If it was not the head of settlement’s responsibility to print off files then whose responsibility was it? As far as Barings Bank’s chief executive Peter Norris was concerned, the lines of responsibility were clear (Board of Banking Supervision, 1995). The issue for management is that ambiguity always lurks. The organization as it exists on paper and in the minds of senior management may be very different from the one that operates in reality. Although organizations can allocate responsibility, responsibility only exists where it is accepted. Otherwise there is no responsibility.

## Beyond information technology systems

The present study also warns against the dangers of focusing entirely upon machine-processed data. The notion of ‘management by walking around’ has something to commend it in that it enables managers (and also IT professionals) to learn what machine-processed information cannot always convey, such as the existence of nonsensical accounts and weaknesses in auditing practices.

To see something one way is not to see it another. It is not what information reveals that matters, but what it conceals (Drummond, 2001). Rumour is the antithesis of IT because it is unsystematic. However, unsystematic does not necessarily mean insignificant. Barings Bank’s information systems were registering a ‘false all clear whilst rumour was pointing to the truth. Rumour has value because it affords a more intimate perspective than more abstract machine-processed information (Watzlawick, 1996). It is like the difference between representing the human body as an anatomical sketch or as a painting of a nude. Both representations are accurate but both depict reality in different ways (Brown, 1989). For practical purposes rumour is by definition information that cannot be verified. It is the existence of rumour that is significant (Watzlawic, 1976) and should always warrant investigation no matter how improbable it seems.

In Singapore the number eight is regarded as lucky because it means ‘to prosper’. However, the number five is considered unlucky because it signifies ‘never’. The five eights account can be interpreted as meaning ‘never prosper’. Fay (1996) suggested that the superstitious overtones of the designation 88888 should have prompted enquiry. The suggestion may seem fanciful yet there is ample evidence to suggest that there may be other ways of stimulating knowledge and insight besides following formal rules of logic (e.g. De Bono, 1978; Chown, 1996; Gagliardi, 1996). Certain people in Barings Bank, including Leeson’s line manager, knew about the existence of the account even if they did not know about the use to which it was put (Board of Banking Supervision, 1995). If they or SIMEX had been a little more curious about the unusual designation history might have been different.

Ultimately what matters is not the information itself, but how much decision makers rely upon it. The present study shows what can happen when managers interpret a particular data set as literal reality. The most important recommendation for practice is that all data should be regarded with suspicion, that is as ‘liars in service of truth’ (Brown, 1977). Whilst the purpose of information is to lead decision makers to reality, the more refined and abstract information becomes, the more likely it is to lead decision makers away from reality. This applies with particular force to over-reliance upon machineprocessed data precisely because such information excludes more intuitive ways of knowing (for a discussion that is particularly relevant to IT see Weick (1985)). For example, in investment banking high profits almost invariably mean higher risk. Intuitively the notion of bountiful ‘risk-free’ profits was doubtful, no matter what the computer printout said.

To be more precise, information becomes dangerous if we allow it to lull us into a false sense of security. Doubt is the best form of protection against malfeasance. To paraphrase the Tao the art of risk management is ‘to know that you do not know’ (Wing, 1988). Above all, what is needed is a willingness to doubt that which we are most sure of.
