# Key roles in intranet implementation: the conquest and the aftermath

RENS SCHEEPERS

Department of Information Systems, The University of Melbourne, Victoria 3010, Australia

Intranet technology has been rapidly adopted by many organizations. From being a largely unknown technology prior to the mid-1990s, numerous organizations today have implemented or are currently implementing intranets. The intranet implementation processes of three large organizations in two countries were studied over a period of 5 years. In particular, the paper investigates the role players who spearheaded the intranet implementations in these organizations. Five interrelated roles during the intranet implementation process are identified: the technology champion, organizational sponsor, intranet coordinator, intranet developer and content provider. In the light of the nature of intranet technology, each respective role is analysed in terms of its significance, key challenges and interrelationships during the implementation process. The paper further examines the dynamics of these roles in the aftermath of implementation as the technology becomes embedded in routine organizational activity.

## Introduction

The mid-1990s marked the global adoption of the Internet and, in particular, the World Wide Web (WWW) in many spheres of society. In organizational contexts in particular, WWW technologies have opened up a wealth of application possibilities. These include applications of the technology (e.g. in electronic commerce) that support external relationships, but also intra-organizational applications such as intranets.

Intranets have been defined as the application of Internet technology and, specifically, WWW technology for disseminating information within an organization and for facilitating cross-departmental collaboration (Goles and Hirschheim, 1997; McNaughton et al., 1999; Damsgaard and Scheepers, 2000; Lai, 2001). Access to an intranet is usually restricted exclusively to organizational members, typically by means of mechanisms such as firewalls, proxies, passwords and role-based access control (Oppliger, 1997; Tari and Chan, 1997).

Intranet technology has been rapidly adopted by many organizations (Eder and Igbaria, 2001). From being a largely unknown technology prior to the mid-1990s many organizations today have implemented or are in the process of implementing intranets. The potential that the technology holds for supporting a variety of organizational knowledge processes has recently sparked a renewed interest in the effective implementation and use of intranets (Scott, 1998; Lamb and Davidson, 2000; Alavi and Leidner, 2001; Damsgaard and Scheepers, 2001).

Traditionally an organization’s central information technology (IT) group has been the home of intraorganizational IT. Much of the literature concerning the introduction of organization-wide IT details the roles of actors within the central IT group in implementing technologies for addressing the needs of various user communities in the organization (e.g. Ginzberg, 1981; Kling and Iacono, 1984; Kwon and Zmud, 1987; Swanson, 1987; Lucas et al., 1990; Walsham, 1993; Cooper, 1994; Sprague, 1995). The ubiquitous nature of Internet-based technologies seems to suggest a shift in this paradigm. For example, some studies have reported that the technology was in fact initiated by actors outside the IT group (e.g. Jarvenpaa and Ives, 1996; Bhattacherjee, 1998). Other research has argued that Internet technologies will blur traditional information systems roles and demand new roles as technologies and media coalesce (Lyytinen et al., 1998).

These studies suggest a different ‘cast of characters’ in the implementation of intranet technology. Many of the available empirical intranet studies only peripherally allude to the role players or describe only certain prominent roles (e.g. those pioneering the technology in an organization). Many intranet studies also tend be ‘one-off’ and do not address the possible evolving nature of the roles over time.

This paper investigates the roles during intranet implementation in more depth by asking three questions. Who are the main players and what roles do they play? What is the significance of the various roles and how do the roles interrelate? Lastly, how do the roles evolve over time?

The paper addresses these questions by drawing upon perspectives from the organizational innovation and intranet implementation literature and by analysing evidence from an in-depth field study. The field study was conducted over a period of 5 years and examined the intranet implementation processes of three large organizations in two countries.

The paper is structured as follows. Various innovation roles are identified by drawing upon the theory of the organizational innovation process. The innovation roles are compared with roles that have been described in the IT innovation and intranet implementation literature. In the light of the theory and the literature, different intranet roles are identified and analysed in the field study. The significance of the roles is discussed and compared across the three cases. The status of the intranets and the roles 5 years after the initial field study is then described. The paper concludes with implications of the study and further research issues.

## Roles in the organizational innovation process

The organizational adoption and use of a new technology can be regarded as an organizational innovation process (Zaltman et al., 1973; Tornatzky and Klein, 1982; Iivari, 1993; Larsen, 1993; Swanson, 1994; Rogers, 1995).

Rogers (1995), for example, described the organizational innovation process as consisting of two broad activities: initiation and implementation. Initiation includes the information gathering, conceptualizing and planning for the adoption of an innovation, leading up to the actual adoption decision. Implementation includes all the events, actions and decisions involved in putting the innovation into use (Rogers, 1995). Implementation concludes when the innovation becomes embedded in routine organizational activity (Cooper and Zmud, 1990; Rogers, 1995). A new innovation is often initiated and adopted by a small group of actors. However, the implementation of the innovation concerns the communication of the innovation to a much larger group of organizational members who should also adopt and ultimately use the innovation (Wynekoop and Senn, 1992).

A number of roles are recognized in the context of the organizational innovation process. Initiation of an innovation is usually attributed to the efforts of innovators and often an individual innovator (Schön, 1963). In some cases the innovator may actually work outside the formal research teams of organizations and, as a consequence, without organizational support. Schön (1963) noted that, due to the considerable resources needed for organization-wide implementation of an innovation, someone with the required power and prestige in the organization needs to emerge to take control of the innovation, otherwise the process stalls.

An organizational sponsor (Kerzner, 1984; Rogers, 1995) plays this role. Although the sponsor role is also labelled a ‘champion’ by some authors (e.g. Maidique, 1980) the term organizational sponsor is used here in order to be consistent with the literature describing this role in IT innovations. Once an organizational sponsor has emerged for the innovation, the innovation process proceeds to an implementation stage. Change agents play an important role when the implementation of an innovation demands changes in the behaviour of organizational members (Lawless and Price, 1992). Change agents communicate the innovation to organizational members and seek to influence their behaviour with respect to the innovation (Rogers, 1995).

In the context of IT innovations, the roles of ‘technology champion’ (innovator), organizational sponsor and change agents are widely recognized. A technology champion is an organizational member who initiates the use of a new IT in the organization (Beath, 1991). Technology champions are sometimes informal experts whose main responsibilities are ‘doing work’ and not experimenting with new technology (Attewell, 1992). In many cases, technology champions lack the necessary authority and/or formal resources and, thus, seek to draw the attention of senior management towards the technology (Beath, 1991). An organizational sponsor is usually an individual senior manager who realizes the potential of the IT in the organization. The sponsor either allocates organizational resources towards further implementation of the technology and/or negotiates such resources with senior colleagues (Humphrey, 1989; Martinsons, 1993). Once the sponsor has taken ‘ownership’ of the technology, the role of the technology champion tends to disappear into the background (Orlikowski et al., 1995). The role of the organizational sponsor in subsequent implementation stages is further described as that of a ‘godfather’ who empowers and supports agents of change in the organization (Beatty and Gordon, 1991).

Implementation of an innovation is an organizational change process and concerns not only the technical but also the political and cultural dimensions of the change (Keen, 1981; Markus, 1983; Walsham, 1993). Change agents come into play in order to effect the implementation of a new IT (Rockart, 1988; Beatty and Gordon, 1991; Orlikowski et al., 1995; Markus and Benjamin, 1996) and intervene as mediators of the technology in the organization (Okamura et al., 1995). These agents play a key role in spreading the new IT to organizational actors. Change agents should be both technically and politically astute and capable of addressing the problems associated with the implementation. Markus and Benjamin (1996) described three models of the change agent role: the traditional model, the facilitator and the advocate model. Simply stated, the traditional model assumes technology causes the change and that the

## Roles in the intranet implementation literature

Much attention has been paid to intranets in the business and computer press and numerous popular articles and books have resulted (e.g. Bernard, 1996; Cortese, 1996; Hills, 1997; Koehler, 1998; Phillips, 1998). However, these sources tend to focus mostly on the promise of the technology and anecdotal ‘success stories’.

A number of published research studies have high lighted some general implementation challenges associated with intranet technology (e.g. Chellapa et $a l . ,$ 1997; Goles and Hirschheim, 1997; Rein et al., 1997; Balasubramanian and Bashian, 1998; Bidgoli, 1999; Bansler et al., 2000; Damsgaard and Scheepers, 2000; Duane and Finnegan, 2000; Lamb and Davidson, 2000; Eder and Igbaria, 2001). Although none of these sources specifically focus on the role players during intranet implementation, the challenges highlighted in these studies (such as creating organizational awareness, control and management support for the new technology) suggest that certain organizational roles need to exist. For example, Damsgaard and Scheepers (2000) argued that there are three main implementation challenges that need to be met during intranet implementation. First, if a sponsor does not nurture the technology it cannot evolve beyond its emergent beginnings. Second, if a critical mass of both users and content cannot be reached simultaneously, the intranet will not progress. Third, if the intranet remains uncontrolled (i.e. ‘grows wild’), it will be perceived to be useless and therefore users will abandon it. In terms of critical mass, intranets require a critical mass of both users and information content in order to become pervasive in the organization (Damsgaard and Scheepers, 2000). Intranet content emanates from a broad range of intranet technology ‘use modes’ (Damsgaard and Scheepers, 1999). These include simple uses such as the publishing of home pages, newsletters, technical documents, product catalogues, employee directories, etc., to more advanced uses such as organization-wide searching for information, transacting with functionality on intranet pages and other organizational computer-based information systems (e.g. databases, data warehouses and ‘legacy systems’), interacting between individuals and groups in the organization (e.g. via discussion groups and collaborative applications) and recording of the ‘organizational memory’ (e.g. best practices and business processes). Various levels of intranets can co-exist in an organization. For example, an intranet may be implemented centrally as a corporate intranet, but decentralized units (divisions, departments or functional groups) and even individuals can simultaneously implement their own ‘child intranets’ (Lamb and Davidson, 2000). These use modes and the ubiquitous nature of intranets suggest that technical and coordinating roles should exist.

A few studies refer more specifically to role players during the initiation or later during the implementation of intranet technology. However, most of the studies are one-off investigations and do not venture into much depth regarding the nature of intranet roles or how the roles are related.

For example, in a case study of two large US organizations it was found that the technology was initiated by decentralized technology champions from a variety of organizational functions outside the IT group (Jarvenpaa and Ives, 1996). The IT groups of the organizations eventually became involved with the technology, but in a reactive manner.

The implementation of an intranet in an Australian university setting was described in another study (Romm and Wong, 1998). In this study, an intranet committee was formed with a project coordinator advocating the technology and attempting to convince university departments to add their information to the intranet.

A further case study reports on the lessons learned during the ‘Global Village’ project, which was the implementation of the intranet at US West Communications (Bhattacherjee, 1998). In this case, the vice-president of finance at US West Communications acted as the organizational sponsor and hired a project manager who was a ‘technological visionary’ for implementing the intranet.

Lastly, an ethnographic study of electronic mail (e-mail) and intranet-mediated organizational change in an Australian university also described the role of an organizational sponsor during implementation (Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 1999). Although the emphasis in this intranet case study was more on changed organizational communication patterns as a result of intranet implementation, the study did allude to the presence of central coordinators and divisional content providers during the process (who seemed to operate as change agents).

Given the innovation process theory and the intranet literature, this paper now examines the role players associated with intranet implementation in depth by means of a field study.

## Field study

Case study research is particularly appropriate in new topic areas (Eisenhardt, 1989) and is a research strategy that allows for an in-depth description of the relationships in a particular situation (Galliers, 1991). The case research strategy was chosen here due to the novelty of the intranet phenomenon at the commencement of this study and the level of detail that was anticipated with complex role relationships in large organizations. The field study reported here was based on an explorative multi-site longitudinal case study design (Yin, 1989) and focused on the intranet implementation processes of three large organizations in two countries. The case organizations reflect a mixture of public, semi-government and private ownership. The research was conducted in the head office environments of two large organizations in South Africa and one organization in Denmark. The intranets of the Council for Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR) (www.csir.co.za), a large semi-government research and development organization and that of Telkom (www.telkom.co.za), a government-owned telecommunications provider, were studied in South Africa. The intranet at the headquarters of the LEGO Group (www.lego.com), a privately owned global toy manufacturer, was studied in Denmark.

The following considerations were taken into account when selecting the research sites. The nature of the phenomenon under study (Benbasat et al., 1987), i.e. intranet implementation within the organizational context, formed the main criterion. At the commencement of the study all of the organizations had been implementing intranet technology for at least 1 year. As such the nature of the implementation processes as well as early outcomes could be studied. The specific organizations were further chosen to allow for ‘literal comparisons’ between the cases (Benbasat et al., 1987). In terms of literal comparison the chosen sites were all large, established, hierarchical organizations. In addition, the two South African organizations were chosen since they exhibited two of the most advanced intranets in the country at the time.

## Data collection, analysis and interpretation

The data for this study were collected over a period of five years between 1997 and 2002. Formal and intensive rounds of data collection were interspersed with periods of informal data collection. An initial pilot study followed by a formal baseline study was conducted between December 1997 and January 1998. Formal interviews (mostly with the same interviewees) were held in October– November 1998 and in January–February 2001. Data were also collected on a more informal basis over the duration of the study by means of regular e-mail and telephone contact, lunch meetings, discussions and by attending presentations at the organizations.

Mostly qualitative (and some quantitative) data were collected from a variety of actors who were involved in the implementation of the technology in these organizations. After gaining initial access to the organization the intranet role players were identified on a peer-referral basis (as done in similar technology studies) (e.g. Howell and Higgins, 1990) and interviewed. The aim was to identify intranet ‘roles’ rather than the specific individuals who were involved with the intranet. Given the duration of this research, this approach largely overcame the problems of variation in position descriptions, new appointments, resignations, etc. Almost 50 formal and informal interviews with various intranet actors in the case organizations were conducted over the period of the study. The interviewees included executives of the organizations (who were involved with the intranet), middle managers in various business units (e.g. the IT, public relations, marketing and production departments), intranet project leaders, programmers and developers of the intranet infrastructure, persons contributing intranet content and intranet users (at corporate and business unit levels).

The formal interviews typically lasted approximately 1.5 h each. The interviews were semi-structured and comprised of open-ended questions derived from the implementation literature and similar research studies. An initial questionnaire (for use by the interviewers) was used in a small pilot study and was subsequently refined in order to improve its understandability and comprehension. The refined questionnaire was used for the initial formal baseline study and served as a basis for reference in the follow-up interviews. The interviews were transcribed and shared with the interviewees in order to correct possible errors and omissions and to evaluate the validity of the interpretation of their ‘story’ (Benbasat et al., 1987; Klein and Myers, 1999). Based on all the data and transcripts, rich descriptions of the various roles were obtained at each case organization.

In terms of theory development, the research approach followed in this study was mainly deductive (Neuman, 2000, p. 49). The roles derived from the innovation process theory and intranet implementation literature served as the departure point and guided the development of the interview questionnaire. The evidence gained from the field study was also examined with these roles in mind. As the study progressed and more evidence became available, the detailed manifestation of the roles in the intranet context was studied. This was accomplished as outlined below.

The evidence was categorized according to the roles derived from the literature and also organized chronologically by event sequences and episodes (Sabherwal and Robey, 1993; Robey and Newman, 1996). This enabled finer levels of analysis and the identification of specific themes (Miles and Huberman, 1984, pp. 130–1) in the data (for example, a role’s main challenges, how the roles interrelate and evolution over time). These analyses also enabled the writing of the case descriptions that follow. Subsequent to such within-case analyses the occurrence of some themes was analysed across cases (Eisenhardt, 1989).

Drawing on the hermeneutic circle, initial interpretations were revised in a number of circular passes of working through the evidence until the ‘pieces of the puzzle’ fitted together (Klein and Myers, 1999) and the final interpre tation left no remaining ‘anomalies’ given the case data (Sarker and Lee, 1999). Interpretation and abstraction proceeded through a process where the evidence would be examined and related to the innovation process theory and the intranet literature. In this manner there was a dynamic process of working between the theory and the empirical evidence (Coffey and Atkinson, 1996), culmi nating in the detailed intranet role descriptions suggested in the paper. The cases are described in the following sections.

## Three cases of intranet initiation and implementation

## The CSIR IntraWEB

The CSIR in South Africa is Africa’s largest scientific and technological research, development and implementation organization. It is a semi-governmental statutory body and carries out industrial contract research in the public and private sectors in specialized technological areas. The CSIR consists of nine divisions that report to a president at the head office. In September 1996 the organization started to implement an organization-wide intranet called the CSIR IntraWEB.

However, prior to the implementation of the ‘formal’ intranet, computer scientists in a few of the divisions started independent intranet experiments within their environments. Their enthusiasm spread informally to more of the divisions and also to the organization’s central computing services group. Technical staff in the computing services group started constructing an experimental organizational intranet home page. The home page effort gained the support of managers in the computing services group and a project leader was appointed within the computing services group to take the idea further. The intranet project leader headed up a small team of programmers.

These efforts came to the attention of the CSIR’s vicepresident of policy and technology. The vice-president realized the organization-wide potential of the technology and played a key role in approving funding towards the initiative. The vice-president appointed a quality and systems manager, with one of her tasks being to coordinate the CSIR intranet in conjunction with the intranet project leader at computing services. The quality and systems manager described the expectations of her role as follows.

The vice-president sees me as a custodian . . . not to do the work, but to coordinate and see that it is done. In other words, to manage the resources.

Development of the intranet progressed further in the organization and it became known as the CSIR IntraWEB. The quality and systems manager formed a steering group consisting of divisional intranet representatives and the intranet project leader. Together with the steering group she initiated a number of organizationwide initiatives in order to further awareness of the intranet. This included a major organizational campaign with intranet posters, coffee cup ‘coasters’ with the intranet information, intranet talks and an intranet ‘treasure hunt’ with prizes to be won. The vice-president remained firmly supportive throughout these efforts and also in convincing his executive colleagues of the organizational advantages of the technology.

The quality and systems manager initiated a project via which divisional and corporate business plans, policies, management models and quality plans were put on the intranet. Given the semi-autonomous nature of the divisions, she mentioned this required considerable ‘lobbying’ of some divisional managers.

The intranet project leader initiated a number of developments for integrating existing computer-based systems in the CSIR with the intranet. These included ‘Web-enabling’ many of the existing systems in order to allow access via the intranet and also new applications such as an intranet-based employee directory that integrated data from the human resources system, e-mail system, telephone system and security access system. In many cases this necessitated the project leader intervening in order to convince staff in other corporate units to tailor their developments and processes to allow for integration with the intranet. The project leader also initiated the implementation of an intranet search engine in order to allow organization-wide searches.

After the initial intranet efforts many divisions and corporate units subsequently designated specific staff members to take responsibility for the content of their local intranet sites. This created the need for intranet design courses, which were set up by the quality and systems manager. Following these courses, a staff member located in the communications unit created a moderately sophisticated intranet site that automated and improved the previous process by which staff members ordered corporate gifts for their clients. In addition, the editor of the staff magazine started to publish the magazine via the intranet while she was trying to persuade her superior to suspend paper publication altogether.

The intranet project leader held responsibility mainly for the corporate part of the intranet and for the integration of even more organizational systems with the intranet. He described his role further.

We are moving towards a development model where my team and I will be responsible for intranet project management. Some development can take place in the divisions, but we will ensure a coordinated approach.

The quality and systems manager retired in 1999, but the vice-president transferred her role to a senior manager in the newly created office of the Chief Information Officer (CIO). The CIO established a CSIR ‘Web Council’ with senior intranet representatives from all divisions for deciding on issues such as content standards, use policies and future directions of the CSIR’s intranet and Internet applications.

## The Telkom intranet

Telkom in South Africa is a government-owned business that provides approximately 40% of all telephone services on the African continent. The organization consists of a head office, service groups and regional offices in the main centres of southern Africa. Several individuals in service groups (mainly IT-related environments) and the regional offices within Telkom were ‘playing’ with pilot intranet sites. These started spreading through informal networks and a large number of ‘island’ sites resulted. A regional technician who created one such pilot site commented that

I am not officially supposed to be involved with the intranet. I just started using it to make my job easier. They can say to me tomorrow to leave it and focus on what I am supposed to do . . . . People are joking with me, but I put my name on all the intranet pages I create.

These independent intranet efforts prompted Telkom’s information executive to establish a small team of four staff members within the IT services group and task them with the responsibility of integrating the island intranets into a cohesive whole. This marked the formal start of Telkom’s corporate intranet in February 1997.

The intranet team consisted of an information specialist, a graphics designer, a Web developer and a network security expert. The team implemented a basic corporate

The task of maintaining the content of intranet sites was seen as an ‘add-on’ to staff members’ existing jobs. In this respect, the information specialist started to quality assure the content of intranet sites. In a few cases she removed links from the central content book to sites that contained outdated information or did not meet the content standards.

The international LEGO Group is one of the world’s largest toy manufacturers. The LEGO Group is owned and managed by the Kirk Kristiansen family in Denmark. The organization provides creative experiences, construction toys, educational materials, lifestyle products, family parks and media products for children all over the world.

Following the development of the LEGO Group’s Internet site, the programmer who was involved in the project, in conjunction with his superior, initiated the idea of an intranet to senior management. A director of the LEGO Group approved a one-man project (the same programmer) and an intranet demonstration prototype was built. The prototype was demonstrated to top management who approved further development. The demonstration happened to coincide with the earlier launch of a new management strategy in the LEGO Group that aimed, among other things, to improve interdepartmental communication. The potential of intranet technology for supporting the management strategy was quickly realized.

Implementation of the envisioned intranet (called LEGO Web) started towards the end of 1996 and early 1997. With strong support from the LEGO Group director, a small team of four programmers was established within the IT group. The LEGO Web project leader headed this team. The director further appointed a LEGO Web editor in the information and public relations department. The intranet project leader and the LEGO Web editor cooperated closely. Throughout the implementation, the director ensured adequate funding was available for intranet developments.

The intranet project team developed considerable intranet functionality. This included organizational-wide search facilities, a number of organization-wide intranet applications (e.g. a global employee directory integrating e-mail, telephone numbers and photographs of employees) and facilities for interaction (intranet discussion groups). Although direct transacting with existing systems was not yet developed functionality was built in order to extract information from various systems (e.g. manufacturing data and product information). This allowed for highquality graphical intranet content such as photographs of products and packaging that are important in coordinating global marketing efforts.

Once the basic intranet infrastructure was in place a decision was made to designate approximately 80 employees worldwide formally as dedicated LEGO Web ‘content providers’. The corporate IT manager reflected on this decision.

We are talking about the hen and the egg problem. We had to get critical mass here, otherwise people would say ‘this is nice, but there is nothing on it’.

The LEGO Web editor controlled the addition of new content to the intranet. She formulated content standards for intranet pages and assured the quality of all intranet content received from content providers prior to its addition to the intranet. The LEGO Web editor actively supported the content providers with training courses and instruction in the use of content standards.

The LEGO Web editor promoted the intranet via a number of articles in the staff newsletter. There was also a formal launch of the intranet attended by the chief executive officer of the LEGO Group. The LEGO Web editor engaged in interdepartmental information exchanges, such as to convince department A that they should provide content on the intranet for department B to use. This proved difficult in some cases and she complained of a lack of middle management participation and a tendency that some departments were advertising themselves rather than providing useful information.

The development of the organization-wide employee directory required telephone data to be available more regularly and in an electronic format. This required process changes in the department responsible for the previously printed version of the telephone directory. The intranet project leader intervened to convince this department to convert their paper process to an electronic process so this data could be used on the intranet. Reflecting on her responsibilities, the intranet project leader mentioned she struggled to deliver on existing requirements while ‘keeping pace’ with the rapid new developments in intranet technology.

As echoed in the following statement, the designated content providers were pleased with their new responsibilities and saw their new task as an upgrade of their work.

As secretary, you usually just type what other people think. Now all of a sudden I have an identity of my own.

Though the intranet was well supported by management, some content providers needed to change old ways of doing things in their local units.

We were working with paper before. Here we have a good opportunity to make it cheaper for the company. The user can always be sure they are working with the right version and they can have direct access to it. In my environment, I am the one that is pushing the intranet.

The roles are those of organizational sponsor and three intranet change agent roles: intranet coordinator, intranet developer and intranet content providers. Prior to the ‘official’ start of the intranet implementation process, technology champions often initiate the technology in the organization. Summaries of the identified roles appear in Tables 1–3.

## Technology champions

Intranet technology champions can be identified in all three cases. In the LEGO Web case the intranet technology champion was the programmer who championed the intranet concept based on his earlier involvement in the development of the organization’s Internet site. In the CSIR case the various divisional computer scientists were the technology champions. In the Telkom case the technology champions were the regional technicians and IT staff in various head office units. Technology champions would interact and learn from each other’s independent experiments via informal peer interaction (the CSIR and Telkom cases). Technology champions introduced the intranet concept in these organizations. This was achieved by appropriating (or ‘reinventing’) the technology (Attewell, 1992) to the particular organizational context. The technology champion(s) would then attempt to ‘sell’ the intranet concept to senior management, typically by means of prototype applications that demonstrated the technology’s potential (the LEGO Web and Telkom cases).

In all cases the efforts of the technology champions were unsolicited. In no case was senior management found to have initiated the intranet. However, it appears that the champions had the freedom to experiment with the technology. The motives of intranet technology champions seemed to vary (curiosity and self-interest).

In the LEGO Web case the technology champion was within the IT group but, as the other two cases demonstrated, technology champions also operated independently of the IT group (as reported in other studies) (e.g. Jarvenpaa and Ives, 1996; Bhattacherjee, 1998). The technology champion role disappeared into the background once an organizational sponsor and intranet agents came on to the scene (as also found by Orlikowski et al., 1995).

## Organizational sponsor

In all three cases an organizational sponsor emerged to take ownership of the intranet technology. In the CSIR case the sponsor was the vice-president of policy and technology, at Telkom the sponsor was the information executive and in the LEGO Web case the group director played this role.

In all of the cases the organizational sponsor effectively took over the intranet concept from the technology champion(s). Even though decentralized child intranets co-existed a single key individual in the organization invariably played the intranet sponsor role. In all cases the sponsor was a senior manager who realized the technology’s organizational potential (based on the ‘selling’ efforts of the technology champion(s)). As the Telkom case demonstrated not all senior managers were sold on the intranet concept initially. This means that the role of the sponsor can involve political risk taking.

In all cases the sponsor played an active role in allocating or negotiating funding and resources towards intranet implementation. The sponsor engaged change agents (such as intranet coordinators and developers) for leading organizational implementation of the intranet. It is interesting to note that, in all three cases, the sponsor personally appointed the intranet coordinator. In subsequent phases the sponsor played the godfather role (as coined by Beatty and Gordon, 1991), particularly in terms of supporting the intranet agents (the CSIR and LEGO Web cases). The sponsor was instrumental in approving use policies and content standards (the LEGO Web and Telkom cases).

## Intranet coordinator

The role of intranet coordinator featured prominently in all three cases. The intranet coordinator role was highly visible during implementation and strongly dependent on the political support of the sponsor (cf. Beatty and Gordon, 1991). In the CSIR case the manager of quality and systems played this role initially and the manager in the CIO office later continued the role, in the Telkom case the information specialist in the IT group played the role and in the LEGO Web case the LEGO Web editor located in the information and public relations department played the role. In all cases the intranet coordinator was located in a central position in the organization.

The intranet coordinator role is that of a powerful change agent with strong facilitating and advocating facets (as described by Markus and Benjamin, 1996). The scope of the coordinator’s role is an organizationwide one, transcending functional boundaries. In all the cases the coordinator role was found to be much more prominent than suggested in earlier intranet studies.

The key challenge of the intranet coordinator is to influence the behaviour of various organizational actors in order to change with respect to the new technology (e.g. the lobbying of middle managers in the LEGO Web case). This challenge is significant and political in nature when the new technology requires significant changes to organizational processes and work practices (Hammer and Champy, 1994, Chapter 5; Rein et al., 1997). In the cases the coordinator can be seen as both a mediator (Okamura et al., 1995) and broker (Wenger, 1998) who intervenes between departments, convincing them to alter information processes and exchange information via the intranet rather than via traditional means.

The coordinator role is instrumental in positioning the intranet as a new medium for carrying organizational information and in achieving a critical mass of both users and content simultaneously (cf. Damsgaard and Scheepers, 1999). In terms of advocating intranet use, the coordinators in the cases employed initiatives such as awareness campaigns, presentations, articles in staff newspapers and intranet treasure hunts. In conjunction with the intranet developers, the coordinator facilitates the development of new intranet-based organizational applications. The coordinator supports the intranet content providers, assists the sponsor in formulating use policies and information standards and quality assures the intranet content.

## Intranet developer

The role of an intranet developer featured in the CSIR and LEGO Web cases, but this role was initially non-existent in the Telkom case. In the CSIR case the intranet project leader played this role and in the LEGO Group case the role was played by the LEGO Web project leader.

The role of the intranet developer encompasses the main responsibility for the technical intranet infrastructure developments. The intranet developer is also a change agent who intervenes where the technical intranet infrastructure requires new systems or changes to existing systems (the CSIR and LEGO Web cases). In the CSIR and LEGO Web cases this role was typically played by a senior project manager within the organization’s central IT group, but with political backing by the organizational sponsor and support from the intranet coordinator.

Although in some cases the organization’s corporate IT group ‘reacted’ to the intranet initiation by outside technology champions, the intranet developer role later became crucial in spearheading more sophisticated use modes of the technology (such as implementing organizational search engines and integrating existing organizational systems with the intranet).

As witnessed in the LEGO Web case, the intranet developer intervened to change systems in order to allow the creation of organization-wide intranet applications (such as the organizational employee directory). In the CSIR and LEGO Web cases the intranet developer worked in close collaboration with the intranet coordinator and was also strongly dependent on the sponsor for both political support and funding for developments.

The initial absence of the intranet developer role in the Telkom case explains the fairly limited use of the technology (only publication and interaction). However, the need for a developer role was realized at the time.

The leadership role of the intranet developer in technical developments was often mentioned in the CSIR and LEGO Web cases. However, compared to the highly prominent role of the intranet coordinator, the intranet developer was the ‘second fiddle’ in the ensemble.

The LEGO Web case demonstrates the impact of rapid new developments in the technology on the intranet developer role, where the interviewee described her struggle between delivering on requirements and keeping pace with new technological developments.

## Content providers

The role of a content provider appeared in all cases (as also mentioned in some intranet research) (e.g. Cecez-Kecmanovic et al., 1999). The content provider plays a role in creating and maintaining new intranet content, but also in converting existing information to the intranet format. As was evident in the LEGO Web and CSIR cases, the content providers would operate as local change agents within their organizational units, advocating use of the intranet in local processes and trying to influence their colleagues’ behaviour.

As is evident from the Telkom and LEGO Web cases the key challenges that the content provider face are the design of appropriate content and dealing with their colleagues’ resistance to change. In this regard, content providers are dependent on the intranet coordinator for direct support, but also dependent on indirect support from the organizational sponsor.

In the CSIR case the content provider who created the corporate gifts application is an example of where the technology blurs roles (as speculated by Lyytinen et al., 1998). Here a regular office worker with minimal training created the intranet content and functionality one may traditionally expect from a systems developer.

In the LEGO Web case the content provider role was formalized upfront, while formalization of the role occurred later in the CSIR case. In the Telkom case these roles remained informal (explaining why some employees saw this activity as an add-on). In the LEGO Web case the comment from the content provider about gaining her own identity gave a glimpse of the potential of the technology in empowering such workers (cf. Clement, 1994; Duane and Finnegan, 2000). These observations suggest some merit in formalizing the content provider role.

## The situation after five years

Five years after the initial investigation as described above commenced the status of the intranets and the role players was again assessed at the three case organizations. The ongoing informal interchanges with the three case organizations over the duration of the study were useful in ‘keeping touch’ with developments. In February 2001 in-depth follow-up interviews were conducted in the CSIR and Telkom cases, while in the LEGO Group case the data collection remained informal (via e-mail exchanges).

## The CSIR and Telkom cases

In the CSIR and Telkom cases the intranets could be characterized as being widely adopted throughout these organizations. This observation was supported by statements such as ‘it is embedded’ (CSIR intranet coordinator, February 2001) and ‘the intranet is sold – we do not need champions driving it anymore’ (former Telkom intranet coordinator, February 2001), by intranet usage statistics at the various sites and by the disappearance of many ‘parallel’ channels of information distribution (paper-based or e-mail-based channels) in favour of intranet-based processes.

The intranets were increasingly relied upon for a wide range of organizational communications, processes and systems. The high levels of intranet advocacy by the various change agents had subsided and, in comparison, the intranets in general became a background issue. In terms of functionality, the ongoing intranet developments became more incremental rather than radical. This trend was quite noticeable in the structure of the various intranet home pages that began to assume a more stable form, with only the content being updated on a regular basis. In terms of the role players involved with the intranet at each organization, a number of developments had occurred as the intranets became more embedded.

At the CSIR the intranet project leader had resigned from the organization. The intranet sponsor remained active in his support for the intranet, but played a less visible role than during the implementation itself. The content provider roles were formally entrenched across the various units of the organization and specific duties (such as content development and site maintenance) were specified in formal job descriptions. The manager in the CIO office still coordinated regular meetings with divisional representatives. However, the nature of the meetings was now quite informal and focused on issues such as ongoing improvement of the intranet at large, evolution in content standards and exchange of best practices.

At Telkom the intranet coordinator was promoted to another position within the organization. Reflecting on her prior role as intranet coordinator she commented that

Once the intranet took off, I kind of got bored with it and wanted to move on to something else. I was not interested to see it all implemented.

In order to oversee and develop the technical infrastructure a dedicated intranet developer and project team was appointed within the central IT group (filling the void of this role that was initially absent). The team designed a central intranet ‘server farm’ where organizational units that chose to do so could host their intranet sites and receive technical support (such as page templates, automatic indexing, back ups, usage tracking, etc.). Central search functionality had been established with cascading searches into unit-level intranets.

## The LEGO Web case

The status of the intranet role players at the LEGO Group had changed after the initial investigation. During 1999 and 2000 the global toy market had experienced some turbulent times. Global sales were down and, in the wake of the slump in sales, an increased emphasis was placed on cost cutting (LEGO company, 2000). The group director, who was the organizational sponsor

## Discussion

This analysis has isolated five key interrelated roles in the initiation and implementation of intranet technology: the technology champion, organizational sponsor, intranet coordinator, intranet developer and content provider. These roles vary in their significance as the implementation process unfolds. Figure 4 is an aggregate representation of how the roles evolve during intranet implementation and afterwards as the technology becomes embedded in routine organizational activity.

The role of technology champion is significant during intranet initiation. Technology champions play the important role of initiating the intranet concept in the organization. As in some other studies (e.g. Jarvenpaa and Ives, 1996) it was also found that senior management did not always commission the intranet. It was often the unsolicited efforts of technology champions that later prompted the formal implementation (or even ‘reimplementation’) of the technology in the organization. As the implementation progresses the technology champion role disappears into the background.

The role of organizational sponsor was significant in all of the cases. This role was found to be crucial in nurturing and protecting the budding technology and its agents throughout the process. The findings illustrated that a premature withdrawal of the sponsor and the change agents from the process can have dire consequences for the overall intranet implementation. Agents that depend on the sponsor’s political support can be left vulnerable when the sponsor withdraws (Beatty and Gordon, 1991).

The coordinator role takes on a special significance, given the ubiquitous nature of intranet technology. Compared to ‘mediators’ (cf. Okamura et al., 1995) that are associated with some forms of IT, the intranet coordinator role takes on an even more visible and political flavour in terms of intranet advocacy and intervention in cross-unit information processes. Organization-wide implementation progress appears to hinge on this central and powerful change agent that can coordinate dispersed intranet efforts and foster intranet usage and content generation in general.

The role of intranet developer is significant in ensuring more advanced organizational application of the technology. The absence of this role in one case was seen as restricting the organization initially to only elementary use modes (cf. Damsgaard and Scheepers, 1999). Some limelight may initially be stolen from the organization’s central IT function as a result of the ‘bottom-up’ intranet efforts throughout the organization. In addition, prominent roles such as the coordinator, sponsor, content providers and even technology champions tend to reside outside the central IT function. However, this study highlights the significance of the intranet developer role (usually residing in the organization’s IT function) in enabling advanced and efficient organizational use of the technology in subsequent stages of the implementation.

The role played by decentralized content providers is significant in creating a critical mass of content for sustaining the intranet implementation process (cf. Damsgaard and Scheepers, 2000). It is interesting to compare the effect of when (and if) the organizations decided to formalize this role. It seems that more accelerated intranet implementation hinges on early formalization of this role. The role of content provider appears essential throughout implementation and in routine use of the technology.

The study suggested that organizations need to monitor the status of their intranet implementation and the change agents spearheading the process. As the implementation process advances and the intranet becomes embedded in routine organizational activities, the need for ongoing intranet advocacy diminishes. As routine use sets in, the excitement associated with the implementation activity during the ‘conquest’ starts to subside. In the ‘aftermath’ the players who were most prominent during the implementation (particularly the sponsor, coordinator and developer) begin to reconsider their roles and seek further career paths. Organizations may want to explore mechanisms for trying to retain their competent change agents for future processes.

Given the extant literature on IT implementation roles in general and the intranet literature in particular, this study made three main contributions. Firstly, although roles such as champions, sponsors and change agents have previously been identified in relation to the implementation of other types of IT, the findings described these roles in the case of intranet implementation in more detail. In particular, it was shown that the change agent role manifests as three distinct roles: intranet coordinator, intranet developer and content provider. Secondly, the study indicated the interdependencies between the various roles and showed that an intranet implementation progress can be hampered when key role players withdraw prematurely. Thirdly, the longitudinal perspective adopted in this study highlighted the changes in significance of the various roles over time.

The findings also raised the question of the implications for IT implementation roles in general. The study dealt only with roles associated with intranet implementation and, as such, the underlying characteristics of the technology limited the applicability of the findings to other IT contexts. The description of the specific roles and why some roles manifest outside the IT function can be partly attributed to the nature of Internet-based computing environments. In this respect, the roles identified here may have some relevance for similar ubiquitous technology environments (e.g. extranets) where issues such as coordination, content creation, sponsorship and infrastructure development are also on the agenda.

## Conclusion

The findings suggest five key interrelated roles in the implementation of intranet technology: the technology champion, organizational sponsor, intranet coordinator, intranet developer and content provider. Technology champions initiate the intranet concept in the organization. The organizational sponsor bears the overall responsibility for the intranet and the associated political risks. The intranet coordinator harmonizes dispersed intranet efforts and fosters intranet usage and content generation in general. The intranet developer role enables advanced and efficient organizational use of the technology. Lastly, the content provider role is vital in establishing a critical mass of intranet content for sustaining the implementation process. The significance of the identified roles changes over time as the implementation process unfolds and as the technology becomes embedded in routine organizational activity.

The research has the following limitations. First, the study intentionally concentrated on intranet role players. This introduces some limitations in extending the findings to IT environments that exhibit completely different underlying characteristics. Second, the empirical base in the study was large, hierarchical organizations, mostly within corporate settings. The roles identified and explored here may manifest differently in other settings. For example, in small or medium-sized enterprises different roles may need to be assumed by the same individual. In organizations that are comprised of autonomous or loosely coupled units some of the central roles identified here may perhaps manifest at unit levels. Third, this study focused only on the significance and relationship between the different roles. The organizational structuring and location of these roles were not specifically addressed.

## Key roles in intranet implementation

Lastly, in the three cases the identified role players were all members of their respective organizations and all the intranet initiatives can be regarded as in-house develop ments. As the intranet consulting sector matures and more ‘intranet-in-a-box’ packages become available commercially, it could be anticipated that the role of intranet consultants and vendors will become increasingly prominent (Karlsbjerg and Damsgaard, 2001). Further research is needed in order to determine the relationship of such external parties with the roles identified here. Research is also needed for examining how the roles identified here manifest in different cultural settings.

## Biographical note

Rens Scheepers is a senior lecturer at the Department of Information Systems, The University of Melbourne. His research focuses on the implementation and use of information technologies such as intranets, extranets, enterprise systems and mobile information technologies in organizations. He is also involved in research that examines the role of information technology in fostering knowledge exchange within communities of practice. He has presented his work at international conferences and in international journals.
