# Evaluating electronic local government in the UK

PAUL BEYNON-DAVIES and MICHAEL D. WILLIAMS

European Business Management School, University of Wales Swansea, Singleton Park, Swansea SA2 8PP, UK

An array of UK government initiatives have been launched since 1997, collectively being known as the local government modernization agenda. This can be seen as an attempt to transform the structures and performance of local authorities in the UK. A parallel set of initiatives, the electronic government agenda, were launched in 2000 and have attempted to extend the use of information and communication technologies (ICTs) within local government. This paper describes the context for and themes within both agendas, the aim being to describe the existing and likely dynamics of the relationship between innovations in ICTs within local government and the modernization of local government. The paper presents an analysis of the electronic government strategies of 22 Welsh unitary authorities and uses this analysis for highlighting a number of issues relating to the progress of the electronic local government agenda within the UK. The paper concludes with a call for longitudinal investigation in this area.

## Introduction

This paper presents the results from a research project that aimed to develop a critical understanding of the electronic local government agenda in the UK. A set of initiatives were launched in 2000, which have attempted to extend the use of information and communication technologies (ICTs) within local government and engender substantial change in the processes and service delivery of local government. This paper terms these as the electronic local government agenda.

There is a substantial body of literature detailing the linkage between the introduction of ICTs and business performance (Beynon-Davies, 2002), but substantially less such material on public sector organizations, particularly government organizations. This paper would claim that the literature on electronic government is still developing and would benefit from more focused studies. Since electronic local government forms a key challenge within the movement towards electronic government, developing a key understanding of this challenge is important for both local government and information systems academics.

In conducting this study the research method was to use the source material published on the electronic local government agenda in the UK over the last 4 years. The research also closely studied the Implementing Electronic Government statements of the 22 unitary Welsh authorities. This study presents some of the analysis of such strategy material later in the paper in order to identify key themes. The focus on Wales is interesting for a number of reasons. First, because all local authorities in

Wales are unitary authorities, they perform all the functions of local government performed by county and district councils in other parts of the UK. Second, under the devolved administration of the National Assembly for Wales Welsh local authorities are taking a distinctive approach to the electronic local government agenda, both informed by but different from the English, Scottish and Northern Ireland experiences. However, recent evidence suggests a possible convergence of aspects of the Welsh model with those of other regions in the UK.

The structure of the paper is as follows. First, something of the local government context in the UK is portrayed, along with some of the restructuring and modernization being undertaken in this domain. Second, the paper describes the context of the electronic government agenda generally within the UK and the electronic local government agenda in particular. Third, the process of implementing electronic local government is discussed. Fourth, a model of electronic local government that this study developed in order to help evaluate the current state of this agenda within Wales is examined. Fifth, the study’s analysis of the electronic local government strategies of 22 Welsh unitary authorities is presented and areas of concern and opportunities for progress are identified. Finally, some of the problems and issues that are likely to emerge over the next few years are discussed, along with definition of the importance of further research.

## The local government context

In Wales, the Local Government (Wales) Act 1994 abolished all the county and district councils from 1

April 1996 and replaced them with a single-tier system of unitary councils. The old structure of eight county councils and 37 Welsh district councils was replaced by a unitary structure of 22 councils. Unitary authorities are responsible for a wide range of services, including education, environmental health, planning, housing, personal social services, waste management and disposal, highways, libraries, recreation, cemeteries and crematoria. The authorities also assume some overseeing function in relation to the fire and police services. National parks are also constituted as local authorities in Wales, but do not form part of this particular evaluation.

Local authorities have both a democratically elected set of council members and a permanent administration of officers (New-Media, 1998). The councils exercise decision-making powers only. Local authorities in Wales are encouraged to operate a Cabinet style of governance structure under a programme for increasing the accountability of decision making. In association with this, members of Council Cabinets are beginning to manage cross-cutting portfolios. This replaces the older-style system of members and officers with direct departmental responsibility and reporting directly to central government.

In terms of officer structure, a head of permanent administration (frequently termed the chief executive) is responsible for day-to-day implementation of policy decisions made by a council. In line with changes in the democratic arrangements, a flatter organization structure is now being introduced into many authorities with cross-cutting responsibilities for officers emulating the cross-cutting nature of Cabinet local government.

An array of initiatives, collectively known as the local government modernization agenda, was launched by the Government in 1997. This can be viewed as an attempt to transform the structures and performance of local authorities across the UK. The expressed aims are to improve local service delivery, enhance community governance and increase public confidence in local government.

The Welsh Assembly Government has approached this modernization agenda in a different way to that adopted in England. In Wales the development of policies associated with the wider modernization agenda has been undertaken in direct consultation with the wide range of partner organizations with an interest in the delivery of high-quality public services.

Three main streams of legislation underpin this modernization agenda (Martin, 2002).

(1) The Local Government Act (1999), which introduced the ‘Best Value’ regime in England and Wales, thereby shifting the emphasis from one of compulsory competitive tendering to a more flexible requirement on local authorities in order to achieve continuous improvement. In response to acknowledged difficulties associated with implementing the best value regime, the Partnership Council endorsed a new approach to implementing best value legislation: the Wales Programme for Improvement. The Wales Programme for Improvement is in effect a contract between the Welsh Assembly Government and Welsh local government, promoting partnership and collaboration by providing local authorities with the opportunity for assessing their capacity for delivering improved services, assessing risk with a view to focusing support on those areas most in need of help and asking themselves whether they could provide better services by exploring different service delivery options (Welsh Assembly Government, 2002).

(2) A second Local Government Act in 2000, which granted local authorities new powers for promoting the economic, social and environmental well-being of their local communities. It also required them to develop community strategies and new operating structures, although a key aspect as identified by the Partnership Council (2001) was to ‘take existing local structures and make them work better’ (p. 5).

(3) A policy statement published in 2001 discussed, inter alia, partial reform of the local government finance regime, a reduction in the number of statutory plans that authorities must prepare for central government and the requirement for developing a more differentiated framework of regulation and a more robust structure of performance management for the activities of local councils.

The main aim of the modernization agenda is improvement of the quality of life of local people. In order to achieve this local authorities must provide the following.

(1) High-quality local public services. Under the service improvement objective, the Welsh Assembly Government has introduced a number of initiatives since 1997, including the best value regime (the Wales Programme for Improvement), policy agreements and the preparation of Implementing Electronic Government strategies.

(2) Effective vision and leadership to the community. This is particularly evident in the requirement for local authorities developing community strategies with the express aim of improving the quality of life of its citizens.

(3) Engagement with local people and inspiring public confidence. There are key pressures on local authorities for establishing a clearer user or customer focus and consulting regularly with local people about the design and delivery of local services.

Martin (2002) argued that, nationally, the modernization agenda is the result of the deployment of a number of levers of change in this sector.

(1) Markets. Authorities are under pressure to improve performance through market testing, contracting out services, comparing performance with other providers and engaging in partnerships and projects with the private sector.

(2) Managers. There has been key emphasis on the importance of strategic management for local government and the introduction of rational planning processes.

(3) Monitors. There is an important role for external review and inspection within local government. For instance, authorities are expected to establish key service delivery targets against which their performance will be assessed in order to determine whether continuous improvement is being achieved. The Wales Programme for Improvement acknowledges the need for robust external regulation, but where the emphasis is very much placed on support for improvement.

## Implementing electronic local government

There are clear connections between the expressed aims of the modernization agenda and that of the electronic government agenda. For instance, within a proposed national strategy for electronic government in England, the term electronic government has been recently defined as ‘exploiting the power of information and communications technology to help transform the accessibility, quality and cost-effectiveness of public services, and to help revitalise the relationship between customers and citizens and public bodies who work on their behalf’ (Local Government Association, 2002, p. 4).

In 1997 the prime minister of the UK announced that, by 2002, 25% of dealings with government should be able to be carried out by the public electronically. These targets were later revised in the Modernising Government White Paper. The UK Government has now set a target that, by 2005, all (100%) government services that can be delivered electronically will be delivered electronically. The Audit Commission’s electronic government project is monitoring the progress local government is making towards this target. The 2005 target only applies in England. No similar targets currently exist for Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland.

The UK Government’s statement on electronic government establishes four guiding principles.

(1) Building services around citizens’ choices.

(2) Making government and its services more accessible.

(3) Social inclusion.

(4) Using information better.

The first phase of an Audit Commission electronic government project involved a consultation exercise in May 2001 with key stakeholder groups in local government. As a result of this exercise local councils in England were invited to submit an Implementing Electronic Government statement to the Department for Transport, Local Government and the Regions by 31 July 2001. Implementing Electronic Government statements were designed for setting out the actions councils intended to take to meet the 2005 deadline. In effect such statements are meant to provide a strategy for the electronic government agenda within each authority.

The findings of the consultation were presented at a workshop in December 2001 and in a report in March 2002. The report includes details of a commissioned survey. One main finding is that, while councils agree that electronic government demands fundamental change, many projects do not question existing ways of delivering services. It is perhaps not surprising to find that 30% of electronic government projects are focused on World Wide Web (WWW) sites. However, most such sites only provide information about services and do not currently allow the customer to either interact with the authority or engage in transactions such as paying council tax on-line.

In the spending review of 2000, the UK Central Government allocated £350 million to enable councils to meet its 2005 deadline. Based on the Implementing Electronic Government statements, £25 million was allocated in 2001–2002 for supporting so-called pathfinder projects: key piloting of innovative technologies such as customer relationship management systems in selected authorities. The balance was to be allocated over 2 years to 398 councils throughout the UK that had their Implementing Electronic Government statements rated as satisfactory.

In Wales, the Welsh Assembly Government and Welsh local government share a common vision that electronic government can join up government services and bring about a transformation in service delivery (Welsh Assembly Government, 2002). In July 2001 the Welsh Assembly Government agreed with local authority partners to invite each local authority to submit, on a pilot basis, a draft Implementing Electronic Government statement by the deadline of 31 March 2002. They also provided guidance on what these statements might include. The final version of each statement (including targets) was required by 31 March 2003. In the interim period the statements were to be evaluated and best practice fed back to the local authorities in Wales.

There are a number of subtle differences in Wales with the approach taken in England. Most such differences stem from the fact that, in Wales, non-hypothecation of funding to local authorities is the norm. Hence, funding for electronic government is not ring-fenced by the Welsh Assembly Government, although the Welsh Assembly Government has issued supplementary credit approvals to authorities for electronic government purposes (£9.7 million over 2 years). Consequently, the Welsh electronic government agenda is founded with the expectation of local negotiation of targets rather than nationally stipulated arrangements and the evaluation of electronic government strategies is not linked to issues of funding.

The evaluation of electronic government is not linked solely to the evaluation of Implementing Electronic Government statements. Many local authorities in the UK are also producing data against a number of performance measurement systems for electronic government. Probably the widest used is the Society for Information Technology Management’s set of key performance indicators for electronic service delivery, issued in Wales as NAW P1.5. These define ten major types of interactions between the customer and local government, including making payments and providing information. Authorities are asked to evaluate on a regular basis the percentage of interactions with the public that are being delivered through electronic service delivery against a vast array of types of service including council tax, environmental health and highways maintenance.

## A model of electronic local government

Figure 1 illustrates the context for electronic local government. The key message being promoted is that ICT is an enabler for organizational change focused around the redesign of service delivery to key stakeholders: customers, suppliers and employees. Hence, ICT is seen to offer the potential for more effective and efficient delivery of value along supply, customer and internal value chains (Porter, 1985).

It is estimated that a typical unitary authority will have 700 different services. Some of these services will be primarily information based. Major examples here are maintaining a land and electoral register and collecting revenues such as the council tax. For a number of such information-based services, national standards and systems are being developed and promoted amongst authorities within the UK. These include the following.

(1) The Local Authorities Secure Electoral Register, which aims to provide electoral registers that are joined up, maintained and managed locally and are accessible on a national level to authorized users. The Local Authorities Secure Electoral Register aims to draw together all the locally held registers of electors and make them available nationally in order to support electronic voting.

(2) The National Land Information Service, an on-line, one-stop shop that delivers land and property-related information from source data providers to conveyancers and home buyers. At present, the National Land Information Service provides access to information held and maintained by local authorities, the Land Registry and the Coal Authority. Negotiations are now under way towards including searches relating to the water service companies and the Environment Agency.

(3) The National Land and Property Gazetteer, which is a single, comprehensive, up-to-date list of addresses. Local authorities are essential participants because they start the process by naming streets and numbering buildings. The National Land and Property Gazetteer requires local authorities to convert their existing lists of addresses into a fully consistent national information system that is held electronically, constructed to common standards and based on unique property reference numbers for each property or piece of land. The National Land and Property Gazetteer is reliant on authorities producing and maintaining Local Land and Property Gazetteers to a common standard.

Other services, such as waste disposal, although not information based will nevertheless rely on effective and efficient transmission of information to stakeholders. For example, effective waste disposal is reliant on the provision of accurate collection times to authority customers.

Services are end-points of business processes (Hammer, 1996) or human activity systems (Checkland, 1987) undertaken by organizations. Information is needed for supporting most human activity systems particularly in terms of transactional information. Transactions are ways of recording service delivery and are critical to the measurement of organizational performance. In line with market testing, local authorities in the UK are required to benchmark their service delivery. Key performance indicators have been developed both by the UK Central Government and the Welsh Assembly Government for evaluation of electronic service delivery.

Information is supplied by information systems, that is organized systems of communication within organizations. Information systems are distinct from ICT as not all information systems rely on ICT. Part of the mission of the electronic government agenda is to increase the number of information systems supported by ICT within the public sector.

According to the National Audit Office (2002a), English national strategy in this area requires electronic government services to be (1) joined-up, (2) accessible in times and places most convenient to the customer, (3) delivered or supported electronically, (4) delivered jointly, (5) delivered seamlessly and (6) open and accountable.

In order for this to occur the informatics infrastructure of public sector organizations must be addressed in terms of both technical and social aspects. In terms of social infrastructure, information, information systems and information technology (IT) have to be planned, managed and developed in organizations, ideally in association with the planning, management and development of work systems/processes within such organizations.

Figure 2 illustrates some of the elements to which many electronic government efforts are now being directed. The components in italics indicate examples of the key elements. The objective of expanding electronic service delivery will involve the following.

(1) Investigating and implementing various access mechanisms and channels for different stakeholders. In terms of service delivery to the customer of local government, face-to-face contact with authority staff and telephone conversation are two of the most commonly used mechanisms for accessing authority services. Consultations recently conducted by authorities suggested that relatively low levels of uptake of electronic service delivery are to be expected in the short term. However, with an eye on the longer term, most authorities are either implementing or investigating multiple-channel access centres that allow customers to interact with the authority using devices such as the Internet-enabled personal computer and even interactive digital television. The aim is to provide access to authority services 24 hours a day, 365 days a year. There is some evidence of increasing interest in access to government services through ICT (KPMG, 2002) that lends support to this strategy.

Re-engineering or constructing front-end systems for managing electronic service delivery. Major investment is currently being undertaken by authorities in order to increase levels of interactivity on their WWW sites. The aim for many authorities is to provide fully transactional WWW sites designed around so-called life episodes such as registering births and deaths and adding names to an electoral register.

(3) Re-engineering or constructing back-end systems for delivering the data needed by front-end systems. In order to enable fully transactional WWW sites, the information presented needs to be updated dynamically from back-end databases.

Hence, for example, when a customer enters personal details such as their name and address into one system for council tax purposes, this information should ideally be available to all other systems that need such data.

## Implementing Electronic Government statements and their analysis

This section presents details of the evaluation conducted on Implementing Electronic Government statements within Wales. The aim is to provide an account of the current state of the electronic government agenda in a significant region of the UK and draw from this some lessons in terms of the dynamic relationship with the modernization agenda on the ground.

Following guidance from the Welsh Assembly Government, most Implementing Electronic Government statements followed a three-part structure including a discussion of the authority’s vision for electronic government (including cost–benefit analyses and performance measurement), key milestones in its envisaged plan for implementing electronic government (including targets and time scales) and discussion of key infrastructure issues associated with the transition to electronic government (such as skills and resources). The statements varied in size between 15 and 28 pages, usually with a number of supporting appendices.

The Implementing Electronic Government statements from all 22 unitary authorities in Wales were evaluated using a grid of criteria generated from the model of local electronic government organization portrayed in the summary above. The intention was to focus around enablers of key process change and aspects of infrastructure.

The model distinguishes between a number of issues of infrastructure and issues surrounding the four major value chains of the electronic government organization. The key themes located on this model were as follows.

(1) Informatics planning. This theme considered the critical activities of informatics planning, including information audit and standardization, process mapping and design, authority strategy and modernization, informatics strategy, risk assessment and cost–benefit analysis.

(2) Informatics management. This theme was concerned with the management issues considered important for implementing the electronic government agenda. These included the form of electronic championing, the form of electronic government organization and the nature of any benchmarking exercise conducted or planned.

(3) Informatics development. This theme considered the existence of a clear plan for development work, consideration of resource issues and whether an audit of appropriate skills had been conducted.

(4) Customer chain. Key enablers for supporting the customer chain were considered under this theme, including customer relationship management, the state of the citizen WWW site, whether electronic democracy had been considered and what consideration had been given to the provision of the full range of possible access mechanisms and channels.

(5) Internal value chain. This theme addressed the state of the current information systems, IT and communications architecture for the authority. In terms of information systems architecture the concern was with the integration and interoperability of information systems and integration with external standards and systems. In terms of the IT architecture this paper wishes to highlight critical technology enablers such as knowledge management, document management, content management and intranets.

(6) Supply chain. This theme considered enablers for the supply chain including the existence of any extranets, evidence of tele-working and plans for or actual implementation of electronic procurement.

(7) Electronic community. This theme considered the degree to which various stakeholders had been consulted on electronic service delivery, the form of partnership arrangements implemented or planned and the existence of any form of electronic community strategy.

Each authority statement was positioned against these seven themes. This enabled the evaluators to identify the current state of electronic government within each authority and the potential for moving forward. It also enabled an effective comparative analysis and aggregation of issues and best practice across authorities.

Two forms of feedback were provided to authorities. Firstly, a two-way feedback session was conducted with nominated representatives at each authority. This involved an informal telephone discussion of their Implementing Electronic Government statement as well as issues, problems and suggestions arising from the electronic government agenda. Typically this telephone feedback took between 1 and 2 h to complete. A significant amount of further data was collected from this feedback session that was not present in the Implementing Electronic Government statements themselves. Secondly, two briefing sessions were held (one in north Wales and one in south Wales) for representatives of local authorities. Here, themes from the collective experience of electronic government across Wales were presented to authorities. The sessions also served to provide an open forum for authorities to reflect on the results of the evaluation. Notes of the discussion were made soon after the completion of each session.

## Analysis of Implementing Electronic Government statements

An aggregate grid was formed from the 22 authority grids that were produced. This aggregate grid served to summarize the regional distribution of Welsh authorities against the evaluation themes. Using the themes identified in the model described above this paper can present the following analysis of the current position of Welsh authorities.

## Informatics planning

Most authorities had made some connection between their electronic government strategy and aspects of their modernization agenda. However, this was rarely elaborated in detail within the statements. There was surprisingly little evidence within the statements of the re-engineering of any current processes. Apart from the general claim that electronic government change is organizational change, there was little evidence of process mapping and redesign besides the general consideration of process changes required at the customer interface. Most authorities claimed to have an ICT strategy in place, but many acknowledged that it needed updating in the light of electronic government and needed to be more closely aligned with their electronic government strategy. Most authorities had attempted an initial cost–benefit analysis, but the majority of authorities had still to conduct a thorough assessment. In terms of financial analyses that had been conducted, most had come to the conclusion that cost savings were unlikely in the short term and that cost neutrality was the medium-term goal for their authority. They also suggested that most of the benefits of electronic government were likely to be intangible (for example providing greater access to services and providing more joined-up service provision). Most authorities had conducted some form of risk assessment and placed the issues of inadequate resources, needed culture change within authorities and low-uptake of services as priority issues to be addressed.

## Informatics management

A significant proportion of the authorities had appointed the council leader and the chief executive as authority electronic champions. However, there appeared to be a variable level of support for the electronic government agenda among elected members of authorities. The general assessment seemed to be that, while a proportion of council members were interested and enthusiastic about electronic government, a substantial proportion of most council’s elected members had yet to be convinced of the case for electronic government. Some authorities had created specific structures for implementing the electronic agenda and had appointed electronic government officers specifically for overseeing this strategy. Many authorities were re-using existing structures for implementing electronic government. Most authorities were only in the early stages of benchmarking their electronic service delivery and those that had completed this exercise placed their existing level of electronic service delivery in the lower quartile.

## Informatics development

Most authorities had created development plans that had clearly evolved from their existing informatics infrastructure. Some authorities had aspired to consider more radical and aspirational solutions for the longer term future based on some early piloting of key technologies. Adequate resourcing for electronic government was seen as a crucial issue by most authorities. Estimates from authorities regarding the investment required for the Welsh region for implementing the electronic local government agenda fully ranged from £20 million to £200 million. Many authorities were actively looking towards various forms of external funding (such as objective 1) in order to finance critical components of their electronic government agenda. One important aspect that was poorly addressed in most Implementing Electronic Government statements was the degree to which authorities believed they had the sufficient internal skills base required for implementing the electronic government agenda effectively over the long term.

## The customer chain

Most of the planning within authorities appeared to be devoted to enhancing the customer chain. Most authorities expressed the wish to re-engineer access to government services using multiple access contact centres supported by sophisticated customer relationship management systems. Only one authority appeared to be well advanced in this area in Wales. This is not surprising when resourcing of this innovation was seen as particularly difficult for the smaller authorities.

Most authority WWW sites were currently content or content plus in terms of the Society of Information Technology Manager’s four-point scale of categories (promotional, content, content plus and transactional). The aspiration amongst most authorities was for fully transactional WWW sites. Only some authorities were using such technologies for facilitating interaction between, for instance, councillor and citizen.

## The internal value chain

In discussions with the authorities it appeared that the enablement of the internal value chain of authorities was at a much more advanced stage than the enablement of the customer and supply chains. However, there was little description of the state of the back-end infrastructure and the integration and inter-operability of back-end systems in the Implementing Electronic Government statements themselves. There was also little allusion to plans for front-end/back-end systems integration in most authorities. Most authorities seemed to be using basic technologies such as electronic mail to good effect internally, many had intranets and many had upgraded their internal communications infrastructure. However, the use of technologies such as content, document and knowledge management was variable. Many authorities expressed concern over the increasing costs and unclear benefits of document management systems.

## The supply chain

In the private sector, electronic enablement of the supply chain was seen as critical to modernization. However, from this study’s evaluation it appeared that the supply chain was currently the least enabled of this paper’s themes within Welsh authorities. Some authorities were piloting aspects of electronic procurement, but few authorities had a clear strategy in this area. Many authorities appeared to be waiting on national guidance and initiatives. Most also did not appear to be using extranets in any serious way and tele-working was being piloted only by a minority. Human resource management issues were frequently cited as major obstacles for introducing tele-working within authorities.

## The community chain

The issue of the electronic community was treated differently amongst the authorities. A minority of the authorities oriented their entire electronic government strategy around the key idea of partnerships with the community. In such authorities the community information plan was the electronic government plan. However, in the majority of the authorities, the electronic community was placed as one but not the only issue in their electronic

## Discussion

This section attempts to depict the current position of Welsh local authorities succinctly against the model of electronic local government described in the previous sections. From this exercise, the paper highlights particular instances of good practice and critical areas of concern. The paper also raises what the authors see as being significant opportunities for progressing the electronic government agenda in Wales.

## Current positioning of Welsh authorities

It is possible to describe the 22 unitary authorities in Wales as being at one of three stages of development in terms of their approaches to and practical applications of electronic government.

(1) Advanced. Such authorities are substantially advanced in implementing most aspects of the electronic government organization. This research would place no more than 10% of Welsh authorities in this category.

(2) Developing. Such authorities have an electronic government strategy in place and a coherent implementation plan for moving forward on electronic government. In order to assess the utility of key aspects of electronic service delivery, such authorities are piloting strategic systems, standards and technologies. This research would place 80% of Welsh authorities in this category.

(3) Not fully engaged. In such authorities there is generally little evidence of progress on any of the aspects of electronic government. Some may be viewed as appearing not to be treating electronic government seriously. This research would place no more than 10% of Welsh authorities in this category.

The key challenge in Wales is to move a substantial number of authorities from the developing category into the advanced category, while at the same time reducing the number of authorities in the not fully engaged category to zero.

## Instances of good practice

There were clear examples of good practice in each of the areas identified in the analysis. Some of these examples are provided below.

Planning. A number of authorities had conducted a systematic audit of the information required for supporting internal systems and processes and key data sharing with partners. Some authorities had started a number of process mapping and design initiatives, particularly in relation to the implementation of contact centre processes. At least one authority was employing consultants for initiating the task of process mapping, these activities being shadowed by in-house staff who will eventually assume the mapping task. A number of authorities were clearly aligning their approaches to electronic government with best value (the Wales Improvement Programme).

Management. A substantial number of authorities had appointed their council leader and chief executive as electronic champions. The authorities the authors spoke to believed that this demonstrated a clear commitment within their authorities to the objectives of electronic government. Such support was clearly necessary for removing barriers in the way of this agenda. Some authorities had implemented a new electronic government organization with clear linkages across service delivery functions. This was seen as critical to demonstrating the organizational push for electronic service delivery.

Development. Many authorities had produced a clear development plan with targets set and resourcing indicated. Some authorities had explicitly linked the goals of such a plan to their modernization objectives and to their established ICT strategy. A number of authorities had wellestablished schemes in place for the training of staff (European Computer Driving Licence was commonly used as a baseline standard). One authority was demonstrating commitment to advancing the electronic agenda by providing drop-in ICT surgeries for its elected members.

Customer chain. One authority was in the early stages of using a multiple access contact centre, including supporting electronic transactions from the authority’s WWW site and employing customer relationship management technology. Another authority had won an award for its WWW site for the second year running. The use of WWW-casting technology to broadcast council-run events over the Internet had also been piloted.

(5) Internal value chain. A small number of authorities had implemented a content management system for supporting maintenance of the authority intranet. Some authorities were beginning to develop explicit linkages between their intranet and Internet presence.

(6) Supply chain. Few authorities were currently enabling the supply chain. One example that points in the right direction is the use one authority was making of an extranet for allowing schools to reconcile payments with the authority’s finance system. The use of hand-held devices for field workers is another instance of innovative practice, which was being piloted by a number of authorities.

(7) Community chain. A small number of authorities had developed an explicit electronic community strategy in support of their community strategy. In one case, the authority had appointed officers for managing electronic partnerships in the community. In north Wales an effective consortium of authorities was sharing expertise and actively engaging in joint initiatives.

## Areas of concern

A number of areas of concern were raised in the authors’ discussions with the authorities. Some of these areas are listed below.

(1) The business case for electronic government. There was suggestion that the difficulty of making the case for electronic government within authorities was a common experience. Many felt that a clearer and concrete linkage between the modernization agenda and electronic government needed to be established. There was some allusion to the need for adapting the private sector model of electronic business more appropriately to the particular context of local government.

(2) Direction from the assembly. Concern was expressed at the level of direction being provided by the assembly, particularly in relation to the prioritization of key aspects of the electronic government agenda. Generally a lack of awareness seemed to exist about the Cymru Ar-lein strategy and its relationship to implementing electronic government.

(3) Resourcing and sustainability. Major concerns were expressed in relation to the level of resourcing necessary for achieving a certain level of electronic service delivery. Associated with this concern, a number of pertinent questions were tabled in relation to the sustainability of funding in the longer term for electronic government. A particular area of anxiety was the degree to which funding of electronic government was currently heavily reliant on the success of bids to numerous agencies.

(4) Partnerships with other authorities. For a number of authorities, a solution to the high costs and risks associated with implementing aspects of their electronic government strategy would be to partner with other authorities in order to deliver key electronic services. However, significant political obstacles were raised as limitations on the degree to which partnerships could be formed in relation to aggregation of electronic services.

Partnerships with other agencies. Significant tangible and intangible benefits may be achieved through the sharing of data between local authorities and other public sector agencies such as police authorities and health care trusts. However, concerns were expressed over the levels of duplication of technologies and systems and the major difficulties of managing partnerships in this area without appropriate direction from the Welsh Assembly Government.

(6) Skills. Few authorities had conducted a systematic audit of the skills required for implementing their development plans. Hence, there was little information on which to base an assessment of the realism of the loose targets highlighted in many statements. Authorities may experience difficulties in the medium term to long term in implementing electronic government effectively if the skills issue is not suitably addressed.

Data sharing and data protection. In order to facilitate joined-up working, data needs to be shared both within authorities themselves and between an authority and its external partners. However, data protection legislation was seen actively to restrict the degree to which data can be shared both within and without authorities.

(8) Benchmarking of electronic service delivery. Many authorities felt that the current Welsh Assembly Government P1.5 set of indicators for electronic service delivery were likely to prove a hindrance rather than a help to the electronic government agenda. The indicators were seen as being too focused upon inputs rather than outputs, on front-end systems rather than frontend/back-end integration and, as such, were too crude a measure for assessing the process of electronic government implementation.

## Opportunities

Although cost savings were seen as being difficult to achieve by most authorities in the short to medium term, there appear to be a number of ways in which a national strategy might achieve significant efficiency gains. In this respect, this study’s findings are clearly aligned with the philosophy of the Freedom and Responsibility in Local Government Policy Statement (Welsh Assembly

Government, 2002), which clearly identifies the advantage presented by the relatively small number of Welsh unitary authorities with similar responsibilities, thus allowing collaboration while retaining accountability.

(1) Aggregation of services. There appeared to be a number of areas of common core services within authorities that were duplicated. With political will, there is a significant opportunity for centralizing and integrating such services. Local government payroll and teachers’ payroll were two examples frequently raised in this respect.

(2) Common standards and systems. Although there were significant differences in the make-up of the authorities, there were a number of core technologies and systems that many authorities needed to purchase in common. Significant examples here are smart card systems. It makes little sense for each authority to purchase their own specific smart card system with its own associated authentication and security protocols. Significant technical rationalization could be achieved through the establishment of guidance in standards and systems.

(3) National electronic procurement. Most authorities the authors spoke to were waiting on national guidance on electronic procurement. The literature suggests that significant savings may be forthcoming in this area.

## Conclusion

This paper has described some of the shape of the electronic agenda in UK local government. It has described some of the context for this and some of the pressures that have influenced its current manifestation. The paper has also described some of the relationships between issues of organizational change and the exploitation of ICT in the public sector.

As compared to the average business organization in the UK local authorities are significantly behind in their exploitation of ICT for supporting organizational activity. However, authorities are currently working under considerable pressure to perform effectively against a large number of different initiatives. They are also experiencing significant constraints in terms of the degree of freedom they have to implement electronic government within the domain of a significant number of other priorities. It was therefore not surprising to find that, while the need for organizational change under the remit of electronic government was clearly understood and was seen as essential by many, there were currently significant barriers to achieving radical re-engineering of structures and processes within local government in Wales.

On the positive side the Welsh authorities were significantly under-selling the efforts they had made over the last decade for improving their back-end ICT infrastructure. On the negative side, most authorities within Wales were only at the piloting stage in investing in key frontend ICT systems and processes. Of greater longer term concern is the important need for adopting a holistic approach to electronic government focused around the innumerable benefits of efficient and effective back-end/ front-end systems integration. As one authority put it

Electronic government is a bit like retail – doing front-end systems work is like redecorating your shop front and making it more accessible – this will attract more customers – but the prettiest shop is likely to fail if customers find that they cannot actual buy anything they require!

This study’s findings were echoed in a recent Audit Commission (2002) review of the first year of electronic government implementation in England. They found too much emphasis on the implementation of front-end systems amongst English authorities and not enough emphasis on re-engineering local government in terms of the entire range of processes and systems. They therefore queried the long-term value of this narrow vision of electronic service delivery to the customer of local government. Key questions were also raised over the sustainability of the electronic government agenda longer term in terms of both the physical (people, finance and technical infrastructure) and non-physical resources (skill, plans and strategies) available to authorities.

The authorities cannot be wholly blamed for this situation. As Van Thiel and Leeuw (2002) argued, there is something of a performance paradox in the public sector. The public sector has been subject to an increasing regime of performance assessment, particularly in the UK. Some of the unintended consequences of this are that public sector organizations adjust their structures and processes to meet the demands of performance assessment. The initial guidance on electronic government evaluation within both England and Wales was focused around key performance indicators for electronic service delivery. Not surprisingly authorities have directed their short-term plans and strategies at improving front-end processes and systems. The shop front is being put in order before the shop counter.

Hence, although the electronic local government agenda is only in its strategic phase in Wales and in its implementation infancy in England key problems and risks are already beginning to emerge. For instance, many public sector organizations such as the Inland Revenue in the UK have experienced significant difficulties in implementing electronic service delivery. A key report published by the National Audit Office (2002b) on the electronic strategy of the Inland Revenue offers some key pointers/ principles for electronic government. After problems with its first attempt to introduce electronic business the Inland Revenue has initiated a number of changes.

(1) A review of targets. The initial target set for the Inland Revenue of 50% of business being done electronically by 2005 is now seen as unrealistic. Planning now considers prioritization in terms of greatest benefit and take-up such as electronic data interchange pay-as-you-earn from large firms.

(2) Performance improvement. Electronic government plans are clearly tied to performance improvement. Efficiency gains such as estimated savings of 1300 posts over the long term as well as effectiveness gains from electronic revenue services (greater accuracy of data, less need for checking and less customer queries) are expected.

(3) Added value. The expectation is that electronic services will not be taken up unless they provide added value to the customer in terms of such things as simplified forms where much of the customer information is filled automatically.

(4) Change management. Electronic government change is not just technology change but business change. It particularly demands a greater customeroriented focus from government agencies.

(5) Electronic business unit. The establishment of an electronic business unit and continuous training is seen as critical. Other critical success factors include clear and visible leadership from the top, good risk analysis and the importance of monitoring projects and learning lessons. Timely evaluations are seen as critical.

Electronic local government is an important area of study for information systems academics for a number of reasons. The ranges of services and activities within local government are significantly different and probably a lot more complex than those of the average business organization. The objectives of the local government organization are also a lot more diffuse leading to strategies that have to meet multiple and frequently competing objectives. In addition, the structures and processes of local government have been subject to quite substantial change in the UK over the last decade. Hence, local government offers a rich vein for studying the impact of informatics planning, management and development strategies on organizational change.

The electronic local government agenda is only just beginning in the UK, but there are few signs that the interest in ICT enablement of government processes will diminish in the near future. A recent KPMG (2002) survey of electronic government indicated that 50% of the UK public now have access to the Internet and two-thirds of the population now want at least one service of government delivered on-line.

Given its increasing importance there is a key need for studying the process of implementing electronic local government amongst a range of UK authorities over the period up to and immediately beyond 2005. Such a study will enable an assessment of the impact that the electronic agenda has made to organizational change within the local government sector, particularly its effect on service delivery. The present authors have plans to initiate such a study in the near future.
