# The chimpanzees’ tea party: a new metaphor for project

managers

HELGA DRUMMOND and JULIA HODGSON

Institute of Public Administration and Management, University of Liverpool, Liverpool L69 3BX, UK

This paper addresses the problem of information technology systems failure. It is argued that conventional approaches to project management assume that success depends upon imposing control. This paper invokes a novel metaphor, namely the chimpanzees’ tea party, for highlighting the limits of this assumption and showing how control-based approaches to project management can be counterproductive. Paradoxically, situations may arise where projects can be more effectively controlled by not attempting to impose control.

Mind is sovereign.

All things are generated by mind.

## Introduction

Organizational history is rich in examples of information technology (IT) project failures. For example, recent research has suggested that between one- and two-thirds of software projects will exceed their budget and timescales (Jones, 1997). In addition, approximately half of the most expensive software projects will ultimately be deemed out of control and duly cancelled (Gibbs, 1994). The Standish Group has been tracking project outcomes in the USA since 1993. In a recent survey of 280 000 applications projects in large, medium and small crossindustry companies, 23% were cancelled before completion or never implemented and 49% exceeded their budgets and time-scales and had fewer features and functions than originally specified (Extreme Chaos 2001, 2001). Some failures are high profile, such as Cambridge University’s ‘bungled’ computer finance system, which cost £9 million to develop (twice the budgeted amount) and caused chaos on the campus when it finally went online (Clare, 2001). A good many more are never reported: a research survey of 1027 projects discovered that only 130 (barely 13%) were successful (Taylor, 2000).

Why does it keep happening despite increasingly sophisticated project management techniques? The question is important because the costs of failure are high. Project managers suffer the frustration and disappointment of seeing their efforts wasted and job insecurity. Organizations incur financial and opportunity costs as IT becomes increasingly central to the competitive viability of many organizations. Society ultimately pays in higher prices, poor service, stress and even danger.

This paper offers recommendations for reducing IT systems failures based upon the image of the chimpanzees’ tea party. The rationale for this approach is described later in the paper. Here it is sufficient to note that what is offered is a supplementary point of orientation to the mainstream project management literature. More specifically, the mainstream literature suggests that the risks of failure can be minimized by managers imposing control. In contrast, this paper suggests that the application of control-based theories of management may actually cause failure.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. First the epistemological basis of metaphors is outlined. This is followed by a description of the chimpanzees’ tea party and an analysis of its relevance to the dynamics of IT project management. The paper ends with a series of recommendations for practice.

## The role of metaphors

A metaphor is a linguistic device whereby one phenomenon is understood in terms of another. For example, the notion of a computer possessing a ‘memory’ enables us to conceptualize its storage capabilities, whilst the notion of a ‘virus’ highlights the potentially contagious nature of computer malfunctioning (e.g. Brown, 1977; Lakoff and Johnson, 1980; Morgan, 1980, 1983, 1996; Schon, 1993).

Metaphors highlight points of correspondence between one phenomenon and another. Yet correspondence can never be total, otherwise the metaphor would be redundant. For example, to suggest that ‘software is like software’ illuminates nothing. Yet to say that software is the ‘engine’ that drives the system facilitates a rudimentary grasp of the role of computer programmers. Metaphors are therefore inherently partial. They illuminate certain features of a phenomenon whilst obscuring others (e.g. Lakoff and Johnson, 1980; Morgan, 1996). For instance, the notion of the mind as a ‘machine’ highlights the mind’s reasoning and analytical powers, but eclipses other capabilities of the mind such as intuition and creativity.

Metaphors are not just linguistic devices for making the task of reading more interesting, they are the basic means whereby people create their relationship with the world (e.g. Brown, 1977; Lakoff and Johnson, 1980; Morgan, 1980, 1983). Metaphors do not exist as absolutes. They are basically human constructions that are used for defining a situation and framing a problem (e.g. Schon, 1993). The choice of a metaphor is crucial because it determines how a problem is perceived. For example, madness can be seen as an illness requiring hospitalization and cure or as a measure of social distance (Brown, 1977).

Some metaphors are so commonplace that we seldom think about them (Lakoff and Johnson, 1980). For example, the concept of time is almost automatically linked to the notion of a ‘clock’. Likewise, when we think of memory we almost automatically equate it with the notion of repository. Metaphors may help us to understand our world, but they can also constrain our understanding because our assumptions become bounded by the choice of metaphor. The eminent physicist Stephen Hawking realized that theory and research in physics was bounded by the notion of the clock, which depicts time as moving forwards. This insight prompted Hawking to reconceptualize time as moving backwards as well as forwards and, hence, to the ‘discovery’ of so-called ‘black holes (White and Gribben, 1992). Likewise, the assumption that memory tells us what has happened obscures another important possibility, namely that memory tells us what is going to happen (Weick, 1979; Drummond, 2001).

## Information technology project management

Managing an IT project is like – what? IT project management takes place within an organizational context. Organizations exist because it is through collective efforts that more can be achieved than by individuals acting alone. However, organizations can only realize their potential if the efforts of individuals are coordinated. Coordination requires a measure of control. The importance of control is reflected in the IT project management literature with its emphasis upon defining objectives, planning, budgeting, requirements development, architecture, implementation and testing (e.g. Skidmore and Wroe, 1988; Alter, 1996). An IT project manager in any large, medium or small organization can employ a variety of methods, techniques and tools for addressing the specific business information systems problem. For example, PRINCE2, which is mandated by the UK Government for large IT projects, is a ‘process-driven’ methodology for project management that provides a standard method of working for defining the organization, planning, control and project processes for a specific project (Bentley, 1992, 1998).

Structured methodologies such as structured analysis and design methodology (SSADM) offer another prescriptive approach to the IT systems development life cycle at the analysis and design stages of an IT project. SSADM, which has been used in computing since 1981, consists of five main modules: a feasibility study, requirements analysis, requirements specification, logical system specification and physical design. It also has three key modelling techniques: logical data modelling, which is the process of identifying, modelling and documenting the data requirements of the information system, data flow modelling, which is the process of identifying, modelling and documenting how data flows around a business information system and entity event modelling, which is the process of identifying modelling and documenting the business events that affect each entity and the sequence in which the event occurs (Cutts, 1991; Yeats, 1991).

Such structured, logical methods of control have been developed in order to enable project managers to meet the central challenge of delivering an IT system on time, within budget and against a specified set of requirements. They amount to a formalized process that is focused but highly mechanistic. The notion of mechanism is basically a metaphor and, like all metaphors, mechanical imagery highlights certain possibilities whilst obscuring others. More specifically, the imagery helps us understand how success can be engineered. Yet, as Meredith and Mantel (1995) acknowledged, there are no purely mechanistic problems on projects. All problems have a human element ‘calling for intervention and control by the project manger’ (Meredith and Mantel, 1995, p. 463, italics added). Meredith and Mantel (1995) have not been singled out for any particular reason. The statement has been quoted merely as an example of a typical textbook statement that sees human influences as potentially destabilizing and urges project managers to impose order (see also Lock, 1996; Lockyer and Gordon, 1996; Maylor, 1999).

A metaphor’s limitations are best seen by going beyond its boundaries. In order to see what is obscured it is necessary to invoke an alternative perspective. The intellectua vehicle for exploring a different metaphor is a technique known as ‘imaginization’ (Morgan, 1993). Imaginization involves examining familiar phenomena through an unconventional lens in order to generate new insights. The most useful metaphors are often those that are dissonant or counterintuitive precisely because they hold the potential for illuminating hitherto unsuspected insights or connections. The ‘tea party’ metaphor was chosen because the spectacle of a chimpanzees’ tea party resonates the precise opposite of order, that is disorder.

## The chimpanzees’ tea party

The ‘chimpanzees’ tea party’ was an attraction once offered by zoos for entertaining young children. A group of chimpanzees are seated at a table. The table is arranged as if it were indeed a real tea party. Mats are provided, food is served upon plates and drink is poured into cups. To the children’s delight the tea party soon descends into chaos as the chimpanzees tip their cups upside down, spill their orange juice onto the ground, bang their plates about, scattering the food far and wide and wear the table mats on their heads. The mess is then compounded as some of the chimpanzees dive under the table pulling the tablecloth with them. Meanwhile, the keeper stands by with a dustpan, a brush and a large bin liner.

For the purposes of the analysis that follows the chimpanzees stand as surrogates for the users of IT systems and the keeper stands as a surrogate for the project manager. What can be seen in this chaotic and dissonant image that may help to reduce the risk of project failure?

## Spilt orange juice: ‘the irrationality of rationality’

A project may be deemed to have failed when it becomes clear that expectations cannot be met (e.g. Bowen, 1987). Lyytinen and Hirschheim (1987) identified three types of expectation failure, that is correspondence failure, process failure and interaction failure. The tea party fails on all three counts. Specifically, it is a correspondence failure as there is no prospect of the design objective of a civilized tea party being met. It is also a process failure as the cavorting promises to wreck budgets and time-scales. It is also a kind of interaction failure in that there is a low level of uptake as regards eating and drinking because the chimpanzees’ attention wanders in pursuit of other interests. Finally, just like so many software development projects, the tea party descends into chaos.

There is no one single issue that repeatedly causes IT systems failure (Sauer, 1993). Success and failure are ultimately social, that is a system succeeds if it commands social support and fails when support is withdrawn. Sometimes support is withdrawn abruptly, for example if the system breaks down at a crucial juncture. Sometimes support crumbles slowly, for example if it is only used because there is no alternative (Sauer, 1993).

In other words projects can fail to meet expectations and yet succeed or they can meet expectations and yet fail. In order to explain this paradox it is necessary to invoke a submetaphor. The defining criteria for subcategorization is coherence but not consistency (Lakoff and Johnson, 1980). The metaphor of order is an over-arching term for a multifaceted entity. One important submetaphor of order is the notion of rationality. Rationality means calculation of the most appropriate means of accomplishing something (Morgan, 1996).

All other things being equal, a project is more likely to succeed if it meets requirements and is delivered on time and within budget. Although the various methods of project management are specifically different, generically they all assume that success can be achieved by following defined protocols and procedures. This is like viewing the cups, plates and mats as the most rational means of enabling the chimpanzees to eat their tea in an orderly fashion. The danger is that the project manager ends up with a formalized process that is preoccupied with the cups, the plates and the mats, that is delivering requirements on time and within budget. When attention becomes focused upon a relatively narrow range of managerial imperatives, important dimensions of the project can be either excluded or treated in a mechanistic fashion, with the result that the project manager ends up losing sight of the totality of the project. For example, the London Stock Exchange became so preoccupied with meeting the next deadline in a frantic effort to deliver the late-running Project TAURUS that they failed to see that the project was no longer worth delivering (Drummond, 1996, 1998). At very least the drama that unfolds is outside the parameters of the project manager’s tool set and, hence, goes undetected until the next milestone or checkpoint. By then the ‘tea party’ may have descended into chaos.

## Banging the plates: multiple realities

PRINCE2 provides bureaucratic controls on the planning and execution of projects. In this way it forces the identification of some of the potential problems that may arise in the project and stresses early conflict resolution, which is a feature of much of the recent literature on project management. However, it is limited by its approach. It focuses on the ‘product’, which is usually a computer program or system and does not provide any guarantees of success. Indeed, there have been a number of high-profile failures of projects, despite their being run in line with the standard (Maylor, 1999).

Most structured approaches embody a ‘get-out’ clause highlighting the potentially destabilizing effects of poli tics and environmental factors. To the extent that the project management literature addresses human influences it mainly suggests that managers can procure commitment and thereby reduce the risk of social failure by careful attention to communications. For example,

Daniels (1993) suggested that IT projects require a communicator, that is ‘A person . . . to ensure that the organization understands the importance and nature of the project, and that the appropriate mechanisms are in place to assist . . . people learning how to use IT’ (p. 102). In addition, Daniels (1993) stated that ‘Education and training play an important role in communicating the designs and purposes of the . . . information architecture. They help managers “pass the torch” to the rest of the organization’ (p. 125). According to Daniels (1993) ‘Once people understand they are determining the company’s future business practices and that process requires their expertise, commitment will be high’ (p. 125).

Again Daniels (1993) has not been singled out for any particular reason other than the fact that her recommendations exemplify a widely held assumption that control can be achieved by the project manager imposing their view of reality upon other actors (e.g. Roman, 1986, p. 283; Meredith and Mantel, 1995, pp. 102–3; Lock, 1996, p. 18; Mantel et al., 2001, p. 29). It is like the keeper sitting down with the chimpanzees and patiently explaining the purposes of each item of crockery, how they should be used and stressing why it is important that the chimpanzees should eat their tea nicely. This approach is insensitive because it fails to take account of the realities of all the different constituencies of the users.

To be more precise conventional approaches to procuring commitment reflect a monochromatic view of reality. The definition of the various objects that together constitute our realities is seldom in doubt. We know the difference between, for example, a cup, a plate and a table mat. A more important issue concerns the significance or meaning of those artefacts in the eyes of different constituencies. From the chimpanzees’ standpoint what does the ‘tea party’ mean? Certainly the cups, plates and mats are vested with very different meanings from how we understand them. The chimpanzees see them as toys, missiles, headgear, percussion instruments and so forth.

The London Ambulance Service discovered this to their cost when it tried to improve efficiency with its new IT system. However, as far as they were concerned the new arrangements meant subordination of those who were already highly subordinate, one reason why there was little commitment amongst staff to making the system work (Report of the Inquiry into the London Ambulance Service, 1993). Likewise, Project TAURUS was intended to pave the way for reforming London’s antiquated paperdriven system for buying and selling securities with farreaching implications for the custodians, private client brokers and company registrars who made a living from handling paper (Drummond, 1996). The problem in both cases was not that key constituencies failed to understand the ‘benefits’ of the proposed new systems, they understood only too well.

One of the frustrations of project management is delivering exactly what users have asked for, only for the users to be disappointed with the results. For example, a health service manager stated ‘What I can’t answer is, “Is Mrs Jones in the second bed from the left getting the same quality of treatment that she was two years ago?” I can’t answer that – and I need to know’ (Strong and Robinson, 1990, p. 86). The slip may come about in the translation from operational needs to formal requirements. It is like trying to describe the outcome of the tea party by quantifying the waste, that is so many litres of spilt orange juice and so many kilograms of trampled or half-eaten pieces of apple and orange. The information is technically accurate, but it fails to reflect the chaotic spectacle of the tea party.

Another way in which IT systems can succeed and yet fail is because control-based theories of management reflect linear intuitive thinking whereby ‘problems’ are seen as having ‘solutions’ (Hatch and Ehrlich, 1993; Hatch, 1997; Drummond, 1998). For example, newspaper advertisements for project managers often stress ‘IT solutions’. Likewise Southwark Council’s large-scale investment in an IT infrastructure known as Project Globe was described as ‘an enterprise-wide solution (Public Finance, 1998). The chimpanzees’ behaviour illuminates a paradoxical and counterintuitive feature of project management that is obscured by conventional perspectives, namely the propensity for ‘solutions creating ‘problems’ (e.g. Quinn and Cameron, 1988; Watzlawick, 1988). Metaphorically speaking, the cups, the plates and the mats are ‘solutions’ to the ‘problem’ of feeding the chimpanzees. In the hands of the chimpanzees, however, the ‘solutions’ create ‘problems’ as they detract from the business of eating and drinking.

Clearly all IT ventures have unexpected consequences. However, it is more than that. The ‘solution’ mindset does not encourage project managers to anticipate problems or to see that circumstances may arise where the cure is worse than the problem. For example, Southwark Council’s new IT-based management system promises ‘A seamless integrated package for every operation’ (Public Finance, 1998, p. 10). The expectation is that ‘A figure is keyed in once and every nook and cranny of the system adjusts accounts accordingly (Public Finance, 1998, p. 11). One might say ‘and then what?’ The distinguished social psychologist Weick (1985) argued that computer-based IT systems defeat their own purpose because they create complexity. For instance, assuming Southwark Council’s system delivers, 20 new and additional committees might be required for perusing the myriad of statistics concerning complaints about noisy neighbours, dogs fouling pavements and so on. In turn they demand more information and so the original ‘allsinging all-dancing’ system becomes a failure because it cannot meet the demands that get placed on it. Moreover, the system did not deliver as planned. For instance, the transfer of payroll responsibilities to schools created chaos because the new system could not cope with the peculiarities of the employment arrangements within schools. In consequence, schools were being fed inaccurate information that led to the wrong payments being made and this seriously undermined schools’ ability for monitoring and managing their budgets (Office for Standards in Education, 1999)

## Recommendations for practice

When control-based approaches to project management fail the intuitive response is to apply more control. For example, when Project TAURUS was falling seriously behind schedule the monitoring group attempted to tighten its grip by demanding more information and by insisting that future deadlines must be met (Drummond, 1996). This is known as first-order thinking, that is attempting to solve a problem within existing parameters (Watzlawick et al., 1974). First-order solutions tend to make matters worse because they end up addressing the symptoms rather than the underlying problems. For example, the London Ambulance Service insisted that their time-scales were non-negotiable despite clear and consistent feedback from suppliers that these were unrealistic (Report of the Inquiry into the London Ambulance Service, 1993). Such insistence created the appearance of control (for a while), but at the expense undermining confidence in the project as the time-scales inevitably slipped.

Indeed, the subsequent inquiry into the London Ambulance Service debacle recommended ‘A properly phased, controlled, implementation under strong project management’ (Report of the Inquiry into the London Ambulance Service, 1993, p. 33). Note the words phased, controlled and strong. They resonate control-based theories of organization that assume that strong, that is aggressive, leadership can overcome any obstacle. For example, in their path-breaking work on process re-engineering Hammer and Champy (1993) urged leaders to blast their way through obstacles. In other words, shove the chimpanzees into their chairs and force them to eat their tea. And when pandemonium erupts, then what?

In contrast, second-order thinking involves stepping outside the parameters of a problem in order to address the underlying issues. In the tea party context secondorder thinking starts not with the chimpanzees but with the keeper. Whereas the project manager’s task is depicted as imposing order upon chaos, in contrast the keeper sees order within chaos (Brown, 1977). That is, although much food and drink is spilt, the chimpanzees eat their tea, albeit after a fashion. Therefore, although the situation appears chaotic it is actually under control. To interfere with the chimpanzees would be to upset the dynamic stability of the tea party. In other words, the keeper gets the chimpanzees to eat their tea through a strategy of inaction.

Whereas the project management literature concentrates upon how project managers can achieve their goals of delivering on time and within budget, in contrast the keeper succeeds by allowing the chimpanzees to achieve their goals. All IT systems imply change. Moreover, most IT systems failures are not technical but social and political failures. Clearly any new system of moderate complexity is almost bound to provoke conflict because it is like trying to move the chimpanzees, that is it entails upset. The point is that change that goes against the grain is less likely to endure than change that follows existing social arrangements and political balances (Wing, 1986). To mix metaphors, lawns get ruined when architects decide where footpaths will go because people then use the lawn as a footpath. A more subtle strategy of control is to study where people walk and place the footpaths accordingly. As Sauer (1993) pointed out this means that project managers need to be attuned to what change means to users and adopt a prismatic view of reality that sees different and possibly conflicting meanings. It also means that project managers require an in-depth understanding of the working environment for which the systems are being developed. For instance, is it possible to develop an air traffic control system without first experiencing the realities of air traffic control?

Above all, what matters is what project managers do with their knowledge and understanding. Although conflict is usually seen as an obstacle, when the chimpanzees fight with one another they are communicating. In other words, conflict is potentially a transaction (Brown, 1977) whereby different needs, wants, ideas and perspective are exchanged. De Bono (1995) argued that Western styles of thinking are too judgemental in their approach, that is too inclined to assume that the task of management is to identify ‘one best way’. De Bono (1995) argued that there may be more to be gained creatively by pursuing conflicting ideas in parallel and seeing where they lead, a technique known as parallel thinking.

According to De Bono (1995) this form of ‘going with the flow’ helps to build success into projects and helps them to become self-organizing. An example of what can be achieved in practice concerns the transition to screenbased trading in the City in preparation for deregulation (the ‘Big Bang’) in 1986, a project that was led by the London Stock Exchange. It was a huge and highly risky venture partly because of the technical complexity and also because deregulation meant the end of a way of life in the City.

Yet apart from minor teething problems this massive project sponsored was successfully completed and provided London with a lasting and effective trading infrastructure. Interestingly the situation faced by the London Stock Exchange closely matched the scenario depicted by Laudon and Laudon (1999), another of the standard texts: ‘There are times when the only sensible way is to employ a new technology that directly opposes an existing organizational culture. When this occurs, technology is often stalled while the culture slowly adjusts’ (p. 75). With the Big Bang set to unleash forces of competition the London Stock Exchange could ill afford the technology to stall. Nor did they try to ‘blast’ their way through obstacles. They did not even try to sell the benefits of the new system to users. Instead, the London Stock Exchange spent £6 million redeveloping the ‘floor’ in order to enable face-to-face trading to continue. Barely 6 weeks later the London Stock Exchange closed its new trading floor because it had fallen into complete disuse. It could be argued that the London Stock Exchange wasted millions on the development. Yet it may have been precisely because the London Stock Exchange showed itself responsive to members’ sense of tradition and fears for the future that the really important IT project succeeded. By allowing users to achieve their goals of retaining a tradition the new arrangements appeared as less of a threat. In other words, instead of imposing the view upon members of ‘you will not need a trading floor any more’ the London Stock Exchange allowed that to become apparent without forcing the issue. Viewed in that way the redevelopment of the trading floor was a small price to pay for ultimate success.

Emphatically this paper is not suggesting that project managers ignore budgets and deadlines. The point made here is that the preconditions for ultimate success may lie elsewhere, that is in acquiring a deep understanding of users’ realities and adapting to these. Such understanding needs to go beyond issues relating to formal requirements. Recall that one of the paradoxes of system failure is that a system can meet all the specified requirements and yet fail. This may be because formal consultation is not enough, that is what is really required is a deep appreciation of the realities of the various users. For instance, the success of the Inland Revenue’s self-assessment system may have owed much to the willingness of project managers to take an iterative approach, that is designing, testing and redesigning the system working hand in hand with Inland Revenue staff, tax payers and other users of the new system (Drummond, 2001). Conversely, insufficient depth can mean that the requirements (cups and plates) are met, only they are made of glass.

Even so, project managers are required to deliver. Yet too much emphasis upon time-scales and budgets can be counterproductive if the result is tunnel vision. It is like the keeper becoming riveted by the cups and the plates and failing to notice that the chimpanzees are in danger of disappearing up a tree or over a wall.

In addition to conducting a conventional costs–benefits analysis project managers should also conduct a formal risk analysis. Yet it is not the analysis that matters but what one does with the results. Whilst risk management can be useful a more subtle strategy is to eliminate risk. The chimpanzees create mayhem as it is. Yet imagine the chimpanzees attending a formal seven-course dinner with elaborate cutlery and place settings: the mayhem created by the tea party pales into insignificance. As academics and professionals we are taught to add. Project managers may have something to learn from the Tao (1986) as it encourages us to subtract, that is, metaphorically speaking, to remove as much crockery and cutlery as possible.

The tea party is an anachronism. Zoos have long since withdrawn it on the grounds that it is unnatural for chimpanzees to be presented in such a highly socialized fashion. Chimpanzees are actually highly dangerous animals that are never approached by zoo staff unless absolutely necessary.

Griffiths and Willcocks (1994) stated much the same about large-scale IT ventures, albeit using different language. In a nutshell they doubted whether organizations should be attempting such exercises. Indeed, a more recent survey undertaken on behalf of the Public Accounts Committee suggested that small-scale projects are more likely to be successful.

One reason for this is that ‘time equals risk’, that is they are opposite sides of the coin (e.g. Bernstein, 1996). Shortening the time-scale immediately reduces risk because there is less likelihood of the project being overtaken by events. However, this is only one potential benefit. One way of minimizing waste from the tea party is to feed the chimpanzees one at a time and feed them one thing at a time. Likewise, dividing large-scale projects into small ones enables organizations to focus their energies on the most receptive areas and minimize complexity, thus reducing the scope for chaos and resistance. In popular parlance the trick is going slow in order to go fast.

## Limitations of the metaphor

Recall that all metaphors are limited. An important limitation of the tea party metaphor is that it largely eclipses the role of vested interests and other external pressures that can lead to failure. It also excludes the role of a project champion in helping to achieve success (see for example Jarvenpaa and Ives, 1991). Indeed, many different metaphors could be invoked for describing various aspects of IT systems development. That in itself might be a fruitful area for research.

The limitations of this work are also acknowledged. Although one of the authors had researched a major IT systems failure and the other had taught IT systems development and is married to a software engineer it is appreciated that neither had ever been confronted with the task of delivering a complex project.

In addition it could be argued that the present paper merely restates the work of earlier commentators using different language, namely that IT systems construction is an organic process that calls for political as well as technical competence and that such processes are difficult to control. However, language is important: the value of invoking a novel metaphor is that it provides a framework for reconceptualizing the process of IT systems construction. More importantly the insights generated by the tea party suggest an alternative and counterintuitive approach to control.

A referee for this paper asked whether the notions behind the metaphor can be embedded in project management methodology or whether it means that the notion of methodology is impossible to sustain. It is suggested that the value of invoking a novel metaphor is simply to provide a supplementary point of orientation aimed at widening the project manager’s repertoire by suggesting that control may sometimes be more effective when it is directed at enabling users to achieve their goals. Again, to mix metaphors, conventional approaches to controlling a river that is liable to flooding lie in building barriers. The alternative is to dig the river deeper so that the water can better attain its goal of reaching the sea (Wing, 1986).

## Mats as hats: implications for research

Sauer (1993) suggested that, if we can understand the processes whereby IT systems go awry, we have a better chance of preventing failure. Yet surely it is important to study success as well as failure and to consider what we mean by success. Returning to the chimpanzees, the mats do actually make quite good hats. The history of IT systems contains many examples of ‘hats’. For instance, text messaging generates more profit for mobile telephone companies than telephone calls, yet text messaging facilities were added to mobile telephones as an afterthought. Likewise, who would have predicted the popularity of pocket computers? Designers seem to have assumed that users would never have the patience to use small ‘fiddly’ screens. Yet experience in service has shown otherwise. Perhaps social scientists have spent too long studying failure. We may have more to learn by studying success and even more to learn by studying the unexpected successes.
