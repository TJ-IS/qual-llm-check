# Adoption of broadband Internet in Korea: the role of experience in building attitudes

SANGJO OH

Department of Electronic Commerce, Dongyang Technical College, 62-160 Gochuk-Dong, Kuro-Gu, Seoul 152-714, Korea

JOONGHO AHN

School of Business Administration, Seoul National University, San 56-1 Shilim-Dong, Kwanak-Gu, Seoul 151-742, Korea

BEOMSOO KIM

Graduate School of Information, Yonsei University, Seoul 120-749, Korea Information and Decision Sciences, University of Illinois at Chicago, USA

Although many countries are attempting to build an information infrastructure and accelerate the adoption of broadband Internet access technology, the results are not always satisfactory. The focus of earlier research on the diffusion and adoption of broadband Internet access has been primarily on the construction of a national-level infrastructure or on macro-level factors such as government policies, market competition and the density of population. This research highlights the individual-level factors affecting the adoption of broadband access, integrating various views on the adoption and diffusion of technology, including the technology acceptance model, theory of planned behaviour and diffusion of innovation model. An extended technology acceptance model incorporating the notion of perceived resources is proved to be relevant in the adoption of broadband technologies. It was found that innovation attributes, such as compatibility, visibility and result demonstrability, have an impact on constructs in the extended technology acceptance model such as perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use and perceived resources. These results also support the idea that congruent experiences and opportunities in adopting a new technology affect user attitudes through the three extended technology acceptance model constructs above. This research suggests that efforts should be made to expand the compatible experience base of broadband Internet in order to facilitate its adoption and use. This paper also provides an extensive review of the adoption behaviour of individuals by incorporating different views and theories.

## Introduction

The rapid development of information technologies is affecting almost every aspect of our lives. Indeed, information technology is now regarded as a key component in gaining national competitiveness on the global stage. Many developed countries have been emphasizing the construction of a broadband, high-speed or premium quality information infrastructure. Broadband access to the Internet is now considered by international organizations as a key to enhancing the competitiveness of an economy and sustaining economic growth (International Telecommunication Union, 2001; Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2001, 2002).

The diffusion of a broadband network in Korea has been an exceptional case, one that has drawn worldwide attention (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2001, 2002; Legard, 2002). The number of Internet users in Korea is over 26 million, sixth in the world as of April 2003. In November 2002, Korea reached a milestone by having 10 million broadband service subscribers and 70% of all households subscribed to this service (National Computerization Agency and Ministry of Information and Communications, 2003; see Figure 1). The adoption rate of broadband access to the Internet per hundred persons is the highest in the world (Legard, 2002; Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2002).

Much research has been conducted in order to explain the diffusion and adoption of high-speed access to Internet technologies and most approaches, including the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (2001) and Lee et al. (2003), have addressed the national and environmental structure, mostly ignoring personal factors. For example, when analysing the development of broadband access in Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development countries, the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (2001) viewed that the bottleneck to growth was not just a technological problem caused by the limitations of local access networks and asserted that infrastructure competition should be the most fundamental policy approach in boosting broadband access.

Some of the factors discussed in earlier research include government policies encouraging the adoption of broadband access to the Internet, the large number of network providers and the highly concentrated potential market arising from a high population density and the configuration of local apartment housing. Korea has some ideal conditions for the wide and rapid diffusion of broadband Internet access. The high population density and the fact that many people live in apartment residences provide broadband firms with easy access to customers and low cost in the last-mile local loops construction. The Korean Government has also played an important role in driving these projects. Finally, there are many firms providing broadband Internet services and, thus, many choices for customers.

However, the factors listed above are just the necessary conditions for broadband Internet connections and explain only half of the real situation. The authors believe that the unknown other half involves an understanding of the structures of customers’ behaviour and that this is increasingly important to broadband firms facing intense competition in gaining and retaining customers in this market.

This research examines individual-level factors affecting the adoption of broadband access technologies and the relationships between those factors. Previous studies on the diffusion of broadband access have only offered limited information on the voluntary behaviour of individuals even though they have explained much about macro-level factors such as government, competition and the market. Previous research has limited implications for firms that interact with individual customers. Since the decision to adopt a broadband connection is made on an individual level, personal-level factors must be considered in order to gain a comprehensive understanding of what makes people adopt broadband access.

## Theoretical background

Understanding the behaviour of individuals has always been a concern for social researchers. The theory of reasoned action (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975) and theory of planned behaviour (Ajzen and Madden, 1986) are widely accepted and often used for explaining individuals behaviour. Along with these theories, the technology acceptance model (Davis, 1989) and diffusion of innovation (Rogers, 1983) perspectives are also widely supported, particularly in discussing the adoption of technology.

Most studies concerned with the prediction of behaviour from attitudinal variables are conducted in the framework of the theory of planned behaviour and, to a lesser extent, its predecessor, the theory of reasoned action (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975). The theory of planned behaviour extends from the theory of reasoned action by incorporating an additional construct of perceived behavioural control in order to account for situations in which an individual lacks substantial control over the targeted behaviour (Ajzen, 1991). The technology acceptance model was derived from the theory of reasoned action and developed specifically for explaining and predicting individual acceptance of information technology (Davis, 1989). While the theory of reasoned action is a general theory about human behaviour, the technology acceptance model is specific to information technology usage.

The diffusion of innovation perspective offers insights that are different from the theory of reasoned action, theory of planned behaviour and technology acceptance model, all of which base themselves primarily on attitudinal variables in explaining human behaviour. In explaining the velocity of adoption, Rogers (1983) identified five characteristics of an innovation: its relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability and observability. The diffusion of innovation perspective does not have any explicit relations with the technology acceptance model, but shares some principal constructs. The relative advantage posited in the diffusion of innovation perspective has almost the same notion of perceived usefulness and the complexity in the diffusion of innovation perspective captures the perceived ease of use in the technology acceptance model, although the sign is the opposite.

## Adoption of broadband Internet in Korea

The theory of planned behaviour, technology acceptance model and the diffusion of innovation perspective serve as good starting points in investigating individual-level factors affecting the adoption of last-mile technology. Further, it is expected that the extension, modification and combination of rich and robust theories will help illuminate the deep structures of broadband adoption and provide meaningful information to both the firms that compete in the broadband Internet market and the governments that pursue broadband penetration as a national policy.

## The technology acceptance model and theory of planned behaviour

According to the technology acceptance model (see Figure 2), an individual’s behavioural intention to use a technology can be explained by their perception about the usefulness of the technology in question and their attitude towards technology use (Davis, 1989). The technology acceptance model also posits that perceived usefulness is influenced by perceived ease of use and both perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use predict attitude (Davis, 1989). Perceived ease of use is the degree to which the user expects the target technology to be free of effort and perceived usefulness is the users’ subjective probability that using a specific technology will increase his or her job performance (Davis, 1989). Attitudes towards behaviour refer to the degree to which a person has a favourable or unfavourable evaluation or appraisal of the behaviour in question (Ajzen, 1991).

As in the theory of reasoned action, the technology acceptance model does not take into account the situation under which an individual lacks substantial control over the targeted behaviour, while the theory of planned behaviour (see Figure 3) incorporates the construct of perceived behavioural control as the notion of perceived resource constraints. The perceived behavioural control included in theory of planned behaviour is defined as people’s perceptions of the ease or difficulty of performing the behaviour of interest (Ajzen, 1991). The theory of planned behaviour consists first of constructs that do not appear in the technology acceptance model and, second, of other constructs overlapping at a minimum level with those of the technology acceptance model (Mathieson et al., 2001). According to the theory of planned behaviour, people act in accordance with their behavioural intentions, while intentions in turn are influenced by attitudes towards the behaviour, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control (Ajzen, 1991).

The technology acceptance model provides a more parsimonious set of constructs for predicting the adoption behaviour of information technology than the theory of planned behaviour while disregarding some of the constructs that may be important in some other situations. Attitude towards the technology is still an important factor in explaining the behavioural intentions of adopting information technology both in the technology acceptance model and theory of planned behaviour.

Research efforts have been made to combine the technology acceptance model and theory of planned behaviour models. Taylor and Todd (1995), for instance, combined the technology acceptance model and theory of planned behaviour in order to investigate the role of prior experience in information technology use. The results of this research suggest that there are some significant differences in the relative influence of the determinants of usage depending on experience. Based on the technology acceptance model, Venkatesh and Morris (2000) incorporated subjective norms in their model and investigated the moderating role of gender and experience. They found that men and women were different in their technology adoption behaviour and that the effect of the subjective norm diminished over time.

In order to extend the technology acceptance model including perceived behavioural control, Mathieson et al. (2001) proposed the construct of perceived resources, which is an individual’s belief that he or she has the personal and organizational resources needed for using an information system or technology. They identified resource-related variables from numerous literatures dealing with resource issues and categorized them into user attributes, support from others, system attributes and general control-related variables. Most of the factors hypothesized to affect perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use fall in these categories (Igbaria et al., 1995, 1997; Agarwal and Prasad, 1999).

Much research has also been pursued in order to verify and compare the validity and predictability of the technology acceptance model, theory of planned behaviour and extensions and/or combinations of the models. Chau and Hu (2001) used the theory of planned behaviour as a basic frame for their model and decomposed attitude by incorporating perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use as its mediating variable. They also include compatibility, which serves as an antecedent of both perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. They compared the technology acceptance model, theory of planned behaviour and their decomposed theory of planned behaviour models. They found that the decomposed theory of planned behaviour did not substantially increase the power for predicting behavioural intention. This research also suggested the possibility that the attitudinal models could not be applied to all contexts. A pure technology acceptance model was applied to the physicians’ acceptance of telemedicine technology and the result showed the need for incorporating additional factors or integrating with other information technology acceptance models in order to improve its specificity and explanatory utility in a health-care context (Hu et al., 1999). Straub et al. (1997) examined the technology acceptance model across cultures and suggested that the technology acceptance model might not hold up well in predicting technology use across cultures.

## The technology acceptance model and diffusion of innovation perspective

Research on the diffusion of innovation has been of interest to many scientific disciplines. Rogers (1983) identified five characteristics of an innovation: its relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability and observability. The relative advantage of an innovation is defined as the degree to which an innovation is perceived as being better than the idea it supersedes and research has found this construct to be one of the best predictors of the adoption of an innovation. Compatibility refers to the degree to which an innovation is perceived as consistent with the existing values, past experiences and needs of potential adopters. Complexity refers to the degree to which an innovation is perceived as relatively difficult to understand and use. Trialability is the degree to which an innovation may be experimented with on a limited basis. Observability is the degree to which the results of an innovation are visible to others. Relative advantage, compatibility, trialability and observability are positively related to the rate of adoption, while complexity is negatively related.

Even if the approaches used in the technology acceptance model and diffusion of innovation perspective for explaining the adoption behaviour of individuals are different, the theories share some of the principal constructs: relative advantage (perceived usefulness in the technology acceptance model) and complexity (opposite to perceived ease of use in the technology acceptance model). Other constructs in the diffusion of innovation perspective, such as compatibility, trialability and observability, relate to prior technology experience or opportunities for experiencing the technology under consideration. These constructs can be treated as external variables that directly affect perceived usefulness and/or perceived ease of use. However, combining these theories is rarely attempted.

Karahanna et al. (1999) compared pre-adoption and post-adoption beliefs by combining the technology acceptance model and diffusion of innovation perspective. They found that users and potential adopters of information technology differed in their determinants of attitude. Potential adopters base their attitude on a richer set of innovation characteristics than users, while users’ attitude is only based on instrumentality beliefs of the usefulness and perceptions of image enhancements.

Experience-related constructs such as compatibility, trialability and observability were not frequently used with the technology acceptance model. Chau and Hu (2001) included compatibility in their decomposed theory of planned behaviour as an external variable to perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use and the effects of compatibility were found to be significant only in relation to perceived usefulness. In contrast, Agarwal et al. (2000) found that relevant prior experience, mediated by general self-efficacy, affected perceived ease of use. The role of experience in the technology acceptance model has sometimes been hypothesized as a moderating variable (Taylor and Todd, 1995; Venkatesh and Davis, 2000). Venkatesh and Davis (2000) found experience moderated the relationship between subjective norm and perceived usefulness in longitudinal research and this was consistent with the result of Venkatesh and Morris (2000).

While there are conflicts in these research findings, constructs in the diffusion of innovation perspective have shown significant effects on the adoption of technology. Relative advantage and complexity are interchangeable with perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use in the technology acceptance model, respectively. Compatibility is related to the fit of a technology with prior experiences, while trialability and observability imply the availability of opportunities for relevant experiences. Compatibility, trialability and observability are expected to affect the constructs in the technology acceptance model. However, it should be noted that the effects diminish over time and by continuous experience after initial adoption (Karahanna et al., 1999).

## Research model and hypotheses

## Research model

The technology acceptance model is parsimonious and explains the relationships between perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use and attitude very well, as has been empirically supported in much previous research (Davis, 1989; Adams et al., 1992; Taylor and Todd, 1995; Agarwal and Prasad, 1999; Lou et al., 2000). This study’s research model incorporates the notion of resource constraints and is based on the research of Mathieson et al. (2001). Mathieson et al. (2001) noted that this model should not outperform the technology acceptance model in situations where there were no significant resource limitations. However, individuals often face resource constraints in practice. In the context of broadband Internet adoption, individuals should have appropriate skills, financial resources and time. There is little difference between the skills needed for using a modem connection over plain old telephone service networks and broadband connection to the Internet. However, when an individual uses the broadband Internet, he or she can have access to additional information and services such as video on demand, high-quality MP3 digital music, broadcasting services and other multi-media services. An individual may also need different skills and experience in order to use or to take advantage of the broadband Internet connection. Time is also required for a user to learn how to set up the system and what he or she can access via the broadband Internet.

Research works on the role of compatibility or compatible experiences in technology adoption, including Taylor and Todd (1995), Karahanna et al. (1999), Venkatesh and Davis (2000) and Chau and Hu (2001), have shown that attitude is becoming increasingly important over time and by continuous experience in determining the technology usage behaviour. Thus, this research used attitude as the dependent variable, tried to identify factors that affect attitude and tried to understand the relations between these constructs and attitude.

This research model incorporated the fit of personal experiences on the Internet with the technology and opportunities for experiencing broadband Internet. The diffusion of innovation model provides us with such constructs as compatibility, trialability and observability. These are related to experiences of the Internet and/or opportunities for experiencing broadband Internet before adoption. It is expected that these constructs will affect attitudes towards using broadband Internet. As Taylor and Todd (1995) and Chau and Hu (2001) suggested, it is reasonable to think that these experience-related constructs directly affect perceived ease of use (as opposed to complexity in the diffusion of innovation perspective) and perceived usefulness (relative benefit in the diffusion of innovation perspective) as external variables and indirectly influence attitude through perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness. While Chau and Hu (2001) and Agarwal et al. (2000) showed that the effect of compatibility on perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use is not consistent, it is expected that these constructs will have effects on perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use and perceived resources in broadband technology use. The research model in Figure 4 demonstrates this perspective.

The second one is based on the extended technology acceptance model, which incorporates perceived resources as a subset of perceived behavioural control in the theory of planned behaviour.

## Internet experience and opportunities for experiencing broadband Internet

Compatibility is defined as the degree to which an innovation is perceived as consistent with the existing values, past experiences and needs of potential adopters (Rogers, 1983). Broadband Internet assumes much of the same traits of dial-up access and its speed is much faster than earlier Internet access technology. Due to this higher bandwidth, various kinds of services, including audio and video data transmissions, are available on the new infrastructure.

Agarwal and Prasad (1999) asserted a positive relationship between an individual’s prior compatible experiences and the new information technology acceptance. They found that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use non-trivially contributed in mediating the effects of compatibility to attitude. However, users of the prior technology would not adopt a new technology just because it is compatible. It is reasonable to think that compatibility by itself does not lead to technological innovation. Perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use mediate the effects of compatibility to attitude as the technology acceptance model maintains. As mentioned, Chau and Hu (2001) and Agarwal et al. (2000) included compatibility in their research and hypothesized that compatibility affected perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. However, their results were not consistent.

When an individual has prior compatible experiences, he or she is in a better position to perceive the usefulness of new technology if he or she found the previous technology to be useful. Prior compatible experiences also help an individual turn to the new technology with ease. In the context of broadband adoption, it is expected that compatibility will affect perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use positively.

H<sub>1</sub>: Compatibility has a positive influence on perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use and perceived resources.

By experiencing broadband Internet technology or by being exposed to the benefits of the service indirectly before its subscription, one can build positive perceptions and attitudes about the technology. Trialability and observability are related to the opportunities for experiencing broadband Internet before adoption and use. Observability consists of both visibility and result demonstrability, with visibility defined as the degree to which the innovation is visible and result demonstrability defined as the degree to which the results of adopting/ using the innovation are observable and communicable to others (Rogers, 1983). If a person has opportunities to be exposed to the benefits of broadband Internet before adoption, then he or she has a better chance of understanding its usefulness. Positive exploratory experiences before the adoption of broadband Internet also help individuals to obtain knowledge on how to use it and adopt it with less effort and time.

## The extended technology acceptance model: the technology acceptance model incorporating resource constraint

The technology acceptance model has a limitation in that it assumes usage is volitional, that is there are no barriers that would prevent an individual from using an information system if he or she chose to do so. By adding perceived user resources to the model, Mathieson et al. (2001) extended the technology acceptance model. They defined that perceived resources are the extent to which an individual believes that he or she has the personal and organizational resources needed for using an information technology. These resources are not an attribute of a system alone, but include skills, hardware, software, money, documentation, data, human assistance and time. They also grouped resource-related variables from the information systems literature into four categories such as user attributes, support from others, system attributes and general control-related attributes. As in perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use, compatible experience affects perceived resources positively because experienced Internet users generally have more skills easily turned into the use of broadband Internet. Experiences before the adoption of broadband Internet also help a person obtain knowledge on how to use and adopt it with less effort and time.

H<sub>5</sub>: Perceived resources have a positive influence on perceived ease of use.

## Research method

## Measurement

The survey instruments were adopted from previous research (Davis, 1989; Karahanna et al., 1999; Mathieson et al., 2001) that showed evidence of the reliability and validity of each measurement. The measurement instruments of the constructs in the technology acceptance model, theory of planned behaviour and diffusion of innovation perspective have long been used in previous research (Rogers, 1983; Zanna and Rempel, 1988; Davis, 1989; Taylor and Todd, 1995; Morris and Dillon, 1997; Karahanna et al., 1999; Chang and Cheung, 2001; Mathieson et al., 2001) and shown high reliability and validity and it was reasonable to employ these instruments in this study.

The operationalization of the constructs followed the previous research (Davis, 1989; Karahanna et al., 1999; Mathieson et al., 2001) and each construct is described in Table 2.

## Sampling and questionnaire distribution

The samples were randomly chosen from universities, firms and households in order to reduce sampling bias caused by demographic factors such as sex, age, education and occupation. The authors attempted to obtain samples covering various demographic backgrounds (see Table 3). Four hundred questionnaires, in paper or electronic format, were distributed and a total of 211 questionnaires (response rate 52.75%) were collected. One hundred and fifty-seven questionnaires were used in the analysis after excluding those with too many missing values or inconsistent responses. The samples included university students, white- and blue-collar workers, businessmen and housewives.

The age of the samples ranged from under 20 years to over 40 years and the mean age was 25 years. The age of over 70% of the sample was between 20 and 30 years. The gender ratio was 48.4:51.6 (male: female). Of the samples, 72% had between 14 and 16 years of education. This category included third- and fourth-year university students, as well as university graduates. Approximately two-thirds of them had used the Internet and broadband Internet for over 3 years.

## Results

## Reliability and validity of the measurement

The reliability analysis verified the precision of the survey instrument and the internal consistency of the measure. Cronbach’s a is widely used for testing the internal consistency of the measurement and increasing the precision of the measurement instrument by precluding the obstructive items from the measurement. Table 4 shows the results of the Cronbach’s a test.

As shown in Table 4, the test moderately satisfied the criterion of reliability.

In order to verify the construct validity (convergent and discriminant), confirmatory factor analysis was conducted and the results are shown in Tables 5 and 6 (coefficients less than 0.5 were suppressed).

The results of the confirmatory factor analysis showed that the items were properly loaded on each factor as expected, except two items used for measuring compatibility and result demonstrability (COM3 and RD4). Those items were excluded from the analysis.

The perceived usefulness explained 27.5% of all the variances and there were many items correlated with perceived usefulness. As any multiple constructs identified were expected to be correlated rather than orthogonal, both Oblim and Varimax rotation methods were used. Because the results were the same the Varimax rotation method was used. This means that the inter-item correlations were high and there were many items that correlated with other factors although the discriminant validity of the constructs existed. Each construct could be distinguished at eigenvalues over 1.0, but there were more items that significantly explained each construct than the number of items used for measuring each construct.

## Analysis

Structural equation modelling permits the direct and indirect effects of each construct from the perspective of the entire model to be found. Regression analysis can replace structural equation modelling, but only at the cost of failing to capture the fitness of the entire model. As noted, the inter-item correlations were high and there were more exogenous variables (measurement items) that significantly explained the endogenous variable (construct) than the number of exogenous variables originally used for measuring that construct. In this condition, the fitness of the original model would have been poor if not manipulated in other ways.

## Discussion

Figure 5 depicts the results of the research. The extended technology acceptance model incorporating the notion of resource constraint was proven to be relevant and applicable to the broadband adoption decision. If people find it useful to use broadband Internet and they are able to use it with ease, then they have a positive attitude towards using broadband Internet.

Compatibility, one of the innovation attributes included in the research, appeared to have a direct and strong influence on perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use and perceived resources. Most Koreans have experience using the Internet before they adopt and use broadband Internet. Broadband Internet provides not only faster speed but also compatibility with previous Internet access technology, which leads to personal perceptions of usefulness, ease of use, resources and, eventually, wide penetration of broadband Internet. Previous experience of the Internet that is compatible with the new version of the Internet make one perceive the latter to be more useful and easier to use and, thus, make the user more prepared for the new technology. Most people were already aware of what to do with the Internet: indeed, the Internet permeated their life and work. They were therefore ready to accept the new but compatible services that broadband Internet offered.

The effects of other innovation attributes, such as visibility and result demonstrability, were partially supported and the effects of trialability were not even found. This means that users’ perceived ease of use or usefulness are independent from trialability of this technology. This might mean that hands-on trial is not a necessary step in adopting broadband access because most users are already experienced with similar technology. In addition, the benefit of a trial might have subsided or become minimal in the face of secondary data and information such as brand recognition of service providers, advertisements and peer group’s experiences. All these forms of information are reliable and the costs of accessing these are negligible. Even if there are more than 20 000 Internet cafés and many computer kiosks with broadband access in public spaces through which people can experience broadband Internet before they make a decision to subscribe to the service, the trialability offered by these cafés turned out not to be a reason why Koreans subscribed to broadband access at their home or at work. The contribution of these cafés and kiosks on Internet adoption and use might lie rather on enhancing the perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness of the Internet.

However, the role of opportunities for experiencing new services in the context of technology adoption should not be neglected. This aspect is particularly true when the new technology is not compatible with previous ones or potential users of the technology do not recognize the need for it. According to the results of the study, opportunities should be given for allowing potential users to understand the new technology so that they can discover its usefulness. While experiencing the new technology, people can perceive the ease of use and, further, they can make themselves open to and ready for adopting the technology under consideration.

The attributes from the diffusion of innovation theory used in this research can be grouped into prior compatible experiences and opportunities for experience. The results of the research generally support the idea that compatible experiences and opportunities for experiencing the technology in question affect perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use and perceived resources, that is these attributes affect the formation of positive attitudes towards the technology by making people feel comfortable and ready in adopting it.

The following are some of the limitations of this research.

First, the research model did not include behaviour or behavioural intentions. There are controversies on the relationship between attitude and behaviour itself. Because most people in Korea are users of broadband Internet, it was not possible to test the relationship between attitude and behaviour. One plausible approach to solving the problem is cross-cultural research. Crosscultural research would also provide opportunities for analysing the effects of cultural differences in technology adoption.

Secondly, the regression analyses were conducted in order to determine the effects of each construct. Structural equation modelling, which shows the effects of each construct from the perspective of the entire model, may also be used. It is recommended that this method be used in further research, along with a refinement of the research model.

Thirdly, the impact of changes in perception and attitudes on the technology usage over a long period of time were not measured and studied in this research. It was considered that the time-lag or delay between initial adoption and this survey may have led to the result that some relations between the innovation attributes and attitudes would not have been confirmed in this research, that is the adopters of a technology may consider innovation constructs related to initial adoption (excluding compatibility) less and less important as the experience of using the technology builds. This problem can be studied by testing research hypotheses using research subject groups with different lengths of usage of the technology.

## Conclusion

The overwhelming rate of broadband Internet adoption in Korea is a rare incident even among developed countries. Korea thus serves as an interesting case for those countries where governments emphasize greater productivity through the use of various information technologies. Previous research (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2001; Lee et al., 2003) on this issue has often approached the case from macro-level structura perspectives such as the density of population, competition in the market and government policies. However, this provides limited implications to companies providing broadband access services and offers only a partial description of the phenomenon.

This research aimed to find personal factors affecting broadband subscription and use, combining different views on the adoption and diffusion of technology. In order to understand the dynamics of technology adoption, attention needs to be paid to how individuals reach a decision to adopt it. The theory of planned behaviour, technology acceptance model and diffusion of innovation model provide us with a firm background with which to investigate the adoption behaviour of individuals.

This research shows that some of the innovation attributes in the diffusion of innovation perspective (compatibility, observability and trialability) can be viewed as related to experience prior to adoption of a technology. These generally affect perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use and perceived resources.

The adoption or diffusion of broadband Internet access was analysed using various technology adoption and innovation diffusion models. People adopt broadband Internet because it is useful and easy and because the resources it requires are at a tolerable level. Compatible experiences of broadband Internet and the opportunities for experiencing it lead users to perceive its usefulness, ease of use and capabilities.

## Adoption of broadband Internet in Korea

By incorporating various views and theories on technology adoption and innovation diffusion, this research suggests a model for explaining factors such as attitude and experience in using broadband Internet access. Innovation attributes have been regarded as constructs affecting the adoption and diffusion of a technology, while the relationship of these constructs with adoption and usage has not been extensively studied. The findings from this research show that compatibility, observability and trialability are antecedents to relative advantage (perceived usefulness) and complexity (perceived ease of use). This shows a relationship between the innovation attributes and also the structure of innovation. Structural and environmental conditions still have a significant influence on the adoption of broadband Internet. However, judging from the results of this research, it is clear that efforts should also be made to expand the base of experienced customers for broadband Internet adoption. Providing compatible or direct experiences of broadband Internet should also be included in government policies and firms’ strategies.
