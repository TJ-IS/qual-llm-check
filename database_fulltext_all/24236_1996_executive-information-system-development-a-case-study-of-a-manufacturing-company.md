# Executive information system development: a case study of a manufacturing company

JOE NANDHAKUMAR

Department of Accounting and Management Science, University of Southampton, Southampton SO17 1Bf, UK

Despite the widespread adoption of Executive Information Systems (EIS) and their increasing importance in organizations, the process of their development is not well understood. The mainstream EIS literature tends to report success stories of EIS in the organizations studied and attribute this to a pre-planned rational process of origin and design of EIS. In this paper, a case study of the EIS development process in a manufacturing company is used to critique this rational view and to illustrate the applicability of an alternative perspective based on an organizational behavioural model. This paper suggests that greater recognition of the social nature of the process of IS development is necessary to understand how systems projects may be better managed.

## Introduction

Since Executive Information Systems (EIS) were first identified by Rockart and Treacy (1982) in the early 1980s, many reports have appeared in the business press claiming that EIS are now successfully providing computer support for top executives. Many authors such as Watson et al. (1991), Fitzgerald (1992) and Turban and Walls (1995) have sought to define EIS as computer-based IS which provide senior managers with data relevant to their work. Despite the widespread adoption of EIS (Rainer and Watson, 1995) and their increasing importance in organizations, we have only limited understanding of how EIS emerge within organizations and how they are developed.

## Character of mainstream EIS research

Although there has been a growing number of studies providing descriptions of the process of EIS development in specific organizations, many of these studies provide only a narrow view of the origin, design and use of EIS. Such descriptive papers have tended to report success stories of EIS in the organizations studied and attribute this to a rational process of origin and design of EIS. For example, Cottrell and Rapley (1991) described the effectiveness of EIS at British Airways as their ability in reporting key performance indicators to executives and providing measures of critical success factors. Cottrell and Rapley argued that EIS success at British Airways was attributable to the origin resulting from the initiative and support of the chief executive and to the design process employing prototyping, evolutionary approaches. Similar views of the EIS development process are presented in much of the descriptive literature (e.g. Houdeshel and Watson, 1987; Rockart and DeLong, 1988; Meiklejohn and Harvey, 1989; Barrow, 1990; Popovich, 1992).

Cross-sectional surveys (e.g. Watson et al., 1991; Fitzgerald. 1992; Watson et al., 1992; Watson et al., 1995) have also tended to reinforce these views of EIS in organizations. For example, Watson et al. (1992) implied that there were well defined measures of effectiveness through value added measures such as timely data and better presentation. They attribute the 'effectiveness' of EIS to the presence of high level planning or pressures and to a prototyping process of design. Much of the EIS literature therefore tends to present a formal/rational view of the EIS development process and implies:

\- the origin of EIS as resulting from a high level initiative or plan;

\- the design and implementation of EIS as following a well conceived sequence; and

\- the outcome of implementing EIS as their effectiveness in providing timely data to executive users.

The mainstream research on EIS development therefore tends to assume a form of deterministic view, which suggests that EIS are ‘designed’ to embed preset goals and cause predicted ‘impacts’. Even if the actual practice does not match this view, this simplistic rational view may dominate the mental models of practitioners. In other words, the designers and users may assume a clear view that the EIS itself will behave to the rule, despite the intrinsic complexity of these systems.

## Social process perspective of IS development

This formal/rational view of the development process of IS has also been adopted in much of the IS literature. Many authors such as King and Scacchi, 1982; King and Kraemer, 1986; Jones, 1991; Orlikowski and Robey, 1991; Kling, 1992; Walsham, 1993, criticized this view as providing only limited insights into IS development practices. Compared with the traditional formal/rational view, these authors suggested a social perspective of IS development process. For example Kling and Scacchi (1982) argued that the origin, development and use of computing systems was rarely a pre-planned rational process in practice, but rather is embedded in ongoing complex social processes.

## Origin

Kling and Scacchi (1982) proposed that participants produce computing innovations such as new IS, influenced by social conditions and technological configurations and were limited by existing computing arrangements. Emergence of a new system is often also a political process as participants seek to innovate their computing arrangements in an attempt to alter existing power relations. Kling and Scacchi (1982) therefore argued that a new computing innovation was not appropriate to a setting a priori, but was shaped by the local computing arrangements, how participants embedded their work in it, and by what they perceived as advantages of new resource distributions.

## Design

The design process of IS is not a standalone activity which can be planned and controlled, rather designers' activities are embedded in a stream of ongoing activities in the social and organizational context (Walsham, 1993). Walsham argued that the systems design involves social processes which vary widely depending on participants' views of many aspects of the social and organizational context, such as various norms and values enacted by the IS designers and management.

Although a few EIS researchers take account of the context of EIS development (e.g. Watson et al., 1991), they view the social and organizational context in terms of a few formal relationships by ignoring the complexity of social relations. Some rich analysis of context such as 'web models' (Kling and Scacchi, 1982; Kling, 1992) however may be found in the IS literature. These models assume broad boundaries around IS and examine how their development and use depend upon a social context. The context was defined in this model by emphasizing the social relations between the participants, the infrastructure available for support and the history of commitments made in developing and using related computer-based technologies.

## Effectiveness

IS literature views effectiveness in four ways: IS may fulfil expectations; meet pre-set goals; be used by users; or be designed in time and cost (Lyytinen and Hirschheim, 1987). Orlikowski (1992) however argued that systems intentionally developed to achieve some pre-set goals such as timely data may be used to meet some other official purposes that were not intended and may also have unintended consequences such as disturbing workflow and creating delays. This indicates the difficulties in representing IS effectiveness through pre-defined constructs.

While the formal/rational view is widespread in IS research, the above examples illustrate that this view is an inadequate basis for understanding the origin, development and use of computing systems which is embedded in ongoing complex social processes. The social process perspective of IS development may therefore be seen as providing an interesting and more useful perspective for investigating IS phenomena.

In order to understand the influences of different organizational and historical contexts within which IS are developed and used, a richer description of the process of EIS development through in-depth studies of practice is required. Despite the development of more useful contextual models such as 'web models' and theoretical concepts in IS literature, the research on EIS however fails to draw on these concepts. The limitations of the mainstream EIS research indicate that in-depth examinations of the development process of EIS in organizations are needed to explore: how EIS emerge within organizations; how these are developed; and what counts as their 'success'.

In this paper, a rich case description of the EIS development process in a large manufacturing company is used to critique the formal/rational view predominantly adopted by the mainstream EIS research and to illustrate the applicability of an alternative perspective based on an organizational behavioural model. This however should not be considered as a refutation of the mainstream EIS literature, but rather as an illustration of the additional insights into the EIS development process that could be gained by applying a social process perspective. First, a case description is presented to describe the chronology of events and activities relating to EIS development and their social and historical context. The case study is then analysed in terms of the social process perspective of IS development to explore the origin, development and effectiveness of EIS. The rich insights gained from this study are finally used to outline some implications for practice.

## EIS development in a manufacturing company

## Research approach

The research method employed in this study involves describing and analysing an in-depth case study to develop rich insights of IS phenomena and specific implications. This approach therefore fits within the interpretative case study approach to IS research advocated by Walsham (1995). It does not aim to produce information suitable for conventional statistical analysis and hypothesis testing, but rather adopts an interpretative epistemology (see, e.g., Walsham and Waema, 1994) that seeks to understand the complex social process of IS development practice.

The case description is based on a three-month field study carried out at MC Group (a pseudonym) – a large manufacturing company. Several days each month were spent with the EIS development team members in their office full time, observing their daily activities and engaging in conversations. Data from the field notes were then written up into a case study describing the EIS development process and important aspects of the real-life context. The case study is not presented in this paper as an objective account of EIS development process at MC Group, but rather as an interpretation of events in terms of EIS development process. This convention to write up qualitative data has increasingly been used by many IS researchers (see, e.g. Walsham, 1995) to develop what Geertz (1973) termed ‘thick descriptions’ of the research context.

It would also be appropriate to reflect on the limitations, of our own approach. In contrast to some field studies such as by Curtis et al. (1988), the focus was on an in-depth study of a single group rather than a cross-section of several projects. While this enabled insights to be gained into the complex interactions of the key players through closer observation of the behaviour of the team members, it also meant that care to avoid generalizations from this single case should be taken. As Walsham (1995) argued, the generalizations from this research based on a single case study should be seen as explanations of EIS development process based on an empirical interpretative research in MC Group. This should provide only a basis for understanding similar phenomena in other settings, rather than enabling the prediction of behaviour in other contexts. On the other hand however Orlikowski and Baroudi (1991) argued that to understand IS phenomena which are context dependent, a single in-depth case study provided a more suitable basis to gain insights into the complex interactions of the key players than multiple case studies focusing on the surface.

## CASE STUDY: MC Group Headquarters

MC Group is a manufacturing company with hundreds of manufacturing centres in many countries around the world. MC Group Headquarters (GHQ), the site under study, formally controls the operations of the group's businesses around the world. This large multinational organization has a corporate structure with a clear management hierarchy of several layers of senior executives. At the top of its GHQ there is a traditional board of executive directors, each of whom has various functional responsibilities, accountable to the group chairman.

## Origin of EIS

The EIS concept began to evolve in GHQ in the early 1980s. The group chairman at that time wanted a system similar to a 'what-if' simulation model already used in the company. The chairman's concept was evaluated by staff in the GHQ Strategy Division but it was found that developing an 'easy-to-use' model for executives was not feasible with the software technology available at that time. This evaluation, however, resulted in some improvements to the data presentation carried out in the boardroom, such as introducing a graphical software package to improve the presentation and timeliness of graphs and charts.

By 1985, many specialist EIS software packages were becoming commercially available and GHQ bought one of them for producing graphs and reports. However, this EIS software was not put into use at that time because the manager who bought the software left the company and the others did not find the quality of the graphs as good as from the existing software. In the following year, further development work for boardroom presentations using the graphical software package was discontinued as the software vendors were taken over, so there was uncertainty about future support from the company. The EIS software was therefore reconsidered in early 1986 as a replacement for the graphical software package. Following this, the manager of GHQ information systems (GHQIS), who was one of the staff involved in the evaluation of the chairman's initial proposal, was sent on a two week training course with the EIS software vendors.

By mid 1986 a prototype system was developed by the GHQIS manager, initially consisting of four EIS projects: group's quarterly results, which were already presented using the existing graphical software; two other financial reports based on information the executives were currently using; and external daily news and stories which the EIS software enabled. During this development work, the GHQIS manager was assisted by staff in his division, the data providers' divisions and by a newly-appointed industrial trainee.

The prototype developed was tried out by the executive support staff and senior data provider managers who were known to the GHQIS manager personally through previous contacts. A few initial users emerged among them. By the end of 1986, the prototype was further improved based on the initial users' comments and it was formally installed for them. To deal with the increasing comments and suggestions from the EIS users, a young female analyst who had been dealing with telephone queries in the IT division joined the GHQIS manager and the industrial trainee, and they became known as the EIS team within the strategy formulation division.

While the new staff member and the trainee carried out full time development work, the GHQIS manager remained as the EIS team leader and also had other responsibilities. He reported to the divisional manager who was responsible for the GHQ information systems and strategies.

In this initial system, both the EIS software and the data were mainly stationed centrally in the company mainframe, which provided a cooperative processing system architecture enabling all users to view the same data. The PC-based software managed a part of the communication and menuing facilities and provided a touch screen colour graphical screen through which the executives could access and view the central data. Executives therefore operated the EIS to display required data by pressing a 'button' with the appropriate data label from the menu options on the touch sensitive screen.

## Spread of EIS

The original EIS projects were continuously amended in response to users' requests and suggestions throughout the year. During 1987 some of the prototypes were redeveloped as it was not possible to amend the existing systems any further. However the users, mainly executive support staff, found the EIS interesting and it was claimed that the competitor news service provided by the EIS helped an acquisition made by the MC Group. By that time user numbers had increased to more than ten, including a few senior executives from the main board.

Following the spread of EIS stories, many other executives wanted to have the EIS implemented for them during 1987 and thus the number of users increased. The number of requests and suggestions for new data also increased so the number of projects started to grow. The spread of the EIS in the strategy division interested many managers as executive support staff began to use a single EIS database where some of the strategy data began to accumulate. Thus it was planned to expand this EIS database to include all strategy data for improving timeliness and consistency. Analysts and data providers who had worked on the initial spreadsheet models for executive support staff carried out development work for this EIS project in association with the EIS team.

As more corporate information was included in the EIS, many data providers and analysts in the lower management levels requested EIS PCs to have access to some of their divisional information. During 1988, therefore, the EIS spread to many users down the management hierarchy. The pattern of use also varied; some users, mostly senior executives, used the summary data and others used the detailed version of divisional data.

By 1989, the original plan to restrict the EIS to a few senior executives in the GHQ was abandoned as EIS spread to other businesses within the group. One reason for this was that many senior executives from the GHQ took their EIS PC with them when they moved on to a senior position in other businesses; some of which were overseas. A few businesses expanded the GHQ EIS locally to implement local information confined to their businesses instead of implementing a new EIS for themselves.

As the EIS expanded over many areas with many different users, the team members were under increasing pressure to deal with user support and development work. Therefore a staff member from the IT division joined the EIS team during 1989. The three analysts who developed models for the EIS projects in the strategy formulation division worked together with the EIS team as associate members of the team. These associate members also had other responsibilities in their division. In addition, the six staff members of the EIS team were joined occasionally by industrial trainees and analysts from other businesses in the MC Group during busy periods. Since the beginning of the EIS development, three different trainees were appointed in succession.

In early 1990 some reorganization was carried out to bring the EIS team under the control of the GHQ information services division, which was responsible to the director of finance and group information services. The divisional manager remained in the same position having more IT responsibilities. The EIS team remained the same and positions of systems manager and development manager were created from within the team. In mid 1992, the EIS divisional manager was replaced by a manager with a strong IS background from the IT division. Some concerns were thus raised among the team members at the future development strategy for the EIS. Under the previous divisional manager the EIS projects had been developed away from the rigid IS methodologies and had been closely linked with the strategy division.

The following description sets out the daily activities of the EIS team members during the period of study.

## Daily activities of EIS team members

## Obtaining user feedback

Since the early stages of EIS development, the systems manager had been interacting with the users to provide support for daily operations and to obtain feedback. This practice evolved into formal interviews with users which were carried out yearly to review the general usage of the EIS. These interviews, two to three appointments per day, were carried out by the systems manager during the less busy periods of the year. Of around 200 users, more than half were in the GHQ and only a small proportion were in foreign countries. Attempts were therefore made to see foreign users when the team members were abroad on other business, for example, while attending software conferences.

These interviews took different forms for different users. For example, for the senior executives, the systems manager gave a quick 'run through' of the system by showing them new information and features and getting them to select the menu buttons. For other executives they consisted of less formal discussion, also involving some demonstrations. The aim of these interviews was to: assess usefulness and usage of the EIS; identify any requests for information or amendments; and find out any problems in using the system. The systems manager claimed that these interviews consistently showed that the main users of the EIS were the support staff (who were senior managers), for senior executives could not do without the EIS. A few executives claimed that the EIS did not support their job but kept the system for other reasons such as to keep in touch with EIS for future jobs. Other users claimed that they could get more timely information from elsewhere, such as from their own division directly. Some executives used external news facilities provided by EIS to see cricket scores.

It was also found that measuring the data/file access gave a false impression of the 'usefulness' of the EIS, although a similar procedure was used by the IT division for this purpose. For example, many users looked at the 'news' in the EIS during a break in work but it was not essential to their work. Furthermore, many senior manager users accessed the EIS information indirectly, such as using their analysts' EIS PCs while discussing issues. It was therefore suggested to introduce a procedure of charging for file access in order to assess the real value of the EIS reports. One of the businesses in the group already charged their users on the basis of the number of reports accessed. Team members claimed, however, that a similar procedure would not suit GHQ. Initial discussions with GHQ users revealed that many users, for example the executive who kept the system for his future job, would discontinue use if charging was introduced.

The interviews frequently identified needs for new EIS information. For example, during an interview with one of the divisional managers, he requested the inclusion of some of his divisional performance information, which he claimed would be useful to other executives. Occasionally requests were made for amendments to the existing reports which led to revisions of the projects. Users very often commented on technical problems such as forgetting their password after changing it, problems in the hardware or software, minor problems with the screens and so on. Most of these problems could be put right during the interviews.

Based on the interviews, the systems manager prepared a report for each user mostly consisting of actions and also describing 'usefulness' and usage. Any technical problems which were sorted out were also recorded. The issues raised in the interview were discussed with the development manager and GHQIS manager; copies of this report were given to them and to interviewers to maintain openness. Most of the actions were taken promptly by the EIS team and investigations were carried out for major issues such as new projects. For example, one of the executives requested a facility similar to a 'news-flash' for all updated reports. It was planned to find out how many users wanted this, and the feasibility of this proposal was investigated. In case of inability to meet user requests for various reasons, it was reported to the user. The systems manager claimed that this helped to sustain users' confidence in the team to respond to their requests.

## Development activities

The development work was carried out mainly by the systems manager and development manager. Development of most of the new EIS projects commenced in response to users' requests, mostly from the senior executives in the data provider divisions. Occasionally the team included some new information temporarily based on 'hints' from users. Later this was included permanently in the EIS. For example, during the stock market 'crash' more share price information was provided and later this was integrated in the system as users found it helpful.

Although there were no standard procedure for EIS development imposed from the division (except for hardware and software procurement) the team had established ways of doing things in terms of screen layout, usage of colours and development procedures. For example, once a prototype was completed by the developer it was then presented to the data providers for formal approval before implementation.

Information required on EIS was also mostly available in the form of paper reports in the data provider divisions. In most cases the paper reports were used to develop the initial prototype. Interactions with the data provider division were normally limited to the senior level, so the prototype was developed by the team alone and then discussed with the data provider division. Normally the senior executives in the data provider divisions decided what was needed for the executives from their divisions. The team accepted the data providers' authority over the system and worked along with it.

Although it took on average four to five months before implementing a new prototype, in some cases the team got exceptionally slow response from the data providers. For example, a project relating to the sales division which had started six months earlier was still awaiting the data provider's response. There had been some structural changes in the division so they wanted to try out the new half yearly data in the system before releasing it. Similarly, another project got delayed by seven months as the data provider was off sick and then retired, so the response came from a new general manager who replaced him. On the other hand, the team sometimes got an exceptionally fast response, for example when the data providers liked the prototype at the first trial and started using it straight away.

## Support activities

As with most of the new project development, revision of existing projects was carried out in response to user requests. The major part of the work on project revision was to carry out amendments to reports as the data providers' needs changed. In addition to reported amendments, the development manager and systems manager normally carried out random checks to ensure that the EIS was running correctly. Most of the EIS reports were updated centrally by the development manager except for news and some other information, which were updated automatically. The data provider staff normally sent the EIS data in the form of spreadsheet files or text files to the EIS team. Some data providers carried out checks on their data before releasing it to the users. In this case, the team specified a time limit for holding onto these projects.

In addition to sorting out minor technical problems reported during user interviews, the EIS team carried out many activities to provide technical support to EIS users in response to user calls. For example, when the divisional manager had moved on to one of the businesses in the group, he had problems in accessing the EIS as his old user account was removed. He contacted GHQIS manager about it and he in turn discussed this with the development manager. It was found that the new user account allowed only restricted access to data. Similarly, the development manager received a message to reset a password as the user had changed his password and forgotten it.

Much of this work also involved assistance from staff in the technical support groups, such as the mainframe application support group and PC hardware engineers. These staff assisted the EIS team in everyday maintenance, for example resetting executives passwords, amending hardware and software problems, and upgrading software and PC hardware. For example, a problem with the alignment of touch screen was reported which was passed on to a member of the technical support group. They often visited the team for an informal chat or sent email through. In this way the EIS team maintained good working relationships with a few staff in the technical support groups.

The EIS software had been upgraded several times since its original introduction. The main EIS software was in the mainframe, so upgrading was carried out centrally over the weekends with assistance from the technical support groups. Any changes in the PC based software were normally carried out for the target users in the GHQ during the day while the executives were out at board meetings. The EIS team tested new software thoroughly before carrying out upgrading. However, during the most recent upgrading, it was decided to revert back to an older version as the new software was not preferred by the users. Occasionally the team waited until any planned upgrading of other systems facilities was carried out before carrying out EIS upgrading. For example, the team was holding back a new version of EIS software until the upgrading of all PCs and operating systems was carried out by the technical support group.

In order to safeguard the sensitive data in the EIS, an authorization procedure was maintained by the EIS team to allow EIS access to users. Each user had to get authorization in writing from general managers of the finance and strategy division to allow access to data on the EIS. To access the confidential data of the model the sponsors of each model had to give authorization. Normally businesses were not allowed to see each other's performance data.

## Routine activities

Within the team very few formal meetings took place to discuss development issues, so most of the interactions were informal. For example, the systems manager discussed issues raised at user interviews with the GHQIS manager, such as possible new EIS projects, and the development manager made comments on this. Following this, the GHQ manager asked the development manager to study the project and as a result actions were taken. These interactions were further facilitated by the teams' physical proximity through the arrangement of their offices, and by closer informal relationships through shared leisure activities during work such as working on crosswords together and having coffee or smoking breaks. The above activities of the EIS team varied over time between busy periods of development and data input when the annual financial reports became available, and quieter periods when systems upgrading and maintenance were carried out. The team members also spent a large portion of their time on office work, reading manuals and reviewing software.

## Case analysis and discussion

The evidence given in the case description suggests that the EIS development process in MC Group was embedded in a stream of social practices which even at this length can only be partially described. While providing an interpretation of events in terms of EIS development process, the case description isolates the EIS development process from other practices going on. Taking account of these limitations of the data, the case description may be analysed in terms of the three aspects discussed in the introduction: origin, development and effectiveness, by drawing on the concepts of the social process perspective of information systems development.

## Origin

In the introduction it was discussed that much of the EIS literature suggests that the EIS emerge from high-level management initiative or planning. The evidence from the case study however illustrates that the EIS emerged in the group headquarters not merely from some form of pressure or high level planning but evolved over time as part of the organizational practice of the key players. How this process may have occurred will be examined in this section through a detailed analysis of the behaviour of the key players, who were heavily influenced by many elements in the social and organizational context. These contextual elements may include various resources, organizational norms and rules.

At the beginning of the EIS programme the staff at strategy division were able to draw on the authority of the group chairman, who showed interest in EIS, to obtain the initial financial and technical resources for the project. The strategy division staff could use this power relationship to continue with this project, to gain unusual resources of authority and funds to develop EIS. Examples of this are obtaining data providers' cooperation, accessing confidential data, acquiring staff to set up the team and, during the later stages, ignoring most of the security recommendations made by internal auditors. During the early stages, the company's evolving practice that the boardroom presentations should be based on high quality colour graphics influenced the evaluation of the software. The strategy division staff therefore sought to experiment with graphic software packages for management reporting. The group chairman's views on data presentation influenced the staff to consider alternative packages. The strategy division's established practices on developing GHQ IS influenced the way the initial EIS prototype was developed and demonstrated. As the EIS team was formed, there were evolving norms on screen layout and usage of colours at the team level which influenced the way the prototype was set up. These contextual elements which influenced organizational practice of the key players were not limited to the immediate organizational context but also related to the wider environment. For example, the close relationship of the staff at strategy division with software vendors enabled them to try out new EIS packages when they became available on the market and to replace the old package. Major innovations in information presentation and retrieval technologies enabled the staff engaged in the design to try out attractive and easy-to-use interfaces for executives. The selection of the EIS software to replace a graphics package was largely influenced by their feeling of uncertainty about the continuing support from the previous software vendor when it was taken over by another company.

Once the system was installed there was no guarantee that the evolution could be managed according to plan. The team had little control over the evolution of EIS in MC Group. For example, the team could not restrict the EIS to a few senior executives, as originally planned, because of the influence of the support staff and the relocation of senior executives from the group headquarters to overseas who took their EIS PCs with them.

## Design

Traditionally, design activities have been assumed to be separable into distinct, well-defined phases which are carried out in a regular order. The case study however illustrated that designers' activities are embedded in a stream of ongoing activities in the organization. In terms of the social process perspective the systems design involved social processes which varied widely depending on key players' views of the elements of the social context, such as various norms and values enacted by these players. For example, the act of designing the first prototype screen drew on the GHQIS manager's knowledge of financial reports used by the executives. This was limited by his skills and authority, and sanctioned by the established design norms from strategy division.

The design process could therefore be seen as being influenced by a broad array of elements from the social and organizational context at the organizational level and at the wider environmental level. For example, design of the sales division EIS was influenced by norms at the team level such as the practice of obtaining formal approval from data providers before launching. At other levels there were influences from the divisional level, such as structural changes in one of the data providers, divisions which delayed the launching of an EIS project, and at the company level the relationship with users influenced report format. As Curtis et al. (1988) suggested these aspects of the social context may be seen as relating to different contextual layers such as design team, division, company and environment, as represented in a layered-behavioural model.

The design process was not a stand-alone activity, but designers' activities were interwoven with other activities of other key players causing dependency. For example, developers were dependent on support groups for resetting executive passwords and carrying out systems maintenance. The team members had to hold back a new version of EIS software for several months until system upgrading had been carried out by another group. Many other development activities were interwoven with activities of data providers and users. For example, the EIS team could not proceed with the work on sales division EIS while awaiting the data providers' response, which was delayed by activities within the data providers' division. The team members' activities were fitted into and interwoven with a stream of other social and official activities as they carried out design work between routine office work, social interactions with other team members and support groups, and discussion on feedback reports. These activities were also frequently interrupted, for example by phone calls to deal with queries.

The case study also illustrated the dual role of team members in performing development and support activities. A high proportion of EIS developers' time was spent on support activities and obtaining user feedback. Brooks (1975) also argued two decades ago that more than half of a typical systems designer's time is spent interacting formally with various people. EIS manuals continuously suggest however that clear planning, management and control of designers' tasks, and formal rules and regulations are needed to improve designers' productivity and to achieve timely systems development (see, e.g., Burkan, 1991).

Access to executive users was limited by MC group's strong hierarchical structures. The designers used mechanisms to get round the lack of access to users, such as appointing a female systems manager with experience in dealing with IS queries specifically to try to overcome this problem. Over time the practices used for getting user feedback became routinized, such as the yearly formal interviews with the users.

Although there were no detailed design plans, the case study illustrated that the team leader's senior position enabled him to exercise control over designers and their activities. For example, most task allocations were determined by the GHQIS manager, such as allocating the development manager to investigate the development of a new project. There was also physical surveillance over the EIS team members as their activities were clearly visible to their superior (the GHQIS manager) because of the physical arrangement of their offices. The designers however did not passively accept this surveillance, but found ways to be out of sight of their supervisor to avoid the likelihood of being watched by, for example taking time off to do cross-words. The designers were relatively autonomous in choosing the timing of their activities and the way in which they were carried out. For example, the development manager and systems manager carried out voluntary checks in their own time to ensure the EIS was running correctly. Some of their voluntary efforts later became useful EIS applications, such as the share price reports. The autonomous behaviour of the EIS team was regulated by organizational conventions and regulations. The designers' activities were greatly affected

## EIS development in a manufacturing company

by the organizational reporting cycles because much of the updating of reports was carried out by the team on behalf of the data providers. The reports determined their daily, weekly and monthly routines, illustrating the temporal divisions of tasks. For the EIS team members, the annual routine contained different sets of activities such as a busy period of updating reports when annual financial reports became available, project development when user interviews were carried out and other periods with more support activities. The EIS team therefore seemed to work to an informal timetable synchronizing their activities within the office and those of other offices within the organization.

## Effectiveness

Most of the EIS literature implies that there are well defined measures of effectiveness of EIS. The evidence from MC Group case illustrated however that the individual participant's view of what would constitute effective EIS varied between individuals in that company. Effectiveness was seen by the team members in terms of usefulness of the data provided by the EIS to the executives. Therefore they sought to introduce procedures to charge for report access. They found the procedure used by one of the other IT divisions to monitor systems access by users as a measure of effectiveness unacceptable. The differences in these perceptions may be related to each individual's viewpoint. For example, in the IT division, the participants' view of effectiveness related to systems view, whereas in the GHQ, the team members' view of effectiveness related to corporate strategy.

The case study also illustrated that executives chose to have EIS installed in case it would be useful in the future even if they found that EIS did not support their current work. Furthermore, executives took advantage of the functionality of EIS. An example of this was timeliness of data and use of the EIS in ways that were not originally planned, such as to see cricket scores. It thus remains questionable whether it is adequate to measure effectiveness by value added.

## The nature of EIS development process

It is clear from the case description and the above analysis that the development process of EIS at MC Group was rarely a pre-planned rational process, but rather embedded in ongoing complex social and organizational practices. Although the emergence, design and use/effectiveness of EIS were analyzed as separate aspects of these practices, these aspects are not independent of one another but are inter-connected in many ways. There were no clear 'cut-off' points between origin, design and use as EIS projects were constantly modified – particularly in the prototyping environment used in EIS development. The contextual elements which shaped the emergence, design and use were difficult to identify separately because each new project design was influenced by the contextual elements such as design standards which were created through development and use of previous projects. The separation of these elements in this analysis was therefore only for analytical purposes. To isolate the EIS development process from other current practices involved many simplifying assumptions as discussed above. Although there was a range of contextual elements influencing the EIS development process it would appear that there were temporal and spatial dimensions to the degree of influence from these elements. For example, at a certain time influences from certain elements such as the relationship with software vendors may be greater on design activities than influences from elements at the team level. Although certain elements in the immediate context might be seen as spatially closer to individuals than other elements relating to the wider environment, this 'distance' did not appear to determine the strength of influences all the time. It is not clear whether such distance is mainly based on the key players' perceptions or whether it had a profound empirical basis. More theoretical development would appear to be needed to gain better understanding of these issues.

The final issue highlighted in the analysis was the team members' contribution to reinforcing the social context. The daily activities of the key players were not only influenced by the social context, but the individual team members also shaped their context. For example, many of the design norms and rules drawn on by the EIS team to get the EIS formally accepted in MC Group also helped to reproduce them. In terms of Giddens Structuration Theory (Giddens, 1984) we may therefore see that the activities of the EIS team helped to reinforce the social context (Jones and Nandhakumr, 1993).

## Conclusions

This paper has provided a rich description of the EIS development process in a specific context and illustrated the insights into the development process that could be gained from adopting a social perspective of IS development and employing an interpretative case study method. By applying the interpretations developed in this study to the EIS literature, the empirical understanding gained of the origin, development and effectiveness of EIS has been illustrated.

It has been illustrated that EIS emerged not merely from some form of pressure or high level planning as

EIS literature typically assumes, but was heavily influenced by many elements at various levels of social context such as technological advances, cultural norms, resource allocation and power relations. The EIS literature which recommends steps for designing successful EIS views the EIS development process as consisting of a pre-planned rational process with sequential stages. The origin, development and evaluation of EIS was found to be embedded in the ongoing organizational practices. The development activities were fitted into, and interwoven with, the streams of other activities.

Most of the EIS literature implies that there are well defined measures of effectiveness of EIS through value added measures such as timely data and better presentation. It was found however that the individual participant's view of what would constitute effective EIS varied between different divisions in the company. The differences in these perceptions on effectiveness may not just be related to the division, but to the individual's viewpoint. Further, the real reasons for requiring EIS may not be the same as the official ones. Users may take advantage of the functionality of EIS to use EIS in ways that were not originally envisaged, as has been illustrated. This means that effectiveness cannot be assumed to be objective and hence capable of being represented by measures and constructs, but needs to be understood in terms of value system of the participants.

The theoretical insights developed on the social process of origin, design and EIS use can be of value to other researchers in EIS development and IS development in general, as a basis for further investigation. In addition, the study period in MC Group constituted a relatively small portion of the time of the development process and hence the description, even at this detail, only provides a partial picture. Further studies of EIS development process and theoretical development are therefore required to increase understanding of the origin, design and use of EIS in practice. Although EIS are typical of IS currently developed in organizations, future studies could investigate whether systems developed purely for users who are not executives have different issues associated with them and whether the current findings are really typical.

It is thought the paper will also be of interest to practitioners involved in developing and managing EIS projects. The social process view of EIS development adopted in this paper may be of value to project managers in broadening their understanding on the development process and in interpreting and applying guidelines provided in the EIS and IS literature. Many surveys have indicated that determining executives' needs is the most problematic area of development.

Most of the recommendations and guidelines provided in the literature for elicitation of executives' information needs, such as formal CSF sessions, however, largely ignore the complexity of the social relations affecting the developers. It was illustrated here that some aspects of social structures such as hierarchical norms largely prevented direct interactions with executives in the organization studied and this also led to a lack of user feedback. These aspects of social context are different in each organization so the generalized guidelines provided in the literature have only limited use for elicitation of executives' information needs.
