# Group problem solving and decision making: an investigation of the process and the supporting technology

PAT FINNEGAN

University College Cork, Ireland

LYNDA O'MAHONY

Apex Systems, Hinckley, Leicestershire

A major problem facing the development of technological support for groups is that many studies investigate group technology in isolation from an analysis of group activity, or focus only on discrete aspects of the process. This paper presents the findings of a study that investigated the nature and extent of group decision making and the use of group supporting technology in Irish organizations. The paper presents a model of group decision making and examines group processes and activities as well as the use of technology at each stage. The findings indicate that group decision making is a widespread phenomenon, which was found to be far more complicated than individual decision making. However, groups were found to need a great deal of control and coordination to enable members to collaborate effectively. Nevertheless, group decision making was found to receive little technological support. The available technology provided only low level support which mainly focused on supporting the asynchronous work of groups.

## Introduction

Competitive pressures in the business environment are forcing organizations to reshape their company structures from hierarchical structures to a flatter, fluid, team based organizations (Leavitt and Whistler, 1958; Drucker, 1988; Tapscott and Caston, 1993). Key areas of importance in the flatter organization are the management of company interdependence, the coordination of efforts, and the timely relay and retrieval of information (Rockart and Short, 1989; Safizadeh, 1991; Ching et al., 1992). This require organizations to rely heavily on the collaborative efforts of their employees (Handy, 1995). Groups have been proposed as the best means by which the organization can manage interdependencies and coordinate collaborative efforts (Lawrence and Lorch, 1967; Galbraith, 1977; Shea and Guzzo, 1987; Johansen, 1988).

The use of groups in such roles raises certain issues. Decision making in groups is considered more complex, as the group has the added burden of increased levels of communication, coordination and collaboration (Nour and Yen, 1992). In addition, the decision making process usually takes longer (Bullen and Johansen, 1988; Nour and Yen, 1992). It has been proposed that the productivity and speed of decision making groups could be increased if decision making processes were supported using computer-based technologies (DeSanctis and Gallupe, 1987). However, in developing supporting tools for group decision making, knowledge is needed about the area of group decision making, and this information is lacking in a form that is comprehensive and integrated, combining group processes with technological possibilities.

This paper presents the findings of a study which examined group decision making and the supporting technologies used in Irish companies. The study consisted of a postal survey of current practices, which was followed up by case studies of the decision making process. In addition to examining current practice, the authors present a model of group decision making. The nature of group activity, communication, collaboration, cooperation and coordination at each stage is documented, as well as the supporting technologies used. The study shows that while group decision making is widely practised in the companies studied, there are poor levels of supporting technology. Further examination revealed that the technology was mainly used to facilitate communication, and that that use of technology was virtually non-existent at important stages of the decision making process. The study showed that while group support technology is useful for facilitating many activities around the problem solving task, groups prefer face-to-face meetings with little or no computer support during the evaluation and choice of alternatives.

## Group decision making and problem solving

A decision making group is defined as two or more people who are jointly responsible for detecting a problem, elaborating on the nature of the problem, generating possible solutions, evaluating potential solutions and implementing solutions (DeSanctis and Gallupe, 1987). In this situation, problem solving and decision making are referred to in the same context. A problem refers to a gap between an actual and a desired result (Huber, 1980) and problem solving is concerned with bridging that gap. Therefore, decisions or choices can be thought of as specific and discrete actions involved in closing this gap (Steers et al., 1985) and thus solving the problem.

Much of the research on group problem solving is based on the process of individual decision making (Simon, 1960; Harrison, 1975; Bass 1983), and assumes that this process will be similar for groups (Mitchell and Larson, 1987). In addition, the literature regarding group decision making focuses on the composition of the group (Harrison, 1975; Steers et al., 1985; Mitchell and Larson, 1987), the variables that affect this (Mitchell and Larson, 1987; Steers et al., 1985), and the consequences for group decision processes and outcomes (Turoff and Hiltz, 1982; Pinsonneault and Kraemer, 1989; Ching et al., 1992; Cole and Nast-Cole, 1992).

In group decision making a distinction needs to be made between the decision making steps and the process by which they are achieved. While the steps involved in the decision making process are important, the means by which the group progresses from problem realization to choice is through the communication of information and ideas, as well as the collaboration of group members. Collaboration, the act of working together in the group, is achieved by coordinating the work of the individual workers (Malone and Crowston, 1990) who cooperate in order to achieve a solution to a problem. More importantly, the communication process is believed to be the key in turning an ill-structured problem into a semi-structured one (Turoff and Hiltz, 1982). However, there can be a certain amount of ambiguity associated with human communication (Nagasundaram and Wagner, 1992), so communication between the sender and receiver(s) is only considered effective when both parties have a common understanding of the issue (Cole and Nast-Cole, 1992). Therefore communication, collaboration, cooperation and coordination are considered vital factors in the group decision making process (Pinsonneault and Kraemer, 1989) and have become the focus of technological support.

Most technologies that aim to support decision making processes adopt a rational view of the decision making process. The rational model is the best-developed and most internally consistent model available. However, this viewpoint is limited as s descriptor of 'real-world' decision making, as illustrated by the failure of Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS) (Kraemer and King, 1988; Jones, 1994). Problem solving is an iterative process which involves continuous redefinition of the problem. Therefore computer assistance must support the groups creative search process and it must support continuous redefinition of the problem situation, thus aiding the whole decision making process (Broome and Chen, 1992).

Groupware and Computer Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW), through various commercial manifestations, sought to expand the area of support provided to groups beyond the area of GDSS by developing technologies to support the collaboration, communication and coordination efforts of groups (Davenport and Travica, 1995). However, these tools were based on assumptions about group activity held by researchers and developers (Bullen and Bennett, 1990) so that the foundations of Groupware and CSCW were not based on studying what groups actually do in organizations and how these groups make decisions in real world environments. Groupware tools have also fallen short of their expectations (Grudin, 1988), and the lack of Groupware acceptance in the past has been explained as being due to what Ellis et al. (1992) called 'designer's naive assumptions about the use of the technology' in the business environment. These technologies are based on what designers assume groups are doing and this may not provide the type of support needed. Ellis et al. (1992), along with Grudin (1988), believed that this could be rectified if designers were to look at group decision making within the company environment. It is believed that not enough attention is being paid to the examination of the basic procedures of group work, and how technology might enhance this (Bullen and Bennett, 1990), as it is often easier to build technologies that support the decision making process rather than describing the nature of decision making (Kraemer and King, 1988). This leads to a technology orientation taking preference over cognitive and management science in the development of group supporting technology.

There is therefore a need to coordinate the development of the whole decision making environment and this should include an examination of the technology. It is proposed that electronic support for group decision making should proceed in an inter-disciplinary manner. Its development should be integrated with developments in other disciplines such as management science, rather than just merely considering them (Ellis et al., 1992). However this will not be easy, as advances

It is important that the group decision making process should be understood as organizational demands call for systems that will integrate and support this whole process. If both disciplines can be integrated, a holistic view of the area can be obtained, allowing developments in both areas to proceed in a balanced manner. Much research in the area of group supporting technologies has mostly been experimentally based (Gray, 1987; Lewis, 1987; Dennis et al., 1990; Smith and Vanecek, 1990; Dickson et al., 1993; Zhang et al., 1994; Davenport and Travica, 1995), making it difficult to replicate the natural process of the decision making group. The organizational setting is a richer location for studies into the advancement of group supporting technologies, as it would provide a comprehensive picture of group decision making in a real environment (Kraemer and King, 1988).

## The research framework

The literature on group decision making raised a number of issues that the researchers consider important in relation to current research on group supporting technology. These issues are all related to the need to investigate the use of group supporting technology in ‘real world’ environments, while adopting a holistic approach to the group decision making process. Specifically, this study sought to:

(1) measure the extent of group decision making in Irish organizations,

(2) investigate the nature of group problem solving in organizational environments by examining the process of group decision making, and

(3) establish how group supporting technologies are used by groups during the decision making process.

It is recognized that the IS field is fragmented and pluralistic by nature in that it incorporates many disciplines (Galliers, 1991). Therefore, choosing one research method is difficult as different approaches have previously been used to investigate these disciplines (Antill, 1985). It is therefore argued that IS research is best understood when conducted using pluralistic models (Banville and Landry, 1989), as either positivistic or interpretivistic methods alone will not provide the richness that the IS field needs to advance further (Kaplan and Duchon, 1988). However, such methodological pluralism is contingent on the problem being studied and the kind of knowledge being sought (Hirchheim, 1985) and therefore should be directly related to the research objective and previous research in the area.

The researchers considered that pluralistic research methods were important here. This was because the need for generalizable results for a study of current practices and use of technology called for survey based research, while in-depth case study type research was required to investigate the nature of group decision making and the supporting group processes. In order to facilitate these objectives, a double faceted research framework was developed. This consisted of a postal survey of the top 100 Irish organizations and 12 of the larger financial institutions, which was followed up by in-depth case studies conducted in five of these companies. The usable response rate to the survey was 55%, and a test for non-response bias using the late returns technique recommended by Oppenheim (1966) and Wallace and Mellor (1988) proved to be negative indicating that non-response did not affect the generalizability of the results. The case studies were conducted using semi-structured interviews with the IS manager in each organization. This was followed by interviews with members of a decision making group who were involved in an important decision making situation, normally of a tactical nature. The seniority of those interviewed varied, but most were considered middle level managers.

## Group activity

Of the companies involved, 95% were engaged in group decision making, with the majority of companies using groups for between 30 and 60% of their decisions. This activity was found to be independent of company size and turnover. However, smaller companies regarded group decision making to be less important than their larger counterparts. Where group decision making was not taking place, it was normally due to company ethos or managerial beliefs. For example it was suggested that ‘the autocratic owner/manager is still in place’ or ‘managers are employed to make decisions and one person should carry the responsibility’.

The majority of companies studied (76%) were using both permanent and ad hoc groups to make decisions. However, permanent groups were three times more frequently used than their ad hoc counterparts. Permanent groups are used for policy making, coordination, communication of information; as well as for monitoring and controlling organizational activities. The life span of these groups ranged from one year to the indefinite. Ad hoc groups were generally established to deal with a specific issue and usually existed for between three and six months. These groups were not as tied to the organizational structure as their permanent counterparts, as they consisted of members drawn from numerous functions and organizational levels. However, it was generally believed that these groups needed to be controlled to ensure that they kept their focus. This was reflected by the fact that all of the ad hoc groups studied had leaders. Most group decision making took place in a face-to-face environment, with only 5% of companies using dispersed decision making groups. Consequently, the organizations studied believed that technological support should concentrate on the communication, collaboration, cooperation and coordination needs of synchronous permanent and ad hoc groups. It was however, recognized that both types of groups had different needs in this regard.

Group decision making was used in all functional areas, but was most popular in the marketing function. Throughout the whole organization, decision making groups were used primarily to make tactical and strategic decisions. It was noted that 90% of companies using groups did so for tactical decisions, while 85% did so for strategic decisions. These decisions were important company decisions and can be characteristically described as unique and risky, with the information needed to solve them often unavailable. However, 44% of these companies relied on formal procedures to help group members make these decisions. On the other hand, less than 40% of companies used groups to make operational decisions as these were generally ‘straightforward enough to be made by an individual’.

Many theories have been proposed regarding group size and group performance (Stephan and Mishler, 1952; Handy, 1993). In this study, 58% of the companies favoured groups of three to five members. Other group sizes, in decreasing order of popularity, were five to eight members, followed by over eight members and, finally, three or fewer members. One manager believed group size to be a trade-off between assembling the skills needed and hindering the level of communication within the group. Another manager stated that group size should be appropriate to the task involved and that three to five people were enough to have cross functional involvement. This is similar to the findings of Dennis et al. (1990).

It has been noted that a trade off exists between group size and the added burden of communication and control in the group (Rao and Jarvenpaa, 1989). This trade off would appear to be dependent on the group type, as permanent groups in this study were generally shown to be larger than their ad hoc counterparts, and had different communication and coordination needs. Ad hoc groups have to set up roles, norms and work practices afresh each time and often find the added burden of extra group members to be a hindrance rather an improvement to their performance. Hence ad hoc groups never exceeded eight members.

In 88% of companies, groups had formally appointed leaders but the role of the leader varied. In 5% of companies the leader had a casting vote. In the other companies the role of the leader was very much concerned with guiding and controlling the group throughout the decision making process by focusing on cooperation, coordination, collaboration and communication. This confirms what Hackman and Walton (1986) believed to be the group leader's primary role. Group size was seen to impact on whether the group had a leader or not. Groups with more than eight members all had leaders. Leadership varied however in groups with fewer than eight people. This suggests that a leader is vital to control and to direct the group once it reaches a certain size. This appears to occur when groups reach eight or more people and is important as group processes of communication, collaboration, coordination and cooperation become more important and more difficult for larger groups.

## A model of group problem solving

The extent of group decision making found in the study merited consideration of how this process took place, and how it was technologically supported. This examination was considered important as decision making groups are receiving increased responsibility within organizations, and further understanding of their activities may be a foundation for more adequate support.

The problem solving processes of groups in this study were found to be similar enough to be used to construct a model of group decision making. These problem solving processes consisted of 'group activities' which were supplemented by individual or sub-group task assignments. This model is presented in Figure 1 and is broadly similar to the work of Harrison (1975), Bass (1983) and Svenson (1990). Although the stages are presented as a sequence, much iteration takes place as the process is not as rational as it appears. More importantly, it should be noted that it is the group processes that occur at each stage that are considered important rather than the nature of the stages themselves. These processes will be explained later.

The decision making process was seen to be more complex for ad hoc groups than permanent groups, as an additional stage was added to the decision making process. This was due to the fact that the final choice of alternatives may be taken away from the group, though the group will make recommendations and can sometimes be asked to perform additional work on their proposal before it is accepted.

## Stage 1: problem realisation

This is very much an ad hoc process that is carried out by a specialized group or by senior management. Meetings (called 'pit stops' by one company) are held on a regular (monthly or quarterly) basis to review organizational activity and to monitor environmental changes.

## Stage 2: planning

In group problem solving the planning stage was found to be a formal part of the process. It was necessary as the activities of individuals or sub groups need to be co-ordinated. Normally, the decision making body (specialized group or senior management) who found the problem must decide the level in the organization where the decision should be made and whether or not to delegate this to a permanent or ad hoc group. A ‘contract’ is normally drawn up between the two groups which outlines the group’s objectives, budget and time frame. Inter- and intra-group communication, coordination, collaboration and cooperation patterns are also established at this stage.

## Stage 3: search for information

The purpose of the search for information is to gain a deeper understanding of the problem. The task of searching for information is normally distributed among group members. Depending on the task of the group, the internal and external organizational environment will be scanned. The information gathered is then brought back to the group. The group compares its problem task in light of the new information, and as a result the group may sometimes reformulate its task. This is a crucial step in the group decision making process, as it is unlikely that the group will drastically reformulate the task after this.

## Stage 4: creation of alternatives

This is a stage where the group operates as more than a collective of individuals. Although ideas are often generated by individuals, alternatives are very much the result of group interaction which generally takes place in a face-to-face environment.

## Stage 5: evaluation of alternatives

The evaluation stage is very much an iterative process. During this stage the group cycles between face-to-face meetings and individual task assignments. Not all alternatives are evaluated to the same extent; some are dismissed early on, while others require greater consideration. Each alternative is compared with the others to determine how far each goes towards filling the gap between the desired and current situation. External advisors are often brought in at this stage and their activities have to be coordinated. One company used between 20 and 24 advisors, but this was exceptional as most groups only expanded by between two and four people.

## Stage 6: choose an alternative

The processes of evaluation and choice are closely tied, as in this stage the group makes choices on whether to accept or reject an alternative. In most of the groups studied the final act of choice was made by consensus, with considerable negotiation taking place, at group and individual levels, beforehand.

## Stage 7: sell the alternative

Stage 7 is only applicable if an ad hoc group is performing the decision task. This stage is important for the ad hoc group, as they must be able to justify their choice and it is on the quality of this alternative that the work of the group will be judged. The permanent group will either accept their recommended alternative, choose another, ask for clarification or reject the alternative. Where clarification is sought the ad hoc group, depending on the issues raised, will begin again at Stage 3, 4 or 5.

## Stage 8: implement the alternative

The implementation stage is the final step in the problem solving process as the means to solve the problem has been established and just has to be implemented. This can be done by the same group or more often it is given to an existing lower level group.

## Supporting group processes

The stages of this decision making model can be grouped together based on the activity of the group. These groupings include foundation stages (2 and 3), action stages (4, 5 and 6) and outcome stages (7 and 8). These stages exhibit different levels of group processes in the form of communication, cooperation, collaboration and coordination. Communication refers to the transfer of information between parties involved in the problem solving process. Cooperation refers to low level working relationships between individuals in a problem solving group. Collaboration refers to working relations where a group goes beyond cooperation between individuals to operate 'as a group' exhibiting 'collective intelligence'. Coordination refers to the synchronization of activities within a group. These group processes were measured by a complex series of questions given to all members of the decision making group.

Throughout all the decision making stages the level of group processes, other than communication, were seen to fluctuate. When closely examined, the group processes were found to be different for permanent and ad hoc groups at the start (the foundation stage) and end (the outcomes stage) of the decision making process, but where the majority of work was done by both groups (the action stage), group processes were found to be similar. The levels of these group processes are shown in Table 1, and indicate that computer support for these types of groups would have to be different during the foundation and outcome stages, but that both types of groups could be provided with similar types of supporting during the action stage. The terms low, medium and high refer to the relative levels of each process, measured as a comparison across stages.

During the foundation stages (planning and searching for information) the levels of group processes differed between permanent and ad hoc groups. The ad hoc group had higher group process levels and generally spent longer at the planning stage than the permanent groups did. This reflects the additional burden which the ad hoc group incurs when it begins to set plans of action and patterns of work within the group. When this is accomplished, permanent and ad hoc groups are similar regarding their work and group processes.

The level of group activity is at its highest during the action stages involving the generation of alternatives, evaluation of the alternatives and the choice of an alternative. Most of the work done by the group takes place during these stages and much iteration exists between the three stages. Permanent and ad hoc groups begin to differ at the end of the action phase which is concerned with the choice of an alternative. This is due to the manner in which the two groups were found to be make their choices. The outcome stages (7 and 8) are the result of previous actions and, unless the group's recommendations are rejected in the case of the ad hoc group, very little extra work is done. During the outcome stages of the decision making process the level of group processes are different for permanent and ad hoc groups, reflecting the roles and positions of the groups within the company. Ad hoc groups will often have to report to a supervisory body to ratify their choice of decision and therefore they have an additional step to permanent groups. Due to the temporary role of the ad hoc group their decision was normally implemented by another group. This differed from permanent groups who normally implemented their solution, thus accounting for the different levels of group processes during this stage. The levels of group processes during the many stages of the decision making processes indicate that there is great potential for these processes to be supported. Indeed, the focus of the current range of group supporting technologies is to support decision making groups through supporting their group processes. It is believed that the support of the processes will give the groups greater flexibility and allow work practices to evolve.

## Group supporting technology

Of the companies studied, 66% indicated that they had computer based support available for decision making groups. However, a gap exists between the perceived importance of group decision making and the importance placed on group supporting technologies. While 81% of companies regarded group decision making as important, only 51% regarded group supporting technologies to be important. The technologies that were used by groups have been classified as being either Level 1 or Level 2 type tools. Level 1 technologies are generic products that provide support for communication and data exchange, while Level 2 technologies provide higher level group support, focusing on communication and coordination. The study showed that in general only low level technological support was available as shown in Table 2, and that this technological support was used less for synchronous work than for asynchronous work. Most of the technology which has been adopted by companies on a large scale can be classified as Level 1 generic group support tools. This only supports communication and data exchange within the company, and hence excludes GDSS type applications. The benefits or support gained from these technologies is very much dependent of the manipulation skills of the user. These technologies are accepted as they provide uncomplicated functionality and require very little planning for their introduction. More advanced technologies require more organizational commitment and planning and this may explain why advanced technology, such as electronic blackboards and anonymous voting systems typical of group decision support systems, are not widely used.

The supporting technologies and their use during the various stages of the decision making process are shown in Table 3. Communication levels are high for groups throughout the decision making process and most of the technology supports this. However, group supporting technologies are mainly used during the asynchronous work of decision making groups. The level of support provided by group support technologies is generally quite low and this support is often supplemented by the actions of the leader. The leader receives most of the communication in the group and uses this to coordinate the group's effort. The tools may aid the leader, but it is the leader who provides the overall direction.

The use of the group support technologies varied during the stages of the decision making process, with such technologies being least used during the generation and choice of alternatives. These are the most important stages in the decision making process, as it is on the quality of the chosen alternative that the work of the group will be evaluated. These stages also happen to be where the group mainly works in a face-to-face environment. The group performs a multitude of tasks when face-to-face. These include providing an overall focus for the group, setting the agenda, controlling who speaks, deciding the relevance of certain issues and directing the action of the group. Manual tools appear to be the only acceptable tools during these stages of alternatives generation and choice, as computer based tools were believed to hinder the natural processes of the group. Even video conferencing was considered a poor substitute for face-to-face interaction during these stages. This result supports the findings of Walczuch et al. (1995) who reported the importance of the appropriate mix of computer based and manual techniques when managing meeting situations, and illustrates the greater trust placed on manual techniques by many groups.

The levels of collaboration actually increase during the face-to-face stages, yet this is when the levels of group support technologies are at their lowest. As the level of coordination increases during the dispersed phases of the decision making process there is a considerable increase in the level of group support technologies used. In the asynchronous environment coordination is harder to achieve, thus increasing the group's effort to do so. This was the only situation where the increase in the level of group processes actually corresponded to an increase in the level of technology being used.

Group support technologies are therefore not being used to support groups during the more important stages of the decision making process. Instead manual tools are preferred. Technologies are used to support the asynchronous work of groups, but only at a low level. It must therefore be concluded that group support technologies are not an integral part of the group decision making process and are only used to support low level routine tasks. Decision taking tasks are too ‘person centred’ to be computerized and this is why GDSS type tools such as electronic blackboards are discarded in favour of manual tools.

## Conclusions

Groups were found to be common to both the hierarchical and flatter organization. The use of groups in the hierarchical organization was found to give the organization the flexibility it needed to overcome its rigid structures when dealing with issues of problem solving, change management and innovation. In the flatter, team based organization, groups were used extensively for decision making and this promoted a greater level of interaction between organizational departments. The use of groups within organizations

## Group problem solving and decision making

was also seen to promote organizational learning and lead to a greater level of organizational awareness.

The group decision making process was found to be far more complicated and chaotic than individual decision making. During the decision making process groups were found to need a great deal of control and coordination so that group members could collaborate. This study showed that the group problem solving process did not necessarily follow a sequential pattern as many tasks took place at the one time. Rather than having one decision making cycle in the group, several were found to be taking place at the same time for each alternative that was being considered. New issues were constantly arising and had to be dealt with as more information became available to the group. The decision making group also needed to reconcile the various styles of group members and focus this into a collective effort. In addition to this, the structure of the group was continually changing, as internal consultants joined the group for short periods of time. These ad hoc members had to be coordinated and brought up to speed on the group's task.

In general, groups were making decisions with very little technological support. This was mainly limited to Level 1 with some Level 2 group support technologies. Group support technologies were not very popular in synchronous group decision making environments. Support in this environment was mainly done through the leader, with manual tools being used on an ad hoc basis. These organizations generally recognized the potential importance of group support technology, but considered them not to be of great use during important decision making stages.

Even where electronic support was provided for groups, the group leader was the most vital element in supporting the decision making group. Leaders were found to direct the coordinated efforts of group members and to enhance group collaboration and control. In addition most of the groups' communication was centred around the leader. This finding suggests that electronic support for decision making groups may be more successful if it followed a leadership principle. Computerization of a rationalized decision making process has not worked well as the decision making process is not of this nature. However, the activities of the leader may be more structured as the objective is to support the communication, collaboration, coordination and cooperation processes of the group. Within this context, group supporting technology may be most suitable operating around the problem solving process focusing on the foundation and outcome stages. In this way the face-to-face activities of the action stages could be directed within the boundaries of the foundation and outcome stages without hindering the more person centred activities required during these stages. However, in this situation it will have to be recognized that permanent and ad hoc groups will have different needs for group supporting technologies.
