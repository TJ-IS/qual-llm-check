# Using the Internet for knowledge acquisition in expert systems development: a case study

KATHLEEN K. MOLNAR AND RAMESH SHARDA\*

Oklahoma State University, College of Business Administration, Department of Management, Stillwater, OK 74078, USA

\*To whom correspondence should be addressed

Knowledge acquisition is a major task in expert system development. This paper proposes one way of acquiring knowledge for expert system development: through the use of the Internet. Internet resources (e.g. Usenet groups, ListServ discussion lists, archive sites and on-line literature/database searches) are knowledge sources. Internet tools such as newsreaders, electronic mail, Telnet, FTP, gophers, archie, WAIS and World Wide Web provide access to these sources. The results of an exploratory study that examined the use of the Internet as a knowledge source are presented here in conjunction with a framework for using the Internet in the planning phase. Four major advantages can be found in this: the availability of multiple experts in multiple domains, the interaction of domain experts and end users, time/cost savings, and convenience. The lessons learned and some additional issues are also presented.

## Introduction

This paper discusses the use of the Internet as a resource in knowledge acquisition for expert systems development. The Internet is a worldwide entity bridging local, regional and national telecommunications networks, connecting more than 3.5 million host computers in over 154 nations. The system has continued doubling in size (number of hosts and networks) every year since 1988 and the growth rate is accelerating (Quarterman, 1993; Bell, 1995; Chapin, 1995). The Internet is a global entity which ‘... defines a common ground for internetworking communities and establishes a community of people and organizations that want to connect’ (Dern, 1992, p. 111).

Internet services fall into two basic categories: computer mediated communication (CMC) services which allow people to exchange messages, and resource sharing services which allow users to access computing resources (Quarterman, 1990). Group information exchange occurs through numerous mailing lists and newsgroups. Internet mail, combined with fast interactive protocols, such as Telnet, File Transfer Protocol (FTP) and the World Wide Web (WWW), encourages collaborative work over the Internet.

Through the use of these facilities, 'The Internet ... provide(s) the electronic home for leading-edge development, active discussions and information dissemination and sets of relationships that span space and time and can obliterate differences in type of computer and operating system ...' [Dern, 1994, p. 17]. A survey in 1995 of over 13 000 Internet respondents (Gupta and Pitkow, 1995) reveals that the majority of users spend their time on the Internet in search of information and knowledge. According to the authors, respondents used the Internet for the following reasons: 83% for browsing, 51% for work-related exchanges, 47% for education, 38% for business research and 35% for academic research. The survey also indicated what Internet users are looking for: 82% for references, 77% for e-mail or papers, 57% for consumer products or service information, 47% for research reports, 44% for government information and 33% for financial information. Cronin et al. (1994) stated that the Internet has the potential to become a major strategic information tool for commercial enterprises. They noted, based on a current survey of Internet practices, that the respondents were using the Internet to support the competitive intelligence function in organizations, for consumer contacts, as a prospecting source for new clients, as a means of customer communications and indirect advertising, and as a source for market research. However, the greatest challenge to using the Internet is in finding out what resources and information are available (Kalin and Tennant, 1991).

The research community has found the use of electronic mail and information exchange extremely valuable. Internet forms an electronic infrastructure supporting widespread communications and collaboration. Researchers use the Internet for projects spanning the sciences (Cortese, 1989). However, research on the use of the Internet as a knowledge source is rare.

The last few years have witnessed significant growth in expert systems. 'Decision making in business requires processing quantitative and qualitative knowledge. Expert systems ... provide the needed methodologies for dealing with qualitative aspects of decision making' (Zahedi, 1993, pp. 4–5). A major task in expert system development is knowledge acquisition. This paper proposes the use of Internet resources in knowledge acquisition. Internet resources (such as Usenet groups, ListServ discussion lists, archive sites and on-line literature/database searches) can serve as knowledge sources. Internet tools such as newsreaders, electronic mail, Telnet, FTP, gophers, archie, WAIS and WWW, provide access to these knowledge sources. This paper proposes that the Internet could be used as a type of Group Decision Support System (GDSS) which DeSanctis and Gallupe (1985, p. 3) defined as '... an interactive computer-based system which facilitates solution of unstructured problems by a set of decision makers working together as a group'. Liou and Nunamaker (1993) have previously documented the advantages of using a GDSS for knowledge acquisition. This paper presents the results of an exploratory study to examine the use of the Internet as a knowledge source. In the next section, we propose the use of the Internet in the expert system knowledge acquisition process. Then we describe an experiment conducted to acquire knowledge for developing an expert system. The results of this experiment are presented and the paper concludes with several lessons learned and additional research issues to be resolved.

## Knowledge acquisition framework and the Internet

Liou (1992) establishes four phases in the process of knowledge acquisition: planning, extraction, analysis and verification. This paper focuses on the planning phase of knowledge acquisition whose goal ‘... is to identify the proper human resources, analyse various knowledge acquisition techniques and design proper procedures to acquire knowledge’ (Liou, 1992, p. 62). The extraction phase which acquires knowledge from the experts, and the analysis phase which analyses the output of this acquired knowledge were also included to help interpret the findings.

The planning phase of knowledge acquisition is divided into four stages: understanding the domain, identifying the experts, analysing knowledge acquisition techniques and designing procedures to acquire knowledge. The methodology adopted for using the Internet in the knowledge acquisition task is summarized in Table 1. The four stages of the planning phase and the extraction phase are presented below as independent stages, but, knowledge acquisition using the Internet is an interactive process that tends to obscure the distinction among the methods and stages.

The first stage requires the knowledge engineer to understand the application domain. Zahedi (1993, p. 231) described this as conceptualization, the ‘... beginning of knowledge acquisition and provides a general familiarity with the domain and allows the developer to break down the domain into modules and to identify the experts and sources of knowledge ...’ In the absence of a generalist expert, the knowledge engineer may use written materials, group discussions or existing standards as possible sources. ‘The knowledge engineer may satisfy the need for this type of knowledge by consulting an expert and/or other sources such as textbooks, solved cases and theories' (Abdul-Gader and Kozar, 1990, p. 63). We propose that the Internet not only provides these resources, but provides this information on a more efficient, convenient and inexpensive basis. The knowledge engineer can check, research and retrieve information from databases, reports, papers and other data stored on various computers accessible to the Internet community when he or she wants, without ever getting out of the chair. In addition, information searches can be directly captured to a computer file (Keays, 1993). It is also an efficient way of understanding the problem domain because Internet resources, especially WWW resources, can allow the knowledge acquisition to progress from aggregate to fine detail. One can collect the summary information on a topic first and then follow the links to detailed information as needed. This approach is similar to the deductive reporting concept implemented in Executive Information Systems. However, the hypertext capability makes it even more powerful.

The second stage is in finding the experts. Identifying appropriate domain experts is crucial (Liou, 1992). Ideal experts should not only have skills in the domain, but also skills in communicating their knowledge, judgement and the methods they use to apply these to a particular task and, of course, they must be available. Multiple experts should be considered when expert systems require diverse knowledge: they create synergy, which often results in an enriched and enlarged domain of expertise (Liou, 1992; Liou and Nunamaker, 1993). We propose that the Internet provides considerable accessibility to these resources. Since newsgroups are organized into specific knowledge domains, many experts frequently read and/or post to these groups. Newsgroups offer the opportunity to keep current on new technologies in a particular domain as well as exchange information. Many experts may have their home pages setup to provide general information in their area of expertise. 'Experts are experts because of what they are able to do with their acquired knowledge' (Hart, 1986, p. 14)

The third stage of knowledge acquisition is in analysing various techniques. The acquisition process and techniques required to elicit knowledge have been identified from the literature (Hart, 1986; Cleal and Heaton, 1988; Carrico et al., 1989; Abdul-Gader and Kozar, 1990; Liou and Nunamaker, 1993; Zahedi, 1993). Zahedi (1993) categorized these techniques into two broad groups: Top-down (or deductive) and bottom-up (or inductive) methods. The top-down methods start with general concepts and then gradually advance into specific details via questioning, object-oriented, quantitative and inventive methods.

The bottom-up methods focus on specific cases and then generalize. These include: example-based methods, protocol analysis and observation. One critical advantage of the Internet is that it provides for an 'interactive interview' process providing almost instant answers to obscure questions through the use of Bitnet/Internet discussion groups, e-mail, newsgroups, etc. (Ladner and Tillman, 1993). Due to the physical distances between the expert and knowledge engineer, limitations on the lengths of responses, and the anonymity of the respondents, the questioning method is likely to be the best approach for knowledge elicitation over the Internet. The questioning method has three strategies: structured and unstructured interviews, and questionnaire (Zahedi, 1993). The unstructured interview should be used in the beginning stages in exploring the domain and determining which respondents would be most informative. The structured interview should then be used in specific follow-up questions to a particular newsgroup and/or individual. The questionnaire approach could be useful in postings to groups for clarification and/or verification of previous answers.

The final stage deals with designing proper procedures to acquire knowledge. The procedure for using the questioning methods for knowledge acquisition over the Internet has been termed computer mediated communication (CMC). CMC is identified as a major resource in using the Internet to acquire knowledge (Quarterman, 1990). Quarterman suggested that the primary effect of CMC is increased human interaction, leading to improved technical productivity through the exchange of ideas and references. One major advantage in CMC is the benefit of contacting many ‘experts’ with varying perspectives, Postings to various groups on the Internet generates a series of e-mail responses. Such a request and reply, has been termed a thread. This type of ‘group interaction’ will probably produce conflicting views to be resolved. Some approaches for conflict resolution are: the Delphi technique, incorporating conflicting views in different modules, group discussion, voting, or choosing a primary expert (Zahedi, 1993). Each of these techniques would be feasible over the Internet and the choice of conflict resolution method should depend on the conflicts, the experts and their effects on the knowledge base.

## Internet resources

The Internet's resources include the knowledge, opinions and expertise shared by individuals through electronic discussion lists and the Usenet, as well as the databases, reports, papers, software and other data stored on various computers accessible by the Internet community. The Internet's resources can be accessed through four types of tools: resource locator tools such as Archie, Veronica, Netfind and Nslookup; information retrieval tools such as FTP; browser tools such as Telnet, Gopher, and the WWW; and communication tools such as e-mail, lists, news groups and Internet relay chat (Dern, 1994). These tools can help 'navigate' the Internet, allow the user to make queries and retrieve information from Internet sites such as online library systems, supercomputers and online databases and allows the copying of files from one computer allowing the user to download programmes and text files etc. from thousands of sites around the world. E-mail, Usenet, Internet Relay Chat (IRC) (and the corresponding WebChat on WWW) and 'Intranets' are ways for computer users to exchange messages. E-mail allows for dissemination of messages between individuals as well as groups. A message may be text, binary objects, broadcast announcements or 'electronic magazines'. Usenet is the 'bulletin board' of the Internet: a worldwide community of electronic bulletin boards organized into thousands of topical groups or 'Newsgroups'. Through these newsgroups users around the world share information, pose and answer questions, and conduct 'group' discussions on specific topics from computers, to news, recreation, science, social issues and miscellaneous subjects. IRC or WebChat allows participation in CB radio-like conversations. Intranets are private networks on the WWW which allow employees to join an electronic discussion group with colleagues around the world. These private networks combine text, graphics and even video to distribute news, answer employee questions, update personnel records and connect far-flung workers (Ziegler, 1995).

In performing knowledge acquisition through the use of Internet resources, it is important to determine what resources are available and how best to acquire 'expert' knowledge from them. A number of advantages in using the Internet for multi-expert knowledge acquisition are proposed based on the benefits of CMC reported by Quarterman (1990) and the research in GDSS knowledge acquisition of Liou and Nunamaker (1993).

## Proposed advantages of using the Internet

Although Quarterman (1990) acknowledged that increased connectivity can lead to information overload and CMC should be used in conjunction with other media, he concluded that there are several distinctive advantages in using CMC:

(1) many users can be contacted over distributed time zones or countries without a danger of being 'shut out'

(2) the user can ignore what is deemed unimportant,

(3) several people can provide input at the same time,

(4) users can be involved in many conferences at the same time,

(5) there is a written record,

(6) many people communicate better in writing (especially when different languages are involved),

(7) there are no physical clues therefore it is easier to concentrate on the topic rather than the person, and

(8) anonymity usually produces greater candor in its participants, including more willingness to criticize bad ideas.

The first six advantages of CMC relate directly to resolving the organizational issues of multi-expert knowledge acquisition. The Internet allows for 24-hour worldwide dissemination of postings to newsgroups and e-mail messages. Internet users select whichever newsgroups they prefer. Multiple senders may post simultaneously to individual or multiple receivers and senders may also cross-post to multiple groups. The last two advantages of CMC could be helpful in resolving conflicting viewpoints since little information about the sender of the posting is known except the senders e-mail address unless the sender wishes to divulge that information. 'Power struggles' become less visible and a more collaborative atmosphere is likely to emerge.

Liou and Nunamaker (1993) found several advantages in using a GDSS in knowledge acquisition. Many of these same findings are also applicable to using the Internet for multi-expert knowledge acquisition. These advantages include documenting knowledge electronically, supporting knowledge extraction from individual experts in a parallel fashion, and providing a collaborative atmosphere to facilitate group interactions. These advantages can also be useful in resolving the organizational and conflict resolution issues of multi-expert knowledge acquisition.

The focus of this research is in using the Internet in multi-expert knowledge acquisition. We propose that using the Internet for multi-expert knowledge acquisition will integrate the previously mentioned advantages of CMC and of using a GDSS, providing an additional expert system knowledge acquisition tool (Table 2). Zahedi (1993) stated that the two major issues of multi-expert knowledge acquisition are organizational and conflict resolution. Organizational issues deal with scheduling and consistency while conflict resolution issues deal with group dynamics. The organizational issues of scheduling and consistency deal with soliciting knowledge individually or in groups. These issues can be solved by the Internet through the use of direct e-mail for individuals or newsgroup postings for group knowledge elicitation. Consistency can be achieved because the Internet provides a parallel extraction process allowing multiple 'experts' to receive the same message simultaneously. Physical constraints no longer become a problem as the Internet provides for worldwide dissemination of messages and responses. Efficiency and effectiveness can be achieved through electronic documentation, 24-h worldwide connections and topic-specific newsgroups. Conflict resolution issues are resolved through group discussions and the anonymity and collaborative atmosphere the Internet provides.

## Research methodology

An exploratory study was conducted to validate the general proposition that the Internet can serve as an expert system knowledge acquisition tool. The objective was to ascertain if any of the advantages proposed in the conceptual section were actually realized, and what lessons could be learned about the use of the Internet for knowledge acquisition. The primary research question is: how should a researcher acquire knowledge using the Internet? The next sections discuss the experimental design, delineates the application domain and outlines the procedures used in employing the Internet as a information resource.

## Experimental method

Case study methodology was chosen for this research because it can be used to explain, describe or explore a phenomenon within a real-life context (Yin, 1989). This study explored the relationship between the theory and practice of using the Internet as an infor-

mation resource.

Propositions reflected important theoretical issues that should be examined within the scope of the study (Yin, 1989, p. 30). The following propositions were presented:

(1) The Internet can be an effective tool for knowledge acquisition.

(2) We are able to determine who the experts are.

(3) We are able to elicit information from these experts.

(4) We are able to resolve the conflicting viewpoints.

(5) It is efficient to use the Internet for knowledge acquisition.

This case study attempted to validate these propositions by using the Internet for the planning phase of knowledge acquisition. The data collected for this study was the 'knowledge' elicited for the expert system through the Internet. This 'knowledge' was in the form of information electronically transcribed over the Internet. The efficiency and effectiveness of this knowledge was measured by the number of messages sent/received, the types and quality of responses, subjective observations and the signal-to-noise ratio. The first step in collecting the data to examine the proposition's questions was to develop a methodology for acquiring knowledge from the Internet. The criteria for interpreting the findings was the reliability of the knowledge acquired for the expert system through the Internet. In other words, can a valid expert system be built using the knowledge acquired from the Internet? This was determined through the knowledge extraction, analysis and verification phases.

## The application domain - multimedia kiosk expert system

A project was undertaken to develop a multimedia kiosk for a south central, state-supported, comprehensive research university in the USA. The goal of this kiosk was to encourage faculty interest in, and development of, projects using presentation and interactive tutoring multimedia. The kiosk gave a brief introduction to multimedia, demonstrated six multimedia authoring packages and discussed their advantages/disadvantages and technical requirements. Three 'levels' of multimedia were determined and the six packages were chosen based on the criteria of selecting two packages for each level and their accessibility to the university faculty and staff.

After the initial introduction to these multimedia authoring software packages, users wanted to know what particular package they should use in developing their specific application. One means of providing this evaluation was the addition of an expert system to the kiosk which would suggest choices based on the user's individual needs. Studies have already been suggested on merging multimedia with artificial intelligence (Sipior and Garrity, 1992). The problem of selecting a specific multimedia authoring software package is a suitable domain for expert systems because the problem is cognitive in nature with distinctly identifiable factors (individual, task, and software capabilities) that can be checked against expert knowledge to derive a recommendation (Prerau, 1990; Edwards, 1991).

The major problem in pursuing the development of this expert system was in finding multimedia ‘experts’ who were versed in the six authoring software packages under consideration. Multimedia use at the university was still relatively new (as is using multimedia in education) and the Internet provided a viable alternative to accessing multimedia expertise not otherwise available.

## The experiment - Knowledge acquisition using the Internet

The proposed methodology for using the Internet for the planning phase of knowledge acquisition is divided into four stages: understanding the domain, identifying the experts, analysing knowledge acquisition techniques and designing procedures to acquire knowledge. These stages are discussed in the following sections.

## Understanding the domain

The first stage, understanding the problem, involves determining the descriptions and relationships between the various multimedia authoring packages for the expert system kiosk. The Internet was used in this initial search to find information on the various multimedia authoring packages. The first step was to use Netnews Filtering Server (netnews@db.stanford.edu) to locate newsgroups discussing, or having previously discussed the six specific multimedia authoring software packages on the kiosk by using the search and subscribe commands. The only newsgroup found in this search was ‘comp.multimedia’. Therefore, a message was posted to this newsgroup asking for attributes/characteristics for each (or any) of the six packages. A parallel step was a Gopher and Veronica search on each of the multimedia software package names and manufacturers. This search revealed a variety of general information on each of the packages as well as postings of specific ListServ groups which have discussed software and references to published evaluations. Another search through Yanoff's (1994) ‘Special internet connections’ suggested a posting to IKE (IBM Kiosk for Education) under the user discussion forum of multimedia technology. Although the 'comp.multimedia' and 'IKE' postings did not prove as fruitful as expected, these combined searches provided valuable general information and suggested places to start looking for the 'experts'. These steps also increased the overall understanding of the problem domain for the knowledge engineer (the first author).

## Identifying the domain experts

The next stage of knowledge acquisition for the kiosk was in locating experts in the domain application of multimedia. The first step in this process was to try to locate 'experts' on the Internet who would be willing to offer their opinions of the six specific multimedia authoring software packages selected for the kiosk. Given its anonymous nature, we felt the only way to locate an expert over the Internet was by posting specific 'knowledge' questions. We proceeded to post questions to those newsgroups and ListServs who were or had previously discussed these six packages and the groups specifically designed to discuss these packages.

Given the initial searches, one particular ListServ group stood out, 'Edtech'. This ListServ had just recently discussed in detail two of the six authoring packages. This group was oriented towards technology and education, the perfect combination for this multimedia kiosk. Over a period of one month several messages were posted to this group, from the initial posting asking for attributes/characteristics of the authoring packages to specific postings comparing two or three packages against each other.

Also attempted was location of additional sources by sending the 'list global' command to ListServ@-bitnic.edu.com.edu. The keywords identified were each of the six authoring packages and multimedia. Five additional ListServ groups were identified and subscribed to: Aware-L (Authorware), Direct-L (Director), Iummedia, Mmedia-L, Multi-L (Multimedia discussion groups). Postings were made to each of these groups based on the area of expertise. For example, postings were made to Aware-L and Direct-L asking for the advantages/disadvantages between Authorware and Director as these two authoring packages are seen to have similar multimedia capabilities and are made by the same manufacturer. Due to the technical nature of the postings (both in questions and responses) most of these ListServ groups received, it was perceived that the responses from the users of these groups would be considered 'expert opinions' within their various authoring packages domains. In addition, since the majority of subscribers of these groups are estimated to be 'lurkers' (Dern, 1994) most individuals who do post responses to the group consider themselves, at least, 'experts' in the area.

## Analysing knowledge acquisition techniques

The next stage in knowledge acquisition is analysing the knowledge acquisition techniques. From the initial phases it was evident that after the acquisition of general information in the field, the most beneficial method would be through the use of CMC in the forms of newsgroups, ListServ groups and direct e-mail. The information which was required for the expert system multimedia kiosk was simply not available elsewhere on the Internet.

The structured interview approach appeared to be the best method for soliciting knowledge in this case, asking specific questions about specific software packages. Very little response was received from the open-ended postings asking about the attributes/characteristics of the six packages. However, good responses were received and/or discussions generated when specific postings were made regarding the advantages/disadvantages and/or specific questions asked regarding particular software packages. This could be expected given the nature of the Internet as an interactive medium with an informal structure where users must concern themselves with 'netiquette' and the proper use of bandwidth. Users do not use the Internet to read and/or post 'dissertations', but more as a quick scan of the specific information they are interested in and more importantly to keep abreast of the latest developments in their domain area.

## Design procedures to acquire knowledge

The final stage in the planning phase was to design the procedures to acquire knowledge. Clancey (1988) suggested using a classification model as a basis for interviewing an expert and structuring how one solves a problem. This particular expert system problem was easily classified and decomposed into finding the attributes/characteristics of each of the six authoring software packages. A hierarchy and the relationships between each entity (software package) was established based on the knowledge engineer's understanding of the domain area and verified by the first response received from the comp.multimedia initial posting. The hierarchy segregated the packages into three distinct levels with two packages at each level. Specific postings were then made to the individual groups based on the groups expertise area in the domain and the relationships between the packages. (Example: since Authorware and Director are considered to be at level 3, postings were sent to the Authorware and Director ListServ groups respectively asking for the advantages/disadvantages of these two packages in relation to the other).

## Extraction

Having completed the planning phase for knowledge acquisition for the expert system, the extraction phase was fairly straightforward. Several messages were posted to the groups identified above over a period of approximately 1.5 months and the responses were saved to computer files. The responses were postings sent back to the group or direct e-mail from the 'experts'. In all cases, the conflicts were openly discussed on the newsgroup/ListServ forums and opinions were provided. (It should be noted that due to the generalized nature of this kiosk and the majority of responses being direct e-mail, there were no major conflicting opinions). The dissenting opinions were mostly due to individual preferences in operating system platform (Dos/Windows versus Macintosh).

## Results

In order to analyse the results from the knowledge acquisition phase, a knowledge base was constructed for the multimedia kiosk expert system using the information received from the Internet. The validity of this knowledge base served as a guideline for interpreting the results of using the Internet as an information resource for knowledge acquisition. Twelve messages were posted to seven newsgroup and ListServ groups. A total of 51 responses were accumulated from the Internet and used in constructing this knowledge base. A breakdown of these responses by type of organization (.com, .edu, .org ...), type of response (direct e-mail or posting), number of separate individuals, and general time of day the message was sent is described in Table 3. Although some responses proved more useful than others, all responses related directly to the topic and therefore no ‘noise’ was perceived.

We also attempted to measure the quality of the responses. Messages and information gathered through the Internet were scored by three 'judges' on a low (1) to high (7) scale. The overall average inter-rater reliability was found to be 0.69. Table 4 displays the average quality of those messages for four specific categories. A general type of group covers a variety of topics such as EdTech/Comp.multimedia. A specific type of group specifically discusses one major subject such as Aware-L/Direct-L or was a direct e-mail message. A general type of question asked generalities about all six products being compared. A specific type of question asked comparisons about two level-related products. Although a statistically significant difference was not found between the type of group $(p = 0.71)$ , the type of question $(p = 0.09)$ , or group/type category $(p = 0.19)$ , this could be due to the small sample sizes, particularly in the specific type of group, and general type of question case. However, from this information two general inferences can be made. First, it appeared from this data that the 'best' quality of responses were received when a specific comparison of products was asked of a group created specifically for that topic (4.81, with an average inter-rater reliability for just this category of 0.84), instead of a general relationship question asked of a more conventional group. Second, the greatest number of responses were received when a specific type of question was asked of a general type of group (25). This may be due to the greater amount of 'exposure' a message would receive when posted to a general type of group.

## Analysis

The first proposition that the Internet can be an effective tool for knowledge acquisition appears to be supported by the development of the expert system for the multimedia kiosk based on information received over the Internet. From the information gathered on the Internet, three major factors evolved which formed a conceptual model for the multimedia kiosk expert system (Figure 1). From this model, eight major questions were generated in order to segregate the six authoring software packages. These eight questions were then resolved for each package under consideration and these conclusions were then used to form the rules for the expert system.

The most difficult knowledge acquisition stage using this approach is expert system verification. Using the Internet as an knowledge acquisition tool is superb for the initial prototype, but the disadvantage is in not having the experts physically available to test the prototype and verify the results. However, overcoming the initial obstacle of creating an original prototype is the first step, and for this purpose the Internet proved to be a valuable source of information. In addition, the Internet proved very useful in verifying knowledge already acquired.

## Using the Internet for knowledge acquisition

The second proposition that experts could be determined over the Internet is supported by the number of separate individuals responding to the postings (37) and the average quality of their responses when asked specific questions (4.3, from Tables 3 and 4). The procedure to acquire knowledge over the Internet focused on the simplest, yet most important statement: in order to acquire expert opinions it is necessary to ask the right questions of the right group. Given the nature of Internet users, it is important to have a well-defined problem scope and attributes and to understand the characteristics of the group you are posting to as well as maintaining proper 'netiquette'. The first steps after gathering initial information and defining the domain expert groups/individuals are to read any appropriate frequently asked questions (FAQs) for the groups and monitor the groups postings as to form, content and current discussions in order to determine if this is the appropriate group and what appropriate question will illicit valid responses/discussions. It is difficult to predict the best timing for a posting, however it is advisable not to overpost too many questions too many times to the same group. Therefore it is important to plan and monitor the postings.

The third proposition that we are able to solicit information from these experts is supported, based on the number of messages received (51) and the overall quality of that information based on the content analysis of those messages (4.1, from Tables 3 and 4). The major advantage that was found in using this technique is access to the experts in the domain field. As can be seen from the category breakdowns of responses (Table 3), responses were received from commercial organizations, educational institutions and also from other countries suggesting that there were both domain and end-user responses. The background articles/research compared the products but did not describe the relationships between the six specific authoring packages. Comparisons were made between two sets of the packages (such as Authorware and Director), but they did not give an overall view of the relationships between the six packages. In addition, the experts on the Internet were more specific on the applications they used each software package for and most importantly, why. The Internet experts could be asked to explain and interpret their recommendations at any time.

The fourth proposition is unable to be supported at this time due to lack of conflicting viewpoints expressed by these experts. However, since multiple responses and/or discussions are posted to newsgroups, conflicting opinions are often openly debated.

The final proposition that using the Internet is efficient is supported by the amount of 'new' information acquired by the knowledge engineer (40 or 78%, Table

3), the low amount of time required per average quality of the response (12 days/4.1, Tables 3 and 4), and the high signal-to-noise ratio perceived (no noise perceived). The interactive nature of the Internet provides for discussions of opinions, exchanges of conflicting viewpoints and a large domain of expertise. If domain experts act individually, the acquisition can be time consuming and difficult. Once the appropriate groups were identified, the actual knowledge extraction phase took less than 1–2 weeks. Answers to postings were received usually within 24–48 h, however the responses were usually brief and the discussions on the newsgroups abated quickly. At times, this makes it difficult to encourage continuing discussions which must also be done on a direct e-mail basis. This knowledge extraction occurred with convenience to the experts involved (they responded when it was convenient for them to do so) and to the knowledge engineer (responses were captured on computer files and assimilated, and repostings for clarifications and conflict resolutions were performed as needed). This can be seen in the times the responses sent and the type of posting (see Table 3). This process proved not only effective but also efficient.

## Follow-up experiment

Since the original experiment was conducted, WWW has become more widespread with better search capabilities. In a follow-up experiment to this research, the types of Internet tools that would be best to use for knowledge acquisition were explored. Internet tools can be classified into two categories; interactive and static. Communication tools would be classified as interactive since the user must 'connect' with other users in order to acquire information. Locator tools, information retrieval tools and browser tools would all be classified as static since the user employs these tools independently from other users. The results from the previous research suggested that communication tools would be the most appropriate for knowledge acquisition. In order to test this proposition, a follow-up search using static Internet tools was conducted to see if the same information obtained using interactive Internet tools could be located. Gopher searches (using Veronica) and WWW searches (using WWW catalogue search tools, Excite and Alta Vista) were conducted using the keywords 'multimedia', 'multimedia software' and 'multimedia software reviews'. These searches returned mostly newsgroup postings, books and site listings. WWW searches returned Jamie Siglar's Multimedia Authoring Systems FAQ (1996) and two other major multimedia WWW sites: Index to Multimedia Information Sources (Gibbs, 1996) and Maricopa Center for Learning and

Instruction Multimedia Authoring Web (Levine, 1996). Although a wealth of information was located on multimedia and even on the specific packages, detailed comparisons such as those required to develop the expert system were not found. One interesting note came from the response to the Multimedia Authoring Systems FAQ questions asking which multimedia software is the best and which software should be used. The reply was that an ‘authoring specialist’ must answer these questions given the subject matter, delivery platform and design specifications of the project. This appears to support the contention that static tools (particularly browser tools) are important for initially understanding the domain and identifying the experts, but interactive tools are still necessary in order to gain specific knowledge.

## Implications/issues

In the following paragraphs, the implications of this research are discussed. We admit that this exercise in building expert systems is preliminary and more complex expert system developments may result in different experiences with the Internet. The expert system created for the multimedia kiosk is general in nature. For the most part, the scope was limited to six authoring software packages; the packages can be distinctly divided without too much controversy and much of the information received can be sufficiently verified. In addition, the expertise can be adequately imparted in written form and secrecy was not required. However, the issues raised by this experiment are applicable to knowledge search through the Internet.

This research demonstrated that the Internet is a viable option as an information resource. Four major advantages can be found using the Internet as an information resource: the availability of multiple experts in multiple domains, the interaction of domain experts and end users, time/cost savings, and convenience. The concept of using the Internet for knowledge acquisition opens the commercial possibilities for an expert system developer who does not have ready access to multiple experts in a domain. In addition, using the Internet as an expertise resource opens the possibilities for extracting knowledge from multiple experts in multiple domain areas. Having both domain expert and end user opinions, a knowledge engineer could gather viewpoints from both which create a more comprehensive knowledge base than would be generated from experts alone. The most important aspect that makes the Internet such a valuable tool is the human connections, not the physical resources. The Internet global community has no boundaries or hierarchies. The Internet provides the opportunity for any individual to connect with some of the greatest minds in the information community. 'The people who communicate on the Internet also provide meaning and understanding – they create a synergy that's not possible with human-machine linkages alone. It's the human-to-human linkages that are important because it's people that ask questions and people that answer questions and people that discuss issues …' (Ladner and Tillman, 1993, p. 48). The time savings in locating experts, conflict resolutions and information assimilation alone could save considerable costs in expert system development and business analysis. Finally, the convenience to the experts, knowledge engineer and businesses can not be understated. Researchers can check, research and retrieve information when they want, without ever getting out of their chairs. 'The bottom line is that the Internet supports the largest and most directly connected community in the world' (Quarterman, 1993, p. 83).

The major drawbacks for using the Internet as an information resource for expert systems development and business operations are the uncertainty of the 'expertise' of the responders and the subjectivity and uncertainty of locating the 'right' experts. The knowledge acquired is the opinions of experts, and dealing with multiple experts involves accuracy and recency problems (Mathieu and Woodard, 1994). The expert system and business operations are dependent not only on the knowledge incorporated into them from the experts, but also on the nature of the knowledge (fact or opinion) and from whom the knowledge is elicited. Once an Internet resource has been identified, it is still necessary to evaluate the resource as one would any other information source. It can be very difficult to evaluate adequately the quality of the resource (Kalin and Tennant, 1991). Most important is verifying the intent and expertise of the experts. Interactive tools such as ListServs and Newsgroups are more likely to receive greater scrutiny of experts and immediate rebuttal of an expert's claim. Static tools such as Gopher and WWW would have a bigger problem in this regard since the information may be biased by the provider and may not be substantiated. We therefore suggest that the user check the status of static information with interactive resources whenever possible when the provider's expertise is not authenticated a priori.

An additional concern is that the Internet is not really owned by any one organization and no-one is in charge: there is no centralized or organized effort for support and documentation. The Internet has been described as having a process-driven focus (i.e. this is what the Internet can do) rather than an application driven focus (i.e. this is how one finds information) (Perry, 1995). Ahrens and Esquer (1993) found that the actual process of finding information using the Internet is both labour-intensive and time consuming and, in addition, using commercial databases such as those available on the Internet for bibliographic retrieval requires specialized training or access to a special librarian. Furthermore, some organizations' e-mail users are becoming overwhelmed with a barrage of useless messages or electronic 'junk' mail which interferes with the user's information processing capability and causes a negative effect of the usefulness of this communication tool (Motiwalla, 1995). However, there are recent improvements to information access on the Internet including new search tools which enable users to locate and summarize information on the Internet (Information Today, 1995), intelligent agents which help reduce information overload over the Internet (Maes, 1994) and an Internet Softbot which provides an integrated interface to the Internet (Etzioni and Weld, 1994). Security on the Internet continues to be a major issue and strategies from data encryption to 'firewall' protection have been suggested.

For all users of the Internet as an information resource, the following types of questions need to be addressed: What is the appropriate group for this posting, or How do I find the experts? Are there experts who are available and willing to respond? What types of security issues need to be addressed in posting to a newsgroup/ListServ and/or using the Internet? What confidence level can be placed in responses received over the Internet? What questions and more importantly subject headers will attract the 'best' responses?

## Conclusions

This exploratory study has demonstrated that the Internet can be used for knowledge acquisition. This research provided documentation demonstrating that the Internet is a viable information source. Information is everywhere and any technique that works is appropriate for gathering knowledge (Carrico et al., 1989). The Internet is just one more technique available in knowledge acquisition. 'The most successful expert systems have not used any one approach to knowledge acquisition ... The only approach which they all have in common is to redesign the system in light of experience and to adopt a knowledge acquisition technique most appropriate to the particular domain' (Cleal and Heaton, 1988, p. 173).

Our experience suggests that static tools such as the WWW can be used by knowledge engineers to develop a familiarity with the domain knowledge. However, interactive Internet resources seem crucial for knowledge acquisition and verification. Many important issues that have been outlined above still need to be addressed. The evaluation of the efficiency and effectiveness of the Internet approach requires further investigation and research. The Internet is not, however, a panacea. It has a long way to go before it becomes the ‘ideal’ information resource, but the ability to use it as a knowledge source, even under limited conditions, opens the door to many other commercial and research opportunities.
