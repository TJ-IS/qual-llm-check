Journal of Information Technology (1996) 11, 261–272

# Reengineering information sharing behaviour in organizations

ANITESH BARUA and SURYANARAYANAN RAVINDRAN
Center for Information Systems Management, Graduate School of Business, The University of Texas at Austin, USA

Organizational restructuring focuses on making business operations more flexible and responsive to customers. This has led to team based decision authority structures and horizontal business processes. Concurrently, the management of information has been decentralized in many organizations. Horizontal processes create information interdependencies among previously independent tasks, and call for high levels of information sharing among decision units. However, the decentralization of information management raises the possibility that units controlling specific information repositories may not readily make them available in the required form to other units, leading to poor organizational payoff. Thus it is critical to create an environment which will promote effective information flows across the organization. We suggest that mechanisms involving organizational outlook, social orientation, equitable information processing capability and communication across decision units regarding information requirements can help mitigate the information sharing problem. Using stylized game-theoretic models, we show that these organizationally oriented mechanisms have a sound economic foundation. We use these results to build a managerial basis for improving information exchange.

## INTRODUCTION

One key aspect of the organizational restructuring revolution of the 1990s is a process oriented perspective (Hammer and Champy, 1993), where an organization is viewed as a collection of integrated horizontal business processes. For instance Colby H. Chandler, the CEO of Kodak, introduced a campaign to encourage 'horizontal thinking', whereby employees can work more effectively with peers in other functions (Kanter, 1989). It was intended to integrate a segmented organizational structure and culture, and shorten chains of command. According to Ron Ashkenas of the consulting firm of Robert H. Schaffer & Associates, as companies become more specialized, and as work becomes more complex, the need is to integrate disciplines rather than separate them (McCartney, 1995). Such integration leads to team based decision making, which in turn creates information interdependencies among previously isolated decision units (Davenport et al., 1992; Applegate, 1994; Davenport and Stoddard, 1994).

Fuelled largely by advances in distributed database and networking technologies, there has been a shift in the information management paradigm. In many organizations, business applications and their associated data are being created and managed by individual decision units rather than by a central MIS group. As a result of a 1993 internal assessment related to the Malcolm Baldridge National Quality Award, a study at IBM recommended a move from centralized host-controlled computing to a decentralized computing environment (Diefenbacher, 1995). Transcanada Pipelines in Calgary, Canada, created functional systems teams and assigned them to 21 business units in an attempt to decentralize applications development and systems support (Moad, 1994). Both American Express and AT&T have created new information management roles for senior executives in various business areas, which complement the CIO's responsibilities (Davenport, 1994). The new roles of the senior business executives include establishing policies for creating and maintaining information repositories, initiating efforts to improve information quality, and negotiating the sharing of information across units.

This new information management paradigm has the potential advantage of enhancing information quality because the primary user unit is in charge of maintaining its information. Thus, it may address the needs of users within corporate departments and decision units. However, unless there are appropriate incentives, the decision units may not make their information accessible to others (Mahnke, 1989). As shown in Figure 1, we suggest that distributed information management and team based business processes may not complement each other in the absence of mechanisms to promote effective information exchange across the organization.

Steve Krantz has shown how cross-functional teams increase the need for seamless information flow across the organization (Diefenbacher, 1995). Bass Taverns, a UK pub retailer, implemented sweeping changes by redesigning business processes, retraining staff, and updating information systems. The restructure resulted in a 300-fold increase in the information intensity of the operations, and the need for much higher levels of information flow across the organization (Massey, 1994).

The communication and information sharing capabilities of information technologies (IT) are causing traditional hierarchical organizations to be replaced with newer forms (Powell, 1987; Drucker, 1988). Unfortunately, the assumption that IT leads to effective information flow is highly questionable. IT can provide the infrastructure for information exchange; however, the ultimate sharing decisions rest with managers and users, and seamless information sharing is by no means a natural outcome within an organization. According to David Hoffman of Andersen Consulting ‘... while technology can open up the doors to information sharing, it can close them if not properly managed’ (Schwartz, 1995). As noted by Keen (1981) and Markus and Pfeffer (1983), while advances in IT break down barriers to information sharing, the biggest impediment to the exchange of ‘right’ information is the fear of negative political consequences of sharing data with others. Goodhue et al (1992) suggest that information systems designed to share data across functions and departments in an organization are liable to face resistance from users because of perceptions of loss in flexibility. Robert Bernard of the consulting firm of Whittman-Hart observes that as firms move away from host systems, and as departments generate their own data, there is a lack of information sharing (Breskin, 1996). From these and many other citations in the business press, we conclude that while a decision unit may have an obligation to provide information support to other units, it may not have the incentive to share its information. This is due to a variety of reasons, including a loss of control and authority, and additional information processing effort and resource requirements.

Our research focuses on the means of bringing about convergence between organizationally desirable and individually preferred information exchange outcomes. We develop economic models of information sharing, analyse them using non-cooperative game theory, and use the insights so derived to develop an organizational model with implications for senior management and corporate MIS. We show that organizational outlook and vision, social orientation, equitable IT resources and skills, and structured communication between decision units are some enabling mechanisms which can help mitigate the information sharing problem.

## Rationale and prior literature

Prior MIS research in the area of information exchange has focused on the interdependence between organizations or organizational subunits, and on partnerships among such entities. Barua and Whinston (1991) address the information and communication requirements of interdependent decision units through the development of an organizational economics model. Boynton and Zmud (1987) emphasize the information economy aspect of IT-intensive organizations, and the need to create linkages among the subunits of the organization.

MIS research also focuses on the impact of politics on various aspects of IT implementation in organizations. Davenport et al. (1992) and Markus (1983) discuss the role of politics in stimulating information flow in organizations, and in providing explanations for organizational resistance to MIS implementation. Markus (1983) states that one unreasonable but commonly held view of organizations is that all organizational members share common goals. Davenport et al. (1992) show that IT, far from eliminating hierarchies and acting as a catalyst for information sharing, has had the opposite effect.

Similar concerns are echoed by industry professionals. Wohl (1995) suggests that for common group activities like information sharing and decision making, we need facilitating mechanisms that go well beyond the capabilities of technological tools which merely offer shared files. According to Cyril Brookes of GrapeVine Technologies, the biggest barrier to information sharing is organizational culture (Power, 1995). Following Schein's (1992) definition, culture is the pattern of shared basic assumptions that an organization internalizes within itself as it integrates into a whole. Elements of culture include whether teamwork is valued more than individuality, whether risk taking

(1995) state that organizational culture can influence the use of IT: groupware by itself may not result in productive collaborative sharing of information, but can produce the desired outcomes when coupled with other measures. Constant communication and trust are the crucial factors in the culture shift to make information sharing a value, according to Robert Vergara of MAP International (LaBarre, 1995).

How can effective partnering be brought about? Konsynski (1993) emphasizes that ‘partnership’ is a working relationship that reflects a long term commitment, a sense of mutual cooperation, shared risk and benefits, and other aspects consistent with participatory decision making. In his view partnership involves stability, permanence, interdependence, flexibility, influence and social/political networks.

The above review provides the rationale for selecting potential determinants of information sharing outcomes. We focus on informal organizational solutions, derived from the notions of reciprocity and continuity of interactions between team members. We show that these mechanisms have a sound economic basis, and how they can lead to organizationally desired information sharing behaviour, even when decision units act in self-interest.

## Inhibitors of information exchange

Before we analyse mechanisms for fostering information sharing, we identify some inhibitors of effective information sharing.

## Workforce downsizing

In the course of reengineering, the credit card division of IBM drastically reduced the number of employees (Hammer and Champy, 1993). The complexity of simple processes can be unnecessarily high as in Ford's accounts payable function with a headcount of 500, compared to Mazda's five (Hammer and Champy, 1993). A restructuring project is likely to simplify and integrate processes, leading to a significant reduction in headcount (Jeffery, 1994). We argue that this downsizing trend acts as a demotivator for sharing information. In an environment of turbulence and uncertainty, being the sole possessor of certain types of information or knowledge will make individuals feel more secure and powerful in their organization.

## The public versus private nature of information

Olson (1965) points out that a private good (whose ownership is exclusive to an individual) is worth more than a public good. In an organizational context, when a decision unit is asked to provide its 'private' information to another, the information becomes a public good, which discourages the unit from sharing it. Note that while the information held by the first decision unit is an organizational resource (which, from an organizational standpoint, should be made public to other units requiring it), the decision unit may be inclined to consider the information its private good. Approaches to encouraging data sharing vary (Stahl, 1994). Ernst & Young recommends including a sharing evaluation in employee's performance reviews. The firm itself rewards data-sharing employees with dinners, T-shirts, and other gifts. McKinsey and Co judges its consultants, in part, on how many times information they have contributed to a shared database is accessed by others.

## Myopic vision

When individuals within a decision unit perceive their rewards as linked with immediate payoffs, their attitude towards sharing information suffers. Providing necessary information to another unit often involves additional processing, and the unit may feel that it will not receive reciprocation from the other unit during the short time period of interest. Thus, while two interacting units could both be better off by providing higher quality information to each other, a myopic outlook can make both units settle for lower payoffs, which also reduces the organizational payoff. To 'foster a culture in which everyone gravitates toward working together to create synergy', the president of Firestar (a technology development company) had to revise both incentive and evaluation systems. Managers began to be evaluated on questions like 'how much business did you develop for the rest of the company, not just for yourself?' (Kanter, 1989).

## Inequity in information processing capability

When one unit is not efficient in the use of IT, it may incur a high cost in providing the information needed by another unit. For example, the inefficient unit may need to collect or process additional information in order to make it available to another unit. The low efficiency of information processing will result in higher costs, which in turn will have a negative impact on the overall information exchange outcome. The more efficient unit, anticipating the inefficiency of the other, provides a lower quality of information than it otherwise would - a natural response. Later in the paper, we discuss this aspect in more detail.

## Characterization of information sharing

Consider two software development teams working on two distinct but related applications. Each team collects information on its programming bugs and system improvement requests, and also benefits from the corresponding information collected by the other. Each team's payoff increases with the precision of the information it collects, and also with that of the information provided by the other. Further, the payoff a team derives for a given level of precision of its own information increases with an increase in the precision of information provided by the other.

As a second example of information sharing, consider a prototype of a new product. The manufacturing group maintains a listing of semi-finished components purchased from external suppliers according to their machine loading requirements. However, the purchasing unit prefers to classify these components by the type of raw materials used. One set of information cannot be directly mapped without additional processing onto the other, even though both sets describe the same product. For recurring information needs it may be possible to write translators that can transform one format or definition into another (Madnick, 1995), but not for ad hoc requirements.

As a third example, consider an investment analysis being carried out by pooling information prepared in two decision units. The two units could use different methods for depreciation of long lived assets. The reported income would differ with the method used, and make the analysis noncomparable.

The first example involves what we call information complementarity, where a higher information attribute level (e.g., more precise information) is always preferred by both units. This is similar to the adage 'the more the merrier'. The second and the third situations typify information coordination, where two decision units require a match in some attribute of information (e.g., format, definition, detail, precision, and so on.

## Stylized models of information sharing

To assess how certain mechanisms impact the information sharing behaviour, we develop two stylized models involving situations of information complementarity and coordination. Stylized models follow an economics tradition of abstracting and capturing the essence of a problem in a parsimonious manner within a quantitative model. In spite of a quantitative formulation, the key objective of these models is to provide qualitative insights. That is, regardless of the specific values of the model parameters, the derived results will hold true.

As an example, consider two precision index levels $k_{1}$ and $k_{2}$ of information collected by two decision units 1 and 2 respectively. The abstraction of the information precision level is best explained through an illustration. For the preparation of an overtime budget, suppose a manager can specify a range of 500–1100 hours. Alternatively, the manager can provide a more precise estimate, stating any one of the following ranges: 500–700, 701–900 and 901–1100 hours. In the first and the second cases, k takes values of 1 and 3 respectively. For the case of two decision units sharing information, we consider a benefit function for the ith unit (i = 1, 2) as

$$
B _ {i} = A _ {i} k _ {1} ^ {\alpha_ {i}} k _ {2} ^ {\beta_ {i}}
$$

where $0 < \alpha_{i}, \beta_{i} < 1$ , and $A_{i}$ is a constant. This is a very general functional form which has the following properties: the benefit to a decision unit (i) increases at a decreasing rate with the precision of information from both units (otherwise one can increase the benefit without limit), and (ii) exhibits complementarity, which signifies that the increase in benefit from a simultaneous increase in precision levels from both information sources is greater than the sum of the increases in benefits due to isolated increases in precision levels from either source.

Of course, there is a cost associated with increasing the level of information precision. We assume the cost for decision unit i to be convex and increasing in the level of precision:

$$
C _ {i} = c _ {i} k _ {i} ^ {\gamma_ {i}}
$$

where $c_{i}$ is a constant, and $\gamma_{i} > 1$ . Convexity implies that the cost of increasing the precision rises rapidly with the level of precision: so if the precision level is already high, it will cost much more to improve the precision level by a given amount than it would if the level was lower. The net payoff to a decision unit is its benefit minus the cost of information sharing.

A stylized model of information coordination is based on the fact that a decision unit achieves higher benefits when its own information attribute matches that of the information provided by the other unit, than when there is a mismatch. However, the other unit's preferred attribute level may be different. In the event of a mismatch of attributes of the exchanged information, both units have to undertake additional processing, which will have a negative impact on their payoffs. An illustrative example with two different information formats (F1 and F2) is shown in Table 1. Unit 1 prefers F1, and receives a high payoff (20) when the information sharing outcome is (F1, F1). Unit 2 prefers F2, and its payoff is maximum for (F2, F2). Since this is a stylized model, the results we derive will be valid regardless of the exact values of the payoffs, as long as each unit has a different preference for formats, and as long as they prefer any match, i.e., either (F1, F1) or (F2, F2) over a mismatch, i.e., either (F1, F2) or (F2, F1), both of which substantially reduce the units' payoffs.

It may appear that a simple transformation of data will solve the coordination problem easily. However, the underlying issues can be very complex. For example, data definitions of entities such as 'customer' frequently vary across organizational units, requiring considerable information reprocessing to map data received from elsewhere into one's own framework.

We use the above models to predict the information sharing actions of decision units. Our basic premise is that the decision units act in their best interest, and that in deciding on its information exchange choices, a unit considers what the other unit chooses to do. We use the concept of a non-cooperative Nash equilibrium (Myerson, 1991), which is a pair of information precision levels provided by the two units such that when one unit chooses its Nash level, the other can maximize its payoff only by choosing its own Nash level. The Nash equilibrium can be derived by setting to zero the first derivatives of the units' net payoffs with respect to their information precision levels, and then solving for the optimal levels. However, to obtain deeper insights, we highlight our results through some numerical examples.

## Analysis and results

First we consider a single information sharing interaction between two units. The payoff matrices in Tables 2 and 3 in the Appendix were derived from the net payoff function by assigning specific parameter values. We considered four precision levels, 1–4, labelled minimum, low, medium and high. In the first example, $c_{1} = c_{2} = c$ , implying equality in information processing capability, while unequal capabilities are considered in the second.

The Nash equilibrium outcome (precision levels 2,2 with payoffs 40,40) marked with \* is inefficient, both from the decision units' and the organizational standpoints. There are two other information sharing outcomes marked \*\* (e.g., precision levels 4,4 with payoffs 44,44) which can increase the payoffs to both decision units and hence the organization. Why do the decision units choose an inferior outcome? It is the absence of a 'punishment' scheme for deviant behaviour that keeps both the units away from achieving more desirable information sharing levels (later in this paper, we discuss what punishment would entail in an organizational setting). Since the interaction takes place only once, by choosing a level of precision higher than the Nash a decision unit risks the possibility of not getting a reciprocal level of precision from the other unit. For example, if unit 1 provides precision level 3, from Table 2 it is evident that unit 2 can obtain a high payoff (53) by providing precision level 2, instead of reciprocating with level 3. Since both units use the same line of reasoning (the possibility of being 'cheated' by the other unit), there will be an inefficiency in information exchange. This is similar to the Prisoner's dilemma problem in economics (e.g., Myerson, 1991).

In the second numerical example (Table 3), where unit 2 has a lower information processing capability, the single interaction payoffs (23,21) are lower than when the second unit is as efficient as the other in which case the single interaction payoffs are 40,40 as shown in Table 2). Note that when the information processing capabilities of the decision units are the same (as in the first example), the maximum payoff outcome for the organization (levels 3,3 with payoffs 45,45) is also the best for each decision unit, and therefore potentially achievable. This is not always true when one unit is less efficient than the other. For example, in Table 3, decision unit 1 prefers the information sharing outcome 3,3 over the organizational optimal 3,2. Thus, an asymmetry in information processing capabilities of the decision units may be unfortunate from an organizational standpoint, since it could prevent the two units from reaching the organizational maximum.

What organizational conditions can lead the decision units to adopt one of the better outcomes marked \*\*, where the payoffs for both units are higher than those of the single interaction Nash? If the decision units interact repeatedly in an environment with no foreseeable end to the information sharing process, any of the better outcomes can be attained, if the organization takes a long term perspective by lowering its discount factor for future payoffs. This is similar to the folk theorem in non-cooperative game theory (e.g., Myerson, 1991), which suggests that any outcome which is desirable from an individual standpoint can be achieved with an appropriate discount factor. Which of these outcomes is achieved depends on the discount factor implicit in the organizational outlook and vision.

What is so critical about long term interactions? First, the threat of punishment for engaging in deviant information sharing behaviour is credible only in a long term setting. By the same token, the knowledge that there is scope to punish deviant behaviour encourages a decision unit to engage in reciprocal information sharing behaviour. In an example of information as power, Davenport (1994) describes how a certain group in an organization resisted transferring knowledge about a new IT to other groups, in order to stay indispensable. We would argue that such behaviour results from the short-term perspective of the group (and possibly that of the organization). In the long run, the group withholding the information would not have received reciprocation from other groups, and this would certainly have had an impact on the group's attitude towards sharing its own information with others.

## Improving coordination in information sharing

The payoff matrix in Table 1 suggests three potential information sharing outcomes:

1 both units provide F1

2 both units provide F2

3 each unit randomly chooses between F1 and F2, with chances which make the other unit indifferent between providing either format.

The third outcome is derived from one's own payoffs and preference (which depends on one's requirements and information processing capability), a knowledge of the other unit's preference, and an expectation about the other's choice. While the units have different preferred outcomes, they do not ignore each other's preferences, because that may result in a mismatched outcome and lower payoffs. It is the information interdependency that forces such cooperative behaviour, even as each decision unit acts in self-interest. However, there is always a chance of a mismatch between the attributes of information provided by the two units. In team based organizations, the decision units are considered as peers; such a relationship makes it organizationally difficult to implement a scheme where one decision unit first announces its intent, and where the second unit makes its choice contingent on the announcement. Thus, any feasible communication scheme to improve coordination of choices must involve simultaneous announcements of intent.

What strategy can be adopted to mitigate the coordination problem? By introducing a structured, non-binding communication mechanism (similar to the notion of cheap talk introduced by Farrell in 1987), where the decision units indicate their intent to provide information of a specified format, we demonstrate that the mismatch problem in the actual exchange of information can be partly addressed. In our current problem setup, this may take the form of a communication process (designed either as a special meeting or as a forum over an electronic bulletin board) where the two decision units announce their intended choice. Each unit selects (for the purpose of announcement only) a format which maximizes its expected payoff, contingent on its beliefs or expectations about the other unit's announcement. If the announced formats match, they are provided during the actual information exchange round. More formally, by introducing an additional round of communication, the units get a second chance to match their intended actions. If so, they will choose either F1 or F2 (depending on the match in the announcement phase) in the actual round of information exchange. If there is a mismatch in the announcement phase, then the decision units have to resort to making a choice based on their expectations about the action to be chosen by the other. The net effect on the coordination between the two units from this communication is positive – as seen in an example in the Appendix, it increases the chances of a match between the choices of the two units.

## A managerial model of effective information exchange

The theoretical results discussed above can be integrated into a two-stage model involving organizational and IT related mechanisms as shown in Figure 2. We suggest that appropriate organizational outlook and social orientation will foster effective information sharing by creating perceptions of continuity, reciprocity, cohesion and reputation. Further, IT factors such as IT resource equity and communication of intent also help increase information exchange effectiveness. For information exchange to be effective, a decision unit must provide and receive relevant information to and from other units (see Barua et al. (1989) for a formalization of information relevance). Two complementary ways to induce relevant information exchange involve fostering a willingness to provide information and using the deterrent effect of punishment.

## Willingness related factors

Continuity implies that there is no explicit end to the interactions between decision units. Co-operative information sharing behaviour among the decision units will emerge when the members perceive their interactions to continue in the foreseeable future. Conversely, when the interactions are based on short term interest, information exchange outcomes will be inefficient for the organization as well as the individual units. As members of the organization are rewarded not only for meeting current budgets and projections, but also for future expected benefits, the perception of continuity is reinforced.

Reciprocity is the willingness to meet the needs of another member or decision unit within the organization, with the anticipation of receiving similar behaviour in return. Covering for someone who needs to take time off is based on reciprocity. Reciprocity does not signify altruism, but is based on self-interest. Employees can acquire power by controlling access to information that others need to do things that are important to them (Markus, 1984). Thus, in an information interdependent organizational world the reciprocity factor assumes critical status – one unit may be willing to provide such access in anticipation of a return favour from another unit. Note that reciprocity and continuity are related but distinct concepts, and that both result from the outlook of the organization, an issue we discuss below.

## Punishment related factors

In an organizational setting, punishment can take various forms including the loss of reputation and credibility, and the organizational equivalent of ostracism for not being a ‘team player’. Reputation is the level of credibility and standing of an organizational member. Members would like to be recognized for meeting commitments, for possessing team player abilities, and for being faithful to functional and organizational goals. Low social orientation can be expected to impact negatively on the desire of the members to develop and maintain their reputation within the organization. When decision units across the organization agree on a specific set of information exchange actions, each expects the others to adhere to the agreed outcomes. The desire to maintain reputation, and be a team player, can ensure that these actions are carried out.

Part of the process of bringing about alignment between decision units is the aspect of being willing to discipline deviant members. Punishing deviant information sharing behaviour involves a cultural or social issue of being able to take collective action against a unit which refuses to reciprocate appropriately. Thus, in an environment where such collective punitive action is impossible to take due to factors such as lack of cohesion among managers and organizational bureaucracy, inefficient information sharing behaviour is likely to be encountered even when the interactions have no foreseeable end. Cohesion is the degree to which members conform to accepted norms of behaviour. If the accepted norm is cooperation, failure to conform may result in social punishment. For example, a strong peer pressure in team based organizations may discourage deviation from existing norms, and act as a surrogate for supervisory roles (Barua et al., 1995). To achieve cooperation in a world of continuity and persistent interactions, the organizational culture must emphasize the importance of cohesion and camaraderie among its members.

## Organizational outlook and social orientation

An appropriate organizational outlook and social orientation can reinforce continuity, reciprocity, cohesion and reputation effects. Outlook is the temporal factor that describes the short- or far-sightedness of an organization. Does the organization stress the importance of short term benefits over the realization of slightly lower benefits that are sustained over a longer time period? A long term outlook fosters teamwork through the perception of continuity of interactions. The aspect of reciprocity is also strengthened, as members of the organization realize that reciprocal behaviour is necessary to secure cooperation from other groups.

Social orientation is the degree to which the members of an organization interact in non-work related settings such as company sponsored activities, family events, athletic leagues, or ‘open-houses’ (Field and Abenson, 1982). Kanter (1989) points out the importance of face-to-face relationships among employees. For example, Digital Equipment Corporation runs a helicopter service to and from their major New England facilities to permit people to get together and develop rapport. As the members of an organization perceive that part of their social needs is being fulfilled by the organization, we can expect a higher degree of camaraderie and organizational commitment. This will result in higher group cohesion and greater desire to maintain credibility and reputation.

## The IT link

To complement the organizational factors discussed above, our model enumerates certain technology related factors. These are coordinating mechanisms to promote an information partnership between decision units, thereby directly leading to better information exchange outcomes.

Training in the use of IT resources is an important mechanism that can help bring about equity in information related capabilities. Earlier, we showed that unequal information gathering and processing skills across an organization can adversely affect information sharing outcomes. Equitable information processing capability is thus posited to have a favourable impact on information sharing. Equitable allocation of IT resources (technology and systems) is also expected to improve information exchange outcomes, by the same reasoning.

The extent to which decision units are provided with opportunities to learn about the systems used by other decision units may provide a better understanding of procedures, goals and objectives of other units, and thereby increase the ability of decision units to engage in the equitable exchange of information. Davenport (1994) provides examples of organizational inhibitors of reengineering process changes, e.g., an organizational culture that supports management along individual functions rather than along cross-functional processes, or a culture unreceptive to innovation, or even worse, a general inability to accommodate change. Mitigating these inhibitors requires motivation and training for the affected employees. Process innovation at Mutual Benefit Life created new case management jobs that required new skills from insufficiently trained employees. By contrast, Federal Express has institutionalized on-the-job training thereby facilitating process innovations. The key point that emerges is that a worker participating in cross-functional team based decision making must learn about the parts played by other members and teams. Additionally, if new IT is being deployed, employees must be given the knowledge to use and apply these technologies.

As we showed earlier, a form of structured communication possibly facilitated by the MIS group, whereby decision units announce their intention to share relevant information of a specified level of detail, frequency or accuracy, can partly alleviate the information coordination problem. This communication is characterized by simultaneity of announcements from the decision units, third party moderation, and multiple rounds of announcements as necessary.

## How successful will these measures be?

In the organization of the future, Charles Handy foresees the increased importance of a corporate culture that fosters the building of trust between team members (Rapaport, 1994). That critical shift in corporate culture to a collaborative, information-sharing culture is clearly difficult to achieve in any organization (Seybold, 1994). Examples of organizations that appear to have been successful in creating such environments include Corning Glass works (Kanter, 1989) and Zeta Corporation (Orlikowski, 1995). What did these organizations do to institutionalize such a culture? Corning Glass Works emphasizes 'six I's': Important relationship, longer-term Investment, Interdependent partners, Integrated organizations, Informed partners and Institutionalized partnership. Long-term investment helps equalize benefits over time. Each partner must be informed about the plans and directions of the other. Institutionalizing the partnership requires a framework of supporting mechanisms (like social ties and shared values) to promote trust.

At Zeta corporation, an incident tracking support system (ITSS) was developed to record customer service calls and how they were handled. Before the ITSS was developed, collaboration between service specialists took place face to face. ITSS reinforced the levels of cooperation and camaraderie in the specialists' team. Some comments from individuals: 'I don't care who grabs credit for my work ... we're all the support department ... this department does well because we're a team', '... we'd be in trouble if we didn't appreciate help ... if we had attitudes like you're interfering, trying to show me up.' The company's evaluation criteria stressed team work, and the communal atmosphere fostered by management contributed to the cooperative environment.

In another instance, Integrated Systems Solution Corporation (an IBM subsidiary) made the transition to a Malcolm Baldridge National Quality Award based management process and organization structure (Jarvenpaa and Ives, 1995). The objective was to move away from management by control to empowerment and teamwork. Products and services were no longer compartmentalized but cut across different areas of the centre. A computer based group support system helped reinforce team participation and innovative thinking via

However, there had to be an accompanying cultural change – where finger pointing was replaced by shared goals, mutual responsibilities and a focus on quality. This was assisted by the introduction of team awards and awards for failed but innovative ideas, and an increase in two-way communication.

Diagraph Corporation (a bar-code and inkjet equipment manufacturer) successfully moved away from a culture where its field representatives guarded their data zealously (Stahl, 1994). Diagraph's solution to encourage information sharing: ranking their performance reviews in large part on the number of 'handoffs' (i.e., customer leads) they provide to coworkers.

CIGNA Corporation Inc (Jarvenpaa and Stoddard, 1995) created cross-functional teams to provide end-to-end services to customers. There were major changes in roles, responsibilities, work flows and IT. The efforts were successful because attention was paid to creating a culture that promoted teamwork, sharing of information across teams, and learning through training.

There are other anecdotes which show how problems surfaced in cases where organizations failed to implement mechanisms to promote an environment of information sharing. At Capital Holdings Corporation (Stoddard, 1995) customer management teams were created, which led to the need for teamwork and a wide array of cross-functional skills. However, because of the prevalent feeling that 'information is power', sometimes information was withheld to influence decisions, creating resistance to the introduction of shared databases. People did not feel they were key players in a 'caste system based on who's in the know'. Collaboration and information sharing were thus inhibited. AT&T Universal Card Services 'empowered' its representatives, but initially missed the point that sharing of information across areas of business was critical (Harrar, 1994). Frito Lay's attempt to decentralize decision authority failed initially because of a lack of information flows within the organization traceable to inadequate improvements in the IT infrastructure (Applegate, 1994). Davenport (1994) points out: '... in most companies, many managers still believe that once the right technology is in place, the appropriate information sharing behaviour will inevitably follow.' He cites an organization which acquired new hardware and software, but which provided little training to its employees. Those who had the expertise with the new system did not share this knowledge, because they feared that giving away their best ideas would work against them in the context of the prevailing company culture.

## Conclusion

The organizational trends of the 1990s include flatter decision authority, business processes which require high levels of information sharing among interdependent decision units or teams, and decentralized information management. While these changes are individually attractive, their collective benefit may not be realized in the absence of a willingness among the decision units to exchange information. Thus, we require an organizational environment oriented towards sharing. Using economic arguments, we developed a conceptual model highlighting certain mechanisms to create such a setting.

Who should take charge of implementing these mechanisms? It is incumbent upon senior management to recognize the importance of information sharing and to institutionalize the perceptions of continuity, reciprocity, cohesion and camaraderie through a long term outlook and a prosocial orientation. Corporate MIS also has to play a major role in coordinating and facilitating information sharing. Traditionally, MIS has been placed in charge of the development of applications and data processing activities of the organization. However, as pointed out by Boynton et al. (1992), the current trend of delegating responsibility for planning, building and operating computing system applications to users is a step in the right direction. We see the corporate MIS group as creator of the enterprise-wide computing infrastructure to ensure equity in IT resources and skills, and as mediators in the process of information sharing between decision units. Fortunately, we are already witnessing early signs of such change in some organizations such as Transcanada Pipelines, AT&T and American Express.

## Effect of a long-term outlook on information exchange

Let the two decision units agree to provide a set of precision levels 3,3 which is superior to the Nash outcome 2,2 in Table 2. The resulting payoff to each unit is 45. When there is no explicit end to the interactions, if a decision unit receives a level of information precision which is less than the agreed upon level, it switches to a punishing strategy of providing only the single period Nash level of precision for all subsequent interactions. Thus, if one of the decision units defects from the promised level in any one period, its payoffs will be as follows: 53 in the period of defection (reflecting the single-period gain from defection), followed by 40 thereafter. We then compute a threshold discount factor, which makes a decision unit indifferent between cooperative behaviour and defection. Summing up the payoffs over an infinite time horizon, we arrive at the following equation

$$
\frac {4 5}{1 - d} = 5 3 + \frac {4 0 d}{1 - d}
$$

where d is the discount factor. If the left hand side of the equation is higher than the right, then a decision unit selects its chosen level; otherwise, it selects the single period outcome. The threshold discount factor for supporting the chosen outcome is 0.62 in this example. That is, as long as the organization values a second period payoff of P as $\geqslant 0.62P$ today, the chosen information sharing outcome can be supported.
