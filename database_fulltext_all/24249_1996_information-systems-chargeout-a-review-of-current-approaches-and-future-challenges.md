# Information systems chargeout: a review of current approaches and future challenges

J.M. VERNER, K. TORASKAR and R. BROWN
City University of Hong Kong, Hong Kong

Based on over 20 years of information systems (IS) and management accounting literature, we present a critical review of IS chargeout research. The need for such a review derives from the conceptual difficulties and empirical limitations observed in past research, and from the management challenge of rapidly growing new information technologies (ITs). Our review systematizes the objectives and cost accounting methods underlying past developments in this area. We describe the features of successful chargeout systems, recognizing the current neglect of organizational-level objectives and follow this with a discussion of the advantages of chargeout systems, and the problems involved in their use. An investigation into the context of new ITs reveals a new surge of chargeout software products that require more organizationally oriented chargeout research. Finally, we present a model of chargeout systems evolution within a wider managerial and business context.

## Introduction

Clearly, the world of computers and IS has changed radically since Nolan discussed ‘internal pricing’ (Nolan, 1974) or ‘chargeout’ (Nolan, 1977) of computer resources. Since its inception, the concept of chargeout (or chargeback) has clearly attracted a certain level of attention in the management and IS research literature. The results are exemplified in McKinnon and Kallman (1987) who relate chargeout systems to methods of cost accounting; Allen’s (1987) evolutionary levels of chargeout; some investigations into the relationships of chargeout with user attitude/involvement (Olson & Ives, 1982); and with the maturity of the IS function (Van-Lengen and Morgan, 1993). However, the research on IS chargeout appears to have faced several limitations to its progress that are reflected in both the definition, and the name, of the research area. It has been variously labelled as internal pricing (Nolan, 1974), chargeout (Nolan, 1977), chargeback (Olson and Ives, 1982), and transfer pricing (Turney, 1977) without much clarification of the reasons behind the different terms. For brevity, we use the term ‘chargeout’ in this paper.

Most of the literature does not seem to follow a clear definition of what constitutes a chargeout system (McKinnon and Kallman, 1987; Cash et al., 1988; Raghunathan and Raghunathan, 1994). A few researchers allude to chargeout systems as systems where users are charged for the IS services that they consume, but the definitions do not go into enough detail (Drury, 1982; Dickson and Wetherbe, 1985). Allen's (1987) classification of levels of chargeout is certainly more detailed, but leaves us with a number of questions; for example, how are these levels related to organizational objectives; is a higher level always more desirable? We find that by far the most useful definition of chargeout is given by Choudhury et al. (1986), who state that chargeout encompasses situations 'where there is a one-to-one relationship, in the mode of a seller and buyer, between IS and the user department'. Chargeout does not occur when this relationship does not exist. We find this to be a very useful definition of chargeout for IS research. By virtue of the prerequisite buyer-seller relationship, it provides for a clear distinction between methods that make arbitrary cost allocations and those with a buying/selling transaction that forms the basis for computing charges.

In addition to the definitional problems, the use of chargeout for IS services has faced a more philosophical challenge that questions the validity of the free-market assumption upon which the chargeout concept is based (Wetherbe, 1988). From the empirical standpoint, there has been little convergence of findings. Olson and Ives (1982) did not support a relationship between chargeout and user attitudes and involvement, and Benbasat et al. (1984) challenged the relationship between chargeout and IS maturity. Sen and Yardley (1989) question the unconditional desirability of chargeout systems implicit in the chargeout literature. Finally, more recent chargeout literature (Raghunathan and Raghunathan, 1994) has explicitly recognized the lack of empirical research concerning higher level issues, such as an organization's choice between chargeout and non-chargeout approaches to IS management.

Much of the available theory of chargeout appears to be quite general, and applies to chargeout of both operational and development/maintenance costs. However, most of the empirical literature in this area examines methods of chargeout that are specific to charging of operational costs; only a small body of work discusses development and maintenance chargeout, most of it as a post script to operational chargeout. Moreover, researchers do not appear to make any distinction between chargeout for application development and application maintenance. In this paper, we use the term application development to include routine maintenance, as well as enhancements, but not 'bug-fixing' as many organizations treat it differently.

Finally, in the environment of computers and information systems themselves, a very important metamorphosis appears to be in progress, with major implications for the future of chargeout systems. While the chargeout literature has been grappling with the basic definition, methods, and issues of IS chargeout over the past two decades, the realm of computers, IS, and the business organizations they serve, has changed dramatically. In the latter part of this paper we argue that the newly transformed IS/IT arena, together with the current state of IS chargeout research, calls for a critical re-examination of the basic paradigm and strategies of chargeout research, so as to redirect it more effectively into the future.

This paper is intended as a first step towards fulfilling the requirement for a more effective redirection of IS chargeout research. Our objective here is to review comprehensively and critically existing IS chargeout research, to delineate its most significant aspects in relation to its past successes and limitations, and to form a solid foundation for the future. The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we discuss the objectives of chargeout systems. This is followed by a review of types of chargeout systems using a multi-level classification. The features of successful chargeout methods are then reviewed, followed by the advantages associated with the use of chargeout in general, as well as the comparative advantages of specific methods. We also provide an organized review of the problems associated with the current use of chargeout. The final section discusses a new set of challenges to IS chargeout arising from the rapidly changing IS/IT arena, and presents a model for chargeout evolution in the context of management and organizational objectives.

## Objectives of chargeout systems

The literature points to many different reasons, or objectives, for the use of chargeout systems. We identify 11 categories of such objectives:

(1) cost recovery;

(2) resource allocation;

(3) effective utilization of IS resources;

(4) IS resource regulation;

(5) IS performance evaluation;

(6) planning assistance;

(7) increased user awareness/involvement;

(8) increased IS staff awareness;

(9) increased management involvement;

(10) outsourcing evaluation; and

(11) meeting contractual or legal obligations.

Overall, the various objectives are not necessarily aligned with each other (Davis and Olson, 1985), and some may also overlap. For example, a chargeout system designed with a major focus of recovering the costs of the information systems may not always be a successful tool for increasing user awareness of, and involvement in, IS. Recognizing the linkage between chargeout objectives and the different types of chargeout systems thus becomes important. Secondly, a chargeout system by itself cannot achieve any of its objectives unless it is used in conjunction with other management control methods (Popadic, 1975; Drury, 1982). Finally, in the existing literature any discussion of the advantages or benefits of chargeout always appears to be intermingled with the intended reasons or objectives for chargeout. In our view, whilst the two are related, they are not one and the same. We discuss the advantages of chargeout systems in a later section. The specific objectives for chargeout systems are discussed next.

## Cost recovery

This appears to be the most common reason given for introducing a chargeout system (Popadic, 1975; Dickson and Wetherbe, 1985; Bergeron, 1986a). If cost recovery is the only objective, and no other considerations are crucial (e.g., equity and fairness) then a fairly simple chargeout system can be introduced. However, there are two sides to the cost recovery objective: the chargeout system must recover all of the IS department's cost over the financial period and each product or service must recover its own costs (Hootman, 1977). The objectives of a chargeout system in terms of cost recovery need to be clear before design and implementation of a chargeout system can begin.

## Resource allocation

Research suggests that charging for IS is important so that resources which are in short supply can be allocated to user departments (Popadic, 1975; Bergeron, 1986a). This is especially important when charging for operational and hardware costs, which are often erratic and unpredictable. In the short term, the ability of an IS department to service the needs of its users is limited by the physical resources of the department (e.g., CPU, memory, peripheral devices, development and maintenance staff) as they are normally in fixed supply (Sollenberger, 1977). This can create a shortage of some services (Popadic, 1975; Bergeron, 1986a) and a method of allocation is needed. When departments are required to pay for their needs, the amount of money a department controls, combined with the cost of the service, will determine which department receives how much service. This is a fairer way of allocating services than other methods which may be more subjective (e.g., based on the political influence of a manager).

## Encourage effective utilization of IS resources

One objective for introducing a chargeout system is to improve the effectiveness of use of IS resources. Choudhury et al. (1986) suggest that a chargeout system produces two effects on IS resource usage in an organization: first to ensure that a department does not waste money on unnecessary IS services; and second to ensure that users make effective use of the IS which they already have. Chargeout systems may be used to allocate resources more efficiently by adjusting prices for peak and off-peak time periods (Choudhury et al., 1986; Allen, 1987). This is an example of ‘flexible pricing’, which is one way to level the demand, leading to more efficient utilization of IS resources (Cushing, 1976).

## Regulate the demand for IS resources

Many researchers have linked this objective with charge-out systems (Popadic, 1975; Cushing, 1976; Bergeron, 1986a). The underlying belief is that once users begin to pay for IS services, and thus become aware of the cost involved, they will naturally curtail any unnecessary spending on such services. Any IS services for which users have to pay will no longer be treated as 'free goods' to be consumed as much as desired (Diehr, 1975). The logic seems to apply particularly to operational costs of IS. If a job run was not quite 'picture perfect' (e.g., the print quality was slightly faded), and users do not have to pay for a rerun, they will mostly run the job again, wasting not only the printing resource but also other scarce IS resources. The result is a relatively uncontrolled, erratic, high demand for IS resources.

## IS performance evaluation

Chargeout systems can serve as a mechanism to evaluate the performance of the IS department and its management (Drury, 1982). Depending on whether an IS department is viewed as a cost or profit centre, chargeout information can be quite useful in assessing the cost control efforts of the department, or its overall profit generating ability for the organization (Hufnagel and Birnberg, 1989). The use of chargeout systems for IS performance evaluation is expected to motivate IS staff to perform their work more cost-effectively (Popadic, 1975). Chargeout systems can also be used for monitoring productivity trends in a department in an attempt to improve its efficiency (Strassmann, 1976). Similarly, the internal prices of the IS department can be benchmarked against prices that are offered by external suppliers (Philipson, 1993). However, it should be noted that this approach is applied mainly to operational costs. Due to the uniqueness of most development and maintenance activities, the associated costs cannot easily be compared with those of other organizations without involving more sophisticated and elaborate approaches (Tate and Verner, 1989).

## Planning assistance

Chargeout systems can assist IS departments in their planning and budgeting (Popadic, 1975; Allen, 1987). They invariably require formalized methods for measuring usage levels of various IS services (Henry, 1990), and such measurements can lead to a more accurate picture of the trends underlying usage. 'A primary operational advantage of chargeout systems is that they decentralize much of the planning and control effort to the user departments' (Wetherbe and Dickson, 1979). User departments are better able to plan for the future when IS services are being charged for. This is due to the increased understanding of their requirements in terms of both physical and human resources. Under these conditions user requirements can be more reliably aggregated to provide better estimates of the needs of the whole organization.

## Increase user awareness/involvement

It is claimed that if a user department has to pay for a service, it will be more concerned with the service provided (Cushing, 1976; White, 1993). In one of the early empirical studies, chargeout systems emerged as the main economic factor contributing to communication between users and the IS department (Nolan, 1977). 'Holding the user/manager accountable for costs … tends to motivate the user/manager to learn more about how to use computer-based systems' (Nolan 1977). Drury (1982) also found that the use of a chargeout system was consistent with higher levels of user cost awareness and user computer awareness.

However, the role of user awareness/involvement as an objective for chargeout systems appears to be much more complicated than thought initially. A major empirical study of chargeout (Olson and Ives, 1982) found that a chargeout system had to be explicitly designed and implemented for the defined purpose of increasing user involvement, or else it did not provide an improvement in this area. This finding appears to contradict findings by other researchers, such as Nolan (1977) and Drury (1982). It seems significant that, in Nolan's study, the maturity of the chargeout system emerged as a major intervening factor which improved user attitudes only through other factors, such as the level of understanding of the IS function and the importance given to training in general. Similarly, the awareness variables were also found to be related to 'implementing supporting EDP integrative mechanisms', such as the communication links between users and the IS department (Drury, 1982). This view was supported by Choudhury et al. (1986), who thought that increased user involvement may be a result of other general policy directives.

## Increase IS staff awareness

The use of chargeout systems can improve the cost awareness of both IS personnel and users (Buse, 1988). When IS staff enter data into the chargeout system (usually through their timesheets) their awareness of the costs of the IS services will be increased. This can lead to reductions in the overall costs of the department as the IS staff are more conscious of the prices being charged to users (Bergeron, 1986a). A chargeout system can also help in raising the awareness of IS management and encourage them to use more effective management techniques in order to reduce costs.

## Increase management involvement

Management involvement is likely to increase as the introduction of chargeout systems and transfer pricing increases communications between departments. This will need to be coordinated by senior management of the organization (Drury, 1982). Popadic (1975) claims that a chargeout system is likely to create more disagreements between users and the IS department than previously. However, he believes that these disputes will eventually escalate to a point where they must be resolved by senior management. In this way, management involvement will increase.

## Evaluate outsourcing

Chargeout systems can be used to evaluate the possibility of outsourcing various parts of the IS department's services (Philipson, 1993). An organization is not able to properly evaluate outsourcing if it cannot determine the costs of producing the services internally (White, 1993). The use of a chargeout system to evaluate outsourcing is particularly applicable when investigating the hardware and operational costs of IS as these costs are more comparable between organizations than the costs of software development and maintenance. Willcocks and Fitzgerald (1994) have suggested that pre-existing evaluation systems, such as chargeout systems, can provide arguments for, as well as against, the use of outsourcing.

## Satisfy contractual or legal obligations

Some organizations require the use of an IS chargeout system to satisfy legal or contractual obligations (Gladney et al., 1975). Legislative change may require the business organization to introduce a chargeout system. In this case, there is little possibility of a chargeout system being avoided. Davis and Olson (1985) discuss the need for chargeout systems which fulfil legal or contractual requirements.

## Types of chargeout systems

Given the variety of objectives for introducing chargeout systems, it is not surprising that many different types of chargeout systems have been reported in the literature. In this section, we identify types of chargeout system and discuss their major characteristics. Following the earlier literature, we find it useful to base our classification of chargeout systems on the cost accounting method used. The following cost accounting methods are commonly associated with IS chargeout:

(1) unallocated costs;

(2) cost allocation;

(3) cost recovery;

(4) profit centre; and

(5) negative pricing.

Before going into detail several important observations are in order. The accounting driven character of this classification calls for inclusion of the first two methods (in the interest of completeness), even though they do not meet the definition of chargeout given by Choudhury et al. (1986). This classification essentially masks out any other factors which may be important for the study of chargeout systems, for example, chargeout of development costs versus chargeout of normal operational costs. Finally, and as a consequence, no single chargeout system can be regarded as the ideal solution to all chargeout needs (Cash et al., 1988). Even within a single organization, particularly one with a large IS department, different departments may be best served by different types of chargeout systems. We now discuss details of the various accounting methods underlying the choice of chargeout systems.

## Unallocated costs

In this most rudimentary approach to accounting for IS costs, costs are not allocated to individual user departments in the organization, but are deducted in full from the organization's income statement. In other words, the department is being treated as an ‘unallocated’ cost centre. This approach may be appropriate for organizations which have little or no experience in the use of IS, i.e., Nolan's first two stages of growth (Nolan, 1973; Gibson and Nolan, 1974). Not allocating the IS costs ‘stimulates user requests and creates a climate conducive to user experimentation’ (Cash et al., 1992 p. 371). Choudhury et al. (1986) also acknowledge the appropriateness of ‘adopting an unallocated cost center approach’ in specific situations. In general, however, this is essentially a nonchargeout approach and cannot support management control objectives, such as the regulation of demand.

## Cost allocation

Since every chargeout system requires allocation of costs to different organizational units (based on some criterion), it is tempting to regard the terms cost allocation and chargeout as being synonymous. However, in the context of chargeout approaches, 'cost allocation' refers only to the simplest form of cost accounting that can be used for developing a chargeout system. Defined in this manner, the cost allocation approach to chargeout distributes IS costs in a manner which is not based on a department's actual usage of the IS department's services. Instead, costs are allocated in proportion to other departmental descriptors such as revenues, earnings or staff numbers. In this context, Choudhury et al. (1986) use the term 'arbitrarily-allocated cost center'.

McKinnon and Kallman (1987) refer to this method as allocation chargeback and consider it ideal for educating both corporate and MIS management in the types and costs of IS resources which are used by the organization. However, we see several reasons why such arbitrary cost allocation should not be considered a proper chargeout method. For one thing, it does not directly serve the purposes of allocating resources or regulating demand. Moreover, under cost allocation there is no buyer-seller relationship between the IS department and the user department as required by the definition of chargeout (Choudhury et al., 1986). Finally, cost allocation chargeout can be quite unfair to large departments which are not proportionately heavy users of IS services, and may provide no incentive for small departments to be efficient users.

## Cost recovery

The term cost recovery refers to allocation of costs to user departments based on their usage of IS facilities (Choudhury et al., 1986). The objective for the IS department is to break even by recovering both the direct IS costs and the department's share of the corporate overheads. This calls for a distinction between costing and pricing (Popadic, 1975). However, we believe that in the context of the objective of cost recovery the term 'charge rate' may be less confusing than the term 'price'.

With cost recovery, the charge rate may be set either above or below the cost of producing a service, depending on whether management wants to provide incentives or disincentives for the use of particular services. Choudhury et al. (1986) found that over 80% of senior IS managers felt that an organization's chargeout system should be 'at cost without the addition of a profit margin'. However, it has also been suggested that cost-based chargeout systems interfere with the evaluation procedures for departments and their management (Diehr, 1975), as they obscure the profitability of different departments.

The cost of producing a service represents the baseline for setting the charge rate for cost recovery. Based on how the cost is calculated, there are three different types of cost recovery chargeout:

(1) average cost;

(2) standard cost; and

(3) flexible pricing.

These are discussed below.

## Average cost

This is a retrospective chargeout process in which the average unit cost of producing a service is calculated at the end of each accounting period, and user departments are charged this unit cost rate for the number of units they consumed in that period. While guaranteed to recover 100% of the costs (Lin, 1983), and easy to explain, implement and audit (McKinnon and Kallman, 1987), average cost pricing has major disadvantages. As many of the IS department's costs are fixed, significant price fluctuations can occur in response to changing demand levels (Lin, 1983), making it difficult for the user departments to budget accurately for their usage. Average cost pricing is a poor method of controlling the use of scarce resources as it further encourages consumption of resources which are in high demand (Lin, 1983; McKinnon and Kallman, 1987). As such, this method 'doesn't really contribute to management control ...' (Popadic, 1975 p. 160). Finally, it is not a suitable method for charging of development costs.

## Standard cost

In contrast to the above, the standard cost rate is determined in advance of the use (based on anticipated total cost and usage of a service), and then applied for chargeout based on actual usage. Standard costs are normally calculated over a longer time period (e.g., a year) than average costs, thus reducing periodic fluctuations. Advantages of such a system include the user's ability to budget more accurately for their usage, and the consistency of charges from one period to next. However, to operate satisfactorily this system must have reliable forecasts of total cost of production as well as the total demand (Lin, 1983). In any event, it cannot assure 100% cost recovery, unless year-end adjustments are made (Allen 1987).

## Flexible pricing

This method attempts to control the demand for scarce resources by charging higher rates for services that are in high demand (Dearden and Nolan, 1973; McKinnon and Kallman, 1987). Flexible pricing is commonly used for chargeout of operational costs of systems, as peak load hours can be charged higher rates in order to stabilize the demand (Cushing, 1976). For development costs, flexible pricing may be used to charge a premium for rush jobs. Similarly, using this method, organizations can also subsidize new technologies to encourage their use (Pliskin and Romm, 1990).

However, it should be recognized that compared to standard costing, flexible pricing is more complex to administer, and more prone to profit/loss fluctuations at the level of the IS department (McKinnon and Kallman, 1987). More importantly, the objective of controlling resource usage may itself be flawed, when users' requirements are indeed justifiable. According to Jackson (1986 p. 177) this is 'a capacity problem, not a charging problem'. Flexible pricing can be particularly detrimental when the demand is price inelastic, as in the case of essential services (for example, printing monthly account statements).

## Profit centre

IS departments are sometimes operated as profit centres (Emmanuel et al., 1990). With the profit centre concept, management decision-making is essentially decentralized to the departments (Spicer, 1988). Each department is free to trade with inside as well as outside customers to maximize its own profit. For IS departments, this freedom may often be limited, prompting Choudhury et al. (1986) to use the term quasi- or pseudo-profit centre. However, Allen (1987) suggests that an IS department can be profitable even when restricted to internal customers, if it helps achieve organizational strategic goals.

Allen (1987) considers two variations of profit centre chargeout: first, standard costs with a mark-up or a flat percentage above cost; and second, functional or transactional pricing, which involves setting prices on a per-transaction basis (e.g., \$1 per cheque processed) and is best suited for operational costs. However, in either case the design of chargeout may also involve prices that are either fixed or market-based. These distinctions seem particularly important in the spirit of a profit concept, and are discussed below.

## Fixed price (or negotiated) contracts

In this type of system, the IS department has negotiated and entered into a detailed contract of service with the user department. Apart from the fixed price, the contract usually covers many other details, for example the delivery date, design specifications, quality considerations, response times, and so on. In the case of software development and maintenance work, it is important that the contract covers the process for re-estimating the price when there is a change to the system's requirements or scope (Cash et al., 1992).

## Market-based prices

In this mode, the internal supplier of a service can charge the user departments as much as an external supplier – in other words, the market price. This approach has been common among organizations which have their departments set up as profit centres (McKell et al., 1979). In general, the management accounting literature (e.g., Emmanuel et al., 1990) supports the use of market-based prices for goods/services transferred between departments, although the transfer price may reflect transaction costs saved due to the internal trade (Cats-Baril et al., 1988). Horngren and Foster (1991 p. 860) argue that 'When the intermediate market is perfectly competitive and when interdependencies of subunits are minimal, transferring products or services at market prices generally leads to optimal decisions'. However, due to the specialized nature of IS, free market and market prices may be difficult to ascertain for IS services, thereby requiring the use of simply negotiated prices.

## Negative pricing

Another method which has been proposed as part of an organization's chargeout system is the concept of 'negative pricing'. Negative pricing has been suggested by Pliskin and Romm (1990) as a method of regulating the use of computing resources for certain applications and technologies which require a 'critical mass' before their benefits can accrue to the entire organization. Examples of such systems and technologies include communications systems (e-mail, groupware), DBMS, network systems software, electronic data interchange, and client-server technologies. If there are not enough subscribers to an e-mail system, for example, a person with an e-mail account will not be able to contact all the necessary people. This reduces the benefits that the user can expect to obtain from the system. The majority of benefits of 'critical mass' systems are only realized when there are a large number of users.

When ‘critical mass’ technologies and systems are being implemented, the IS department should consider actually subsidizing the users to encourage them to use the system and experiment with its capabilities (Allen, 1987; McKinnon and Kallman, 1987; Pliskin and Romm, 1990). Thus, negative pricing can be seen as a special case of flexibly priced cost recovery method. Once there is a sufficient number of users, the department may reduce the subsidies and eventually commence charging for the services. In fact, Pliskin and Romm (1990) stress that negative charging is not a long-term proposition, but should only be used during the implementation and initiation phases of critical mass technologies. Traditional IS need not be negatively priced as there is little chance of stagnation in the level of demand for such services.

## Features of successful chargeout systems

The success of chargeout systems has been discussed both from a user-oriented behavioural perspective and a design perspective. For the first perspective, the success of a chargeout system is judged by the impact it has on users' decision-making behaviour. Bergeron (1986a) defines a successful chargeout system as 'one in which the users analyse their data-processing charges with the intent to control the spending of data-processing resources'. Similarly, Henry (1990) asserts that 'If users have confidence in the costing/chargeback process, they will use it to make decisions to improve the enterprise'. However, we find that this ex-post-facto approach to the success of chargeout is neither adequately developed in the literature, nor very useful from a designer's standpoint. Moreover, it essentially ignores any specific priority objective for which the organization may have intended the use of chargeout in the first place. For example, if cost recovery is the intended objective of the system, then its success should not be measured in terms of the impact on user's decisions, as suggested by this approach.

From a design-oriented perspective of chargeout success, Popadic (1975) calls for an integration of the chargeout system into the planning and control systems of the organization. However, most of what the literature has to offer in this area can be seen as a generic set of desirable characteristics of good chargeout systems, which are described below. Clearly, such a generic list should not be viewed as critical success factors for chargeout systems, but only as guidelines.

## Understandability

Chargeout systems which are understandable are easy to explain and easy to audit (Canning, 1974; Joy, 1977; McKinnon and Kallman, 1987). While ideally the understandability by both users and IS personnel is implicit, Allen (1987) believes that understandability for the users is more important. If the chargeout system is difficult to understand, users are less likely to use its output information to alter their spending patterns for services.

Chargeout systems may be difficult for users to understand when they are designed ‘around the computer instead of the data’ (Nolan, 1977). Instead of measures of computer resources (such as CPU, memory, input/output), if the users and managers are held responsible for data related measures (e.g., the number of transactions) chargeout systems will be more understandable to users, and the chargeout information will be used more often and more effectively (Wetherbe and Dickson, 1979). Perhaps the most telling statement on the need for an understandable chargeout system was put forward by Cash et al. (1992 p. 375):

'Again and again, evidence suggests that an IT charge-out system that is a gross distortion of the underlying electronics but that the user can understand is vastly preferable to a technically accurate system that no one can comprehend.'

Bergeron (1986a) found that the meaningfulness of costs to users is important for the success of the system. He found that this is true for both operational and development costs. When the information provided by the chargeout system is more comprehensible and detailed, there is a greater likelihood of it influencing the behaviour of the users and determining how they use the IS. If users are able to understand the methods which are used to calculate the costs, they are less likely to complain about the charges. Unfortunately, this is not an easy task in practice. Based on one empirical study, making the users aware of the IS costs is one of the more difficult problems facing the IS department (Drury 1982). Drury found that an increase in user awareness and understandability of costs cannot be achieved by the use of chargeout systems alone. Other management control processes must be used.

## Fairness

A successful chargeout system should be fair in the way in which it distributes charges to different departments (Dearden and Nolan, 1973; Hootman, 1977). If the system is not fair, some people will be disadvantaged by the use of chargeout. The fairness of a system is especially important when the chargeout information is being used to measure people's performances. Fairness in a chargeout system can best be achieved by setting prices in a manner which will maximize the profits for the organization as a whole (Diehr, 1975). The objective of a chargeout system should be 'to reduce corporate level sub-optimization' (Turney, 1977). In this case, the information from the system should provide a fairer reflection of the ability of particular departments to add to the overall profitability of the organization.

Interestingly, the literature distinguishes between actual fairness and perceived fairness of a chargeout system (Popadic, 1975; Hufnagel and Birnberg, 1989). From the standpoint of the chargeout success, it may be more important that people feel that they are being treated fairly, rather than whether they are actually being treated fairly. Three factors are believed to reduce managers' perceptions of the fairness of chargeout systems (Hufnagel and Birnberg, 1989):

(1) a high degree of interdependence between user divisions and MIS;

(2) restrictions on divisional managers' freedom to purchase computer services from external services; and

(3) a tendency for costs to exceed market prices.

## User controllability

A chargeout system is more likely to be successful in achieving its objectives if user departments are able to control their own costs by changing their patterns of behaviour (Bergeron, 1986a). If users are able to change both the types and qualities of services they receive, the chargeout system will have a higher chance of succeeding. However, user controllability ultimately depends on how well a user can potentially utilize the chargeout information to plan IS usage. In this context, the predictability of the charges becomes very important (Allen, 1987). If costs are not sufficiently predictable the chargeout system cannot influence user behaviour properly, as the user cannot determine which actions will be in the department's and the organization's best interests.

## Realistic pricing

It is important that the charges from the chargeout system are closely comparable with the prices that are charged in the open market (Popadic, 1975; Allen, 1987). If not, users will become disheartened and frustrated with the performance of the IS department (Olson and Ives, 1982), and may start to investigate the use of outsourcing their IS requirements. Thus realistic pricing appears to be a prerequisite before a chargeout system can help an organization to evaluate outsourcing alternatives properly.

## Performance review linkage

As a part of their routine operation, chargeout systems naturally produce cost data in a variety of ways that are well suited for performance measurement and comparisons of different organizational units. Therefore, from the organization's standpoint, the success of a chargeout system may well depend upon whether the chargeout information can be readily utilized in the performance review process for both user departments and the IS department (and its staff). In particular, comparisons between different departments or different managers should be relatively easy if standardized measurement units are used for chargeout. Thus, it would be short sighted if the chargeout design does not exploit the linkage between chargeout information and performance measurement.

Bergeron (1986a) found that user accountability for both development and operational charges was an important factor in the success of an organization's chargeout system. He defines a chargeout system as having user accountability if the user must explain and account for any variations from the estimates in the department's IS budget. The explanation of such variances is important as variations from the budget will affect the accuracy of the planning and control measures implemented by the IS department for the financial year. Making users explain these variations is a form of performance review.

## User involvement in budgeting

User involvement in the preparation of IS department budgets for both operational and development costs is important for the success of an IS chargeout system (Bergeron, 1986a). User involvement in the budgetary process includes users providing estimates of their potential IS requirements for the financial year. The use of chargeout information will be increased if users are involved in the preparation of their own part of the IS budget (Bergeron 1986b). In this way, there will be more incentive for them to use information from the chargeout system. Users will be more discerning in their use of IS services, and will be careful in their consumption and spending patterns.

## Proportional usage charges

A chargeout system is more likely to be acceptable to users, and thereby successful, if the amounts charged to different users (for a particular IS service) are proportional to their individual usage levels for that service. With this feature users are more likely to utilize past chargeout information to plan and control their hardware and operational costs. However, this may not apply to system development costs due to the lack of simple metrics understandable to users. On the other hand, operational costs can be measured in units, such as the number of transactions processed.

## Consistency

The consistency of the charges produced by a chargeout system is another factor which leads to successful chargeout systems (Popadic, 1975). Here, consistency of chargeout demands that when similar services are provided they should have similar charges (Canning, 1974; Popadic, 1975; Hootman, 1977). Charges should be levied independently of which department is being serviced. This is especially important when charging for hardware and operations where it may be quite difficult to design and administer a system which has stable prices. It is important that prices are stable, at least in the short- to medium-term, so that users can rely on prices when modifying their consumption patterns, and plan to limit their usage of IS services (Canning, 1973). Ironically, as we see below, consistency is easier to achieve for development costs. Two applications with similar functionality can normally be charged at similar rates.

Hootman (1977) uses the term ‘reproducibility’ instead of consistency. He notes that the concept of reproducibility may be contrary to the objectives of the chargeout system. For example, if the objective of the chargeout system is cost recovery, prices may change in different months (resulting in a lack of consistency and reproducibility) when the level of demand for particular services changes. One of the problems in achieving consistency in operational prices is the variation in the standard chargeout rates between different computers (Snell, 1992). This variation means that different applications may be charged differently if they are run on different machines. Depending on the design of the chargeout system, it is possible that the prices charged may vary significantly, particularly if demand is erratic (Hootman, 1977). This problem must be overcome to provide an equitable and successful chargeout system.

## Top management support

Last, but not least, the support of top management represents another factor which promotes the success of a chargeout system (Buse, 1988, p. 48). Top management support for the chargeout systems can be essential in overcoming problems typically associated with wide-ranging organizational changes during the initial implementation of any information system. Chargeout systems are often introduced as part of an organization-wide transfer-pricing scheme and senior management are normally involved in the initial decision to introduce the system. Since top management can often act as an independent arbiter in the event of a dispute, their continued support for the system can be crucial in resolving disputes during the initial chargeout operation.

## Advantages of chargeout systems

Most of the literature dealing with the advantages of chargeout involves the global axioms of improved efficiency leading to overall IS cost reductions (Buse, 1988) and benefits to the organization as a whole (Raghunathan and Raghunathan, 1994). However, given the many problems associated with chargeout, this axiomatic view of its advantages appears to be clearly inadequate from a research standpoint. Whilst, over the years, a host of authors have made scattered references to a wide variety of advantages of chargeout, the current literature lacks a systematic and comprehensive account of these advantages. Such an account is clearly a prerequisite for an effective programme of future chargeout research. We find it useful to view the advantages of chargeout in terms of the following categories:

(1) improved resource allocation and IS function efficiency;

(2) improved communication and planning;

(3) accuracy of IS costs determination;

(4) user autonomy and motivational benefits; and

(5) improved user accountability.

Before detailing specific advantages, it should be recognized that almost all of the advantages should be qualified as 'potential' advantages. This is especially important since the advantages of using chargeout are somewhat dictated by the method of chargeout used. Moreover, in a specific situation the realization of such advantages will clearly depend on the 'fit' between the method of chargeout and the organizational objectives behind the use of chargeout, as well as on the quality of its implementation. Yet, we find this organizationally-oriented categorization to be the most useful mapping of the advantages of chargeout for promoting much needed organizational chargeout research. Finally, whereas the objectives of a particular type of chargeout system refer to the specific, intended-by-design, benefits of that system, its advantages also include some unintended byproduct effects which are nonetheless beneficial.

## Improved resource allocation and IS function efficiency

Resource allocation advantages of a chargeout system are possibly more noticeable when an organization is charging for hardware usage and operational costs. In these circumstances, peak and slack periods may occur on a daily basis, and user behaviour can be more easily affected by changes in prices. With improved efficiency in the usage of IS resources, there is the possibility that the overall costs of the IS function will be reduced (Buse, 1988). This is an important benefit of using a chargeout system because, today, IS can make up such a large proportion of the costs of running an organization.

## Improved communication and planning benefits

Improved communication between the IS department and user departments may be helpful to the planning process. This has been recognized in the context of user involvement in budget preparation, and related increases in user communication in the presence of chargeout (Bergeron, 1986b). When communication links are strong, the IS department is more in tune with the needs of the users and has a better understanding of any proposed requirements of the users. However, in some situations, such information may be misleading for some people because different chargeout procedures can produce different cost estimates for the same quantity of usage of a resource.

## Accuracy of IS costs determination

A chargeout system is also useful for determining the truce costs of particular services and informing user departments of these costs (Philipson, 1993). However, care must be taken to distinguish between the costs to the organization and the prices which are charged to the user departments (Popadic, 1975). These costs may be different, depending on the chargeout system used, so different departments may need to use the chargeout information in different ways.

## User autonomy and motivational benefits

Because of the distributed nature of managerial authority and control inherent in the chargeout concept, any chargeout system is bound to promote a sense of greater user autonomy compared to the nonchargeout environment. This has been particularly recognized in the context of profit centre chargeout in which a higher degree of autonomy allows the IS department to specialize into areas of excellence (Choudhury et al., 1986), and has a motivational impact on IS managers as well as IS employees to perform better (Turney, 1977). Allen (1987) believes that under this approach ‘companies introduce new technology sooner and with better results’. In particular, when IS costs begin to take up an increasingly large proportion of the organization’s total costs, then selling some of the IS services to outside customers can be particularly desirable (Clemons and Weber, 1990).

However, these advantages must be weighed against two important characteristics of all service departments, namely, their monopolistic position with regard to internal customers, and the predominantly fixed nature of costs such as hardware and personnel costs (Turney, 1977). Particularly, the IS department holds specialized skills and intimate knowledge of the internal business organization, thereby enhancing its monopolistic position. Finally, both the accounting research (Dittman, 1972; Lococo, 1983) and the IS chargeout literature (Sobczak, 1974; Turney, 1977; Hufnagel and Birnberg, 1989) reveals concerns over whether the profit centre concept necessarily maximizes the profits of the organization as a whole. Jackson (1986) raises more basic questions related to the required profit level for a department, and whether IS departments should be making any profit at all!

## Improved user accountability

The use of a chargeout system has the benefit of making the user or manager more accountable for the decisions which are made (Nolan, 1977). When people are being held accountable for the economic performance of their departments, and are having their performance measured, they strive harder to improve their performance. Here, it is interesting to note that whereas the legitimacy of this accountability derives from user autonomy, the motivation to strive harder for higher performance clearly depends on whether the accountability is actually expected by the organizational culture, and managerial procedures, in place.

## Problems of chargeout systems

Compared to the advantages of chargeout, the literature deals more extensively with the problems of chargeout in practice. We have consolidated a wide variety of such problems into ten categories as discussed below. Where possible we critically examine relevant factors which lead to a better understanding of the problems of chargeout.

## Costly to implement

In some situations, a formal chargeout system may be undesirable because of its overall costs. Even the periodic administrative and managerial costs associated with the routine use of a chargeout system may outweigh its benefits (Popadic, 1975). Similarly, if IS services do not constitute a large enough portion of an organization's expenses, users will pay little heed to any chargeout information, and any changes to their behaviour will have little impact on their total costs (Popadic, 1975).

An additional cost to be considered here is that of educating users about the chargeout system, and about the information which it provides. Users need to be educated as to the reasons for their being charged, and how charges are calculated. If users do not understand the charging mechanism it is difficult to implement a chargeout system and force them to pay for services which they previously used to get free (Philipson, 1993). This makes education of the users essential, but the chargeout system costly.

The costs of designing, implementing and using a chargeout system may all depend on the type of chargeout system selected. Sometimes the complications involved in charging for IS services may become ‘a cost accounting nightmare of questionable utility’ (Wetherbe and Dickson, 1979). Rather than using an overly complex system, some organizations may be better off with a simpler chargeout system or even none at all. On the other hand, even if an organization decides not to use a chargeout system because of its cost, some other form of management control of IS costs, such as a ‘steering committee composed of top executives’ (Nolan, 1973) will eventually become necessary as the IS costs spiral up.

## Stagnates IS growth

Research suggests, quite strongly, that IS chargeout systems should not be used in organizations which are relatively new to IS (Nolan, 1973; Gibson and Nolan, 1974; Popadic, 1975; Allen, 1987; McKinnon and Kallman, 1987; Pliskin and Romm, 1990). When the IS function is not yet mature, user departments may be unsure of the technology and may need some prompting to experiment with IS services on a trial basis. This will help them to understand some of the benefits which may be available from the use of information technology. Once the use of IS matures, users normally become aware of the benefits that IS can provide. Then, charge-out systems can be safely introduced.

The stagnating influence of chargeout may be particularly detrimental in the area of research and development (R&D) related to IS. It has been suggested that a chargeout system may reduce user departments' level of R&D (Canning, 1973; Cushing, 1976; Drury, 1982). Users may be unwilling to experiment if they are forced to pay for the organization's IS related R&D. Explorations into new hardware/software platforms are inevitable when an organization is implementing its IS strategy, as part of its competitive business strategy, and any management control technique which discourages R&D may prove costly in the long term.

## Causes a short-term focus

Use of IS chargeout may divert managerial attention away from the organization's IS and business strategy, and result in a short-term focus in general. 'The lack of explicit consideration of strategic MIS planning may result in a planning process with a short-range time frame' (Dickson and Wetherbe, 1985 p. 137). Long-term IS goals may be neglected when chargeout systems are introduced in organizations that use financially driven evaluation procedures for departmental and managerial performance reviews (Popadic, 1975). For example, when a user department cannot afford the charges for a system development project, the project may be dropped even if the system has major long-term benefits. In a nonchargeout environment, alternative corporate funding sources are more likely to be tapped so that such a system can be developed. This short-term focus, brought about by the use of chargeout, may partly be rooted in the inherent difficulties of assessing the costs and benefits of systems that have longer time horizons (Earl, 1992; White, 1993).

## May favour external purchases

One problem associated with chargeout systems is that external purchases ('outsourcing') may be favoured because internal prices seem high. Restructuring of a business, which is often accompanied by the introduction of chargeout and transfer pricing, gives users the opportunity to find external suppliers for their IS services. This, combined with cheaper prices, may attract the users to outsourcers. Despite apparent cost savings, the purchase of IS services from external suppliers may be dysfunctional for the organization as a whole due to the accounting complexities of incremental (or marginal) costs (Choudhury et al., 1986). Although the average cost of the internal service may be higher than the outsourcing, its incremental cost may be lower (Diehr, 1975). Of course, outsourcing may indeed be cheaper, in some situations, due to the vendor's economies of scale (Dué, 1992). However, Cash et al. (1992) report external suppliers initially setting artificially low prices to procure the business, only to increase prices once a steady business relationship has been formed.

## Non-integrated IS development

Popadic (1975) suggests that with the use of chargeout systems, ‘there is the danger that a non-integrated approach to systems development will evolve'. This problem can be seen as a direct consequence of decentralized decision-making encouraged by the use of chargeout systems. Different user groups may choose the cheapest option to suit their own needs, and reject an alternative that is more compatible with the rest of the organization. Such non-integrated development is often more costly for the organization because of department level duplication. Similarly, the use of chargeout systems might make it harder to develop systems which span a number of departments in the organization. There is no set way in which the IS department can split the costs of a multidepartmental system equitably between departments (Dickson and Wetherbe, 1985).

Dickson and Wetherbe (1985) also suggest that 'Compared with chargeout, using an MIS steering committee to establish priorities adds a centralized, high-level perspective to planning decision making'. This corresponds with Drury's (1982) observation that 'where chargeback systems are in use, a steering committee may conflict with it' (Drury, 1982). Steering committees usually include members of centralized senior management, whilst a chargeout system is designed to decentralize and delegate the control of IS services to the user departments.

## Dependence on economic conditions

In the absence of a chargeout system for IS services, the entire IS function can be seen as being relatively insulated (and protected) from any financial fluctuations of the organization and those of the nation's economy (Drury, 1982). Examples of such fluctuations include major changes in the yearly balance sheet of the organization, or simply an economic recession. Under these conditions, use of chargeout systems indirectly favours the departments which are wealthier (Canning, 1973) or more powerful. In hard times, departments that have more funds to spend may get preferential treatment simply because they can afford to pay for IS services.

## Creates controversy and disputes

The implementation of a chargeout system has the possibility of creating friction within the organization due to disputes over the prices which are charged to users (Choudhury et al., 1986). If users think they are being treated unfairly, they may question the charges, and have reduced satisfaction levels with the performance and services offered by the IS department. This may reduce the possibility of good quality systems being developed. On the other hand, if the IS department personnel feel that their department is being paid inadequately for their services, they may be less likely to provide a quality service.

## Monopolistic IS operations

The use of chargeout for IS services can, in effect, create an IS department monopoly over provision of IS services (Diehr, 1975; Turney, 1977). Once the chargeout is in place the IS department potentially becomes organizationally endorsed and eventually the only agency to fulfil the IS needs of the organization. Under these circumstances it may charge users higher than normal prices. This may have adverse effects on the organization as a whole, as some departments may not be able to afford the IS services that would be truly beneficial for them and for the entire organization.

## Decentralized IS function

Due to prior organizational factors, such as organizational culture or management style, it is quite possible that the IS function in an organization may be fully decentralized to begin with. When fully fledged separate IS services are in existence in user departments, there is no need for organizational level IS chargeout as all requests for services will be directed to the particular user department's IS function. The use of normal budgeting procedures in the organization and its departments will ensure sufficient management control over the user department, thereby making any chargeout system unnecessary (Popadic, 1975).

## IS investment evaluation difficulty

Willcocks (1989) discusses problems which are caused by the use of chargeout systems when an organization is evaluating investments in IS and suggests that chargeout systems (among other things) only serve to make such evaluations more complex than necessary. 'Such internal procedures [including chargeout] merely confuse the issue' (Willcocks, 1989). One source of such confusion is the wide variety of divergent cost estimates that can be produced by chargeout systems. This argument does not imply that chargeout systems should not be used, but recommends discretion in the use of chargeout information.

## New challenges, new products and chargeout evolutionary model

Thus far, we have presented a consolidated and critical review of the IS chargeout research to date. While the need for such a review derives from the conceptual difficulties and empirical limitations observed in past research, its value (for directing future chargeout research) can be traced back to the management challenge of the rapidly growing new ITs. In this final section, we first delineate the new IT challenges facing chargeout research, and highlight the growing number of software products intended to meet that challenge. Consistent with an evolutionary view of chargeout systems development (VanLengen and Morgan, 1993), we present a model which guides chargeout evolution without losing track of the larger context of organizational, managerial and control issues related to IS chargeout.

## A new set of challenges for IS chargeout

We find that a whole new set of challenges have emerged for chargeout research, as a result of the dramatic changes in the realm of computers and IS in the past couple of decades. Radical transformations have occurred in computers and business information systems since the mid 1970s, due to a whole set of new hardware and software technologies. This includes hardware technologies such as desktop or personal computers (PCs), computer networks and telecommunications technology; new software technologies include powerful database management software, 4GLs and CASE tools, application generators, and object oriented and AI-based programming approaches. The result, from the standpoint of chargeout systems, is a highly complex and fundamentally transformed IS/IT arena. In the IS environment of the 1960s, the dominant entity was the giant mainframe computer running centralized applications for large numbers of users throughout the entire corporation. Today, the mainframe is replaced by numerous powerful PCs, standalone or networked, running a whole new genre of software applications such as client/server, EDI and document management, groupware, hypertext and multimedia software, not to mention complex network management software.

With the latest information technologies, such as the PCs and laptop computers, many user departments can bypass the centralized IS department altogether and purchase both hardware and software from within their own budgets. This can lead to some problems in that, today, an organization may not have any idea, and no way of finding out, what their computing and IS related costs really are. To address such hidden cost practices within the organization, we believe that the chargeout framework must explicitly consider how the IS budgets are set up, and reported, throughout the hierarchy of the organization.

Other problems that arise have to do with the servicing and maintenance of microcomputers, any networks they are connected to, and any computing help required by the user department. Traditionally, the IS department may have provided this help but later larger organizations have set up information centres (IC) which deal with many of the user department's problems. As a result some of the problems were then transferred to the ICs. In some organizations, the collapse of the IC concept has resulted in departmental information specialists providing some of the help, but the same old chargeout problems reappear for services that cannot be provided from within the user department itself – that is network charges and maintenance, hardware maintenance and so on still remain.

The above issues are merely illustrative, and by no means exhaustive, of the kinds of challenges for chargeout we now face. In fact, we believe that such complexities suggest the need for a critical re-examination of the basic paradigm and strategies of IS chargeout. Research investigations specifically dealing with the newer information technologies clearly support our view. For example, Marcus (1987) found that chargeout was not useful in the case of office communication systems. Concerns are expressed over the applicability of chargeout for development of experimental and innovative IS applications, which almost always involve new IT (Perry, 1988). In this context, negative pricing chargeout certainly needs to be investigated further, particularly for the critical mass technologies of telecommunications (Pliskin and Romm, 1990).

The compounded difficulties of IS chargeout in view of the new ITs are clearly confirmed by practitioners. Cafasso (1995) calls for new approaches to chargeout of client/server costs due to the distributed computing involved. Reporting on Amtrak's second attempt to establish a successful chargeout system. Burch (1994) concludes that corporations should spend more effort on establishing the business requirements for their chargeout systems before becoming too involved with technical solutions. The challenges faced by today's capacity planners and performance analysts of computer systems (Capacity Management Review, 1994) clearly suggest the need for a major reform of the chargeout systems in view of open systems, distributed and cooperative processing, multiple platforms, and on. In the context of electronic mail, Schrage (1993) states: 'Media without market discipline breeds pathologies of wastefulness ... IS needs to invest more thought in designing chargebacks than it does in adopting CASE'. Keller and Allen (1993) see the need for a sound cost/benefit framework first to identify the costs in question, before designing chargeout systems. In conclusion, there is a clear need for more research targeted at the new ITs with regard to innovative methods of chargeout.

## New generation of software products related to chargeout

Apart from highlighting the new challenges to IS charge-out, we find that the latest practical developments in this area reveal a distinct trend towards a new generation of software products related to IS usage and costs. Ricciuti (1991) reported on several data centre management software packages used by IS managers. Today, the long list of such products includes: Red Brick's Universal Data Warehouse Blueprint (Business Wire, 17 July 1995); Horizon Technology's LANrecord 1.0 (Business Wire, 1 June 1995); Computer Associates' CA-Unicenter (Business Wire, 24 May 1995); ISICAD's InfoManager 2.0 (Edge Work-Group Computing Report, 3 April 1995); Patrol Software's Cost/Patrol (Computer Finance, 1 December 1994); ABT's PWW 2.0 (Business Wire, 21 September 1994); Legent's MICS system and Pace Applied Technology's Kommad-III (Butler, 1992); and OpenVision Technologies' OpenV\*Accounting and Make's Netmaker-XA (Paul, 1994).

From the standpoint of this paper, what is important about these software products is not their specific details, but some general implications they collectively reveal in relation to the challenges of chargeout theory and practice. Two important characteristics of these new software products are that

(1) All of them seem to be motivated by one or more new ITs (e.g., powerful PCs, massive database systems, CD-ROMS, LANs, and client-server applications involving heterogeneous platforms)

(2) Their scope and functionalities at least claim to be aimed at broader areas of IS management planning and control (e.g., capacity planning, design changes, and business value of IT)

However, the real thrust of the new software systems still seems to be mainly the automation of metering the resource units, counting transactions, and tracking the flow-rates so as to assign them to a particular user or department, rather than to focus on appropriate chargeout methods. Therefore these software products may be of limited value to management in making a decision regarding a particular chargeout methodology (Popadic, 1975). In reality they are simply carrying out a chargeout support function that was performed reasonably easily, in the past, by the central mainframe computer before the new ITs arrived. On one hand, it is reassuring to see that the espoused objectives of these new software products seem to respond to Popadic's (1975) call for integrating chargeout with broader IS management and control functions. On the other hand, we see major difficulties in fulfilling these larger objectives unless the research on chargeout takes into account the practical developments in this area.

## A model for chargeout systems evolution

Chargeout should be part of an evolutionary cycle that includes higher level management objectives within a business strategy. In Figure 1 we present a simplified view of how the chargeout strategy fits within an organization and the factors affecting its evolution.

The process should begin with specific organizational chargeout objectives with regard to IS/IT usage. These objectives should be decided by higher level management based on the organization's business strategy. The overall design framework of the chargeout system should be chosen to fit those objectives. Next, within the particular chargeout framework the exact costing method should be assigned. The organization may need to acquire one of the newer tools to measure usage, particularly if networks are involved. Departmental and total IS/IT charges, together with the original chargeout objectives, usage levels and actual costs, can be used to evaluate the success of the chargeout method used. The costs may be quite different from the charges and this may be directly related to the original objectives of the chargeout method; for example, the objective may be to increase user awareness/involvement and a profit centre chargeout method selected to achieve this. The results of a chargeout systems evaluation, together with an evaluation of the business value of IS/IT, then feed into the setting of the next business strategy and revised IS/IT chargeout objectives, if necessary.

In the context of the above discussion, chargeout acquires a new strategic significance, and it becomes clear that related choices should not rest at a lower management level. Currently, there is considerable discussion of the business value of IT. Information on costs is clearly relevant to whether or not IS/IT is worth its actual cost. Thus, the most important challenges for chargeout systems in the future appear to be at the higher levels, where organizational objectives behind the use of chargeout can be tied to the business value concerns regarding IS/IT. The need for focusing chargeout research on to these challenges is also signalled by two sets of conflicting views from practice; those heralding and advocating the use of chargeout for new information technologies (Butler, 1992; Herman, 1993; Comaford, 1994; Paul, 1994), and those criticizing the same (Computer Finance, 12 January 1994; Gallant, 1994). We contend that, to meet these challenges, chargeout research must explicitly take into account the practical developments related to IT use and its chargeout, and the research in other important areas of IS, such as strategic IS planning (Lederer and Sethi, 1988) and the economic value of IT investments (Toraskar and Joglekar, 1993).

## Conclusion

We have reviewed over 20 years of IS chargeout and management accounting literature in terms of the many different objectives and costing methods used for IS chargeout systems, as well as their successful features, their advantages, and their problems. We find the buyer/seller relationship between IS and a user department to be the defining characteristic of chargeout situations. The different objectives identified for the use of chargeout systems included: cost recovery, resource allocation, more effective utilization and regulation of IS resources, IS planning assistance, increase of management, user and IS staff awareness/involvement, outsourcing and IS performance evaluation and the satisfaction of legal and contractual obligations. These objectives are not always aligned with each other and may overlap. The various cost accounting methods, which tend to characterize different types of chargeout systems, included: unallocated costs, cost allocation, cost recovery, profit centres and negative pricing. The success of a chargeout system is dependent on a number of different criteria: understandability, fairness, user controllability, realistic prices, linkage with performance review, user budgeting involvement, proportional usage charges, consistency, and the support of high level management. The most important of these are understandability, perceived fairness and consistency. Although there are a number of advantages in the use of chargeout systems, including improved organization-wide IS function efficiency, improved communication and planning benefits, improved user accountability, autonomy and motivational benefits, there are also a number of problems. In some cases the problems have been found to outweigh the benefits. These problems may include short-term focus, stagnation of IS growth, decentralized or monopolistic IS function, costs in implementing the chargeout system, the favouring of external purchases, dependency on economic conditions and creation of controversy and disputes. Finally, we investigated the context of new information technologies, revealing a number of new chargeout software products, and the associated chargeout problems which require more organizationally-oriented chargeout research in the future. To motivate such research, we presented a model of the chargeout systems evolution within a wider business and managerial context.
