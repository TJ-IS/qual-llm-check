# Computer decision support for capital budgeting: some empirical findings of practice

SIMON HO

The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shatin, Hong Kong

RICHARD PIKE

The University of Bradford Management Centre, Bradford, UK

While recent surveys suggest that capital budgeting systems in business organizations continue to become more formalized and sophisticated, a constraint on the use of sophisticated capital budgeting techniques seems to be the degree of support provided by firms' capital budgeting information systems (CABIS). This paper, based on a survey of 146 large firms, outlines the current status of CABIS and their usage pattern. It was found that although two-thirds of sample firms have specific computer application systems for capital budgeting, most CABIS are relatively unsophisticated and less than adequate in providing the information and decision support capabilities required to use sophisticated capital budgeting techniques effectively. In particular, the barrier and problems encountered in the use of probabilistic risk analysis were explored. Implications of these findings are discussed.

## Introduction

Computer-based information systems have traditionally been considered primarily in terms of their internal effects on operational efficiency – by doing things faster or at less cost. Since the late 1980s, however, information systems technology (IST) has played a crucial role in the development and implementation of corporate strategies. Organizations are increasingly using IST to affect the way they compete by changing key elements of competition such as cost, price, service and quality. The issue of how to make best use of IST resources for reengineering business processes and enhancing organizational effectiveness has received a great deal of attention from both management information systems and corporate planning executives (Johnson et al., 1988; Burn and Caldwell, 1990; Niederman et al., 1991; Short and Venkatraman, 1992; Hammer and Champy, 1993; Earl, 1994; Venkatraman, 1994; Volonino et al., 1995).

In recent years, significant advances in hardware technology and software tools, increasing use of accounting data in managerial planning and control, and the application and expansion of corporate databases, have contributed to strengthening the role of information systems in corporate strategic planning. The potential of IST for contributing to strategic planning has been particularly enhanced by new developments and expansion of the use of decision support systems and expert systems.

Capital budgeting decisions form part of the resource allocation and long-range corporate planning process within business organizations (Zinkhan and Zinkhan, 1994). As the most important management decisions in a firm, they help to shape a firm's future opportunities by influencing corporate factors such as strategies, finances, technology, processes, and people. The increasing complexity of making large scale, nonroutine investment decisions in rapidly changing environments has led managers to consider the development of computer-based capital budgeting information systems (CABIS) that incorporate sophisticated modelling and database capabilities.

In recent years, one of the major facilitators of the increased use of sophisticated capital budgeting techniques (SCBT) has been the widespread use of microcomputers and associated user-friendly modelling software (Lambert, 1988; Smith, 1994). Data are now more readily available and can be analysed with more flexible statistical techniques and models in an interactive, end-user computing environment. This should make it easier for managers to understand and use SCBT such as probabilistic risk analysis in a more cost-effective manner, and with less dependence on traditional operational research specialists. The objective of this study is to examine the practice and problems of managers utilizing modern computer decision support resources to support their strategic capital investment decisions. In particular, as probabilistic risk analysis has been the subject of widespread attention in recent years, the current study will also investigate the difficulties and problems encountered in the use of risk analysis as a decision support tool in large organizations.

## DSS concepts and potential

As one of the latest developments on the information systems scene, some authors have argued that the full effectiveness of a CABIS is only found when it is embedded in a decision support system (DSS) environment (Gordon and Pinches, 1984; Srinivasan and Kim, 1986). A DSS can be defined as an interactive computer-based information system which provides managers with easy and flexible access to decision models and data that could assist them in solving relatively unstructured problems. This also suggests that a DSS should consist of four basic elements: user, database, model base, and the software interface system which links the other three components together. Sometimes a DSS also includes some features of the newer applications systems such as executive information systems (EIS) and expert systems (ES) (Doke and Barrier, 1994; Eierman et al., 1995; Rathman and Mannino, 1995.

A typical DSS should support all phases (i.e., intelligence, design and choice) within the strategic decision making process, and assist in integrating the decisions of all managers in the process. With a DSS, customized queries and reports can be generated at a manager's workstation when making decision. In theory, a DSS differs from other types of information systems in that it can be used to address ad hoc, unexpected problems, and it provides decision support within the available time frame. It can evolve as the decision maker learns more about the problem, and can also be developed and operated by non-computer professionals. A DSS aids the human decision making process rather than providing a means to replace it (Alter, 1980; Sprague, 1980; Bonczek et al., 1981), placing greater emphasis on the use of information technology for improving decision effectiveness rather than efficiency (Keen and Scott Morton, 1978; Keen, 1980; Sprague and Watson, 1993).

When applied to capital budgeting, a DSS allows planners to request unanticipated project-related information from the database through English-like query languages. This information can then be analysed with flexible statistical techniques, standard quantitative models and self-developed financial models stored in the model base. A DSS for capital budgeting plays a far more important role when future cash flows are uncertain, as calculations of the effects on projects of possible future circumstances are possible via probabilistic analysis and risk simulation. Gordon and Pinches (1984) argue that a computer-based DSS environment provides the kind of conditions within which SCBT can become more effective. Certainly, despite its potential benefits, a DSS has also some limitations. DSS products can be conceptualized as existing on three levels (Sprague, 1980; Sprague and Watson, 1993): specific DSS, DSS tools and DSS generators. A specific DSS (usually simply referred to as 'DSS') is used by a decision maker to deal with a specific problem or set of related problems (e.g., capital budgeting). DSS tools are independent hardware and/or software elements (e.g., programming languages, statistical packages, optimization packages, database management systems) that enable one to build a DSS generator or a specific DSS. A DSS generator is a software package enabling rapid and easy specific DSS creation. More commonly known as financial modelling packages, typical DSS generators usually contain a number of analytical tools such as nonprocedural modelling languages, what-if analysis, goal seeking analysis, financial analysis, statistical analysis, forecasting and trend analysis, optimization, probabilistic risk analysis (risk simulation), database management, report generation, interactive graphics, and consolidations.

In particular, many people agree that probabilistic risk analysis or Monte Carlo simulation is among the most useful tool that a DSS generator can provide for capital budgeting decisions. Many experts have argued that increased quantitative information on the risk inherent in a project, gained from risk analysis, can lead to better decision outcomes (Coats and Chesser, 1982; Hertz and Thomas, 1983; Hosseini, 1986; Karady, 1986). Hertz and Thomas (1983) advocate that the use of risk analysis provides a systematic and logical approach to investment decision making, helps communication within the organization, allows managerial judgement to be presented in a meaningful way, and ultimately improves firm performance (see also Cooper and Chapman, 1987). explore the risk associated with various critical variables of a capital project. Appropriate probability distributions (e.g., normal, uniform and triangular) are specified for the input variables in the model, and their impact on dependent variables (i.e., project expected return) is determined by combining the results of repeated sampling from the input variables. A Monte Carlo simulation can then show the probability that a various level of return might be generated from a capital project. Such analysis by a lone decision maker was not possible before the advances of DSS concepts and technology.

In the past, there has been evidence that the use of DSS generators or financial modelling packages is becoming more widespread (Grinyer, 1983; McGregor, 1983; Bhaskar et al., 1984; Higgins and Opedbeeck, 1984; Shim and McGlade, 1984). However, when these packages were implemented, it was found that in most cases only deterministic models and a few basic DSS tools were actually used. For instance, McGregor (1983) found only about 10% of UK firms surveyed used the risk analysis module of FCS packages in investment decisions. The recent advances in information technology have led to wider adoption of financial modelling packages and to the development of CABIS in some organizations. This may result in the belief that SCBT, such as probabilistic risk analysis, have now become more commonly used in investment analysis. However, there is no up-to-date evidence available to indicate the level of sophistication CABIS have reached in the UK in recent years. Given the many technical and managerial developments in the 1980s, it would also be interesting to know whether many of the traditional difficulties and problems encountered in using risk analysis still concern managers today.

Furthermore, Gordon et al. (1979), in researching the role of information systems in supporting capital budgeting, found that information systems-related impediments create barriers to the effective usage of SCBT in many American organizations. Their findings revealed that most of these formal information systems contain very restrictive types of accounting data (i.e., ex post, internal and financial). Ex ante (e.g., projected cash flows and discount rates), external (e.g., reaction of competitors), and non-financial (e.g., technological changes) data for capital budgeting were not usually part of the firm's formal information systems; such information had to be collected through other unstructured, inconsistent and less reliable channels. They suggest that a broader and more comprehensive information system is required in order to use SCBT more frequently and effectively. With the increasing use of relational database management systems (DBMS) software, it would be interesting to know how comprehensive and supportive are the existing databases of CABIS in large firms.

Past literature on DSS has concentrated on general, rather than decision-specific issues. This may explain why any benefits to be derived from implementing a DSS for capital budgeting have been slow to emerge. Although the last few years have seen a significant increase in the use of financial modelling systems to support decisions such as short term financial planning and cash budgeting, knowledge of their specific use in supporting strategic capital budgeting decisions is quite limited. Also, most surveys on financial modelling practice were undertaken before, or in the early stages of, the impact of the concepts of DSS, relational databases, end-user computing and artificial intelligence. The current study provides a more up-to-date picture of the current practice of CABIS within larger UK companies and offers a further link between DSS and capital budgeting.

## Survey findings

Primary data were derived mainly through questionnaires sent by mail and supplemented by interviews with capital budgeting specialists in eight randomly selected CABIS-using organizations. The pilot-tested and validated questionnaires were distributed to named finance directors in the largest 350 companies in the TIMES 1000. After two follow-ups the whole process resulted in responses from 154 companies, 146 usable for this study (an actual response rate of 42.5%). Extensive comments written on the survey questionnaires provided additional insights into the current usage of CABIS by large companies. The characteristics of the sample firms, presented in Tables 2 and 3 for industry and size, suggest that respondents were representative of the original sample. The distribution was spread fairly evenly between heavy industrial, natural resources, consumer industrial, and services firms.

Several tests were conducted to ensure the reliability and validity of the data obtained. First, the measures of all DSS tools usage status from the last 40 questionnaires received were compared with the results of the first 40 in order to check for any possible bias. This technique indicated no significant difference ( $\alpha = 0.05$ ) between early and late responding firms, thus suggesting that non-response bias was minimal in the data collected. In addition, three financial variables (fixed asset, return on capital employed and gearing) were compared for 50 randomly selected responding companies and 50 randomly selected non-responding companies. The hypothesis of equal means for the two groups was not rejected regarding the three variables ( $\alpha = 0.05$ ), again suggesting that the respondents were representative of the original sample.

The questionnaire items were designed to examine the extent to which firms utilize computer decision support in capital budgeting. Mainly based on Kroeber and Watson's (1990) DSS framework, major issues covered include the current status of CABIS adoption, types of hardware and software used, range of capital budgeting data available, familiarity and use of various decision support facilities, difficulties in the use of risk analysis tools, and other system features available or desired.

## Adoption of CABIS

Although information systems do not need to be computer-based, developments in computer technology have had such a profound effect on information handling that one would expect most companies surveyed to have a computerized CABIS. To confirm this, respondents were first asked if their companies use specific computer application systems to assist their capital investment decision process. Of the 135 firms providing usable responses to the question, approximately 63% (84 firms) claimed to have a computer-based CABIS; 51 responding firms (37.8%) are not currently using such a system, but a number of these firms expressed their intentions to instal one in the near future. This confirms that computer-based CABIS are quite common among large UK firms.

Chi-square analyses indicated that the use of a CABIS is significantly influenced by the size of the firm, with larger firms most likely to have a CABIS (p = 0.000). Further industrial analysis indicated that such systems are not used as much in the retail/distribution and banking/finance fields. This may be due to their relatively simple capital budgeting requirements which can often be handled by traditional methods. A few respondents claimed that they could see no significant benefits in having such a system due to the lack of adequate data sources.

The primary objective of any CABIS is improved capital budgeting, certainly a difficult goal to measure. During interviews, the major motivations and benefits which current users of CABIS mentioned include: the ability to explore more alternatives; better quality decision making; more effective planning; better understanding of the business; response to changes faster; faster decision making; and gaining competitive advantages. Due to the intangible nature of many benefits of a DSS, the traditional cost–benefit analysis is seldom used for justifying or evaluating the value of a DSS. Moreover, almost none of the firms interviewed carry out post-implementation audits of their CABIS.

## Computer hardare and software selection

Building a DSS for capital budgeting involves decisions about the computer hardware and software to be used. In terms of hardware, the major options are the organization's mainframe computer, a minicomputer, or personal computers. Each option offers advantages and disadvantages. In the current study, of the 84 CABIS surveyed, $61\%$ are run on microcomputers, $9\%$ on minicomputers, $10\%$ on mainframe computers, and the remaining $20\%$ on a combination of machines. Only five firms mentioned that they use bureaux network services.

The current study indicates that the microcomputer has been the dominant choice of hardware, which contrasts with Grinyer's (1983) results of heavy use of mainframe and bureaux. Interview findings indicated that several factors affect the choice of type of computer, to support the DSS. These factors include what kinds of computers are available in-house, the range of users of the DSS, the data needed of the DSS, the required computational power, and the software requirements of the DSS. Clearly, there is a trend towards end-user computing via personal computing resources.

Indeed, the rapid adoption of micros for financial modelling in the last few years is very striking. A total of over 80% of CABIS users in the current study have used micros, either alone or together with other larger computers. Some DSS require complicated models (e.g., linear programming and simulation) and substantial processing power. However, this has become less of a problem as personal computers have becoming increasingly powerful and many operational research/management science models have migrated from mainframe to personal computers. Many DSS generator software products (e.g., Micro-FCS and IFPS/Personal) are now also available in personal computer versions with nearly all of the similar mainframe features. Nevertheless, as indicated by several respondents, a major limitation with micros is their limited system security features. There are also integrity problems in linking up the micros with the central computers and corporate databases, although those problems have reduced in recent years.

Another important decision is whether the specific DSS for capital budgeting is to be created by means of DSS tools or a DSS generator. Much of the current excitement about DSS for capital budgeting is in fact due to the ease with which such a DSS can be developed with a DSS generator, particularly the financial modelling type. In practice, spreadsheets can also be used as a DSS generator for simple DSS work. In the 84 firms using CABIS, 75% used some kind of spreadsheet package (40 firms using LOTUS 1-2-3 and 18 using other spreadsheet products) or structured modelling package (8 using FCS and 5 using EPS) to develop the CABIS internally; and the remaining 25% either use a high-level language (mainly BASIC, FORTRAN or COBOL) and a few stand-alone statistical and DBMS routines for in-house development, or have bought a complete commercial package from outside vendors. Most firms use CABIS developed in-house because of their unique investment decision process and system requirements.

Spreadsheet packages are solely for microcomputers but most structured modelling packages such as FCS, EPS and IFPS can be used on more than one type of computer. The current study again shows that spreadsheet packages are dominantly used in building CABIS. Despite their ease of use and rapid feedbacks, interview findings indicate that some common DSS generator functions were either not available (e.g., goal-seeking) or are more difficult to use (e.g., risk simulation). According to some respondents, compared with structured modelling, spreadsheets have several other definable weaknesses such as program-data dependence and lack of an audit trail to trace edits or updates. In choosing between a modelling package and a spreadsheet, most users basically trade the power of the fully featured model for the simplicity of an easy-to-follow spreadsheet. In practice, managers tend to choose spreadsheets for small, simple, single-user ad hoc problems. However, as some of the spreadsheets have recently taken on a number of advanced modelling functions (such as regression analysis), the line that separates spreadsheets from traditional modelling packages is blurred (Bodily, 1986; Seila and Banks, 1990, Uyeno, 1992).

## Availability of information

As mentioned earlier, effective capital investment decisions require substantial amounts of internal and external data, which should be both forecast and historical in nature. It is believed that without a supportive database, managers would find it very difficult to use sophisticated tools such as risk analysis. The required basic information for risk analysis, depending on the decision phase, could include data on cash flows, project lives, discount rates, competitors' behaviour, macroeconomics, and post-auditing. However, external, non-financial and ex ante types of information were not usually available in a firm's formal information system (Gordon and Pinches, 1984; Abraham and Wankel, 1995). Adapted from Haka's (1987) instrument, sample firms in this survey were asked to indicate how often these major types of desired information for capital budgeting analysis were obtained from their CABIS.

The responses, summarized in Table 4, indicate that project-related financial data, such as cash flow estimates, cost of capital, and economic life of projects was seen to be quite adequate by the respondents. Over 75% of the respondents rated the availability of such information as ‘often’ or ‘very often’. In general, these systems carry the historical customer and sales information needed to predict project cash inflows, and information on costs to analyse the profitability of projects.

At the other extreme, macroeconomics, competitor behaviour and post-audit review information was found to be far less adequate than project cash flow-related data. For example, the availability of competitors' information was rated relatively low (mean = 3.54) with about 47% of respondents reporting that their formal information systems supply little or no information concerning the behaviour of competitors.

Furthermore, the quality of cash flow information must be questioned for the majority of the firms surveyed since their systems do not generate macroeconomics data, making it difficult to analyse the demand and profitability of projects with any great accuracy. Where macroeconomics information is gathered, market trends, industrial trends and the economic climate are usually considered. Some respondents also claimed their systems had access to information which should be capable of identifying threats and opportunities to the firm from the environment. Most macroeconomics data can be reasonably easily quantified and incorporated into a computer system, but some firms found that qualitative data on technological trends, political climate and public opinion have often to be manually scanned, analysed and stored. Overall, macroeconomics information is both qualitative and quantitative, requiring a marriage of computer support and human judgement.

It is generally believed that where timely data are available to measure the accuracy of the forecaster's estimates, the inaccuracy and biases of estimates should be reduced (Grinyer, 1983). However, just over 35% of the respondents in this study claimed that sufficient data on individual projects are available for post-audits (mean = 3.85). Several executives interviewed reflected that post-audit data are used mainly to compare the estimated costs with the actual costs to date for some large projects, and as an educational aid to improve managers' forecasts. Very little post-audit data are actually used for corrective actions in the project, or as an evaluative tool for project forecasters. It can be concluded from the above findings that existing CABIS in many cases, if not most cases, can only support sophisticated capital budgeting analysis to a rather limited extent.

## Decision support tools used

The survey examined the level of respondents' awareness of available DSS facilities, and which decision support tools are most frequently utilized in existing CABIS. The summary of responses shows that firms are most familiar with discounting facilities such as net present value/internal rate of return computation: 74.8% of CABIS users always use it and 16.8% have tried it. The second most favoured function is the 'what-if' analysis. It is frequently used by 36.5% of respondents. Many respondents considered what-if analysis to be one of the most frequently used tools as well as the most important. The third most favoured function is forecasting/trend analysis. It is frequently used by 34.4% of sample CABIS users. Many capital budgeting models incorporate some form of forecasting technique, particularly for sales and revenue projections. Use of the remaining functions is of a very limited nature, ranging from 8.7–25.5% of firms using them frequently. Apparently, firms were least familiar with the backward iteration (goal-seeking) function – to search backward from a target to determine the necessary inputs, with 27.2% having never heard of it and only 8.7% always using it. Furthermore, over 40% of user firms do not have access to external and internal databases, and only about 15% of firms frequently use a database management system (DBMS) in their CABIS. Executives interviewed also indicated that they prefer the DSS database as a separate entity, not as part of the corporate database, since these DSS do not rely much on transactional data.

Despite the general recognition by managers that risk analysis is of primary importance in capital budgeting (Bierman, 1986; Ho and Pike, 1991; Smith, 1994), probabilistic risk simulation is used ‘frequently’ by just 6.7% of user firms, with another 35.6% having ‘ocasionally’ used it. In principle, the availability of financial modelling packages should facilitate the construction of computer simulations. In some modelling systems, all that is needed to perform the simulation is to enter the command ‘Monte Carlo 1000’ to get 1000 iterations based on random number generation. From this process, the user can then obtain the probability distribution of the outcome variable such as ROI or IRR, rather than a single point estimate. However, the findings show that managers seldom produce probability distributions of return on investment or internal rate of return via Monte Carlo type simulations.

Although the models in the existing DSS include a variety of strategic, tactical and operational subroutines, most of them are deterministic what-if models and have not always been as helpful to executives as the model builders have hoped they would be. Other problems encountered by many sample firms are the difficulty of keeping models up-to-date and the lack of integration of models. Overall, except for NPV/IRR computations, the majority of user firms do not extensively employ the other decision support functions listed. In particular, probabilistic risk simulation is used less frequently than most of the others, and indeed in over half of the companies is never used.

## Barriers and problems in using risk analysis

Although the perceived importance of risk analysis in many firms has been increased in the last few years and the trend will continue, comparatively few actually used this tool for capital budgeting decisions. Until recently the technique has required access to specialized computing facilities, and the low usage is therefore not surprising. But, as Smith (1994) shows, the introduction of sophisticated DSS generators and spreadsheets with the necessary statistics and simulation facilities, combined with the widespread availability of powerful personal computers, means that Monte Carlo simulation can now be implemented with ease using facilities readily and conveniently available to most managers. Utilizing personal computing facilities to which most managers now have ready access, Smith (1994) uses a spreadsheet model to demonstrate that risk analysis results can be produced quickly and in a format that permits decision making without resource to a specialized knowledge of statistics.

To understand more about the factors affecting usage, the respondents were asked about the extent to which they have experienced each of the 11 problems commonly identified in the literature which could block the effective use of risk analysis. Again, a six-point scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 6 (very much) was used. In general, the first three out of the 11 problems listed can be considered common, as their mean scores are relatively high and over 60% of the respondents rated these three problems as ‘some’, ‘much’ or ‘very much’:

(1) Managers' understanding of techniques (69%, mean = 3.96)

(2) Obtaining input estimates (62%, mean = 3.79)

(3) Time involvement (60.8%, mean = 3.73)

(4) Cost-justification of techniques (57%, mean = 3.16)

(5) Human/organizational resistance 56%, mean = 3.61)

(6) Trade-off between risk and return (56%, mean = 3.52)

(7) Understanding output of analysis (55%, mean = 3.69)

(8) Finding suitable methods (47.8%, mean = 3.35)

(9) Lack of top management support (47.7%, mean = 3.34)

(10) Managers cannot agree on estimates/judgements (43%, mean = 3.22)

(11) Lack of computing resources and assistance (25.6%, mean = 2.62)

Summarizing these responses, while firms experience several barriers to the use of risk analysis, two main difficulties emerge: understanding the techniques and obtaining input estimates. This indicates only moderate progress in overcoming those persistent problems which have been recognized in the literature over the last two decades. It seems that knowledge of risk analysis has still not reached many intended practising managers through appropriate channels. Also, it proves that without a supportive database, managers may find it difficult to carry out any sophisticated or meaningful risk analysis.

While it may not be necessary to have formal training programmes, it appears that at least managerial interaction and involvement during all phases of the risk analysis process is important to ensure understanding and acceptance. Interview findings reveal that user acceptance can be enhanced when managers realize that the information provided conforms to their decision process and can be helpful to them in their decision responsibilities. Regarding the problem of collecting probability estimates, further development and refinement of practical methods for eliciting the subjective probabilities necessary for risk analysis are required. With some prior discussion and training, it is believed that such assessment can generally be obtained. Hopefully some of the above problems are increasingly solvable given the continuous improvements in information technology, managers' increased knowledge of new management techniques, and our understanding of cognitive psychology.

## Other system and model features

Respondents were also asked to describe other special features of their CABIS, if any. Features mentioned included:

(1) Designs to evaluate projects in line with corporate objectives

(2) Automatic spending profiles by product/monthly capital requirement prediction

(3) Multiple depreciation types (4) Full recognition of a company's projected tax situation

(5) Working capital return upon termination of the project

However, very few of the respondents mentioned that their systems contained any expert systems elements. Also included in the survey was the question: 'What other, if any, features and facilities would you prefer?' The replies to this question can provide some insight into the design of future CABIS. Features mentioned included:

(1) The ability to tailor the program to the individual investment analysis

(2) Menu-driven Beta analysis

(3) Statistical testing of distributions

(4) The ability to handle multi-projects (5) Critical path analysis, decision tree and critical loops

(6) A more user-friendly interface with improved graphics

While there are many variations of capital budgeting models employed, there appears to be at least one consistent characteristic of models that prove important - the models must be flexible and able to pursue alternative analyses.

In order to have some idea of the overall perceived effectiveness of existing CABIS, users in the current study were asked to rate, on a six-point scale ranging from 1 (not satisfied) to 6 (very satisfied), their overall satisfaction with their CABIS. Despite the rather limited use of sophisticated decision support tools, about half of the respondents were quite favourable about their CABIS. Not one responded ‘very unsatisfied’, while only 7.7% stated that they were stated that they were ‘very satisfied’. The results show that many users are still ambivalent about their CABIS and believe that there is much room for further improvement of these systems.

## Discussions and conclusion

The recently developed DSS philosophy implies that any capital budgeting information system should have a well-designed database, carefully thought-out decision models, and an easy-to-use conversational interactive query facility to allow decision makers to request unanticipated information and combinations of decision models. In particular, the impact of various combinations of financial variables can be readily simulated via a DSS, under varying social, political, economic and technological conditions, in a timely manner. However, the actual practices of financial modelling packages are somewhat different from the normative DSS literature.

Although about two-thirds of sample firms make use of capital budgeting information systems (CABIS), the systems appear to be less sophisticated than might be expected, particularly in terms of model building, analytical procedures and the data available. Most existing CABIS were developed from financial modelling packages with inherent statistical analysis, forecasting, model building and probabilistic simulation facilities. However, many manager-users have little knowledge of these facilities, and many systems currently in use are of the traditional 'file-drawer' type (Alter, 1980) with limited probabilistic modelling capabilities to predict the outcomes and probabilities of alternative courses of action. Except for NPV/IRR computations and a few other basic functions, the majority of financial modelling users do not employ other decision support tools extensively.

Input data were also limited, often including mainly historical sales and cost, but little or no external data. Only a small number of firms employ well-designed databases as part of their capital budgeting information systems. The use of sophisticated decision tools in many firms has been restricted because external data are not adequate to permit the development of meaningful relationships between variables. Overall, our research suggests that only small improvements have been made in terms of system sophistication since the studies in the mid 1980S (Grinyer, 1983; McGregor, 1983; Higgins and Opedbeeck, 1984). According to the survey findings, only a small portion of CABIS seemed to be at the 'real' DSS level as prescribed by Gordon and Pinches (1984).

The implementation problems identified certainly require further attention. For instance, more studies into the motivation for selecting particular types of risk models and information will be valuable. Overall, a factor which is likely to restrict executives' willingness to adopt new decision support tools is the perceived incompatibility with existing values, beliefs or working procedures. Insofar as managers believe that such group interaction leads to a consensus which is valuable in the capital budgeting process, they will favour the use of such DSS concepts for CABIS more (Burn and Caldwell, 1990). The increased focus on a user-friendly interface between system and user may also overcome some of the behaviour constraints mentioned earlier.

It is important to recognize that the current usage of new decision support tools is not static as the development of model building and simulation capabilities for capital budgeting is a relatively long evolutionary process involving many human, computer and organizational resources. It may change as social, technological, organizational and investment conditions change. As business competition becomes more intense and environmental changes become more difficult to forecast, managers become more conversant with information technology and data collection techniques, and will probably demand more risk analysis information. Some evidence of this has emerged from this study, particularly among those young business school-trained managers who are directly involved in capital investment decisions.

Nevertheless, in theory, any firm should attempt to determine the potential benefits and costs before establishing a DSS-oriented CABIS. Based on contingency theory, the implementation of such a CABIS may not be suitable for some companies. The information economics theory and cost-benefit analysis would suggest that some firms do not really require such a comprehensive system. In addition, the concept of 'bounded rationality' has much to offer those designing and using a CABIS because of the multiple goals, cost of information, limitations of human information processing capabilities, and incompatibility between decision criteria and managerial reward structure that often occur in practice. Future research may investigate the influence of these factors on a decision maker's attitude towards the use of sophisticated decision support tools.

One major contribution of this study is the descriptive evidence provided on the current status of CABIS adoption, the level of system sophistication, and the difficulties encountered in the use of some sophisticated decision support tools. These findings may encourage executives to reevaluate and improve their own CABIS practice in the light of the practices and problems of others. Given the wealth of theoretical literature relative to DSS design and the absence of information describing real world practice, this study offers a more comprehensive empirical base for future research in this field.
