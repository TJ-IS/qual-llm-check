# Technology driven and competency driven approaches to competitiveness: are they reconcilable?

MARILYN E. BOOTH and GEORGE PHILIP Information Management Division, The Queen’ s University of Belfast, UK

Based on a series of structured interviews with several senior management ® gures, this paper explores two different approaches to competitiveness within the literature, namely the technology-driven approach and the competency-driven approach, and asks whether they can be reconciled with the real world experiences of corporate managers. The technology driven approach is based on the belief that information technology (IT) offers a powerful source of competitive advantage. Popularized by, and based on the work of, Michael Porter there is a corresponding emphasis on planning and the use of \`generic’ strategies. The competency driven approach, on the other hand, holds that each company possesses unique sources of advantage, which it must exploit to derive maximum bene® ts. The focus is on ¯ exibility, while no \`recipes’ for success are offered. Given that these two approaches appear to be diametrically opposed, 16 senior management ® gures from leading edge companies were interviewed to determine which, if either, of the two views was more in tune with reality. This paper details the ® ndings.

## Introduction

Competitiveness in today’ s modern business environment is becoming an increasingly important issue as companies seek to cope with the effects of recession, and an ever more global and fast-moving marketplace. Success depends on a large number of factors, and opinion is divided on how companies can best achieve it, with different stakeholders in the organization taking differing views.

The economic performance and overall condition of ® rms has a wider impact upon the economic (and social) conditions of the country concerned. Those with strong, highly successful companies will share the prosperity of those ® rms (Porter, 1990). Companies cannot afford to see themselves existing and competing in an isolated vacuum, since their actions have implications for more than those directly involved in the business.

The complexity of the competitive environment is such that many issues have to be taken into consideration, while many would put forward ideas on how to improve company performance. One attempt to simplify the complexity of the issue is shown in Figure 1. The \`diamond of competitiveness’ shows some of those concepts which companies need to consider in order to survive and gain ground over rivals.

From the diagram we can see the rich network of relationships between the factors which can lead to, and have an impact on, competitiveness. Companies which have strong linkages between these factors will tend to achieve better performance than those with weaker linkages. Given that companies have so many issues to take into account, can there be any one path to success? How do companies make sense of this complexity, and can there be any consensus of opinion on such a heated and widely-debated issue?

A review of the literature reveals that during the past 15 years or so two different, yet complementary, strands or approaches have been identi® ed for achieving competitiveness and corporate success. One is the technology driven approach and the other the competency driven approach. In this paper we take a detailed look at both these points of view and, using the experiences of a number of corporate managers who have been at the deep end of business for a substantial period of time, we seek to establish if these different views are reconcilable. We have interviewed several managers from the manufacturing and service sectors for this purpose ± to ® nd which, if any, of the differing views are most prevalent within the business community.

## The technology driven approach

Popularized by Michael Porter in the early 1980s (Porter, 1980; Porter, 1985; Porter and Millar, 1985), this approach is based on a belief that IT alone has the capacity to provide companies with a source of competitive advantage by offering unique ways to innovate, to reduce costs and to increase differentiation (the cornerstone of Porter’s generic strategies).

Porter’s (1980) model of industry structure with its ® ve external threats (rivalry within the industry; bargaining power of customers; bargaining power of suppliers; threat of new entrants; and threat of substitute products) has been widely used as a \`framework for investigating the role that IST can play in a ® rm’ s competitive strategy’ (Ives and Learmouth, 1984, p. 1194), providing the basis for much of the literature touting technology as a potential competitive weapon (Cash and Konsynski, 1985; Ives and Learmouth, 1984; McFarlan, 1984; Grif® ths, 1989; Jackson, 1989; Sabherwal and Tsoumpas, 1993).

While Porter sees the way forward for companies in terms of overcoming external forces through generic strategies and exhaustive planning, others have questioned the wisdom of such a stance for the world in which we live today, an environment characterized by \`dynamism’, \`hostility’ and \`heterogeneity’ (Miller and Friesen, 1983; Miller, 1988). Miller and Friesen (1986a, b) also question the pursuit of generic strategies as a viable option, while Miller (1992) warns of the threat of imitation if generic (pure cost, differentiation or focus) strategies are blindly followed. Others also recommend a more ¯ exible approach to the whole area of strategy and strategy-making (Hamel and Pralahad, 1989; Mintzberg, 1992; Hamel and Pralahad, 1994). Quinn et al. (1990, p. 58) argue for a move away from \`traditional strategic formulas’ such as those advocated by Porter.

Despite these and other criticisms (Hendry, 1990), Porter’ s model still provides a starting point for many researchers investigating the strategic potential of IT. This can be effectively shown not only in the increase in literature on the linkage between IT and strategy itself (Philip et al., 1995), but also in the ever-increasing attention being paid to the work of Porter. A citation analysis was carried out on the 1985 paper How information gives you competitive advantage (Porter and Millar, 1985). Figure 2 shows how citations have grown, and still are growing in number. We can interpret this as showing Porter’s model as a starting point for many researchers in this area, although with the caveat that perhaps not all the papers will be complimentary in nature.

Earl (1989) used empirical evidence to support the claim that IT was an enhancer of economic performance, while success stories such as those of American Airlines, Citibank and McKesson provided further impetus for trusting the claims of supporters of the technology driven approach. Companies such as Otis Elevator (Jelassi, 1993) and Mrs Fields Cookies (Haeckel and Nolan, 1993) have shown that IT can offer signi® cant advantages. However, more recently there has been a shift in focus, as for many companies the promised bene® ts have failed to materialize (Hammer, 1990; Willcocks, 1992; Mahmood, 1993). Indeed, despite the wide-ranging body of literature extolling IT’ s virtues, more recent evidence has tended to suggest that bene® ts gained will not be sustainable ± if they arise at all ± while the focus on case studies may have obscured or magni® ed the true value of IT investments (Kettinger et al., 1994; Segars and Grover, 1994). What holds true for one particular company at one particular time, in one particular situation, may not be replicable in a different setting.

Hopper (1990) suggests that the time when such conditions held true may have disappeared for ever ± if it ever existed. Instead of seeing technology as a source of competitiveness, Hopper (1990) predicts a new era where technology will have a ubiquitous presence ± a necessity without which companies would be unable to function: deeply ingrained into the very fabric of the business.

Such a shift in focus is re¯ ected in two similar surveys (Niederman et al., 1991; Galliers et al., 1994) which suggest that attitudes to IT have changed signi® cantly over the last ten years in both the UK and the USA. The studies suggest that the managerial focus of attention has shifted from implementing IT solely for competitive advantage, to the management of that technology, a factor often regarded as being the key to successful Information Systems/Information Technology usage (Lefebvre et al., 1994). Indeed, technology management issues may prove vital as attention shifts towards ® nding ways of improving the sustainability of IT-enabled advantage. Issues such as strategic alignment and management involvement and support for the use of technology are often viewed as being important (Coulson-Thomas, 1991; Earl, 1993; Miller, 1993; Venkatraman et al., 1993). Management of the IT resource is also emphasized by those involved in the MIT management in the 1990s research programme (Scott Morton, 1991). As with Hopper (1990), the technology is viewed as having an integral part to play in company success, although Venkatraman (1991) suggests that traditional organizational constraints may have to be removed if IT is ever to realize its true potential. Hammer and Champy (1993) take a similar view, recognizing that any advantage gained from IT, while it will be valuable, will also have to be sustained by continually evaluating the way in which processes themselves are performed. For these authors business process reengineering (BPR) is a way of changing the traditional ways in which IT has been used to \`mechanize old ways of doing business’ leaving \`existing processes intact and (using) computers to speed them up’ (Hammer, 1990, p. 104). Using IT to reengineer the business also calls for recognition of technology management issues, as Fiedler et al. (1995, p. 17) point out: \`As IT is used to enable more radical and strategic change to be achieved, it becomes increasingly important that it is planned and co-ordinated to support the overall organization’s business plan and goals.

## Competency driven approaches

While Porter sees the success or failure of a ® rm in terms of overcoming the ® ve forces of industry structure, more recent approaches to strategy have suggested that success is achieved through exploiting the unique strengths and capabilities which the organization has at its disposal. These can have an internal focus, such as the \`core competencies’ espoused by Pralahad and Hamel (1990) and Doz (1989), or Itami’ s \`invisible assets’ (1989). Alternatively, the ® rm’s competitive position can be assisted by the \`distinctive capabilities’ (Kay, 1993) which it can call upon.

For Drucker (1988) and Nonaka (1991), oganizational knowledge and information are the keys to survival in the knowledge-based society of the not-so-distant future. Doz (1989, p. 48) sees the core competencies of the organization as an \`experience-based tacit know-how which is acquired, developed and improved over a period of time’ , the \`collective learning’ within the organization. Along with Kay (1993), it is suggested that it is the ability to recognize these competencies and when to exploit the presented opportunities which are a sign of strength. Signi® cantly, technology is often seen as one of those assets and technology exploitation one of the most valuable core competencies of all (Hammer and Champy, 1993, p. 100).

Closer links with, and greater recognition of, customers and their requirements (part of the ethos of Total Quality Management (TQM)) are also central to Itami’s (1989) invisible assets. It is argued that these will be achieved through the use of branding and reputation, factors which will themselves be in¯ uenced by distribution channels, corporate culture and the technological skills which the company uses. However, within this approach it is recognized that no one skill or resource is suf® cient on its own ± it is the combination of any of these which are most important. For Clemons (1991) technology is just one resource which must be integrated and used in combination with other resources to give a unique response to competitors. Land (1994) takes a similar view, with technical competence combining with skills in other areas, including the organization’s \`core business activity’ .

This view is at its most detailed in John Kay’ s (1993) \`structure of strategy’ , where the distinctive capabilities a ® rm possesses are a potential source of competitive advantage if applied to the appropriate markets. Flexibility is at the heart of Kay’ s approach to strategy. Despite this major difference from Porter, there are similarities between the two views: both see strategy as a way of relating the business unit/® rm to its environment, and recognize that for advantage to be worthwhile it must be sustainable and unique to the ® rm concerned.

Kay recognizes four major sources of strength available to a ® rm: reputation, innovation, architecture (or internal and external relationships) and strategic assets. The strengths, and even necessity, of each of these will vary from industry to industry and from organization to organization. It is suggested that there are three basic ways in which companies will try to use these capabilities to overcome rivals: through pricing and positioning; advertising and branding; and the exploitation of vertical relationships. Like Miller (1992), he suggests that the company should try to use these methods to create strategies, and nourish strengths, which are hard to imitate, arguing in the case of architecture: \`the distinctiveness of a ® rm’s relationships may rest in the group of contracts taken as a whole. While any part of it can be reproduced, the complexity of the set de® es imitation’ (Kay, 1993, p. 64).

Recognition of the importance of business relationships is also highlighted by recent literature on collaborative advantage (Gomes-Casseras, 1994; Moss Kanter, 1994) and the networked organization. Marking a departure form viewing suppliers and customers as threats which have to be beaten down, Burnes and Whittle (1995, p. 14) suggest that suppliers should be viewed as integral components of the business, as \`assets rather than liabilities or threats and as partners not opponents’. Developing and cultivating these relationships is seen as a realistic way of reducing the costs of ® nished products, with efforts being mirrored throughout the supply chain. Coleman and Bhattacharya (1995, p. 6) see such a rede® nition of relationships as both a challenge to, and an advantage for, suppliers, with \`whole supply chains . . . being restructured to become more competitive’. In some respects, such actions with their emphasis on rede® nition and change are a link with IT-enabled BPR.

In connection with actual formulation of strategic plans, Kay’ s non-prescriptive and wide range of strategic options leads to more ¯ exible approaches to planning for the future. Kay (1993, p. 4) reasons that \`the strategy of successful ® rms is adaptive and opportunistic’, thus sharing a link with Mintzberg (1987, 1990, 1992), who sees strategy-making as a developing craft, with strategies sometimes emerging out of a ¯ uid process of learning shared by all those involved in developing and implementing the company’s competitive responses. Hamel and Pralahad (1989, p. 66) take a similar view, claiming that \`most managers, when pressed, will admit that their strategic plans reveal more about today’ s problems than tomorrow’s opportunities’. Mintzberg also warns of this danger (1994a, b, c), that of mapping present and past problems into the future.

So, the two approaches ± technology driven and competency driven ± differ not only in the resources which are believed to offer advantage, but also in their very nature. The technology driven approach can be equated with a very formal and rigid stance, with competition preferably only occurring on one particular front or issue. The competency driven approach is represented by the development and use of skills and advantages unique to every oganization, thus creating the possibility for a more varied, as well as a more ¯ exible, approach to competing.

Having identi® ed these two approaches, and given that, in theory, they are so in opposition, we were interested to ® nd out which (if either) was most prevalent within the business community. Would there be a clear link between academic theory and business practice, and would any one view emerge to be more widespread across industry types? Similarly, since the technology driven approach had been maintained for so long by case studies, could it have any relevance across the board in the real world of the mid 1990s?

## The present study

## Methodology

A selection of 16 ® rms was chosen for interview from the Belfast Telegraph’s list of top 100 companies in Northern Ireland (Simpson, 1995). Eight of those selected were involved in manufacturing, eight in services; 15 were operating on a multinational level, with subsidiaries on all parts of the globe. Only one company had restricted its geographical operations to Northern Ireland. Thus, while research was ® rmly located in Nothern Ireland, it can by no means be considered to be provincial in outlook.

The companies were chosen from the above list, although this was found to have one major ¯ aw, in that it included companies who had reported a loss in their last ® nancial statements. It was decided to exclude those companies, and aim for those which had made a pro® t. Thus attention was even more focused on proven companies, with 11 of the 16 being the leading company in their respective lines of business, either in Europe or on a global level. The status of each interviewee and ® nancial information from each of the companies involved can be seen in Table 1.

Pro® t was the initial measure of success for inclusion in the study: in a small region like Northern Ireland where the majority of companies (approximately 33 000) are small, local operators, the performance of these (and the remaining companies in the top 100) stands out. That success has been acknowledged in many ways ± by the achievement of local and international recognition for consistently high levels of quality (companies 5, 9, 12, 14 and 15), by awards for export success (company 1), and by a growth in interest by overseas companies in the creation of partnerships (companies 1, 2, 8 and 11), as well as by continued year on year growth and expansion.

A semi-structured interview schedule was developed in order to gather the relevant details on attitudes to success and competitiveness. This technique is recognized as offering a high degree of ¯ exibility (Babbie, 1992): a list of questions can be prepared, but the interviewer and the interviewee can both shape the direction of the discourse, and unanticipated issues can be explored in detail as they arise. Conversely, later questions which have already been discussed, or proved irrelevant, can be omitted. A senior executive from each company was approached with a request for interview.

Chief Executives were preferred, because of their involvement at the strategic level, as well as their ability to take an overall view of the ® rm. Dess and Davis (1984) suggest that chief executives will be able to offer an appropriately rich level of anecdotal and well-founded evidence. Each interview lasted between one and two hours, and was taped with the consent of each respondent, for ease of future reference.

The questions asked ranged from the abstract and open-ended to the very speci® c and the intention was to gauge which of the approaches to competitiveness (competency or technology driven) was most prevalent. The ® rst question asked held true for each company: What do you think has been the secret of your success so far? Other questions involved the interviewer giving a description of Porter’s competitive framework (with relevant diagram). The choice of strategic and competitive responses was also probed by asking about sources of competitive advantage, and activities and people involved in the whole strategy-making process.

Approaches to planning were also raised within the interviews, although this tended to depend on the representative’s level and position within the company. Questions relating to Mintzberg’s (1987) emergent strategies were framed, and the responses obtained were used to gain a picture of the ¯ exibility or otherwise of the competitive responses of the company concerned. A full list of questions can be seen in the Appendix.

While the work of Michael Porter was used to try to test the technology driven approach, it was decided to use John Kay’ s (1993) structure of strategy in a similar way to test the competency driven approach. Here the importance of the four concepts of reputation, architecture, innovation and strategic assets was probed. Kay also stresses that a company’s strengths may only be suitable in certain areas or markets: a question was asked to see how companies felt about this issue, asking the representative if they felt their company could be equally successful in other, unrelated areas of business. A positive response led to further questions designed to probe which competencies or skills would be transferable.

The question \`what do you see as your major sources of competitive advantage?’ was included in order to establish which, if either, of the two approaches to competitiveness was most prevalent. This question dealt equally with the work of Porter and Kay, while the responses obtained made it easier to gauge which approach was being followed, without having to ask direct questions on either competencies or technology.

Despite the emphasis on prepared questions, an attempt was made to tailor each interview to the company concerned, by drawing on previously published information, as well as clues and responses given during the course of each interview. An initial decision was made by the interviewer not to ask directly about information technology, despite its centrality to the technology driven approach. If, at the end of the interview, technology had not been referred to, the interviewer raised the topic. There were several reasons for this, most notably a wish not to in¯ uence the response obtained (e.g., by having the respondent proffer what they felt to be the \`right’ answer). A more \`natural’ attitude was assumed when and if IT was mentioned by the respondent.

## Discussion of results

## Managers’ views on Porter and the technology driven approach

When Porter’s model of industry structure was presented to the interviewees, four representatives admitted to having previously heard about it. Company 8 was the only company which claimed active use of the model in the determination of future competitive actions, claiming \`If you don’t look at this [the model] you’ ll be dead in the water’. Signi® cantly, this representative was the only interviewee to be involved full-time in strategic planning activities. This ® nancial services company uses the components of the model as a basis for identifying and categorizing threats, with telecommunications-based new entrants and substitute products being the primary concern. The need to keep looking ahead was stressed, and in this case the model seems to provide a useful tool, but only because it lays down a context through which the company can predict future events. For example, the future impact of the Internet on the whole structure and the way in which ® nancial services are delivered has been an issue within the company for some time. The interviewee from company 5 said that he was sure that it (Porter’s model) was constantly in the minds of all those formulating strategy within the company.

The remaining companies found it easy to map their own positions with regard to each of the ® ve forces, moving through each and identifying factors within each category. This accessibility has to be one of the model’s continuing good points, allied to the fact that it would seem to be applicable to many business situations, although this universality and simplicity was criticized by two representatives. Before the interviewer raised the topic, one representative mirrored Porter’s approach in that their company (company 1) had been actively striving to change the structure of its industry, an industry in which it is currently number one in Europe. The emphasis was very much on investing in new product development: increasing product sophistication in order to increase entrance levels into the industry. Here the Business Development Director recognized the need to keep ahead in the engineering industry. New features and product enhancements are exceptionally important to this particular company as only one product line is produced. Company 4 was also conducting itself almost exactly in line with Porter’ s views on customers and suppliers. It was ® rmly under the control of one single customer, while it too strives to gain control of its own suppliers.

While these two companies actively agreed with the work and suggestions made by Porter, the rest were more inclined to dismiss his suggestions. As each interview progressed, several ¯ aws became apparent with this particular representation of the competitive environment, raising the following questions.

## Is each threat discrete?

Each of the threats is not necessarily a separate entity. A supplier of raw materials can also be a potential entrant into the business ± this issue arose with company 1, which has had to develop a policy whereby it will only deal with suppliers who agree not to start producing the same products. It was mentioned above that this company operates in a relatively fast-moving business environment. Again, because of its limited product range, attempts to ® nd a more secure way of competing become paramount. In this case such an agreement with suppliers is seen as essential. This highlights the changing nature of relationships with both suppliers and customers which larger companies were keen to highlight.

A competitor could also be a potential entrant in a different market area. This issue arose with clothing manufacturing company 4, which supplied \`own label’ products to a chain of retail outlets. The market which this store operated could be considered as a microcosm of the external marketplace. While some suppliers were particularly strong in some areas they may have been underrepresented in others. This situation should hold true for all companies where different business units are involved in products of a wide range of differing products. In this situation, a potential entrant would be equally well-placed to come into the industry producing a substitute product.

## Are \`forces’ always seen as \`threats’ ?

It would seem that, in general, companies do not always adopt the type of antagonistic approach recommended by Porter. Within management literature there is now great emphasis placed on collaborating and forming alliances with other companies (even competitors). A similar point has come through from practising managers ± that companies are interlinked in many ways and depend on each other for their continuing success.

The supplier seems to be seen as an integral part of the business: the representative from company 1 referred to them as \`long-term coproducers of equipment’. So, despite the need for control over supplier actions, it was recognized that the best way to do this was through partnership and agreement, as opposed to heavyhandedness. Indeed, partnerships with suppliers were the focus of attention for seven other companies (companies 2, 5, 7, 12, 13, 15 and 16), with shared savings and contributions to continuing mutual success and survival being cited as reasons for the move to suppliers being viewed as part of the business. Company 4 was the only manufacturing company still to resist forming partnerships with suppliers, perhaps because of its relationship with its single customer, where the emphasis is very much on control.

Company 12 (already noted for reengineering efforts) has taken several steps towards a complete rede® nition of relationships with suppliers ± a step towards the virtual corporation (Davidow and Malone, 1992). Engineers and designers from Northern Ireland had recently spent time working within their supplier’ s factory on the European mainland. This not only gave both parties a unique insight into one another’ s working practices, but also acted as the impetus for reducing costs and improving quality right throughout the supply chain.

Relationships with customers are also receiving much attention. All mentioned the changing face of customer requirements: how today’ s customer is more aware, and more demanding, and therefore seen as having more bargaining power. In some quarters this was attributed to increased attention put on total quality management techniques ± customers have received high levels of customer service, and their expectations are continually expanding. However, far from seeing customers as threats, they are viewed in a similar way to suppliers as part of the reason behind the company’s continuing success, with each one deserving special, tailored service. This re¯ ects the belief expressed by Hammer and Champy (1993, p. 18), that \`there is no longer any such notion as the customer; there is only this customer’. This view was expressed by 12 of the study companies who are recognizing that, yes, each customer is different, and different techniques must be employed to retain and maintain the customer relationship, as power shifts into the hands of those customers and buying patterns change. This was felt equally by both sectors, with six companies coming from manufacturing and six from services. Companies 1, 6 and 13 did not, at the time of the interviews, acknowledge any attempt to tailor responses to individual customers, while company 4 only has the one customer.

For companies 5 and 7, both involved in the manufacture of fast-moving consumer goods, the changing nature of consumer buying patterns, as well as the increased muscle of customers, has led to a rethink of the way in which products are sold. The power of the multiple retail outlet has increased, while consumers are ® nding new ways to shop (e.g., shopping for convenience goods in all-night garages). As a response, companies 5 and 7 now offer what would have once been thought of as \`extra’ services, such as 24-hour delivery and callout, customer hot/care/help-lines, and agreements with individual customers. The consumer has been given an even greater choice of product.

A focus on customer service and satisfaction was also exhibited by the remaining ten companies, with similar measure to those of companies 5 and 7 being taken. For those involved in ® nancial services, IT plays an important role in their increasing ability to target products at different types of customer. However, for company 8, this use of IT has been taken one step further, allowing them to tailor responses to any customer and their individual requirements. This aspect of customer relationships is also currently being researched by company 15 (see below). In some respects, increasing demands on ¯ exibility and speed of response have led to murmurings of a radical rethink of the way business is conducted within one company, a step along the road to a reengineered virtual company: company 5’ s Marketing Manager stated that \`Organizational structures are changing to allow organizations to respond a lot more quickly than the old bureaucracy where you had Sales Director, Sales Manager, Assistant Sales Manager . . . There’ s a lot more latitude now.’

Such reorganization was seen as the only way in which this company could effectively respond to fast-changing conditions within the marketplace, with responsibility for making decisions moving to those who implement them in practice ± for example, allowing salespeople to make their own agreements with a particular small customer. For company 12, such restructuring has also led to a shift in accountability, with individual departments becoming cost-centres, able to negotiate their own costs with customers and suppliers. Company 7 also placed a similar emphasis on creating structures conducive to ¯ exibility of response, promoting what it termed \`cross-functional management activities’ to get rid of the constraints which they felt traditional organizational departmentalization had inevitably imposed.

Despite changing attitudes to customers and suppliers, the competitor is still, more often than not, viewed as a threat. This too is changing with companies recognizing the bene® ts they can derive by collaborating with direct rivals. However, at present collaboration is restricted to noncompetitive issues by all but one company. The general consensus was that rivals would only be seen in this way where there was no overlap between the services which each company was currently able to provide.

## What about internal strengths?

This point is linked to a company’s ability to change its external environment, according to Porter an essential role of the strategy-making process. He suggests that companies should compete by trying to restructure the nature of competition. More recent work has tended to highlight the importance of internal factors, and suggests that a company will be more successful if it chooses a market where it can make good use of the skills it already possesses. While the ® ve forces were recognized as important by all the companies concerned, great emphasis was placed on the skills and management of those within the company ± a link with the competency-driven approach to competitiveness.

## Competitive responses

Porter’ s views on strategic types were also presented to company representatives. When asked how this related to their own experiences only companies 4 and 15 were following pure strategies of differentiation. When asked their reasons for doing so they both gave similar responses. It was not because they could not produce a high quality product at a cheap price, but because they did not see the point. They both claimed that customers were willing to pay a higher price for the high quality products which they saw themselves as being able to provide. Both do have province-wide reputations for quality, so perhaps this level of self-belief is justi® ed. Company 4 is also in a unique position, with its single customer, which itself has a reputation for high levels of quality. Because of the relative stability of this relationship, cost has ceased to be an issue when company 4 produces goods for this customer.

The other representatives dismissed the idea of generic or pure strategies as \`rubbish’, all stating that they were continually trying to attract customers through a range of cost and differentiation measures. The interviewee from company 8 raised an interesting point, that it would only use generic strategies if it just had to consider existing rivals within the industry. However, the threat from new entrants into the marketplace meant that to compete on just one basis would be fatal. This is symptomatic of the state of ¯ ux which the ® nancial services industry currently ® nds itself in, with new entrants coming from all sectors of business (e.g., Marks & Spencer and Volvo). In such an unstable environment, where the rules and players are constantly changing, new and innovative modes of competition are a must. Contrast this position with company 4’ s situation, where relative stability equals the ability to compete on just one front. This issue was also raised within company 5, where it was claimed: \`We need to look at a certain level of differentiation if we’ re going to compete effectively with a lot of these new players coming into the marketplace . . . to do that you’ ve got to be looking at taking cost out of the system’ (company 5, Marketing Manager).

So, because these companies are ® nding themselves having to ® ght against competition from a wide variety of sources, the variety of responses used must change accordingly. While generic strategies may have proved viable for certin companies in the early and mid 1980s (Porter, 1980; Hambrick, 1983; Dess and Davis, 1984), today’ s environment calls for a more multifaceted approach to competition.

The other interviewees all stressed that they would not want to be considered as the cheapest producers or service providers within their respective industries, despite the fact that this held true for at least one company. All stressed that customers would pay a given amount for the unique features they were producing. Instead, they reasoned that customers were not interested in cheap products, rather products or services which offered them value for money: \`Customers will pay marginally more for a service that they trust’ (company 2, Chief Executive).

The general consensus was that high levels of quality are more or less expected by today’ s customer, although there are limits to the price which they will be willing to pay for that quality.

Cost and differentiation were not the only methods used to overcome rivals ± many links were also discovered with Kay’ s approach to strategy. Architecture and the exploitation of vertical relationships have been discussed above: as companies move away from viewing customers and suppliers as threats, they begin to see that they can utilize relationships throughout the supply chain for mutual bene® t. Signi® cantly, it is here that IT was mentioned most: technology is expected to play an important role in developing and sustaining these relationships, with Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) being investigated as a means for linking with customers and suppliers. Electronic links are also seen as a valuable means of maintaining contact with overseas suppliers and subsidiaries.

## IT as a competitive weapon

When asked \`What are your main sources of competitive advantage?’ , only one company (company 10) responded with the direct answer \`information technology’. As a justi® cation for this opinion, it was claimed that this ® nancial services company could still gain such advantage only because that technology was still suf-® ciently new to provide a unique advantage over rivals. This new technology had only been introduced after a recent merger with another company in the ® nancial services sector. IT was viewed as a way of reducing the time spent by staff on housekeeping, and allowing them to spend more time interfacing directly with the customer, getting to know and anticipate their needs.

Companies 8, 13 and 15 did not directly state that IT was being used strategically, although this was implied in other areas of the discussion. They too used technology to differentiate their products from that of competitors. In company 8 a new system had recently been introduced to provide a highly customized response to individual customers. The interviewee recognized that it was only a matter of time before other companies would try to replicate the system. Company 13 is currently using IT to ensure that staff are being used ef® ciently ± however, in the past acting as a ® rst mover with new technologies had created strategic advantages (signi® - cant reduction in labour costs compared to those of rivals) and belief in IT’ s strategic potential. Admittedly, this particular respondent could not speak for his company as a whole.

The managing director of company 15 was particularly enthusiastic about the role which he felt IT could play within the organization. While IT is currently used to enhance design and offer a fast response to customer enquiries, this was viewed as the \`tip of the iceberg’. It was claimed that the company would, within the next two years, be using IT to transform the organization into a virtual company, one which would depend on electronic links and networks to communicate with customers and suppliers, with direct links to home-based customers. Customers themselves would have signi® - cant online involvement in product design, while their requirements would be passed directly on to the relevant parts of the organization ± thus changing the whole process of buying and producing the products which the company has to offer. Company 7 had already been through such process reengineering, with the primary motivation being to ® nd a new way to compete in order to survive and grow in the 21st century. However, IT was not mentioned as a factor in this change.

Of those who did not cite IT as a source of advantage, all but one were keen to stress how vitally important the technology has become for them. Reasons given for this importance included the simpli® cation of managing data on transactions with customers and suppliers: \`We wouldn’t be here today if we hadn’t implemented major IT programmes 8 or 9 years ago. If we hadn’t computer-based systems we couldn’ t handle our customers now on paper like we used to’ (company 2, Chief Executive).

In this case, IT has become the only realistic and manageable tool by which most support activities are performed, due to the sheer volume of transactions which occur on a daily basis.

This was the most popular reason expressed for the utilization of IT ± it provides a means by which essential functions are speeded up, and it reduces the amount of time taken to accomplish day to day tasks. For certain companies, such as company 1, this has become the primary reason for using IT, despite the fact that initial applications (in the late 1970s and early 1980s) may have provided some form of advantage over rivals. In such a company IT is perceived as no less important than in a company which continues to rely on whatever strategic potential they feel IT has to offer.

It was mentioned above that one of these companies felt that the only reason it could achieve advantage through IT was because its technology was still relatively new. They also stressed that the advantage already gained would probably not be sustainable for very much longer. The representative from company 5 drew the diagram shown in Figure 3 to try and explain the impact which he believed IT has had. Figure 3 shows the size and length of advantage gained from two innovations or new products. Innovation 1 provides the company with signi® cantly more advantage than innovation 2 for a longer period of time. Further innovations would provide even less advantage for a shorter period of time. This was mentioned by four companies ± that advantages were now less sustainable than was previously the case. When asked what had caused this change, technology (including IT) was offered as one possible explanation, while there was also the recognition that any advantages gained from IT were exceptionally dif® cult to sustain: \`. . . there are a lot of advantages that IT can bring you, but I would say they are only temporary. IT is so transient that you can only expect to gain limited advantage from it . . .’ (company 5, Marketing Manager).

The fact that 13 of the representatives interviewed failed to mention IT without being prompted is misleading if taken in isolation. When the subject was raised at the end of the interviews, respondents were very keen to stress its importance to their continuing success and even existence. Perhaps we can see this as a movement to the ubiquitous presence predicted by Max Hopper (1990), with IT being so deeply ingrained into the very fabric of the business that it is taken for granted by those who use it.

## Managers’ views on competencies and competitive advantage

## The importance of distinctive capabilities

It was mentioned earlier that companies did not just see competition in terms of either cost or differentiation; many mixed strategies were being followed, some of which closely mirrored the suggestions put forward by John Kay (1993).

Architecture has, to some extent, already been discussed. There is a growing recognition that relationships, both internal and external, will be vital if companies are to continue being successful. The ability to respond fast to the customer’s needs was directly attributed to company internal architecture or company culture by four companies. All four were keen to stress their total quality ethos, and intiatives to improve communication with both customers and employees. The executive from company 4 suggested that one of the many reasons why his company managed to keep its single customer was its emphasis on fostering a \`family’ atmosphere and the practice of \`managing by walking about’ . Relationships with suppliers are seen as a stable way to compete in a fast-moving industry, and a realistic way of reducing cost and improving product quality throughout the supply chain.

Innovation, and its importance, was stressed without prior prompting by six of the study companies. It was here that mixed strategies were at the fore, with companies focusing on innovation in terms of both cost and differentiation. However, this was regarded as a poor way to gain sustainable advantage, since a new innovation can soon be copied, thus re¯ ecting concerns expressed by Kay himself.

Reputation was also regarded as important, expecially for those producing convenience products, or delivering services directly to the (nonbusiness) consumer. Again, Kay’ s views were directly re¯ ected in the use of advertising and branding to nourish this particular distinctive capability. Companies 5, 7, 8, 9, 13 and 16 stressed the importance of branding, with all adopting intensive marketing campaigns (no ® gures on actual spend were available) to raise their pro® le in the public consciousness. Three industry categories are represented here ± food and drink manufacturing, ® nancial services and retail ± all fast moving and fast changing sectors, where the struggle to remain competitive has intensi® ed and where reputation may prove important in the attraction of customers. For companies 5, 15 and 16 this is especially true, since the consumer will often buy on impulse and may just look for the product whose name comes into their mind ® rst. However, reputation in the ® nancial services arena is about more than just brand ± here relationships tend to be built on trust, which may have to be built up over a long period of time.

Reputation was also linked to a strong tradition of good relations with customers, with those relationships being fostered through elements of TQM and improved communication channels with the customer. The importance of this factor is reciprocal: the companies stressed that they would themselves only do business with suppliers who were renowned for their reliability. Only one company dismissed the importance of reputation, claiming that companies do not enter into markets to achieve this ± their main focus should be on growth and product quality. This interviewee pointed out that reputation in itself was an inadequate measure of reliability. Signi® cantly, companies did not talk about any one of these factors in isolation. Reputation and innovation were both linked to strong architectures. Good relationships and the capacity for fast response are the result of a focus on the customer and/or supplier.

The area of strategic assets is an interesting issue in a Northern Irish context: elements associated with this were mentioned by seven of the study companies. Two companies, namely 1 and $^ { 4 , }$ stressed the importance of government-sponsored assistance from bodies such as the IDB (Industrial Development Board), whose remit is \`to encourage the introduction and development of internationally competitive companies’ (DED, 1994, p. 18). These particular companies have been given ® nancial assistance in expanding their business into new areas of the province. While many Northern Irish companies have bene® ted from government-sponsored intervention, others have not been so fortunate, with UKwide legislation proving a barrier to competition for the companies involved in food and drink manufacturing. However, one other strategic asset ± that of incumbency in the marketplace ± was highlighted by two of the representatives questioned. For company 8 this incumbency was intrinsically linked to reputation and the degree of trust which has built up over the years between the institution and its customers.

Kay claims that the most valuable advantages will come when competencies or capabilities are applied in the most appropriate markets. When the interviewees were asked if they felt their companies could successfully move into unrelated areas a mixed response was obtained. Company 3 has an active policy to promote and encourage the development of core management skills, and its representative claimed, \`I think it is very important to stay close to what your core business is, the things that you understand’. Movement into a similar, related business line only would be considered here, a view shared by companies 1 and 16, who both emphasized the importance of core skills.

Representatives from six companies stated categorically that they would not consider moving into any unrelated activities. Two of these had already done so and failed, largely because of bad timing. The remainder felt that the uniqueness of their company’ s skills and past experiences would make it dif® cult to adjust to something new.

Companies 5, 7, 9, 11, 14 and 15 all expressed con® dence that they would be equally successful in other lines of business (7 and 9 already are). There was a marked difference between the attitudes of those involved in service, and those in manufacturing, on this point. Only one service company felt that it could transfer its skills to another market area, while only one manufacturing company said that it would not consider moving to other unrelated activities (see Table 2).

Ironically, it was a focus on quality of service provision which led the managing director from company 11 to believe that a move would be possible (\`Good service is important in any industry’). Within company 5, it was felt that its core skills, especially in marketing, would enable a move to any other industry. Perhaps such an attitude is unsurprising given that the interviewee had direct responsibility for all marketing activities within the company. Other skills felt to be transferable across industry boundaries included an emphasis on staff and their development, as well as the vision and commitment of management.

Support for the competency driven approach is also strengthened by the fact that the one company which directly mentioned IT as a source of advantage (company 10) was not using the technology in isolation: the importance of staff development (in terms of training) and customer relationships was also emphasized. There was a recognition that technology should be used in conjunction with the other skills and resources possessed by the company: \`In the whole mixture of things which build competitive advantage, we believe that [IT] is a very powerful way of managing costs and making the business of relating to customers much more straightforward’ (company 10, Managing Director).

Again there is the underlying recognition that companies in the ® nancial services sector must continually be on their toes in an industry where customers can be poached from many directions. The two other ® nancial services companies involved in the study also stressed the danger of relying on any one strength: as a result the Chief Executive from company 9 remarked on the situation facing those involved: \`You’ve got to be changing all the time to try and keep ahead’.

## Approaches to strategy-making

Here the actions taken were more in line with the technology driven approach promoted by Michael Porter, although an interest in ¯ exibility of competitive response was also apparent, re¯ ecting a link with the work of both Kay and Mintzberg. The need for such ¯ exibility was mentioned by seven of the study companies (companies 1, 5, 8, 9, 11, 12 and 14). For company 12 a more ¯ exible strategy has resulted in a reengineered production line, with the time taken for a product to get to market from its conception having already been reduced by 30%.

Company 1 has made the decision to reject strategic planning documentation as a direct consequence of the perceived need for ¯ exibility and ever faster response times. The particular product produced by this company is an essential item for many customers, and demand can ¯ uctuate from region to region according to chance events. In such an environment the key to continuing survival becomes the ability to turn those chance events into opportunities and respond almost instantaneously to changing conditions. As a result, strategic decisions now tend to be made \`on the ¯ y’ . This company subscribes to Mintzberg’ s (1994c) \`fallacy of predetermination/planning’ which asks if exhaustive planning really is appropriate for our unpredictable world: \`If you write strategy, the danger is that it will be outdated within 3 months. If you go back to that strategy you may be focusing your attention on the wrong areas in the business’ (company 1, Business Development Director).

The managing director from company 9, however, reasoned that planning was one way to impose order on the unpredictability of the external environment: \`I think that even though it is an unpredictable world, you’ ve got to have a sense of direction. It’s no use gliding down the stream, saying ©I hope this takes me somewhere niceº .’ This company has taken a different route to company 1 in its attempts to become more ¯ exible, preferring to focus on what is considered to be a unique blend of qualities.

Company 2 had rejected strategic planning because of the total failure of the planning process: a failure which was attributed to lack of top management knowledge on what was going on at lower operational levels. This is perhaps due to the size of this particular company. The chief executive claimed that, in a perfect world, strategy would be formulated by front line managers, but as yet they didn’t have the breadth of knowledge required for the task. Such a solution would still not address the fact that \`the business planning guys would sit and write all these beautiful plans, but we didn’t actually use them in day to day implementation’ . The hurly-burly of business life meant that those at the sharp end tended to be too busy to take any notice of the plans which had been prepared for them.

Of those who did formulate and develop written strategy documents, ¯ exibility and the need for constant appraisal of those plans was stressed. For the representative from company 9 stategy-making is, literally, a \`living process’ . The result of more ¯ exible strategies for company 5 has involved the introduction of more adaptable and versatile equipment into the production side of the business. As a result of this need for fast reactions to changing circumstances, the representatives from these companies also emphasized that strategies could not be detailed statements of every future action to be taken: \`On a 3 year document all you’ re doing is highlighting where you want to get to: the road that you take may vary. As long as you’ ve got the view of where you want to go in the longer term then the road that you take will de® ne the nature of the business’ (company 5, Marketing Manager). There was very much a recognition that plans were only guidelines, they could change and were not written in stone. In this case strategy is less associated with detail, and more with the general direction which the company will take.

Companies 9, 11 and 12 completely rejected the idea of emergent strategies, claiming instead that in any instance where they have been able to take advantage of new opportunities, they have only been able to do so because the opportunities had been anticipated and prepared for. These three companies have embraced the ethos of the so-called \`learning organization’ which enables them to react fast to changing circumstances and opportunities. Company 8 highlighted a similar point, in that it actively uses forecasting from both economic and social experts when determining the future strategic direction to take. It was stressed here that planning and preparation were both necessary, and that there was no luck or chance involved in being able to take advantage of opportunities as they arose. The general consensus was that \`you make your own luck’. Paradoxically, for these companies, planning had allowed them to become more ¯ exible and responsive. Even one company which had rejected strategic planning stressed the need for preparedness in order to be able to respond quickly to unforeseen events.

While most companies rejected Mintzberg’s views on planning, there was much evidence that the dif® culties associated with the fallacy of detachment (Mintzberg, 1994c) have been addressed, and were not a problem for the study companies. All those who had strategy documents took a team-based approach to their development, with one company (14) even involving their customers and suppliers in a process which was based heavily around consultation. In all cases the Chief Executive (or an equivalent ® gure) was directly responsible for the process, building a team which represented all signi® cant departments and interests. This provided an added incentive for the senior ® gure: \`the bene® t is that the executives know hands on what’s happening to the business’ (company 4), Thus, the Chief Executive has the opportunity to learn more about the business, while strategy is in the \`dirty’ hands of those directly involved with the day to day operations of the company.

Timeframes for strategy formulation varied from the formulation of annual plans, through to a ® ve year plan for company 4 and a ten year plan for company 7. Company 4 can, perhaps, afford the luxury of such a long-term plan due to the relative stability in which it operates. Company 7, however, does not have the same degree of stability, although a degree of certainty is achieved through its size and position as a global leader in its ® eld.

## Conclusions

Elements of both approaches to competitiveness were uncovered within the activities of the study companies. However, only one company corresponded entirely with Porter’s (1980) suggested approach ± one-to-one relationship with a dominant customer, a generic differentiation strategy, and an exhaustive approach to planning. More importantly, IT was not mentioned during the course of this interview and was rejected as a possible competitive weapon.

Where technology was mentioned as a de® nite source of advantage, it was not mentioned as an isolated factor, but in conjunction with other factors and resources. This would tend to give credence to the competency driven approach, a view strengthened by the recognition of internal architecture and employee skills.

The four distinctive capabilities put forward by John Kay (1993) were very much in evidence. Architecture proved an interesting issue because it was a focus on architecture, as opposed to IT, which can be linked to a move to the networked or virtual company. The exploitation of vertical relationships (Kay) is being facilitated by IT (Porter) to bring about the required rede® nition of relationships. Another company had been using quality initiatives to move towards a similar point, a point where customers and suppliers are part of, and involved in, the business. In this respect, the two approaches have much in common. Company 15, which made explicit its drive towards becoming a virtual company, was extremely con® dent about IT’ s strategic potential. IT will enable them to take the ® nal step in this process, but again the change has been driven by an ethos of total quality, and relationships between customers and suppliers.

While the competency driven approach would seem to be more apparent in choice of actions taken, the technology driven approach to putting those activities into practice is still the more prevalent. Planned and controlled strategies were much in evidence, although a recognition of the fast-moving nature of the business environment puts the emphasis ® rmly on ¯ exibility of response.

Evidence both for and against the two approaches to competitiveness was found within each company. Views of the competency driven approach on sources of competitive advantage, particularly in the use made of IT, proved more apparent, although elements of the technology driven approach were more popular in the overall strategic planning process. Several conclusions can be drawn:

(1) Competitive advantage is more likely to be the result of a unique combination of factors, rather than any one factor in particular.

(2) Any advantage gained, from whatever source, will not be sustainable.

(3) Where IT is viewed as a source of competitive advantage it will be used in conjunction with other skills.

(4) Great changes are occurring in the relationships between ® rms and their customers and suppliers. Most reject Porter’s (1980) view of these as threats, rather they are seen as partners in the business.

(5) The need for improved exploitation of vertical relationships will act as a driver in the increasing importance placed on IT.

(6) Because of IT’ s role in building architecture, the competency driven and technology driven approaches can be seen to be interlinked.

Each company had its own criteria by which success was measured, although all agreed that to continue being successful they had to perform a delicate balancing act between the customers, employees and shareholders of the company. If we consider the \`diamond of competitiveness’ presented at the beginning, we can see again the complexity surrounding a company’s search for competitiveness. The companies involved in this study have been successful in their searches, and have achieved their objectives by blending together a unique combination of skills, resources and advantages, and by picking and choosing from elements of both of the identi® ed approaches to competitiveness.
