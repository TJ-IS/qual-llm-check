# Diversity in the emergence of electronic commerce

IAN GRAHAM, GRAHAM SPINARDI and ROBIN WILLIAMS
University of Edinburgh, UK

This paper reviews the diverse forms taken by electronic commerce and the varied perspectives taken by observers to analyse it. Nine case studies are used to develop a taxonomy of electronic commerce implementations. Three trends in the emergence of electronic commerce are identified: a change in emphasis from competitive advantage towards the gaining of sector-wide improvements in efficiency and effectiveness, the formation of electronic markets, and a process of incremental rather than radical development within communities.

## Introduction

Electronic commerce is the use of electronic networks for communication between the information technology systems of organizations. Examples include computer reservation systems, where airlines place reservation system terminals in travel agents, and electronic data interchange (EDI), the exchange of structured text data between computer systems. Over the last 30 years the technological barriers to the use of electronic commerce have reduced: data processing within firms has been increasingly computerized and standards have been developed for transferring messages between systems. The potential benefits of electronic commerce are lower data processing costs, through the elimination of rekeying data, and service improvements due to data being transferred more quickly and more accurately (Emmelhainz, 1993). The costs are the initial agreement of data standards, the development of interfaces for internal systems and the cost of the service used to transfer the data. Public and private organizations in a wide range of sectors, manufacturing and service, are assessing whether there is a net benefit to be obtained from adopting electronic commerce, and, crucially, how it can be used to maximum advantage. It is this interplay between the technical feasibility of electronic commerce, its value within the firm and the need to enrol a community of users that this paper explores, seeking to understand the processes shaping it and the trajectories along which it is developing.

## Electronic commerce: technology or business innovation?

Electronic commerce may be considered from two contrasting perspectives. First it may be viewed as an information technology, with interest centred on the software, data transmission systems and messages. Alternatively it may be viewed as an innovation in business practice, with interest focused on the harmonization of practices within the community of firms linked electronically and its impact on business policy and practice. Early texts on electronic commerce have tended to concentrate on its technical aspects (Pfeiffer, 1993). At a technical level electronic commerce requires agreement on standard messages to be exchanged between the IT systems of trading partners, a communication medium linking the two partners and communication protocols to handle addressing and transmission. The technology, through data harmonization, message standardization and software, embodies interorganizational trading practices which may simply replicate existing nonelectronic ones or allow the evolution of more novel forms of trade. Outside this technical core there is freedom for users to configure electronic commerce to meet their needs by deciding on what information is exchanged, with whom and in what form.

## Conceptualizing electronic commerce

McFarlan (1984), in an early study of electronic commerce, focused on its use to gain competitive advantage, giving as examples the use of EDI by McKesson in pharmaceutical distribution (Rockhart and Scott Morton, 1984), American Hospital Supply (AHSC) in medical supplies (Short and Venkatraman, 1992) and airline computer reservation systems (Hopper, 1990). In each case large firms were developing proprietary electronic links with customers. The development of these systems led to two sources of competitive advantage: they differentiated the service supplied from its competitors, and they led to the system provider being used as a preferred supplier. For the customer the benefits were the faster service and the simplification of purchasing.

The potential for the supplier to lock-in customers was particularly significant; parallel procurement processes were removed and competing systems were barred contractually. The potential anticompetitive use of airline reservation systems has led to them being regulated in both the US and Europe (CEC, 1992). These early electronic links were seen as an element in the rationalization of supply links; firms reducing the number of their suppliers and entering into partnerships with a reduced supplier base (Johnston and Lawrence, 1988).

Studies of these early examples of electronic commerce led to the emergence of the 'hub and spokes' model of electronic commerce, where electronic commerce is developed largely by a single firm to gain a competitive advantage. Studies in the 1980s of these early electronic commerce communities coincided with a growth of interest in strategic information systems generally (Madnick, 1987; Somogyi and Galliers, 1987). An analysis of electronic commerce based on its competitive benefits pervades the MIT Corporation of the Nineties study (Scott Morton, 1991) and the output from the European SISNet academic community (e.g. Ciborra and Jelassi, 1994).

The model of single firms developing proprietary systems was challenged in the late 1980s as electronic commerce systems emerged based on cooperation between apparently competing firms, particularly in developing links with suppliers. The possibility of using proprietary systems to lock-in suppliers is a less attractive strategy than locking-in customers, as a firm does not want to become dependent on particular suppliers. In a sector, if each firm develops proprietary systems for linking to its suppliers, not only are development costs duplicated but also suppliers must bear the additional cost and inconvenience of maintaining parallel systems.

The conflict between competitive advantage and operational efficiency means that firms must decide whether to cooperate with competitors, develop proprietary systems or wait to see how the technology is developing (Suomi, 1991). The decision to cooperate with competitors to develop electronic commerce is a strategic decision because it implies a convergence of business practices within the sector and restricts the future use of electronic commerce to gain competitive advantage. In extremis this is a choice between developing sole proprietary electronic commerce, as in the cases of McKesson, AHSC and the airlines, or joining a completely open global community. In practice this choice may be less clear, because a group of firms may collectively develop electronic commerce to maximize their operational savings within the community but also differentiate themselves from firms outside the community. This formation of local, sectoral communities was seen in the European motor industry (Organization for Data Exchange by Teletransmission in Europe –

ODETTE) (Sacco, 1992) and the UK retailing sector (Article Numbering Association – ANA) (McGuffog, 1993). Firms may also agree common message and data standards, but seek to gain a distinctive competitive advantage through their integration with business processes. The cooperative nature of these initiatives implies a shift from electronic commerce to gain competitive advantage to its use to reduce costs by improving operational efficiency.

A further perspective on electronic commerce views it as an extension of business process reengineering (BPR) to interorganizational relations (Hammer and Champy, 1993). IT is viewed in BPR as an enabler of radical changes in how organizations carry out their operations. It has been claimed that faster, lower-cost interfirm communication, with its potential to allow the differentiation of services, will lead to the impacts of electronic commerce going beyond the automation of existing interorganizational linkages and facilitate the restructuring of supply chains (Malone et al., 1987) and the restructuring of processes within firms (Swatman and Swatman, 1991). In the same way that proponents of business process reengineering have seized on IT as the lever to drive radical change within organizations (Davenport, 1993), writers extending the concept of BPR beyond the boundary of the single firm have, in business network redesign (Venkatraman, 1991), viewed electronic commerce as the lever to trigger radical change across networks of organizations.

## Taxonomies of electronic commerce

We have seen that electronic commerce has been viewed from a range of perspectives, with distinctions between strategic advantage and operational efficiency, competition and cooperation, and claims that it will become a lever for radical industrial restructuring. It is useful to develop a taxonomy of electronic commerce to capture these diverse concepts; to identify the extent to which they are being achieved in particular business contexts; and thus to identify the forces shaping electronic commerce.

Malone et al. (1987) divided electronic commerce implementations into two categories: electronic hierarchies ('integrating tasks and functions across a predetermined set of organizational boundaries') and electronic markets ('where multiple buyers and sellers conduct business through an electronic intermediary').

This taxonomy was further refined (Benjamin et al., 1990) to include a division between transaction processing ('routine transaction processing applications, such as order entry or invoicing') and task support ('non-routine task support for managerial analytic and design functions, that contribute to decision making'), leading to a two-dimensional taxonomy of electronic commerce (see Table 1).

The hierarchies versus markets dichotomy in Benjamin's taxonomy was derived from neoinstitutional economics, notably of Williamson (Williamson, 1975). However, their use of the term hierarchy to include long term relationships not governed by short term price negotiation is ambiguous. Although relationships between firms in long term interdependent relationships are not simply market based, neither are these relationships hierarchical, where one party is able to direct the operations of the other. By hierarchy Benjamin et al. (1990) imply long term trading relationships characterized by high levels of trust. These relationships are neither pure market relationships, as it is not short term competition which is determining prices, nor are they hierarchies. Williamson (1986) has termed this type of relationship 'obligational contracting', but they may also be seen as links in the formation of network industrial structures (Thorelli, 1986).

Clarke (1992) proposed a contingency model for analysing the sectoral impacts of electronic commerce, using Porter's five forces model of business policy (Porter, 1980) to link the implementation of electronic commerce to the sectoral context of the firms involved. Clarke splits electronic commerce implementations into passive implementations, where electronic commerce has 'happened' rather than been championed by one or more major players, and stragic implementations where electronic commerce has been introduced as a barrier to entry, as a means of gaining competitive advantage or 'as a facilitator of improvements within an entire industry' (Clarke, 1992). Clarke's key distinction is therefore not between competitive advantage and improvements in operational efficiency, but between electronic trading developing through incremental innovations and it being driven by the more radical visions of focal organizations.

Clarke's model assumes that electronic commerce affects the nature of interfirm relationships. Clarke's most conservative passive impact is 'functional reallocation' where functions are transferred between firms. Clarke's model ignores electronic commerce which simply automates existing relationships, concentrating on the potential of EDI to lead to industrial restructuring. He states: 'In some industries, EDI's impact may be limited to modifications to structure and processes within individual companies. For the most part, these appear likely to be industries which are stable and large scale, and product rather than service oriented' (Clarke, 1992).

## Empirical studies of electronic commerce

## Methodology

To investigate empirically the development of electronic commerce the University of Edinburgh research team undertook during 1993 nine case studies of electronic commerce communities. These were selected to cover a broad range of industrial settings and approaches to electronic commerce. They therefore included examples of the proprietary systems set up by early EDI adopters, and open, collaboratively-developed systems more typical of those emerging today. They covered electronic markets as well as networks, and more complex data exchanges as well as electronic trading. The studies involved semi-structured interviews with participants in the development of electronic commerce, including both management and technical personnel from the organizations involved. Which actors were interviewed depended upon the structure of the trading community and the different forms of collaboration. Thus some studies centred on an individual 'hub' organization and its immediate trading partners, while others focused on the evolution of electronic commerce within communities of firms involved in more or less formal associations, in which case interviews were undertaken in appropriate firms and within the body coordinating the community. The interviews addressed the processes by which the technology of electronic commerce, for example message standards, software, and telecommunications service, was negotiated across the network of interested parties. The variety of respondents in each case threw light upon the diverse perspectives and strategies of the various organizations and groups involved. Where systems had been applied, the interviews also examined problems experienced in implementing and using EDI, and the implications of electronic commerce for the organizations involved and in particular for the relationships between organizations.

## Discussion of cases

## 'First movers'

The earliest initiators of electronic commerce (of those studied) were Ford and Kwik-Fit. The Ford Motor Company became involved in electronic trading through wider developments in internal information integration.

In 1985 Ford established Fordnet, a proprietary network covering all their European sites and including links with their most significant trading partners, including main dealerships and key suppliers. The interorganizational links in Fordnet allowed Ford to gain a competitive edge in integrating their systems with their customers and by encouraging their more significant suppliers to switch to EDI. Ford adapted the German automotive industry's VDA message standard because it was available at the time. Ford joined ODETTE, the EDI project established by the European automotive industry association, but later withdrew because they sought to gain an advantage by introducing electronic commerce ahead of their competitors, without waiting for sector-wide messages to become available. Ford developed standard systems for use by their trading partners, to minimize the difficulties of trading electronically for their partners. Ford's strategy caused problems for at least one of their largest suppliers, who were themselves developing electronic trading, when the supplier tried to integrate Ford's idiosyncratic system directly into their mainframe systems (Webster, 1994). When the ODETTE message standards were implemented by other manufacturers, Ford's policy forced suppliers to maintain parallel systems. Large car dealerships also needed internal systems capable of linking to both Ford and other manufacturers to avoid the danger of being bound to Ford by nonstandard systems.

Ford's policy of using electronic commerce for competitive advantage was challenged as EDI became widely adopted by other automotive manufacturers using standards developed by the European collaborative community, ODETTE. As the costs for suppliers of dealing with two incompatible standards became apparent and the competitive benefits of a proprietary system for links with suppliers eroded, Ford have agreed in principle to migrate to the EDIFACT message standard when it becomes adopted in the US. The network externalities of increasing EDI diffusion mean that it may be in the interests of all players, even the largest, to move to open systems.

A similar history of idiosyncratic system development was seen in Kwik-Fit: an initial stage of using EDI to gain competitive advantage ahead of the agreement of open standards, followed by an erosion of the advantage as competitors emulate the system, leading to pressure to align with common industry standards. During the 1980s Kwik-Fit, a fitter of tyres and exhausts, grew rapidly, displacing existing small regional businesses. Kwik-Fit were early users of electronic commerce, setting up their first proprietary links with their suppliers in 1982. Electronic ordering was one element in a move to gain an advantage over competitors through the use of integrated stock-management systems, allowing them to generate automatic orders on their key suppliers with minimal purchasing department involvement. This proprietary system was relatively easily implemented because no negotiation was required with either other fast-fit firms or their suppliers, who simply accepted the system as developed by Kwik-Fit. The system was introduced to give Kwik-Fit a cost advantage over competitors, maximizing their benefits from internal logistics systems. As competitors have introduced EDI, the tyre and exhaust suppliers, who have to maintain two different systems, want to migrate their Kwik-Fit orders to the non-proprietary standard. However, Kwik-Fit are unwilling to change because the current system meets their needs.

These early attempts to develop proprietary EDI can be seen retrospectively as transitory anomalies. In 1990 Kwik-Fit became involved in an EDI project called Fleetnet to introduce EDI between car fleet leasing companies and their suppliers. Initiation of Fleetnet was stimulated by the experience of two of the car leasing firms being asked by a customer to trade electronically. This acted as a catalyst for the car leasers to extend EDI to some of their own suppliers. The leasing companies were interested in using EDI to reduce the handling of invoice paperwork.

In contrast to Kwik-Fit's past experience with proprietary EDI, Fleetnet was cooperative and implementation was delayed because of the need to adapt generic standard messages to meet the particular needs of Fleetnet members and to develop product codes. Development of message formats has been influenced by awareness that cooperating with competitors might be for common good, but should not stifle competition. Thus message formats have optional fields to allow them to be customized for individual firms. Suppliers see that EDI ability provides some advantage over competitors who do not have EDI. Fleetnet allows leasing companies to do on-line assessments of whether repairs are reasonable by matching data held on that car with the work planned by the servicing company. This is done by staff previously employed handling manual paperwork, such as invoice checking. Fleetnet's main focus so far has been on invoices, which were high volume and standardized, and so highly suitable for transmission by EDI. Re-keying and checking were a major cost for leasing companies and responding to queries was a cost for suppliers. Participation in Fleetnet, though a cooperative community, was intended to give Kwik-Fit a competitive advantage by linking them into a network of major customers. Although competitors could join the community, Kwik-Fit's investment in IT and their existing experience of electronic trading made some of the advantage sustainable.

Both Kwik-Fit and Ford have passed through phases of using proprietary EDI to gain a competitive edge in their own sector but both were drawn into more complex associations to develop electronic commerce cooperatively in a diverse community. Their early proprietary systems had their competitive advantage eroded by competitors emulating them using nonproprietary standards. The cost implications for suppliers of maintaining dual systems created pressure to migrate to open industry standards.

## Markets

The proprietary airline reservation systems and AHSC's hospital supply system became electronic markets when they opened up to carry the products of competitors. However, examples of electronic markets, bringing together many buyers and sellers, are rare. The cases in our study include Origo in life assurance, which was developing an intermediary system linking insurance companies and brokers, and two cases where existing intermediaries, the procurement division of the Scottish Health Service, and Whitakers in book distribution, were developing electronic commerce.

The Supplies Division of the Common Services Agency (CSA) was established to provide centralized purchasing for the entire Scottish Health Service and is the largest procurement body in Scotland by turnover. This large turnover of orders, the large number of suppliers involved and the need to provide their customers, the Regional Health Boards, with a high level of service made EDI attractive.

The CSA was keen to ensure the use of open standards, so as to avoid proprietary links, which they saw had been developed by some pharmaceutical companies to gain competitive advantage. As a public organization, the CSA decided that they should implement EDI in a way which provided value for money and did not engender anticompetitive trading relationships. In pursuing this they faced the problem that they do not have hierarchical power over the Regional Health Boards, and so EDI implementation depends on persuasion. This weakness has been accentuated by the emergence of local Health Trusts, which include hospitals, which can take over responsibility for their own purchasing, buying direct from suppliers or wholesalers. With the health service reforms and the fragmentation of the Scottish Health Service into trusts, the role of the CSA is in question. In this context, the CSA has seen EDI as a way of reinforcing their role and value to the newly independent hospitals. Thus the CSA sought to develop a central EDI service, integrated with their contractual procurement negotiations through the provision of an electronic catalogue, which will then allow the trusts to buy in a 'turn-key' solution. The CSA's EDI policy may therefore be seen as a strategic move to defend its position by becoming an electronic intermediary between health trusts and the suppliers of drugs and supplies. As the range of products offered through this system increases it will become less like a service linking the two sides and more like an electronic market, offering the hospitals an array of competing products. However, the CSA's electronic commerce strategy is under threat from the major healthcare suppliers offering electronic ordering services which bypass the CSA, using electronic ordering as one element in a policy of developing long-term ‘strategic partnerships’ with hospitals.

A similar pattern, whereby existing intermediaries developed electronic commerce as a defence to the threat of being displaced by electronic markets operated by third parties, was seen in book distribution (Spinardi et al., 1996). In the late 1970s a consortium of large publishers and WH Smith had established teleordering to provide a simple electronic book ordering service. This service used Whitaker's Books in Print as the database that incoming orders were matched against to identify the publisher to which the order was routed, either electronically or by fax or post. The system operated using proprietary messages and customized hardware. However, the development of open EDI in the book trade in the late 1980s led to the formation of a competing service – First Edition – in 1992.

First Edition was established as a joint venture between Vista and Book Data. Vista is a computer services company which has a dominant share of the UK market for book trade supply and order systems. Just as EDI could be seen as a logical extension of these internal systems so too could it provide a way for Vista to expand its business. Book Data, as a competitor to Whitaker, had a more strategic reason for getting involved in EDI. Both Book Data and Whitaker compile and sell databases of bibliographic information. They differ in that Whitaker's Books in Print is a compilation of all current books, whereas Book Data's database contains only those books which publishers pay them to list. However, Book Data's database was developed especially with the library market in mind and contains much more information, such as contents and other bibliographic details, whilst Books in Print provides only a 'bare bones' listing.

The launch of First Edition thus spurred Whitaker to appreciate the importance of teleordering for the future success of Books in Print. Teleordering has been redeveloped to provide full interconnection with other network services, using any message formats desired – essentially establishing itself as a book trade specific network. Moreover, Whitaker has established another service to run alongside teleordering – BookTrack – which will compile book sales data. Teleordering thus provides a clearing house for book ordering, whereas First Edition is more suited for specific company to company trading links. In the book trade, electronic ordering emerged as a third party service that was more attractive than EDI for the many small publishers and distributors who lacked the technical expertise to install EDI and the sales volumes to justify acquisition.

The life assurance example was different to the health service and book distribution cases in that there were no existing intermediaries between the life assurance companies and the independent financial advisors or brokers who sold their policies. This meant that there was no foundation upon which to build electronic trading, but also a strong incentive for the life assurance companies to avoid their distribution channel falling under the control of a third party electronic intermediary.

The life assurance industry in the UK comprises over 30 insurance companies selling policies through a mix of two channels: through tied agents who represent a single insurer and through independent financial advisors (IFAs) who select a policy meeting the needs of their clients from the range of competing products. The links between insurers and tied agents are ideally suited for the development of proprietary systems for capturing client data and describing the insurer's products, but proprietary links with IFAs would force the IFAs to maintain multiple parallel nonstandard systems. The potential market in linking life assurance offices and IFAs led to the development in the 1980s of two competing viewdata systems controlled by third parties. In 1988 IT managers in two of the major life offices realized that they shared a common interest in developing more sophisticated links with IFAs independent of third-party control. After contacting other life offices Origo was founded. It was supported by 20 life offices to coordinate the collaborative development of electronic commerce. They faced two key difficulties: first ensuring that the system would be flexible to accommodate the diverse range of products and information required to enable the life offices to continue to compete through product differentiations; and second guaranteeing that the system could not be abused by IT suppliers, telecom services, IFAs or individual life offices. The requirement for the system to be independent led to a labyrinthine ownership of the technology. Origo established a company, Insurance Trading Exchange Ltd, trading as The Exchange, as a joint venture with AT&T to sell electronic commerce services to the IFAs and life offices. Although AT&T own 30% of The Exchange they are not guaranteed the contract to operate the service, which is periodically put out to tender. To gain IFA involvement, Origo was instrumental in establishing the Large Intermediaries Group, a consortium of the larger intermediaries who were expected to become the major users of the electronic commerce system, to provide an IFA input into the specification of the system. To accommodate the flexibility required by the life offices, Origo developed the common trading platform which enabled the life offices to specify electronic forms on the system for each of their products, therefore enabling a life office to collect the information which they actuarially consider relevant and launch new products overnight without forewarning their competitors.

These three cases demonstrate that although from an economic point of view electronic markets should offer advantages over conventional intermediary structures, the tendency towards monopoly which these cost advantages bring creates obstacles to their formation. In the Origo case this results in a complex institutional structure to ensure the electronic intermediary's independence.

## Limited impacts

Although electronic commerce implies exchange between computer systems in two firms, it does not imply that the system in each firm is integrated with other systems. Users enrolled into communities may use an isolated microcomputer to receive orders by EDI which are then printed off and treated as if they had been received by mail, with no integration between EDI and internal systems. As a first step into electronic trading this minimizes the complexity of being electronically linked, but also limits its potential impacts. Ford provided early users of Fordnet with a complete stand-alone package. Ford benefited from lower data processing costs within Ford but the system had only limited impacts for its trading partners. Another case of the limited use of electronic commerce was seen in Honeywell's Scottish manufacturing plant.

EDI implementation at Honeywell was as a result of bilateral negotiation between firms with no sector EDI user group or community involved, based on international standard messages and using an EDI software package developed for IBM's suppliers. EDI was initially suggested to Honeywell by a small local supplier which was receiving orders electronically from IBM and identified this as an opportunity to enhance its service to other customers. Honeywell started sending EDI messages using the same message standards as IBM. When messages were exchanged Honeywell found that IBM's messages and the supplier's software were not compliant with the published standard but had been customized to suit IBM's particular trading practices, allowing only one item per electronic order. The supplier had to modify their software to accommodate Honeywell's orders. From this initial link further links were established with other suppliers. However, only a small proportion of Honeywell's procurement was electronic and the internal impact of EDI within Honeywell was small, with the inventory control system generating electronic orders in parallel with paper orders. In the suppliers the impacts were even less, with electronic orders received by a stand-alone personal computer, printed off, checked and then manually entered into the supplier's order processing system. Such EDI was merely a substitute for the postal service or fax. To gain the benefits of EDI – rapid, accurate transmission of orders – but without the need for suppliers to invest in computer hardware and software, Honeywell developed EDI-fax whereby its stock control system automatically generates and transmits a fax order. This system was very successful, with 200 suppliers (representing 80% of orders) connected in one year, compared to only three suppliers using full EDI after three years.

## Cooperative communities

Where the potential users are planning to develop electronic commerce within an existing trading community the dangers of one player abusing its position are less than in the formation of electronic markets. However a mechanism is still needed to agree on standards and coordinate cooperation. We saw earlier that the unilateral approaches seen in Kwik-Fit and Ford were eventually replaced by participation in cooperative bodies. This development of electronic commerce through interorganizational cooperation was seen in the newsprint and magazine distribution sectors.

The newsprint sector is characterized by an international market for a standardized product dominated by a handful of paper producers supplying a large number of geographically scattered local newspaper printers. Two contrasting developments were studied in this sector. On the one hand, the European Commission funded a paper industry pilot for EDI (known as PIPE), and then later provided support for this grouping to form a European paper industry group, EDIPAP. The focus of this initiative was to develop an electronic trading community in an industrial sector which already operated at a European level. In addition, a single UK printer – West Ferry Printers – pushed EDI onto its suppliers, and instigated a UK newsprint EDI user group, SIGNET (special interest group for newsprint EDI technology). Whereas the EDIPAP process could be seen as the outcome of a political initiative to bring together artificially a diverse range of companies that resulted in protracted message development by committee, SIGNET could be seen as emerging organically from specific business requirements. West Ferry's interest in EDI stemmed from the development of a new reel tracking system, and EDI was seen as a way of obtaining detailed reel data. Additionally, EDI provided supply chain information as to the whereabouts of paper shipments. However, EDI implementation has not proceeded rapidly in the newsprint community, probably because the development of sophisticated reel tracking, with which to integrate EDI fully, would have required substantial investment at a time of recession. Instead, the tendency has been to more superficial implementations, automating some existing functions, rather than providing new ones.

The magazine and newspaper distribution sector is markedly different to the newsprint sector: it is geographically segmented, the product has a short shelf-life and, in the UK, it is dominated by two firms, Menzies and WH Smiths. In the magazine/newspaper trade, initial EDI implementation was carried out by a few large organizations, which sought paperless exchange of high volume transactions, especially for 'claims' on unsold copies. The messages developed by these companies became the de facto sector standards and the expectation of 'network externality' benefits led to more explicit cooperation through the formation of a user group, NewPET (news trade and publishing EDI technical group). With a small number of organizations exchanging high volumes of data, EDI diffusion has been rapid. There are also potential benefits of extending EDI beyond the automation of existing data flows. For example, obtaining sales data via EDI could provide publishers with the information necessary to carry out sales analysis to determine allocation levels for individual retailers – a function previously undertaken by the wholesalers. However, this remains largely a technical possibility, since it is dependant on the cooperation of the wholesalers for its large scale implementation.

NewPET typifies the tension between cooperation and competition within EDI user groups. On the one hand, building a community of potential trading partners requires the development of common messages for EDI transactions. On the other hand, sharing new advances in EDI may involve surrendering hard won competitive advantage. As both the dominant company in the magazine trade and also the initiator of EDI in the sector, IPC have come to have the greatest reservations about NewPET. Rather than following the consensual and slower process of developing new messages within NewPET, IPC has tended to push ahead with its own initiatives without consultation, so as to gain the advantage of being first. However, this approach has generated concern within NewPET as to the wider utility of the messages thus developed.

## Network industrial structures

Close task integration across networks of firms has been viewed as an example of the emergence of network industrial structures, where relationships are neither hierarchical nor market based, and as an example of the 'virtual corporation' (Davidow and Malone, 1992), where integration leads to financially independent entities behaving increasingly like a single firm. Electronic commerce holds out the prospect of providing the informational integration to support this task integration. In this scenario electronic commerce goes beyond the exchange of orders and invoices to include, for example, the exchange of engineering data. It is this goal of informational integration which underpins the American Department of Defense CALS (computer-aided acquisition and logistic support, recently renamed continuous acquisition and life-cycle support) programme (Smith, 1990) and the equivalent British Ministry of Defence CIRPLS (computer integration of requirements procurement, logistics and support) programmes to automate data exchange within defence projects. Both programmes embody the long term goal of replacing the existing hierarchical structure of major defence projects, where prime contractors oversee activities in contractors who may themselves subcontract activities to lower levels, with a network structure in which all parties share access to a central database.

The electronic commerce implementation studied in the aerospace sector was instigated to exchange complex design data between the four national prime contractors involved in the Eurofighter project: BAe in the UK, Alenia in Italy, DASA in Germany, and CASA in Spain (Spinardi et al., 1995). It was intended to speed up data exchange, reduce staff time for manual input, and reduce errors and synchronization problems. It was particularly critical for Eurofighter, compared to previous joint projects like Tornado, because parts designed by one company were to be manufactured by another. Ideally the firms involved would like electronic data exchange to feed directly into internal systems which allow onward processing to manufacturing systems. The partners agreed to adapt an existing, but never used, AECMA (Association Européenne des Constructeurs de Matériel Aérospatial) message for product structure exchange. Though a suitable revision was agreed, this proved far from sufficient to allow the data exchange to operate. In practice data harmonization has proved far more difficult than message standardization. Although it was agreed what data elements a message should contain, the interpretation and internal usage of these data varies between the companies. Previous manual interpretation of these differing practices was possible, but electronic computer-to-computer transfer requires a much higher level of harmonization in working practices. This is very difficult, time-consuming and expensive to achieve because of the level of internal computerization with which the partners have embedded their differing working practices.

## Classification of cases

In categorizing the scope of electronic commerce we distinguish electronic trading networks that conservatively embed existing industry relationships; incrementally change relationships; or radically restructure industrial relationships. We argue that this is more helpful than Benjamin et al. (1990) dichotomy, which seeks to address a similar dimension, between 'routine' transaction processing and 'non-routine' task support. First, their distinction is somewhat circular: where the information exchanged covers 'non-routine tasks', as was the case in Eurofighter, by exchanging it the exchange becomes routine. Moreover, the key underlying distinction is between the transfer to electronic means of existing interorganizational communication, where data harmonization should not be problematic and the impact on the structure of the relationship small, and the exchange of new information, where the problems of harmonization and uncertainty of its impacts are much greater.

This is exemplified in our case studies: the greatest problems of data harmonization were observed in Eurofighter, magazine distribution and life assurance, where new information exchanges were being developed; much less difficulty was encountered in the exchange of orders, especially in the proprietary system introduced by Kwik-Fit. In this case, and at Ford and Honeywell, electronic commerce simply reproduced existing paper transactions, whereas in all the other examples electronic commerce, though starting with existing transactions, evolved to include novel transactions, incrementally changing the data exchanged electronically. Given the literature on the scope for radical restructuring through electronic commerce, our schema caters for this possibility; however, none of our cases included radical change.

Drawing on these nine cases, a refined typology of electronic commerce has been developed based on the structure of the community and the scope of electronic commerce.

## Structure of the community: electronic markets and electronic webs

A distinction can be drawn between electronic markets and electronic webs. Electronic markets bring together dispersed buyers and sellers, replacing existing intermediary institutions. Electronic markets may be subdivided into electronic market places, which present the purchasers with an array of priced products, and electronic auctions, which simulate the real-time auctioning of lots, and therefore require a system running interactively. Electronic Webs\* automate the interchange between organizations in stable relationships. The commitment of both parties to these relationships overcomes legal uncertainties and minimizes unwillingness to invest in EDI.

In the automotive, electronics, vehicles, magazine and newsprint cases electronic commerce has been implemented within close communities of organizations with long term stable relationships. These can therefore be seen as exemplars of electronic commerce reifying network industrial structures through the creation of an electronic web. The books distribution and health service cases, though initially implemented between pairs of organizations in long term stable relationships, are evolving into electronic markets where the system becomes a market intermediary, presenting buyers with a range of available products from an array of competing primary suppliers. The Origo insurance system developed from a videotex system to present insurance brokers with the products of rival insurance companies was essentially an electronic market from its inception.

## Scope of electronic interchange: conservative, incremental and radical implementations

Conservative implementation is the transfer of information previously exchanged by other means, in which existing practices provide a template from which electronic transactions can be derived. In conservative implementations issues of form and semantic content have been largely reconciled, so standardization activities focus on developing an electronic message structure, frequently through adapting an existing paper message.

Incremental implementation is the extension of electronic trading between partners through step changes involving the exchange of data not previously exchanged. This may involve standardizing information which was not previously standardized, so the data harmonization problems are more complex.

Radical implementation corresponds with the concept of business network redesign (Short and Venkatraman, 1992), where electronic commerce is used radically to restructure relations across a community of organizations. No examples of such radical implementations were seen in our survey.

Electronic trading networks may embed existing industry relationships, incrementally change relationships or restructure industrial relationships. In the electronics and vehicles examples electronic commerce simply reproduced existing paper transactions, whereas in all the other examples electronic commerce had started reproducing existing transactions but was evolving to include novel transactions, incrementally changing the data exchanged electronically. None of the cases included radical change.

## Conclusion: underlying trends in the development of electronic commerce

Analysis of these nine cases uncovers three trends in the evolution of electronic commerce: a change in emphasis from competitive advantage towards the gaining of sector-wide improvements in efficiency and effectiveness, the formation of electronic markets, and a process of incremental rather than radical development within communities (see Figure 1).

## Change in emphasis from competitive to operational benefits

As electronic commerce has developed, a shift in emphasis has occurred towards gaining benefits in operational efficiency. The reasons for this are four-fold. First, any first-mover advantage will be challenged by competitors who will seek to emulate the first-mover's system. Second, trading partners do not want to become tied to one system, particularly in electronic market systems. Third, in many industries, no single organization has sufficient dominance to impose electronic trading. Fourth, the benefits of open cooperation to gain operational efficiency advantages may exceed the competitive benefits of single-mover strategic implementations, particularly where the dominant enterprises are purchasers. Strategic systems designed to lock-in customers have a strong tendency to open up to competitors due to competition regulation and the additional value to the system user of greater choice and the system provider of greater trading volumes. These pressures eventually led Ford to decide to migrate to open standards and has also led McKesson, one of the early examples of proprietary strategic systems, to open its electronic commerce system to American open standard messages (Gebhart, 1992).

The underlying pattern is now for electronic trading to be initiated conservatively in ‘web’ communities; this minimizes the uncertainty faced by participants and the complexity of agreeing the form of trading. Systems then develop incrementally, either towards becoming markets or towards reconfigured relationships, and the communities grow and overlap, creating an electronic quilt rather than a homogeneous global electronic trading community.

## Formation of electronic markets

The potential economic benefits of electronic markets of lower search costs and greater competition have been recognized (Bakos, 1991), but their formation is inhibited by the need to bring together sufficient buyers and sellers to create a viable marketplace; unlike electronic webs they cannot be easily initiated by a small core of firms. Descriptions of the formation of early electronic markets, particularly in airline reservation systems, show how electronic links between major firms and their customers, established to lock the customer into a single supplier, came under pressure from customers and from competition regulation to open up to competitors, leading to systems offering the products of a range of suppliers, albeit controlled by the founder. However, this pattern has been difficult to replicate in other sectors which lack a core firm with the strength to overcome the suspicion of customers that they will be tied in to a monopoly intermediary. The cases demonstrated two further routes by which electronic markets form. First, existing market intermediaries may switch their links with trading partners to electronic commerce, then incrementally develop the system into an electronic market. Their existing links, expertise and the fear of other electronic services usurping their market position make the development of an electronic market attractive. This was seen in the book ordering and health service systems. Second, close communities trading intensively may cooperate to create a new electronic intermediary. This was seen with the creation of Origo out of an alliance of life assurance companies and brokers. However, the complex institutional structure created in this example demonstrates the difficulty of avoiding the creation of a powerful new third party intermediary.

## Incremental development rather than radical change

Early accounts, which viewed electronic commerce within the context of strategic information systems, stressed its potential to restructure interfirm relationships. Most of these arguments had their roots in institutional economics, suggesting that the reduction in transaction costs with electronic commerce would lead to activities increasingly being outsourced. In practice the impacts of electronic commerce on industrial structure have been slight. Claims have been made that electronic commerce facilitates radical restructuring of industries. However even in the example used by Short and Venkatraman (1992), American Hospital Supply, the restructuring was driven by a single core organization and came about through an accumulation of incremental innovations in the use of IT. The common pattern was for companies with stable customer communities to use IT links to replicate existing links. The systems then develop incrementally, widening the range of information exchanged.

This study found no evidence that electronic commerce will precipitate rapid industrial restructuring. However, through the development of electronic markets (as seen in the life assurance sector) and the extension of data exchange in design activities (aerospace) it is hypothesized that electronic trading will have progressively more significant impacts.

The research demonstrated the impracticability of radical restructuring across organizations; none of the implementations studied could be classified as radical. All the communities studied started as conservative web implementations, except Origo in life assurance which started as a development from existing videotex systems. There is also evidence of systems evolving from webs towards markets, as was seen in the book industry, with intermediaries developing competing systems to link purchasers to publishers, and the Scottish Health Service, where health service reforms are leading the system to become a market intermediary linking hospitals to suppliers. In the electronics and vehicle systems studied there was no evidence of the system evolving beyond existing interorganizational transactions.

The barrier to the radical restructuring of industry is the difficulty of coordinating radical change across a network of organizations. Radical change both increases the complexity of the new processes to be agreed and increases the risk and uncertainty faced by participants. Emergent electronic commerce communities tend implicitly to accept existing industrial structure rather than tackle the difficulties of radical change. This closure of existing industrial structure means that any industrial restructuring occurs incrementally.

These considerations suggest a need for caution in assessing the claims that business process reengineering can achieve radical restructuring of interorganizational relationships through the adoption of electronic commerce and other IT systems. Whilst proponents of BPR cite instances where such radical restructuring has been achieved (Scott Morton, 1991), examination of their cases reveals that these were achieved through incremental programmes of technological and organizational innovation (Copeland and McKenney, 1988). Only in retrospect can these changes be presented as the result of a programmatic strategy.
