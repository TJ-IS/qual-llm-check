# An empirical evaluation of the impact of CASE on developer productivity and software quality

R.T. COUPE and N.M. ONODU

Operational Research Group, University of Birmingham, UK

This paper presents the findings of research into the impact of computer aided software engineering (CASE) tools. It is based on a questionnaire survey of software developers in UK organizations. CASE had a positive effect on developer productivity and the quality of applications software. It particularly improved the reliability and accuracy of applications software, though this was sometimes offset by a deterioration in software efficiency. There was little evidence of productivity being ‘traded’ for quality, since developers citing productivity gains also tended to report quality improvements. The extent to which organizations were able to realize the benefits of CASE depended on the experience, competence and training of individual developers. This has significant implications for the selection and training of personnel and the use of consultants. Methodologically, the findings also serve to underline the importance of explicitly taking account of prior development environments, when using retrospective methods to evaluate different software technologies.

## Introduction

There is a need to develop new software and upgrade existing systems to meet competitive challenges, to plan effectively, and to manage the day to day running of organizations. The pressure is increasing for developers to work more efficiently and produce better quality systems more quickly than before. The updating and maintenance of old systems as well as the amount of development backlog is becoming a burden for many organizations (Suomi, 1990). Chief executives are therefore looking for technology that will support more effective and faster decision making, produce more accurate information, improve customer service and corporate image and reduce nonproductive work time (Schumann, 1989). Hence, they need to have the most cost-effective software development technologies, and well-trained and qualified development staff to produce high quality information systems efficiently. Application software, therefore, needs to have high flexibility and reliability, appropriate failure correcting facilities, a good human/computer interface (Parnas et al., 1990), and must meet users' functional requirements.

Computer aided software engineering (CASE) is new development software aimed at satisfying many of these demands. It is competing with fourth generation languages (4GLs) and third generation languages (3GLs) to gain a share of the software development market, bolstered by claims that it delivers superior quality software and increases developer productivity. CASE can be defined as a fully integrated, automated software development tool, for producing effective information systems that can help organizations fulfil their objectives.

However, potential users are typically cautious about adopting new technology, especially if the benefits are not clear but the costs and risk involved in the acquisition of this technology are quite real for all to see (Morrissey and Wu, 1979). It is, therefore, important for organizations to know whether or not they are likely to benefit from new investment in CASE technology, and whether the improvements, if any, in system quality and staff productivity justify this expenditure. Earlier studies of the impact of CASE tools (Hayley and Lyman, 1990; Wijers and Van Dort, 1990) necessarily included many new adopters with little experience. Although there are still relatively few organizations using CASE software, more of them now have developers who have substantial experience with CASE and are better able to judge its value.

The purpose of this paper is to evaluate the impact of CASE on developer productivity and application software quality and, hence, draw conclusions about its commercial effectiveness. Findings from a survey of software developers are used to assess the relative benefits and drawbacks of using new CASE tools, compared with other software technologies. The experience of these practising professionals in building information systems with the different software technologies offers insights into their impact on productivity and software quality. The research design takes account of other factors, such as training and experience, which can have an important bearing on the quality of the final system and how it is built, as well as influencing perceptions of development technologies (Liang, 1986).

## Existing work

There have been relatively few studies aimed at assessing the impact of CASE. To some extent these have produced conflicting findings. This may, in part, be because the studies might have included developers who were using different CASE tools, and had widely differing computing backgrounds. The findings might also tend to undervalue CASE technology because of the high proportions of new CASE users. About half of the developers sampled by Hayley and Lyman (1990) had used CASE for under two years, and those sampled by Wijers and Van Dort (1990) for less than one year.

Notwithstanding this, there is evidence that CASE is viewed as improving productivity during analysis and design (Selamat et al., 1994b), while other developers have attributed productivity improvements to data flow diagramming tools and the data dictionary (Norman and Nunamaker, 1989). Evidence about the impact of CASE tools on software quality is somewhat contradictory. Results from surveys from the New York Life CASE support team indicate that CASE reduces defects in software and hence corrective maintenance (Zagorsky, 1990), thereby improving productivity. In contrast, Selemat et al., (1994b) found no evidence of a quality improvement, though no respondents reported a drop in quality. Developers also believe that CASE improves team morale and communication between developers and end-users, and encourages the learning of software methodologies and structured techniques (Zagorsky, 1990). Two surveys addressing the reasons for failure of CASE found that it reflected training deficiencies and an inadequate knowledge of CASE implementation (Selemat et al., 1993, 1994a).

It appears that CASE may, therefore, be meeting some of the needs of potential users, though the results of studies of the principal reasons for acquiring CASE also show some differences. Burkhard and Jenster (1989) cite both speeding up development and improving the accuracy and quality of software, while Dutch developers are motivated by potential quality benefits alone, rather than by both these and productivity gains (Wijers and Van Dort, 1990).

Conclusions regarding the impact of CASE need to be placed on a firmer footing, by surveying a population with a higher proportion of experienced CASE users. There is also a need to investigate how CASE affects productivity in the various stages of the software, and to consider in greater detail its impact on the various aspects of software quality. This research aims to meet these needs, and gauge the effects of CASE on both productivity and quality, and determine if gains in some areas of software development are at the expense of losses in others.

## Methodology

The principal research instrument was a mailed questionnaire survey, targeted at larger UK organizations, during the spring of 1993. Costly new software technologies tend to diffuse hierarchically, the initial adopters being companies with the largest turnover as these are likely to be able to take more risks (Hayley and Lyman, 1990). Given the lengthy training needs of CASE (Souza, 1991), it is these early adopters who are likely to offer the best opportunity for assessing the potential benefits of this software development technology.

## Research design

Software developers' perceptions were used to measure comparatively the productivity and quality impact of CASE, 4GLs and 3GLs. The views of practising professionals enable the operational qualities of the various software technologies to be reliably judged across a wide industrial spectrum. It is difficult to supplement these with objective measures, since few IT departments keep detailed resource utilization and productivity records regarding applications development (Parkinson, 1990).

Developers were asked to compare recent software development they have undertaken using either CASE or 4GL technology, with previous applications they have developed using other development software. Despite the risk of memory lapse, this ‘retrospective method’ is an assured approach in comparison with other methods, since each individual developer must make a direct comparison between different software technologies (Coupe, 1994).

The questionnaire design enables a distinction to be drawn, for instance, between those comparing CASE with 4GLs and those comparing it with 3GLs. Though the majority of existing studies of CASE use a retrospective approach, few make explicit comparisons with previous development environments. In this research, CASE developers who previously used 4GLs are distinguished from those who had only used less productive 3GLs. Factors such as personnel experience and qualifications, the applications area, and knowledge and use of a SAD methodology, can also affect developer productivity and software quality, and are taken account of in the research design and analysis.

## Sampling

A sample was drawn from the population of UK companies operating at or above £50 million at the end of 1992, from the key British Enterprises directory (Dun and Bradstreet) 1993 directory. Questionnaires were mailed to IT managers, with the request that they be completed by software developers actively using CASE, or if CASE were not in use, a 4GL developer. In all, 1400 organizations were targeted and 127 questionnaires were returned, a 9% response rate. Over 5% of the questionnaires were returned incomplete because the organizations concerned used neither CASE nor 4GL technologies, and it is likely that this was also true of a significant portion of the other nonrespondents.

## Sample representativeness

To verify the representativeness of the sample, the standard industrial classification (SIC) of the sample companies was compared with that for the population. Overall, the survey respondents were reasonably representative, though responses from mining and light industrial companies were slightly under-represented, while those from service industries were somewhat over-represented.

Sample representativeness was also assessed by sending short, supplementary questionnaires on postcards to a random sample of nonrespondents, and comparing the responses from these with those to the main questionnaire and with the survey population. The distribution of SIC business areas on the postcard survey was the same as those of the population and the main survey. In addition, responses to key questions assessing respondents' attitudes to productivity and quality changes did not differ between the main and supplementary surveys, confirming that the characteristics of nonrespondents and respondents are comparable. Hence, the research findings presented here can be regarded as representative of the perceptions and experience of developers working in leading UK companies.

## Characteristics of the organizations

As can be seen from Figure 1, most of the responding companies were from the manufacturing industry, particularly heavy manufacturing, such as manufacturers of electronic equipment, transport equipment, industrial machinery and concrete products, and from the wholesale and retail trade. Transportation and other various service industries were also quite strongly represented. There were no responses from developers working in public administration, finance, insurance and agriculture. This reflected the fact that there were very few of these groups in the population.

The organizations that responded varied considerably in employee size and company turnover. The number of employees ranged from 200 to 55 000 with an average of almost 500, while the turnover also had a wide range with an average of £564 million (see Table 1).

## Productivity findings

## Definition of productivity

Software development productivity can be defined as the quantity of software produced per paid hour of labour (Byras, 1982). However, it is clear that respondents commonly based their personal productivity perceptions on their own productivity experiences and on the number of people it takes to complete development tasks, without also taking account of their own or their teams' combined wages. Doke's (1989) more general definition of productivity, relating level of work output to labour input, is therefore more pertinent to this research.

Survey respondents were asked to assess how CASE tools affected overall team productivity during the various stages of software development, compared with the development technology – either 4GL or 3GL – that they had previously used or were concurrently using.

## Overall productivity

Respondents judged that CASE markedly improves productivity, suggesting that there is a potential for considerable productivity benefits to accrue to organizations that invest in CASE software. Improved overall productivity was reported by developers making comparisons with 4GLs as well as those who contrasted CASE with 3GLs, although as expected, productivity gains were regarded as much more substantial by the prior 3GL users (see Table 2: Mann-Whitney, p < 0.05). While 86% of developers with 3GL experience thought CASE increased productivity, this was true of only 55% of those with 4GL experience. Only 7% of those with 3GL experience thought CASE was slightly less productive, whilst 34% of the previous 4GL developers perceived a productivity deterioration, half of which were classified as 'large falls'. The productivity gains from replacing 4GLs with CASE, therefore, appear to be less marked and less certain.

## Explanation of overall productivity perceptions

Even when controlling for developers' previous computing experience (3GL/4GL), the way respondents judged the relative productivity of the technologies showed considerable variation. Prior 3GL developers' response varied between a 'large increase' and a 'slight fall' in productivity, while for those with 4GL experience, their responses spanned the full range of perceptions, from a 'large increase' to a 'large fall' in productivity. These wide differences in perceptions might be attributable to the following influences.

## Prior development environments

There could be other important differences between respondents' past development environments, besides whether 4GLs or 3GLs were used, that affect their views of CASE. For developers with 4GL experience, competence in structured methods was important, since those who had previously used structured methods, especially data flow diagrams (DFDs), rated CASE productivity more highly (chi-square, p < 0.01), suggesting that greater competence in structured methods enables more productive use of CASE software. It would appear that either enhanced training in these methods, or a greater emphasis on them when recruiting CASE developers, would improve developer productivity, and hence organizations' returns on CASE investment. Competence in structured methods did not appear to have the same importance for developers with 3GL experience.

The particular 4GL(s) and 3GL(s) that respondents had previously used were also examined, but were found not to affect respondents' perceptions of CASE productivity.

## CASE development environment

It might be expected that the character of the CASE environment, notably the particular CASE tool used and the types of applications being developed, would help to account for respondents' differing productivity ratings. However, the only significant effect was that prior 3GL developers rating CASE productivity highly were especially employed in light manufacturing and service industries.

## Experience of CASE

The quality and amount of CASE experience was also examined for its effect on productivity ratings. Among prior 4GL developers, those who had less CASE experience – measured in terms of numbers of projects – gave lower productivity ratings (M-W, p < 0.05), suggesting that inexperience is hampering efficient use of CASE. This was not true for those with prior 3GL experience, while the amount of CASE training in itself did not appear influential.

## Developers' knowledge and abilities

Developers' knowledge and abilities, in areas other than CASE or structured methods, might also affect productivity ratings. Developers' qualifications, occupational experience, experience of the organization in which they work, and their programming abilities were used as measures. Prior 3GL developers, a group with quite diverse computing qualifications (HND, MSc, City and Guilds), rated CASE productivity more highly (chi-square(CHI), $p < 0.01$ ). This was also true of the 4GL developers who had a lower opinion of their own programming abilities (CHI, $p < 0.01$ ). Since it is a technology that places fewer demands on programming skills, such developers might tend to value CASE productivity more highly. In the past, they would tend to have used 4GL technology less productively than the other more able 4GL programmers.

## Management commitment

Differences in the quality of management commitment to the use of CASE technology did not appear to affect the productivity ratings given to CASE software. Job status did affect the ratings of prior 3GL developers, with project leaders viewing CASE productivity more favourably (CHI, p < 0.01).

## Productivity and development life-cycle stages

In view of the operational characteristics of CASE tools, the programming and testing stages of the conventional six stage life-cycle were combined to give the following five stages: strategy and analysis; design; programming and testing; system implementation; and maintenance.

## CASE compared with 3GL

Compared with 3GLs, CASE was seen as substantially improving productivity in every life-cycle stage (Table 3). The bulk of developers regarded CASE as achieving greater productivity: maintenance (83% citing greater productivity), design (77%), programming and testing (74%), and strategy and analysis (63%). The productivity benefits for system implementation were less highly regarded, with only 36% citing improved productivity. Nevertheless, there was no life-cycle stage where more than 13% of respondents experienced a loss in productivity, while on drop in productivity was reported when using CASE tools for programming and testing activities.

## CASE compared with 4GL

The more modest overall productivity gains from CASE reported by prior 4GL developers, compared with those with 3GL experience, is confirmed in the effect across development life-cycle stages (Table 4). Respondents found that there were substantial productivity gains during the earlier stages: strategy and analysis (55% citing greater productivity), design (51%) and programming and testing (51%). During the later stages, however, productivity gains were balanced by losses: 25% citing losses during system implementation outweighed the 19% who perceived gains, while the 47% who thought CASE improved maintenance productivity were counterbalanced by the 35% who had seen a deterioration.

While less striking than for 3GLs, there are important productivity benefits to organizations using 4GLs, notably with respect to the analysis, design and coding of systems. It is likely that the recruitment of developers with greater experience of CASE and structured methods, as well as the gains in experience by existing users, will enhance these benefits, and reduce the risk of a productivity fall during implementation and maintenance.

## The quality impact of CASE

## Definition of software quality

The quality impact of software technologies, including CASE, has always been a much harder issue to address. This reflects, in part, the difficulty in defining and measuring software quality. It is important that the definition covers the extent to which a system correctly and reliably implements functional elements so that the required output is produced with minimum defects. It should also take account of the end-user's perception of software quality which emphasizes a good human-computer interface, high responsiveness, and efficient access to appropriate information (Hoplin and Suresh, 1989).

In this study, software quality was considered to be a combination of eight distinctive quality characteristics or factors (McCall et al., 1977; Arthur, 1985; Watts, 1987). Their relative importance can vary according to the type of end-user and the application area: banking systems, for instance, need to have a high degree of correctness. The factors are:

\- Capability: produces the output end-users requires

\- Reliability: does the software work properly without failure?

\- Usability: can the end-user operate it easily?

\- Efficiency: executes with a minimum of processing time

\- Accuracy: precision in calculation and output

\- Reusability: reuse of programs or modules in other applications

\- Security: software and data protected from security breeches

\- Interoperability: Ease of interfacing with other programmes

The definitions of these factors were included in the survey questionnaires, so that every respondent would use a standard framework to evaluate software quality. In addition, respondents were asked for an overall view about the relative impact of CASE and other technologies on quality.

## Overall software quality

Developers perceive that CASE improves the quality of applications software. As with productivity, the additional quality benefits offered were more marked in comparison with 3GL than with 4GL technologies (Table 5): 77% of prior 3GL users, but only 33% of 4GL users, regarded CASE as producing better quality software. None of those with 3GL experience rated CASE as worse than 3GLs, suggesting that a switch from 3GL technologies to CASE virtually guarantees improvements in applications software quality. Even with a switch from 4GLs to CASE, though fewer improvements were reported, there is only a small chance of deterioration in software quality: only 7% of prior 4GL users rated CASE as worse (interestingly all of them cited 'much worse') than 4GLs, while the bulk (some 60%) thought the two technologies to be of an equivalent standard.

## Quality and training

Developers with more training gave CASE the highest quality ratings (CHI, p < 0.05) confirming that, in general, its benefits can be better exploited by formal training than by individual effort (Gibson et al., 1990; Souza, 1991). With regard to software quality, 67% of prior 3GL users with at least one month's training regarded CASE as much better, compared with only 19% of those trained for less than a month.

## Quality and structured methods

CASE tools involve the use of structured methods in order to coordinate the development process and achieve certain specified standards: 74% of the developers who had used 3GLs believed that the need to adhere to structured methods improved the quality of applications, with 15% regarding it as the predominant factor (Table 6). They were particularly the developers who believed CASE has a positive effect on software quality (M-W, p < 0.05). 4GL developers believed the CASE method had a weaker impact on improving system quality, though 40% cited an influence, which 13% regarded as predominant (Table 6). None of the 3GL developers and only 20% of the 4GL developers believed that structured methods had a negative effect on quality.

It is evident that prior competence in structured methods influences the additional quality benefits of CASE software. It is likely that CASE is benefiting organizations by enforcing the effective use of structured methods.

## Quality and organizational experience

Developers with greater experience of their organizations particularly believed that CASE improved software quality (CHI, p < 0.05). This indicates that the quality benefits of CASE tools can be more fully exploited by personnel who know the business of organizations and the needs and preferences of their end-users.

## Evaluation of software quality characteristics

## CASE compared with 3GL

Developers' assessments using the eight quality factors confirmed the overall quality finding. For all but efficiency, CASE was rated better than the 3GLs used previously (see Table 7). Accuracy and reliability were the quality factors showing the most improvement, with 74% and 68% respectively citing CASE as better. Reusability and capability offered the next highest quality gains, with 63% and 60% reporting improvements, despite 7% and 11%, respectively experiencing deterioration. The quality gains derived from improved usability and interoperability were more modest, with 43% and 38% citing improvement. Though only 31% believed CASE offers enhanced security, nobody viewed it as inferior. Only with respect to the efficiency of software produced was CASE rated worse than 3GLs: the 32% who thought it inferior outweighed the 25% who regarded it as better.

It is clear that the most striking quality benefits are the applications working more successfully due to enhanced reliability resulting in fewer failures, and greater accuracy giving more precise output. This is in line with Zagorsky's (1990) findings. It is these qualities that most strongly influence the overall quality superiority of CASE tools (Kruskal-Wallis (K-W), p < 0.01 for reliability and p < 0.05 for accuracy). Although CASE may not be viewed as being more productive for carrying out maintenance, superior accuracy and reliability are almost certain to lower the amount of corrective maintenance needed, and, thereby, enhance the productivity impact of this technology. The only loss in quality concerns the efficiency of CASE applications which may not run as quickly as 3GL systems, a drawback offset by increasing hardware speed and cheapness.

Factors such as CASE experience, training and type of application, were investigated but did not explain why developers' ratings of the various quality characteristics differed.

## CASE compared with 4GL

When the same eight quality factors were examined in the context of 4GL experience (see Table 8), the relative strengths and weaknesses of CASE perceived by prior 3GL developers persisted, albeit less strongly. Once again, the greatest improvements concerned the reliability and accuracy of software, where 47% and 40% thought CASE superior (fewer than for 3GL respondents). At the other end of the scale, efficiency is rated again as the worst quality feature of CASE, though its downgrading means that 33% cite it as inferior to 4GLs and only 7% as better.

The other quality factors, viewed as important benefits of CASE by prior 3GL developers, were also viewed relatively less positively by those with 4GL experience, CASE was thus assessed as only marginally superior to 4GLs with respect to reusability and capability, while the ratings of security, usability and interoperability had slipped to the extent that CASE was considered to be inferior: though the majority viewed CASE and 4GLs to be equivalent, the developers rating them as worse outnumbered those who thought them better (Table 8).

It is again apparent that certain key quality factors, such as accuracy (CHI, p < 0.05), have a greater impact on respondents' overall quality assessment of CASE than usability and interoperability. It seems that CASE is more highly regarded than 4GLs in terms of the aspects of quality that developers view as most important.

3GL and 4GL developers hold consistent views about the quality impact of CASE tools, placing the eight quality factors in the same order. The difference in absolute ratings awarded by developers from the two groups underlines the importance of explicitly taking account of the key features of prior development environments, when using a retrospective method to evaluate software technologies.

## The trade between productivity and software quality

Productivity can form an inverse relationship with software quality (Case, 1985; Burkhard and Jenster, 1989) so that, for instance, improving quality reduces productivity. On the other hand, better quality analysis and design can improve productivity, by reducing the initial development errors that can consume up to 80% of development costs (Boehm, 1979; Case, 1985).

## Organizational impact

Developers find that CASE is more productive and that it produces systems of higher quality than other software development technologies. These benefits are likely to have an impact on the success of the IT department within the organization and on business success as a whole. The quality improvements, which in particular increased reliability and accuracy, should raise confidence in the use of these applications, enhance end-users' regard for the work carried out by IT departments and help organizations to achieve their goals.

IT plays an important part in the running of organizations. By using technology such as CASE to reduce system development time within IT departments, these systems can therefore be put into operation sooner. This will allow other departments within the organization to benefit more quickly and should make IT departments more responsive to the changing computing needs of organizations. In addition, CASE reduces development time and requires less maintenance, which will help to reduce the backlog of systems development (Dos Santos and Peffers, 1993), giving developers more time to concentrate on other matters which may generate savings in personnel costs.

## Conclusions

CASE has a positive impact on the system development process and applications software. Developers believe that CASE improves productivity, particularly during the earlier stages of the development life-cycle. It also enhances software quality, notably accuracy and reliability, thereby generating further productivity benefits through the reduction in corrective maintenance. Despite some loss in the efficiency of applications software, the decision to adopt CASE tools does not involve a conflict between optimizing productivity or software quality. By improving both, it would appear to represent a shift towards the resolution of this conflict.

The characteristics and qualities of the developers themselves exerted an important influence on the extent to which organizations were able to realize the potential benefits of CASE tools. Greater competence in structured methods and fuller training enhanced the software quality gains. Competence in structured methods also improved the productivity gains, while insufficient experience of CASE hampered its efficient use. There is also evidence that the quality benefits of CASE may be better exploited by software developers who are more thoroughly acquainted with the organizations in which they work. This suggests that organizations should carefully consider the balance between internal personnel and externally recruited consultants and the tasks they are assigned.

Developers' views were strongly influenced by the sorts of development software they had previously used. This is significant methodologically. It highlights the importance of explicitly taking account of previous development environments when using the retrospective approach to evaluate software technologies.

It is, therefore, very likely that investment in CASE software will prove cost-effective through its positive impact on productivity and the key features of software quality. It is, however, clear that organizations will need to exercise care in the selection and training of personnel, and the use of consultants, in order to ensure that full advantage is taken of what CASE technology has to offer.
