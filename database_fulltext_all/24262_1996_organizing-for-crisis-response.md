# Organizing for crisis response

LINDA JO CALLOWAY and PETER G.W. KEEN\*

Pace University, School of Computer Science and Information Systems, 1 Pace Plaza, New York NY10038, USA
\*International Centre for Information Technology, St John, US Virgin Islands

Crises are turning points in organizations. When crisis strikes, fast-response management depends on quickly configuring and deploying information and communications. This paper brings together research and practice in crisis response management, information technology and news media fast-response methods to derive principles for using information technology as an organizational resource. Firms increasingly recognize the need to view their information technology (IT) platform as a key business resource for just-in-time scheduling, distribution, coordination, service and logistics. If they see it as an equally key resource for just-in-time crisis response, it then creates a powerful base for crisis response management. Exxon Valdez and the Gulf War illustrate these requirements. They provide a stage model of crisis response. The model is not intended as a general description of crisis, but as a more specific modelling of organizational response capability. This stage model is illustrated by Dow Corning's recent silicon-gel implant crisis.

## Introduction

This paper discusses the role of a firm's information technology platform as an organizing resource used to meet the growing need for fast-response crisis management. The paper views such a capability as an infrastructure, not an application, in that it constitutes a cross-organizational, and even inter-organizational, multipurpose base that enables a variety of crisis-response facilities. Conversely, the absence of such a base disables them. Crises create a discontinuity – 'before' and 'now' mask a shift that demands a response. How quickly and effectively a firm responds often affects its reputation, credibility, integrity of operations and market performance. We regard organizational crises as those times when circumstances create a discontinuity in an organization's concept of business as usual, and when the organization must implement the right decisions if it is to avoid significant negative outcomes – real or symbolic. Usually, crisis situations are those with elements of high importance, high immediacy and high uncertainty (Nunamaker et al., 1989). Real-time crisis response depends on having the information technology infrastructure to respond, the organization design flexibility to respond and the management and social communication capability to respond.

This paper integrates a new stage model of crisis response with the concept that information technology is, in and of itself, an organizing resource that enables organization design flexibility. It brings together research and practice in the areas of crisis response management, information technology and broadcast news media to derive principles for using information technology as a resource for organizing crisis response.

First, the role of information technology as an organizing resource in today's international organizations is established. Then the global contexts driving today's international crises are discussed. The information technology, management and human communications infrastructures needed for fast-response are discussed along with the role of normal fast response capabilities in crisis management. We present a simple conceptual model of the stages of crisis response that brings together expertise and experience from the fields of information technology and news. The simplicity of the framework is intentional. Its originality comes from the fact that neither field has fully recognized the contribution it can make to the other and vice versa. A key implication is that organizations already have most of the information technology needed to organize for fast crisis response.

## Information technology as an organizing resource

The ability of today's organizations to use information technologies successfully depends on integrating information technology strategy with business and organizational strategy within the firm, and then linking these strategies by an appropriate IT platform (Keen, 1991). The platform infrastructure defines the set of shared hardware, software, communications, information-sharing capabilities and service base available to the firm. Electronic linkages redefine relations between organizations and their members, and between the organization and external businesses and entities creating metabusiness. Information technology no longer just supports existing business activities; it shapes organization identity (Keen and Cummins, 1994).

Telecommunications-based information technology is usually described as a force for work and process reorganization (Hammer, 1990), as a force for backward integration that links business services, or as a way to understand and maintain competitive advantage as in Porter and Millar (1985). Porter's value chain is a dominant influence in information technology literature as a strategic advantage and as a tool for redrawing competitive boundaries (Cash and Konsynski, 1985). Galbraith (1973) first described information use as a coupling device for organization across hierarchies and functions. Snow et al. (1992) extended the notion to the networked organization, predicting the organization would eventually become a switchboard (Snow et al., 1992; Davis and Davidson, 1991).

## The context for global crises

Today's corporations are global and dispersed. Information technology now allows real-time coordination of complex organizations, and even industries, worldwide. The fluctuations in today's financial marketplace rely on fast-responses of less than 20 seconds. Foreign exchange markets are coordinated worldwide by information technology-based services like Reuters. Walmart, Dillards, Dayton Hudson and Toys 'R' Us use point of sale data capture plus satellite transmission to streamline their core logistical chain of inventory management and merchandizing, ordering and distribution. They use both satellite transmission and point of sale data capture to alert product and area managers to sales trends in a day instead of weeks. American Airlines and British Airways similarly set pricing and profits in real time by operating 'yield management' systems. The United States airline industry changes fare prices twelve million times a day. Its major reservation systems each process over 2,000 transactions a second. Time-based competition is an axiom of business, and it demands responsive computer and communications capabilities.

As early as June 1990, Business Week listed 45 manufacturing companies with sales estimated over \$3 billion that generate over 40% of their sales from outside the country in which they are based. These include Procter & Gamble, Time Warner, Canon, Sony, Volvo, Coca-Cola, Xerox, IBM and Gillette. It is no exaggeration to say that there are no purely domestic markets anymore. Globalization extends the influence of corporations' policies and risks. Total foreign exchange transactions now exceed \$880 billion a day (Financial Times, 2 April 1993, p. 1).

When crises strike these global firms and industries, they are of equal potential complexity, global scope, immediacy and impact as any routine events. These crises occur in a world with just-in-time systems in the stakeholder communities and in the public audience. In a just-in-time context, crisis avoidance and reaction become more difficult. Crisis management and recovery become more dependent on a corporation-wide management response. A company with real-time business systems should have real-time crisis response systems.

Crises are fundamentally time-based. The global impact of the US stock market crash of October 1987 provides another example of crisis ramifications fuelled by information and communication technologies. Within hours, the crash affected markets in Tokyo, Singapore, Hong Kong and London. As the following recent crises show, crisis management must now move as fast.

## Exxon Valdez: the two billion dollar slow crisis response

On 24 March 1989, the Exxon Valdez ran aground and began spilling crude oil into the waters off the Alaska coastline. Days afterwards, the news media knew much more about the spill than Exxon did. Exxon executives were depending on the wire services for their information, not the other way around. Exxon's public relations spokespeople, based in Texas, were telling the press and public that if they wanted any information from Exxon they could try phoning overloaded Alaska. Even their own public relations people could not get through (cf Small, 1991).

By mid-1990, Exxon had spent well over two billion dollars in cleanup costs, and their name was nevertheless, if not mud, then sludge. In a world of fast-response, real-time communication, misperceptions or perceptions become reality very quickly. Exxon's bad public relations reflect slow crisis response in a context of fast media response. To a journalist, someone else's crisis is the working definition of news. The media got the Exxon Valdez news out.

What surfaces from the Exxon Valdez press coverage is that the media had just-in-time technology and used it, and that the management of Exxon did not (Calloway, 1991). For example, CNN is capable of responding to a crisis situation in seconds. A pivotal event for CNN was when the news wires reported a successful Challenger space shuttle take-off on 28 January 1986 at the very same second when CNN viewers saw Challenger explode. The New York Times offers 'all news that's fit to print'. CNN offers the news as it happens – or appears to happen.

The interpretation and commentary on news as we see it happen (or think we see it) is framed in seconds. That is why Exxon was pushed onto the defensive. The people at Exxon were faced with interviews and information presented on international cable broadcasts first, and then responding. Conversely, both the US military and Saddam Hussein depended on CNN live coverage of the Gulf War to track the Iraqi SCUD missiles exploding in Israel and adjusted their targeting in real time.

## Organizing for fast response: network television news coverage

Although we have become used to hearing about just-in-time manufacturing, it is more difficult to adjust to just-in-time war, with just-in-time weapons deployment, military briefings, news releases and media feedback. In World War II it was usual to watch newsreels in the movie theatres and cinemas that were two months old. In Vietnam, the time lag between a fire fight and the television screen was down to 24 hours. During the Gulf War, an Iraqi SCUD missile exploded simultaneously in a Tel Aviv neighbourhood and in the American living room (Clark, 1991).

According to Toffler and Toffler (1991), organization, communication, logistics, administration, reward structures, leadership styles and cultural assumptions are similar in war and in business. Second wave warfare (for example, Vietnam) depended on mass production, mass armies and the machine-age machine gun. In third wave warfare, time is even more crucial than in the past because events are accelerative, demanding faster decisions, instant communication and instant response. Third wave warfare (for example, Desert Storm) depends on the demassification of output, the demassification of media and a vast electronic infrastructure. 'The way humans make wealth and the way they make war are inextricably connected' (Toffler and Toffler, 1991, p. 46).

Television network news is in the business of fast crisis response.\* Analysis of the technology platforms created by the media in the Gulf shows how they extended their existing international media platforms. They extended their reach with mobile satellite telephones and communications, mobile broadcast communications, and laptop computer access to global news database services and corporate e-mail. ABC could reach its remote reporters in Saudi Arabia and later in Iraq by dialling a four-digit extension number. These 'branches' on the backs of flatbed trucks in the desert were virtually part of the head-office PBX.

By extending operations used for business as usual, CNN gained competitive broadcast advantage during operations Desert Shield and Desert Storm. CNN owed its international news coverage advantage during the first hours and days of the war to its ability to mobilize existing communications resources immediately (their four-wire phone and attendant connectivity), to maintain an anchor within Iraq, and to channel this information through microwave and satellite linkages to Atlanta. CNN attributed its success to three things; its previous mid-East alliances with communications officials, its connectivity in international hotel chains, and the familiarity of key people in Iraq, Jordan and Saudi Arabia with CNN broadcasts (Calloway, 1993). All of these assets were products of business as usual for CNN. The four-wire phone that provided CNN with its broadcast coverage in the first days of Desert Storm has been used by the military and news broadcasters for over 60 years. The four-wire technology was used by Edward R. Murrow in London during World War II – ‘This is London, the bombs are falling’.

## Organizing normal operations for fast crisis response

The Exxon Valdez case illustrates the enormous cost of slow crisis response coupled with the complicating factors of fast-response access by news media and other observers.

Few corporations and industries seem to view their IT platforms as the obvious basis for fast-response crisis management. Before the silicon implant crisis, Dow Corning saw its IT capability as an organizational resource for operations, not crisis. Now, Dow Corning has implemented many of the communication and coordination procedures needed to use its technology infrastructures to respond fast to a crisis.

Those companies that can respond fast in normal operations are often those companies that have the best chance for fast-response management in a crisis. El Sherif and Gray (1991) hypothesize that concepts for fast response decision support systems developed for crisis management can also be successfully extended to a range of fast response operational systems. However, we approach crisis management as an extension of fast response, not the other way around. Crisis situations happen only occasionally, and accidents and mishaps (which include harmful exposures) that lead to crises and disasters are usually caused by multiple factors. Even after the crisis is over, the relative contribution of each factor is often not clear.\* It is therefore more useful in practice to extend fast response technologies and platforms from normal operations to crisis response, rather than to imagine a new set of infrastructures and applications. Cost justification for platform investment is also more easily based on improving normal operations, rather than on direct investment into crisis control.

## Organizing for fast response: infrastructures

The new realities of time-based competition, time-accelerated observation of crises, and time-dependent credibility in reaction demand a planning approach that deals with crises as normal accidents. Ad hoc and contingency planning approaches that consider crises as exceptions to normal operations are less effective. The information and communications systems needed to support these new realities can be described in terms of a corporate IT platform or infrastructure and a management communications infrastructure.

## Information technology infrastructure

The business functionality of the corporate IT platform can be defined in terms of reach and range (Keen, 1991). The relevant parties that can be contacted fast define the reach of the platform, and the types of information and media that they can access and manipulate within that reach defines the range of the platform (see Figure 1 below).

The ideal reach is to be able to connect to anyone, anywhere, just as the phone system reaches across the world. This is currently an impractical short-term target, but one that will become more realistic in the coming decade as standards allow different systems to interconnect, much as different telephone systems do today. The ideal range is that any computer-generated transaction, document, message, or telephone call or any computer-mediated digital image, video or sound can be used in any other system, regardless of hardware or software.

Today, there are widespread differences in proprietary vendor-specific software, operating systems, and information storage and management techniques. Only systems built on the same database management and operating system base can automatically and directly share messages, transactions and data (Keen, 1991).

## Management communications infrastructures

The IT platform supports crisis communication only if social interaction is encouraged and facilitated. Most companies depend on crisis teams for decision making and management during crisis. Effective action by the teams depends on whether members can organize into cooperative groups, and whether the groups are supported by appropriate information and communications technology. The globalization and location independence of certain functions of routine work have contributed to the phenomena of location independent teams – teams whose members co-exist in different locations and time zones (Drucker, 1988). The research groups at Johns Hopkins University provide a good example of location and time independent teams. Johns Hopkins has centres up and down the Baltimore–Washington corridor, in Europe and as far away as the People's Republic of China.

Companies organize work groups in ways that lead to very different communication patterns. Four typical patterns of organization connectivity are identified, which determine how members communicate (Wu, 1989; Calloway and Wu, 1990). Connectivity is classified along two dimensions: relation multiplexity and relation intimacy. Relation multiplexity is a measure of the number of individuals with whom a person interacts. Relation intimacy is a measure of the strength of the relations.

![](/api/attachments/HX7QF833/fulltext/images/cf0ff91395d836fc8ce3ce61d2a36a7bc8dc3eaa45b1223ba19567e47ee943ec.jpg)  
Figure 3 Types of communication patterns in groups

(1) Isolated: group membership is fleeting with little multiplexity and low intimacy.

(2) Collective: there are many interactions with a central person, but they are low in intimacy.

(3) Separated: interactions are intimate within groups but there is little interaction across groups.

(4) Multi-dimensional: there are intimate interactions within and across groups, and interactions occur with many individuals.

Individuals mostly use the communication patterns that they are accustomed to. Their accustomed multiplexity determines with whom they communicate. Their accustomed intimacy determines how openly they usually communicate (Wu, 1989). It becomes important, therefore, to think about what communications patterns are being developed through work teams and other work socialization processes. During crisis, it is realistic to depend on prevalent communications patterns.

## Integrating the communications and information technology infrastructures

The multiplexity dimension in Wu's framework maps onto the range dimension of Keen's (1991) framework. The intimacy dimension is analogous to Keen's reach dimension. Individuals whose work is isolated (for instance, real estate brokers) can typically work with their own PC or workstation, perhaps with access to databases. Work groups that are collective (for instance, bank tellers) are often supported by centralized data processing. Tellers have only a dumb terminal for information and electronic communication. Separated work groups (such as groups organized according to department or function such as accounting) can be supported by their own computer system or local area network based on special purpose applications. Work groups that are multi-dimensional (such as research and development organizations) are best supported by a combination of local and company-wide systems, and depend on an integrated information technology platform (see Figure 4).

A centralized computer system with a set of applications can provide support for a company-wide crisis management team. However, these centralized applications will not usually support a widely dispersed and location independent organization, inter-organizational crisis management or international team. Personal computing LANs and workstations will not be helpful in supporting communications or providing information to any but co-located teams. Fast-response international crisis management teams require multi-dimensional support, which depends on an integrated information technology platform.

Most companies do not have an integrated information technology platform to support distributed teams (Keen, 1991). The more common pattern is one of highly fragmented and incompatible technology bases, especially where local area networks (LANs) are the main building blocks. Linking LANs to wide area networks and from there to remote LANs is the single most difficult technical challenge facing large organizations today. Technology boundaries become fast-response communications boundaries. Organization-wide connectivity can be provided even in these situations, however, by using a multi-dimensional communications tool like electronic mail.

The most obvious capabilities needed for fast crisis response are listed below.

(1) Technology links and tools that are immediately available and continuously at hand are needed, such as electronic mail via laptop computers. Cellular technologies such as mobile, wireless phones obviously extend communications at hand through their location independence. Also, business TV links to all locations could be provided.

(2) Knowledge of the tools and how to use them is also required. This is not a trivial problem, given the lack of standardization. The humorous cliche about not being able to program a VCR masks a real problem about advanced phones, personal computers and information systems.

(3) Formal procedures for employing these tools in everyday and/or crisis situations; these include procedures for routinely posting news to e-mail bulletin boards (and expecting managers to read them).

(4) Clear senior management alerting mechanisms, which facilitate immediate triggering of information and follow-on alerts.

## Stage models of crisis

In this section we first discuss several influential stage models of the entire crisis management cycle, and then describe our stage model of one phase of crisis management, the response phase.

## Stage models of the complete crisis management cycle

Several researchers have identified phases in the evolution of crisis. Steven Fink's (1986) phase model of crisis as an organizational illness views crisis as a disease, with as many as four different and distinct phases: prodromal crisis, acute crisis, chronic crisis and crisis resolution. Crises, like illnesses, usually present warnings termed prodromes. Sometimes these are obvious, such as union leaders telling management that their strike deadline is in 26 hours. Other prodromes are oblique and hard to recognize. A warning memo was written 13 months before the Three Mile Island nuclear power plant episode in Pennsylvania in 1979. The acute stage of the Three Mile Island crisis began on 28 March 1979, although the crisis itself began at least 13 months earlier. The acute crisis stage is characterized by avalanche-like speed and intensity. The ability of management to control the acute stage of crisis depends in part on the planning accomplished in the promodal stage. The chronic stage is often the longest stage in a crisis, and includes the clean-up phase and the post-mortem. The undamaged reactor on Three Mile Island was shut down for six and a half years, and investigations into the incident are still ongoing. Resolution is the fourth and final stage in Fink's model, when the patient is well and the crisis is over. He warns us, however, that the light one begins to see at the end of one crisis tunnel is usually the prodromal light of an oncoming crisis (Fink, 1986).

Mitroff's phase model (1988, 1993) of crisis management shows that nearly all crises pass through five time phases or stages: signal detection, preparation/prevention, containment/damage limitation, recovery and learning. Crises do not occur in isolation. The simultaneous occurrence of multiple crises is the norm (Pearson and Mitroff, 1993). Signal detection in organizations refers to discovering and managing early prodromal warnings. Signal detection requires awareness of the crises that are likely to occur as well as conscious efforts to uncover hidden signals. Crisis warnings might be hidden, ignored or blocked by members of the organization as happened with the space shuttle Challenger. The aim of preparation/prevention is to do what is possible to prevent crises from occurring and to manage effectively those which still happen. The stage includes creating crisis teams, crisis training and simulation exercises. The intent of the third phase, damage containment and limitation, is to limit the effects of a crisis. The success of containment depends on planning, since these activities and mechanisms are virtually impossible to invent during the heat of a crisis. In the next phase, recovery, the organization reinstates normal business activities. The organization benefits from planned procedures which identify key activities that must be performed to serve the most important customers, including establishing alternate sites such as those needed for computer operations. Finally, the organization enters the last phase which is learning, where the organization critically examines the lessons learned from experiencing a crisis, and integrates these lessons back into its crisis management processes (Pearson and Mitroff, 1993).

Rice (1990) organizes applications of communications technology to crisis management using the three stages of organizational information processing. The input stage considers crisis prevention and identification, the conversion stage considers crisis response and management, and the output stage considers crisis communication and learning.

## The stage model of crisis response

Drawing on the above work, we develop a stage-based model that incorporates technology as an enabler for the response stage, which is similar to Fink's acute stage and Mitroff's containment/damage limitation stage. Thus, our model is not intended as a general description of crisis but a more specific modelling of an organizational response capability. We accept that feedback is a key element in overall crisis stage models. Our model assumes the crisis event has already occurred. We are interested in how the organization responds once it recognizes the crisis. Time is a critical determinant of possibilities for feedback, and our model therefore focuses on the linear aspects of immediate response.

Once an organization has established fast-response infrastructures and knowledge capabilities, it must begin to integrate existing operations with its organization for crisis response. From our analysis of normal fast-response management capabilities, crises such as the Exxon Valdez oil spill, and our study of fast television news response during the Gulf War we postulate that there are five fairly distinct stages in crisis response. The aim of the model is to provide a means for identifying the capabilities needed when organizing for crisis response.

(1) Recognition is the point at which someone is alerted that something is happening and takes on the responsibility of signalling this to some person or group in the organization. Pearl Harbor was a disaster before it became a crisis, because available information was not recognized by decision-makers.

(2) Mobilization is the first movement to respond to the signal of recognition. It may be a narrowcast call to an emergency number or key executive, or a broadcast call to action across the organization and beyond.

(3) Anchoring establishes the key contact point(s) for channelling information and action. It may be a location, such as the CEO's office, or a spokesperson (mouthpiece).

(4) Warranty and sourcing establishes who speaks on behalf of the organization, and who provides expert opinion or a warranty for information and interpretation. The warranty assigns credibility; who, for instance, certifies that there is no danger of a meltdown in a nuclear reactor accident. Very often, the media bypasses an organization's sources and selects its own experts.

(5) Channelling is the process by which new information moves to and from the anchors and to and from the sources.

Figure 5 diagrams the crisis response model. Each stage may be passively or actively handled; in the active case, information technology is a major enabler (see Table 2). The passive response almost surely means there is no IT platform. Designing a fast-response system for each of these stages requires business processes, practices, training and technology.

The focus of this model is on the technology platform needed to enable fast-response capabilities. However, without a management philosophy, personnel training and a collaborative culture, the availability of the platform itself is unlikely to ensure its effective use. With that caveat, we provide recommendations for action.

(1) Analysis of historical crises: map the reach (parties involved) and range (type of information available) for crises that affected the firm in the recent past. Where would more reach and range have speeded up response and/or avoided misunderstandings? For example, would a video link from headquarters to all branches or an e-mail link to the public relations office have helped?

(2) Map the existing IT platform reach and range as shown in Figures 2 and 4.

(3) Identify under-used or unused tools now available within the platform. For example, e-mail might be in place, but with no code yellow/orange/red bulletin boards available or emergency contact numbers.

(4) Identify key parties not linked into the platform, but who can be easily included. Public relations firms and outside academic experts might easily be added to the e-mail reach.

(5) Analyse impacts of the loss of parts of the platform, such as the network being down.

(6) Identify on-line team formation and contact resources: directories, lists of managers by function, lists of existing and prior crisis teams and expert lists.

(7) Define new platform investment needs using the historical analysis from (1) to provide an actuarial base. Work backwards and estimate how many crises, if eliminated, would be needed to pay off the new investment. How likely are these crises to occur if we do not provide the additional information, the reduced response time or make the critical parties more available?

## The Dow Corning breast implant controversy

The stage model is used in this section to review the recent Dow Corning silicone-gel implant crisis during 1992. The analysis is aimed at key points along the crisis continuum where Dow Corning reportedly learned to implement and use its information and communications platform to help respond. Of course, these efforts could not mitigate the problems with the implants themselves. In September 1993, Dow Corning's lawyers proposed a \$4.75 billion fund for breast implant cases, the largest payout for a class action lawsuit in history (Boodman, 1993).

## A brief history of the silicone-gel implant crisis

Implants were first marketed in the US in the early 1960s, when such medical devices were unregulated. Dow Corning Corporation introduced the breast implants first; they provided about 10% of Dow Corning's revenue when the crisis erupted. In 1988, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) requested safety data from Dow Corning and other manufacturers. In November 1991, an FDA advisory panel ruled that evidence submitted by manufacturers of implants failed to prove that their products were safe. However, the FDA decided to keep silicone breast implants on the market until further tests could be conducted (Ingersoll, 1992).

In December 1991, Dow Corning lost a key litigation suit in San Francisco. The plaintiff won \$840 000 in compensation and \$6.5 million in punitive damages after the court found her Dow Corning silicone breast implant had ruptured, causing a painful disease of the immune system (New York Times, 17 December 1991). On 6 January 1992, FDA Commissioner Dr David A. Kessler said that the FDA ‘cannot assure the safety of these devices at this time’ (Ingersoll, 1992).

By 10 February 1992, the crisis had reached the point where Dow Corning felt it had to restructure its key executive lines to cope effectively (Hilts 1993). On 20 February 1992, an FDA advisory panel unanimously voted to restrict access to implants (Mann, 1992), and on 19 March, Dow Corning announced it would withdraw permanently from the silicone-gel implant business (Powers, 1992).

## Stage model of crisis: Dow Corning silicone-gel implants

The following analysis captures how Dow Corning used (and didn't use) information and communications infrastructures to respond during the implant crisis.

## Stage I: crisis recognition

The brief history of the implant issue shows the evolution of crisis recognition. The implant industry was slow to recognize the impending crisis. Although Dow Corning had hundreds of internal documents and memos on possible issues with their silicone-gel implants, and although the FDA had issued requests for safety data in 1988, Dow Corning appeared to proceed with business as usual until mid-1990. Existing alerting mechanisms apparently went untriggered.

In September 1990, Dow Corning decided to establish a database to track media coverage of the issue. As of June 1991, when the database system was operational, 75% of all stories filed on the issue were negative. By this time Dow Corning was prepared to take a communications approach towards equalizing the coverage. For example, when the database alerted them that secrecy was considered a primary issue in the crisis, they could mobilize a response. They released all their research on breast implants and put it into the public domain.

The Dow Corning original strategy for crisis recognition was passive. There was no interactive communication with, or even identification of, many of the stakeholder communities.

## Stage II: mobilization

The tracking database was established by early 1991 and fully operational by June. The goal was first to determine a fundamental set of communication objectives for stakeholders that would apply to all geographic regions, including England, Australia and Hong Kong and the United States. They planned to base the set of communications objectives on inputs from the stakeholders, once they discovered who the stakeholders were. After the objectives were established, they could prepare to be flexible as issues of time, geography and culture came into play. 'Even corporate policy needed to change depending upon the cultural environment, the regulatory environment and the medical environment on this particular issue' (Carmichael, 1992).

By late 1990, Dow Corning had set up a daily newswire to go out to all employees on their electronic mail global network. Dow made sure that their response to any issue was first on the internal database before it was out on an external database. They had also set up an information centre with a free number for external stakeholders to use, staffed mainly by Dow Corning employees (New York Times, 1 January 1992). Neither Dow Corning nor anyone else had records of who or how many women had breast implants in the United States or worldwide. Estimates varied between 1 and 2 million.

## Stage III: anchoring

In a global environment, different regions have different message requirements. The United States breast implant population is 80% cosmetic. In the United Kingdom the percentages are completely reversed. Dow Corning needed multiple anchoring points to manage the issue globally. In the United States and United Kingdom, culture and medical environment are very different; the United Kingdom has socialized medicine and a different regulatory climate, for example. Policies put in place in the United States were not going to work in the United Kingdom; and sometimes they worked neither in Australia nor in the United Kingdom. Dow Corning relied on their corporate video conferencing to discuss the crisis internationally.

## Stage IV: sourcing and warranty

In June 1991, Dow Corning had identified the stakeholders, and was prepared to take a communications approach to managing the crisis. The primary stakeholders were plastic surgeons, patients, breast cancer support groups, nurses, professional associations, Dow Corning employees and those with implants who were not patients, or who were never registered as patients. This last general group was most difficult to reach, because they had no support groups registered, and no records were kept of their names or addresses. This unknown group was also the largest group, especially in the United States, where no records were kept on the 80% of cosmetic implant patients. Curiously, the media was not referred to as a stakeholder, though one of Dow's communications objectives was to balance the media reporting by the end of December 1991. The objective was achieved by the end of April 1992 when about 48% of the reports were negative (Carmichael, 1992).

In April 1991, secrecy was found to be the biggest issue. 'So what we did in July of 1991, based on this issue tracking system, was release all of our research on breast implants and put it in the public domain. We did that July 7, 1991' (Carmichael, 1992). Dow Corning eventually developed a core group of regionally deployed spokespeople who were backed-up by electronic communications and local experts. The most frequently covered issue was regulation and the regulatory environment on the issue. The second most common topic was cancer. To the Dow scientists and also the regulatory environment, cancer is not and was never an issue with this product. Dow Corning concluded that cancer as an issue is far more understandable by the general public. Also, the general public is likely to feel more threatened by cancer than immune system disorders, which were the threat.

The court decision in December 1991 awarding \$7.34 million to the plaintiff who had a ruptured Dow Corning breast implant brought Dow Corning's credibility to the forefront of the crisis debate. The free hot-line information centre was quickly in difficulty with the FDA. The FDA sent a letter to Dow Corning on 30 December 1991 that accused the company of giving out misleading information about breast implants on its hot-line. 'These statements overstate the safety of breast implants and minimize known or suspected side effects,' said the letter. The agency said operators had told callers that the silicone implants were '100 percent safe ... there have never been health problems with implants or silicone' (New York Times, 1 January 1992).

## Stage V: channelling

Dow Corning depended on video teleconferencing to manage and coordinate the policy issues in the United Kingdom, United States, Australia and Hong Kong. The Washington Times reported on 1 March 1992 that one national teleconference on silicone-gel breast implants was viewed by thousands of women in medical facilities in 28 cities. They were conversing electronically with a panel of eight medical experts.

Dow Corning already had a global information network in place that supported IBM-based electronic mail systems. They implemented what one spokesperson called a ‘multi-tier, preestablished distribution list’ to use their electronic mail network to send information around the world instantaneously.

In Carmichael's opinion, the employees of Dow Corning were the people who benefited most from the fast exchange of information via information technology. 'Database and information technology became very key … When you get a call at 5 o'clock in the afternoon that a story is breaking, it's showtime in Australia and you better be sure that you're ready'. When people in Australia were already getting questions based on a report by CNN or whoever handled coverage, Dow Corning was able to send them a press release by electronic mail. 'It doesn't work to say, "Just a minute, it's 5:00, nobody's here". It just doesn't work that way' (Carmichael, 1992).

## Some conclusions after withdrawal

Barie Carmichael offered two caveats in using communications infrastructures. First, during the crisis, interactive, real-time communications were (and are) the key. 'If you think the challenge is to get the right message out in the least amount of time with the best packaging, you'll fundamentally miss the point, because you need to have information flowing back, in just as much of a real time dimension, so that you understand what it is you need to say' (Carmichael, 1992).

Second, she cautions managers to remember that the speed with which information can come at you will no doubt be faster than your ability to provide accurate information. It is necessary but difficult not to succumb to a news deadline that is faster than your information base, simply because you have the technology base. By far their most serious problem area was sourcing and warranty. Throughout the crisis, Dow's sourcing and warranty decisions seemed to lack credibility, and often seemed to address the wrong issues entirely. The same issue faces fast-news organizations like CNN.

## Discussion and further research

We can be very sure of the following trends in the 1990s:

(1) There will be an increasing number of inter- and intraorganizational crises.

(2) The accelerating pace of fast publicity and commentary will continue.

(3) There will be increased reliance by firms on the reach and range of their information technology base in more and more areas of business.

This paper suggests that these three trends are linked. Fast-response crisis management will move higher up the agenda of the organization, with periodic Exxon

Valdez-like fast onset incidents and the more slowly developing Dow Corning-type incidents to jolt them. Investments in information technology, already about 50% of US business capital spending (Keen, 1991), will be equally high on the agenda. The opportunity for organizations to mesh these concerns to create a solution to the problem of crisis management capabilities is possible and practical.

Information technology has historically been very limited in its media: information meant data and database management systems; text meant e-mail and word processing. The media were equally limited in their information and communications technology base: any long distance transmissions meant voice grade phone lines, still pictures were slow to transmit and usually in black and white, and image meant film.

Now the media have entirely converged. Digital information and compression technologies have fully integrated text, data and image with video, multimedia on a personal computer, massive bandwidth availability and location independent cellular VSAT laptops. The next convergence should be one of insight and experience. Using an information technology perspective brings an understanding of the opportunities created by the platform approach. The media bring an understanding of crisis response. The organizational design strategies made feasible by information technology underpin the convergence needed in flexible transnational organizations. The technology transfer tradition in communication research brings the understanding of team organizations that need supporting and managing. The interaction between technology and social relationships is a major target for research.

This paper has brought together two major themes in the organization literature. First, the focus is on the importance of crisis awareness and management in an era of transnational business organizations. Second, the essential nature of information technology allows it to act as a force and a resource that organizes business activities. The major value of these recommendations for organizing for crisis response is that they are independent of the organization's structure. They do not require the organization to make major changes to accommodate new strategies for and during crisis response.

An additional advantage is that this approach to crisis response exploits the IT platform needed for business as usual in a world of just-in-time opportunities, thus minimizing incremental investments. In many ways the simple message from our paper to IT managers is ‘use what you have as a crisis resource’ and to crisis managers, the message is ‘you probably already have what you need’.

In terms of research, our approach highlights a specific domain of crisis evolution, response and resolution. We believe that the role of technology is critical in crisis response and also in crisis evolution in terms of mechanisms for alerting and diagnosing. Our crisis response model thus highlights an action approach that organizations can adapt and manage when the crisis hits.
