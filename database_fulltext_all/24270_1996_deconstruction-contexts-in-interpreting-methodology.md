# Deconstruction contexts in interpreting methodology

HEATHER WATSON and TREVOR WOOD-HARPER

Department of Mathematics and Computer Science, University of Salford, Salford M5 4WT, UK

This paper considers how a methodology's theory and practice shapes contexts for interpretation. With these two terms as starting points, we also address a paradoxical situation: any description of interpreting contexts is bound to leave something out. To address this, we propose deconstruction as a double strategy for critically interpreting contexts in each situation. This relies on terms of existing oppositions in conceptual frameworks but seeks to displace the limitations they impose on how we conduct inquiry. Since meaning is context-bound but contexts are boundless, we argue that inquiry should be conducted through critical perspectives, and we describe this in terms of a systems analyst's expertise in conceptual triangulation: the defining of an unknown point in relation to two known extremes.

## Introduction

Boland (1981) argues that the formal languages of data in organizations are used to legitimate the ways managers understand the world, and in this sense, language is important because it affects how situations in systems design are perceived as meaningful. Likewise, King and Kugler (1993) consider how rhetorical schemes influence outcomes on decision-making processes. In relation to database design, Stamper (1977, p. 293) describes a central problem as, 'how to select the appropriate universe of discourse and how to relate the data structures within the information system to the real entities in the world.'

Given that much traditional development practice is characterized by defining a universe of discourse, by focusing on terms of reference, and by maintaining data dictionaries, it is somewhat surprising that analysts have often given little thought to how their assumptions about language affect development. For example, in assuming that organizations are the same for everyone, analysts often focus on meaning as a general structure. In this view, language is transparent, and data models are objective ‘mirrors’ of reality (Klein and Hirschheim, 1987). These general structures are then used to derive databases that merely reflect what is meaningful in the organization. In viewing language this way, words and their associated data structures are assumed to have meaning on their own. Stamper (1987) criticizes this since it gives rise to the belief that meaning is unproblematic and that information is therefore easy to share using databases that reflect a single, objective, organizational reality.

In addressing such assumptions, Lyytinen (1985) draws on theories of language and considers how information systems development methodologies can be characterized by the language views underlying them. Likewise, Boland (1987) considers how we have traditionally avoided the elusive aspects of information by viewing it as structured data; this neglects the process of inward-forming that arises as people interact with structured data. Beynon-Davies (1992, p. 210) argues that formal information systems take their contexts from the informal information systems that surround them. Similarly, Lee (1994) considers how information ‘richness’ or ‘leanness’ is not an inherent property of electronic mail (e-mail) since the meaning of a message emerges through the interaction of it as a communication medium within its organizational context.

In extending research on meaning and context, we emphasize information systems as linguistic phenomena and focus on language as a way of making sense of our experiences in developing and using information systems. This is similar to Goldkuhl's (1987) view that we should see information systems not just as technical artefacts but as linguistic structures with the purpose of informing people using data to support their actions.

Our argument proceeds as follows. We begin by describing a post-structuralist approach to textual analysis. Using this to reflect on how a methodology's theory and practice shapes contexts for interpretation, we then draw on Lyytinen's (1987) taxonomic perspective as a case study. This addresses a paradoxical situation: a taxonomy of contexts. With contexts as its starting point, taxonomy is bound to leave something out. We argue further that the same applies to contexts in interpreting methodology.

Since meaning is context-bound, but contexts are boundless, a tentative approach is therefore to conduct inquiry through perspectives. We consider how

Lyytinen's (1987) research on perspectives is potentially critical insofar as these work within existing categories, attend to their limitations, and suggest changing contexts to allow for new insights and unanticipated perspectives. In making our case, we use the term ‘critical’ in a general sense concerning how we understand and explain types of inferences through which we make interpretations. This includes the kinds of evidence relevant to them, and the criteria through which they are judged.

Using conceptual oppositions of theory and practice in methodology, we make a case for critical perspectives in terms of a systems analyst's expertise in conceptual triangulation: the defining of an unknown point in relation to two known extremes. In proposing deconstructive perspectives for further research, our purpose is to suggest that critical categories are not just tools used in interpreting methodology; rather, they are problems to be explored. By disrupting oppositions in current approaches, we intend to prevent these from being taken for granted. We hope, therefore, that the following argument will contribute towards further reflection on the way systems analysts deconstruct contexts in interpreting methodologies.

Before analysing Lyytinen's (1987) taxonomic perspective, we describe our research approach below.

## A post-structuralist approach to textual analysis

In operations research, Taket and White (1993) propose a post-structuralist agenda to criticise binary oppositions such as 'hard' and 'soft' in methodologies, and they consider the asymmetrical power relations that result from the way soft approaches are marginalized by a process of 'feminization'. Beath and Orlikowski (1994) use deconstruction in their critical assessment of James Martin's (1989, 1990a, 1990b) information engineering to argue that it contains contradictory assumptions about the role of users and analysts during systems development. Likewise, we use a post-structuralist approach to textual analysis to develop a deconstructive perspective derived from the work of Jacques Derrida (1973, 1977a, 1977b, 1982, 1986).

Deconstruction is a post-structuralist theory and, as such, the term 'post' does not mean that deconstruction replaces structuralism as a chronologically more recent theory; rather, it depends upon structuralism as a prior system of analysis. While structuralism considers language as a system, it tends to assume that the codes making up the system have a longstanding stability; this presents a static view of meaning, which neglects its more dynamic aspects (Culler, 1975). As such, post-structuralism was considered appropriate as an example of how to work critically with a tradition of methodology that has focused on formal structured data over the dynamic organizational contexts that surround computerized information systems.

We set out some basic characteristics of deconstruction below, and we illustrate these with examples from information systems.

## Preferred readings

Deconstruction is a way of questioning linguistic systems to consider how both texts and our common practices of reading them are founded in particular assumptions about language (Culler, 1983; Norris, 1991). As such, deconstruction is concerned not so much with following the intentions of authors as it is with developing conceptual implications of what authors have written (Beath and Orlikowski, 1994).

This is appropriate to methodology insofar as focusing on recovering an author's intentions may result in a misleading view. For instance, although a methodology recommends a certain range of activities, the resulting theory is not like the script of a play since methodologies do not simply reproduce a previous pattern of activities. Instead, the general structure suggested by these activities is a basis for specific instances of critical inquiry in situations that authors may not even have anticipated. This is evidenced, for example, by soft systems methodology's (SSM) explicit focus on exploring the different views of problem situations that derive from each stakeholder's values and beliefs (Checkland, 1981).

In developing conceptual implications for subsequent critical inquiry, deconstruction therefore begins by considering how texts themselves apparently offer preferred readings (Beath and Orlikowski, 1994). The word ‘apparently’ in the previous sentence is important: deconstruction considers that a critical reading of any text begins by identifying a particular discourse in it. In relation to this, a methodology’s stated theory can be understood as its preferred reading. For example, Checkland (1981) locates SSM mainly in Burrell and Morgan’s (1979) interpretive but also in their radical humanist paradigm. In practice, the process by which systems analysts arrive at identifying preferred readings includes their abilities with the linguistic codes they use to manipulate the meaning of texts. An example of this in SSM is the construction of root definitions in defining the purpose of a human activity system. These draw on all verbs in the English language using the familiar CATWOE criteria to identify the minimum number of activities required to achieve each system’s purpose (Checkland and Scholes, 1990, pp. 288–90).

Because such codes are linked to cultural structures and values, the assumptions and ideologies that analysts bring to the situation affect how they interpret meaning. This link to wider cultural contexts has an implication for the theory and practice of methodology: language and cultural ideology are larger than the methodology itself, so this must find accommodation in existing linguistic and cultural codes that may be contradictory. Evidence for this arises in SSM, for instance, with the emergence of a second cultural stream of inquiry that 'overlaps' with the initial logical stream (Checkland and Scholes, 1990, pp. 44–53).

## Disruptions to preferred readings

This link to wider cultural contexts means that the discourse of a methodology is unlikely to be unitary and unambiguous. Evidence for this is suggested by the way we speak generally of there being a gap between a methodology's theory and practice. This lack of unity becomes further specified at the level of theory in methodologies such as SSM with its modes 1 and 2 (Checkland and Scholes, 1990). It becomes even more marked in multiview methodology and total systems intervention insofar as these are constituted by a range of other well-established methodologies (Flood and Jackson, 1991; Bell and Wood-Harper, 1992).

A consequence of using existing linguistic codes is that texts describing a methodology's theory contain disruptive elements, points of rupture or gaps which when perceived and carefully examined, admit other marginal or nonpreferred readings, ones that call into question the apparently obvious or familiar meaning found. In prescriptive methodologies, practice is considered as a disruptive effect if it deviates from theory. In such cases practice is also considered a negative disruptive effect insofar as the methodology's theory is intended to guarantee its successful use, e.g., structured systems analysis and design methodology (SSADM) (CCTA, 1990).

However, disruptions of preferred readings also emerge in relation to nonprescriptive methodologies. These often centre on how a methodology's stated theory differs from its practice, thereby focusing on a methodology's ‘proper’ use. Jackson (1989), for example, argues that the democratic principles advocated by Beer's (1985) viable systems model (VSM) are often undermined in authoritarian contexts. Against Checkland's (1981) preferred reading, he also argues that SSM is not representative of Burrell and Morgan's (1979) radical humanist paradigm since it is ‘essentially regulative’ (Jackson, 1982, p. 28).

A deconstructive reading thus investigates texts to find the points at which their constitutive rules and codes 'undo' themselves. However it is important to emphasize that deconstruction is not merely a negative endeavour since points of rupture can be viewed as opportunities for learning and for change (Willmott, 1994).

## Limitations of binary oppositions

From a deconstructive perspective, binary oppositions are not valueless or neutral. Rather, the apparent ‘naturalness’ by which the first term is chosen implies a hierarchy wherein the first term is given priority over the second. To return to Taket and White (1993), for example, the pairing of ‘hard’ and ‘soft’ tends to reflect the way analysts have traditionally given priority to technical matters, while neglecting social aspects of development. Hierarchy thus involves a power relationship. Insofar as one of the two terms (usually the first) holds the superior position, hierarchies are based on a conflict of interests.

Even so, recognition of such oppositions is not sufficient. Consequently, deconstruction seeks the term by which such oppositions may be if not reconciled then at least understood in a relation of tension (Ray, 1984). Sometimes this leads to the emergence of a third term that entails interests represented by each of the terms in opposition. The emergence of the concept, action research, may be understood as a third term for addressing such tensions between theory and practice not only insofar as it relates theory to practice and practice to theory in a cycle of learning, but also in the way that this is described as a way to 'tackle' difficult and 'ill-defined problems' (e.g., refer to Checkland, 1981, p. 153). This is shown in Figure 1.

Nonetheless, the priorities given to theory in this opposition continue to be reflected in the way one term controls another; hence, even in action research we usually speak of putting theory into practice, not the reverse (Watson et al., 1995).

## Différance and supplement

Derrida seeks to address such tensions through his theory of différence and supplement. Différance refers to the way that concepts such as formal and informal are not only different from each other, but that such differences also imply a deferral from one concept to the other in the sense both of a submission and of a postponement (Culler, 1983). For example, while informal aspects are often subordinated by formal or technological concerns, the meaning of a resulting formalized system is postponed or incomplete without the informal and often dynamic organizational contexts in which it is subsequently embedded in use.

Supplement refers to the way an element is added to or is considered secondary to another more complete structure or system. To extend our example, the way informal aspects of information systems have often been considered of secondary importance in relation to formal or technical matters is an instance of supplement at work. But even here in our treatment of formal systems, an example of supplement occurs in the way we view daily activities of data entry as being secondary to the general structure of a database in the sense that these elements can be added to or removed from our formal systems without substantially affecting their structure.

Nonetheless, as Derrida (1977a, pp. 141–64) argues, if a structure can be added to it is not complete, and if the supplement can be added, it cannot be merely secondary. Implications of this will become clearer when we discuss Lyytinen's (1987) treatment of contexts.

This section has set out our research approach and some of its basis characteristics. The result has been a simplification of a complex tradition of post-structuralist studies. For more detailed considerations using examples from philosophy and literature, readers are referred to Culler (1983) and Norris (1991). The next section uses Lyytinen's (1987) taxonomic perspective as a case study.

## Lyytinen's (1987) taxonomic principles: object system contexts

In reflecting on the ways a methodology's theory and practice shape contexts for interpretation, we draw on Lyytinen's (1987) taxonomic perspective since this considers theoretical concepts and recommendations for further research to change the way we currently go about information systems development (ISD). That is, Lyytinen's focus on contexts derives from a concern over the 'recurrent reasons for IS failure' and again, he links this to dynamic situations to 'changes in language and organizational contexts' (Lyytinen, 1987, p. 35, our emphasis).

We can still learn much from Lyytinen's research. For example, his exploratory analysis continues to address a paradoxical situation: a taxonomy of contexts. This situation is paradoxical because with contexts as its starting point, a taxonomy is bound to leave something out. Lyytinen's tentative solution is therefore exemplary. He proposes a 'preliminary taxonomy'. The word 'preliminary' is important insofar as it calls attention to the way Lyytinen's argument contains a disruption that questions the categories or general principles that would preclude him from proposing any final taxonomy. Hence, Lyytinen creates not just a taxonomy, but a 'taxonomic perspective'. This is potentially critical, because it works with existing categories, attends to their limitations, and so recreates new insights and unanticipated perspectives.

That is, unlike the view so often taken by analysts that everyone perceives the organization in the same way, Lyytinen (1987, p. 6) explains that people have different perceptions. Moreover, he describes the 'object systems' that derive from these perceptions as phenomena seen by the development group that identify a 'target of change' (Lyytinen, 1987, p. 6, our emphasis). After Welke and Konsynski (1982), he names these viewpoints 'object system contexts' and he then postulates that there are three object system contexts organized hierarchically as follows:

(1) technology,

(2) language, and

(3) organizational (Lyytinen, 1987, p. 11).

Over the past few years, critical reflection on this has suggested the possibility of two related presuppositions that seem to contradict Lyytinen's general argument regarding context. While these do not detract from his main interest in how methodology guides choice of object systems, they do, we feel, bear closer consideration. We describe these presuppositions and consider their implications below.

## A mastery of the total context?

In this section, we consider limitations in how we perceive and describe contexts. We then relate this to methodologies. Since our research approach focuses on the study of language, we begin by quoting Lyytinen's description of implications for the notion of 'object system contexts'. This indicates,

... the open-ended, situation-dependent and cyclical nature of IS intervention. In the beginning change is conditioned by the initial perception enabled by the language used. However, during the development process language and perceptions are in constant flux (Lyytinen, 1987, pp. 8–9).

We will consider the notion of cycle in due course since it is relevant to our description of conceptual triangulation. At this point, however, we point out that the above structure is reflected in Lyytinen's own argument. This makes it an example of the process he describes. Hence, in the beginning his initial perception of taxonomic principles is enabled by the language used, but this language and perception then changes as his argument develops. In reflecting on this, two presuppositions stated in Lyytinen's beginning seem to contradict developments in his subsequent perceptions. That is, in proposing taxonomic principles for systems development, he writes with reference to object system contexts:

We believe that these three are exhaustive and segment the systems development domain into three distinct realms, which respond differently to introduced change.... The three contexts are hierarchically ordered (Lyytinen, 1987, p. 11, our emphasis).

First, given that Lyytinen is dealing with contexts, his assertion that these three are exhaustive might seem to be inappropriate. Second, what effect does ordering context through hierarchy have on subsequent research? In constructively criticising these two presuppositions that claim to bound context with an exhaustive hierarchical description, we intend to contribute to Lyytinen's (1987, p. 4) proposal for 'a thorough and critical investigation of the basis on which current development methodologies build.' We thus hope that our comments provide some further insights into how we think about deconstructing contexts in interpreting methodologies.

In considering this, we learn from Lyytinen's (1987, pp. 34–5) recommendations. As he proposes in extending theoretical foundations of methodology:

... the language context should be dealt with as an independent sphere of the IS change. This necessitates that the richness of insights gained from the study of language should be carefully adapted to the specification of methodologies.

This suggests an approach to interpretation that has been summarized by a well-known literary critic, Jonathan Culler (1983, p. 123) as follows: 'Meaning is context bound, but context is boundless.' The post-structuralist criticism described by Culler below is therefore a potentially rich source for specifying methodology insofar as it draws from the study of language, particularly in relation to Austin (1975) and Searle's (1977) speech act theory and Derrida's (1977b) critique of their presuppositions.

Culler (1983, p. 123) consolidates Derrida's criticism as follows: in specifying a theory of speech acts this must in principle explain all features of contexts that could affect their success or failure. However, as Austin (1975, p. 148) himself recognizes, this would demand a total mastery of context, and this is in principle and in practice not possible (also quoted in Culler, p. 123). Austin therefore proceeds by exclusion, for example, by including only ‘serious’ speech acts in his theory (Culler, 1983, p. 122).

This is reminiscent of the way analysts have often neglected the way meaning varies with organizational context in favour of developing a general static structure. Like Austin, analysts have therefore frequently taken only the formal aspects of an information system seriously. This has resulted in tendencies to exclude informal or dynamic aspects such as politics from design (Beynon-Davies, 1992). To return to Derrida, such matters would be considered supplementary to an analyst's focus on formal design structures. Nonetheless, failures of technical information systems due to social aspects (e.g., Davis et al., 1990; Meyers, 1993) suggest that the conditions supplementing such designs are very often far from secondary.

This position of excluding something that may turn out to be highly important converges on the paradoxical situation presented by Lyytinen's taxonomy of contexts. In principle taxonomies inevitably exclude, but a taxonomy of context undermines itself by its own example: in classifying contexts it excludes contexts. Here also, Culler's (1983, p. 125) summation of Derrida is potentially insightful. He explains how context is therefore not exhaustive but instead boundless in at least two senses:

(1) Any known context remains open to further description since there is potentially no limit as to what may eventually be relevant.

(2) Context is unmasterable insofar as attempts to codify it can be reintroduced onto the contexts it sought to describe resulting in new contexts that exceed prior formulations.

Examples of the first sense are scientific discoveries in which previously neglected factors become relevant to describing the behaviour of certain objects such as the existence of ether. In information systems development (ISD), the emerging significance of social factors might offer a similar example. In the second sense, Culler (1983, p. 124) describes Wittgenstein's (1969) example of not being able to say 'bububu' to mean 'if it does not rain I shall go out for a walk'. Paradoxically, this denial results in a connection making it possible to do just that. In ISD, the emergence of complementary approaches despite claims of paradigmatic incommensurability might offer a comparable example (e.g., Avison and Wood-Harper, 1990; Flood and Jackson, 1991).

For now, however, we confine our discussion of these two senses of boundless context to Lyytinen's argument. He moves, for instance, from contexts to 'super-contexts'. This supplement is discussed below.

## From contexts to super-contexts

In explaining super-contexts, Lyytinen gives examples of boundless context in the first sense by adding to descriptions of mapping types. His initial explanation is given as descriptive and instrumental mappings (Lyytinen, 1987, pp. 23–4). When explaining the limitations of instrumental mappings in terms of functional and deterministic assumptions in mainstream literature, he cites Kling and Scacchi's (1982) criticism of 'discrete-entity' analysis and their development of web models as a 'richer family of models' (p. 25, our emphasis). He further explains that their 'analysis can be expanded to cover all descriptive and instrumental mappings in all three super-contexts' (p. 25, our emphasis). This results in a further extension to his initial description. He links discrete-entity and web models, redescribing them as 'deterministic', 'emergent' and 'contingent' mappings respectively (p. 25).

These supplements enrich Lyytinen's argument. Nonetheless, with regard to the second sense of unbounded context, they have the paradoxical effect of undermining his initial announcement of an exhaustive hierarchical description of three contexts. Moreover, even his expansion from context to super-context has this effect (Lyytinen, 1987, pp. 22–5). Paradoxically, his initial formulation of general features restricting each viewpoint to the prespecified context of object systems (e.g. pp. 11, 14, 17) is variously exceeded by contexts taking the form of supplements that further unbound his exhaustive description of three contexts.

Below, we argue that this paradox of describing a taxonomy of context also arises in how we explain a methodology in use: inevitably, such explanations are limited since they exclude further contexts, further supplements.

## A paradoxical situation of describing methodology in use

In this section we consider a paradoxical situation of describing methodology in use. We then consider hierarchy in methodology in terms of theory and practice. We suggest what we hope is a richer way of describing methodology in use: It has an intermediary status between theory and practice. We explain this in terms of a systems analyst's emerging expertise in conceptual triangulation.

## Limitations in perspective: blind spots and conceptual traps

Lyytinen's taxonomic perspective concludes by describing a paradoxical situation of methodology in use arising from limiting a field of view too narrowly. He summarizes this as follows:

Instead of getting what one sees and seeing what one gets, one does not get what one sees, and one gets what one does not see (Lyytinen, 1987, p. 35).

We redescribe this paradoxical situation: structures have blind spots, be they cars or methodologies. In discussing conceptual traps, Vickers (1970) uses a 'lobster pot' metaphor to describe the way such conceptual structures are only traps for those who cannot solve the problems that the trap sets. Likewise, as a way out of limiting a field of view too narrowly, Lyytinen (1987, p. 35) proposes a more careful reflection on what methodology can lead one to see and get when engaged in systems development. Such reflection returns to this paradox that is simultaneously critiqued and reiterated in Lyytinen's taxonomic perspective. That is, before naming a hierarchy of exhaustive contexts, he explains features of systems development as follows:

The components of the definition of systems development form a complicated web of social, technical, and cultural phenomena. The components are not independent of each other, nor are they completely dependent (Lyytinen, 1987, p. 7, our emphasis).

However, in relating our argument back to the second sense in which context cannot be totally mastered, we see Wittgenstein's (1969) example of denying the potential for further comparison reiterated in Lyytinen's taxonomic perspective:

The proposed framework suggests that only those methodologies which fall in the same taxonomic class should be compared, i.e. STS-methodologies cannot be compared with technical design-oriented methodologies because they differ in terms of their covered contexts (Lyytinen, 1987, p. 34).

But Lyytinen's denial paradoxically makes a comparison possible. Moreover, our field already has an established tradition that does indeed compare social and technical perspectives. Look again at Lyytinen's example: STS (Sociotechnical systems) – the two contexts are already implicated in a comparison that he then claims to deny.

Furthermore, Lyytinen then critiques this limited view of contexts himself. In naming ways of extending theoretical foundations, he unbounds context in the first sense by citing multiview methodology (MVM) as an example for further research in solving the issues of interlinking object representations in different contexts during concrete systems work (Lyytinen, 1987, p. 35). However, MVM explicitly combines social and technical perspectives. Again, in the second sense of unbounded context, this creates a new context that exceeds Lyytinen's prior formulation.

This tendency to unbound an exhaustive description is paralleled in a second related presupposition: hierarchy. Below, we establish a further link between paradoxical situations in taxonomy and methodology using hierarchy in theory and practice to consider expertise in conceptual triangulation.

## Between theory and practice: the intermediary status of methodology

Traditionally, the relationship between theory and practice is explained as a hierarchy (Schön, 1983, p. 24). In this view, ‘general principles’ occupy the highest level and ‘concrete problem solving’ the lowest (Schön, 1983, p. 24). Consequently, analysts often describe their intermediary role as an ‘attempt to put theory into practice’ (Wood-Harper et al. 1985). They also recognize that methodologies can be explained as ways of bridging the gap between theory and practice (Checkland, 1985, 1981; Checkland and Scholes, 1990). As stated previously, this view is still reflected even in action research in the way we continue to order the expression: theory is named first, practice is second.

To return to our introduction, traditionally methodologies have avoided the dynamic aspects of meaning by presenting us with theories that focus on information as structured data. This may confuse attempts at theory-building in the following way: if information is conceived of as structured data, then this may be reflected in how we conceive of a methodology's theory with the result that the meaning of a methodology becomes somehow 'contained' in the theory we create. Reddy (1979) refers to this as the 'conduit metaphor' in which communication works through the physical transfer of thoughts and feelings. Such a view might give rise to a concept of theory that is fully formulated. We then think of theory and practice in terms of a concept-instance relation, and practice becomes derivative, a matter of 'after the fact' thinking (Schön, 1963). This conception of methodology is shown in Figure 2.

In practice if we take a methodology's theory literally as given, it becomes a prescription for our actions in developing a system. Practice then becomes a supplement to theory: so long as an analyst follows the appropriate stages correctly, a methodology's meaning becomes an object guaranteed by the theory (e.g., CCTA, 1990). This view may also affect how we conceive of people using information systems insofar as they also become supplemental: so long as they follow the user's manual correctly, the success of the information system is guaranteed. Further evidence for this conduit view is suggested by Checkland and Scholes' (1990, pp. 17–18) criticism of the way hard methodologies conceive of problem-solving in terms of means and ends and proceed by focusing on the 'how' since the 'what' has already been decided. In contrast to this, SSM addresses situations in which means and ends are themselves problematic.

At this point, we return to the notion of cycle initially raised in our discussion of limitations in how we perceive and describe contexts. That is, action research explicitly criticizes the view of practice as 'after the fact' thinking by arguing that the relation between theory and practice is not hierarchical, but cyclical (Lewin, 1951; Warmington, 1980; Checkland, 1981; Trist, 1991; Susman, 1983). Hence, action research addresses how analysts' practical experiences can be linked from an understanding of individual cases through the abstract concepts that constitute their subsequent explanations. Lyytinen (1987, p. 9) describes this more specifically in terms of the multiple forms of representing object systems, for example, 'free form text, semiformal notation such as structured English, graphical descriptions, and formal mathematical notations.' With such descriptions, we thus explain methodology as theory, which we then relate to practical case studies that continue to inform and so effect changes in existing theory and practice.

In this way, action research records a methodology's history of interpretations by continuing to address a gap between practical interventions and their subsequent descriptions. Recognizing this cyclical relationship in a methodology's theory advances an established practical commitment to develop a more self-reflective approach to critical inquiry.

We summarize three theoretical consequences of the gap between description and action as follows:

(1) Due to methodology's intermediary status between past descriptions and future action, texts describing theory and practice are incomplete; they explain no single or prescriptive meaning.

(2) Instead, methodologies are only guidelines for iterating potential meaning in contexts presented by situations that have yet to occur.

(3) Iterability therefore refers to a methodology's repetition as meaningful experience that is never repeated exactly since it is affected by the infinite contexts into which it is subsequently embedded.

So, while authors can describe both theory and practice in a range of case studies, the paradox of describing methodology in use, remains an obstacle for potential users. For systems analysts, the above theoretical points have the following practical consequences:

(1) Any description, even of using a methodology, is just that – a description – so in each intervention, systems analysts continue to work towards establishing meaning in specific situational contexts.

(2) By continuing this cyclical relationship between theory and practice, moving from description to action and back again in each intervention, analysts learn to deconstruct contexts, thereby exercising their critical skills in interpretation.

In discussing the nature of mappings in his preliminary taxonomy, Lyytinen (1987, pp. 32–3) comes to a similar conclusion:

Other taxonomic classes offer a richer view of mappings. Some point out that changes in the language and organisation contexts are cyclically related and therefore deterministic relationships between these phenomena cannot be found. Instead, development outcomes depend on the quality of interpretive and reconstructive processes by which developers try to understand and explicate emergent interactions between organisational and communication activities (our emphasis).

Lyytinen thus critiques the inevitable exclusions of taxonomy by his own supplementary example: he attends to limitations of his preliminary taxonomy by differing to other contexts, to other taxonomies, and this opens further contexts for comparison. In this Lyytinen is exemplary: he serves a warning that is fit for imitation.

We expand upon this below in terms of a systems analyst's expertise in conceptual triangulation.

## Interpreting context: expertise in conceptual triangulation

In moving from theory to practice, an analyst's role in relation to methodology is affected by contexts arising from each organizational situation (Lyytinen, 1987, p. 11). As Hirschheim and Klein (1989) explain, this requires further negotiation to develop an appropriate 'match' between analyst and each organizational situation. Drawing on Schön's (1983) terminology, this tendency for a situation to 'backtalk' to the analyst is currently reflected in several methodologies (e.g., soft systems methodology, multiview methodology and total systems methodology). This becomes even more explicit in research focusing on the way dominant metaphors in situations affect interpretation, (e.g., Flood and Jackson, 1991; Kendall and Kendall, 1993; Walsham, 1991).

Consequently, we describe systems analysis and design as interpreting a three-way relationship between analyst, methodology and situation, and we continue to express concern that parts of this relationship are still missing in many of our methodologies' descriptions (Avison and Wood-Harper, 1990, p. 21). This is summarized in Figure 3.

The figure suggests that systems analysts are developing a methodological expertise in conceptual triangulation: the fixing of an unknown point making it a vertex of a triangle, the other two points being known. Schön (1983, pp. 50–69) describes this movement as maintaining a relationship between active experience and reflective observation. In moving from technical rationality to reflection-in-action, we understand that theory is generally too abstract to be applied, but that practice is individually too personal to let us converse actively within the divergent groups that make up organizations. By active conversations, we mean conversations that do things, that lead to more than 'just talk' (Goldkuhl and Lyytinen, 1982; Goldkuhl, 1987). This is what systems analysts do in successful interventions: they conduct active conversations that build information systems.

Figure 3 Information systems development arises from a triangle of interpretation

Even so, describing analysts' expertise in conceptual triangulation continues to be ambiguous due to the uncertainty they face in each situation. Schön (1983, p. 40) explains their uncertain position as being somewhere in between a means and an ends because 'in real-world practice, problems do not present themselves to practitioners as givens' (our emphasis). In discussing social system design, Ulrich (1983, pp. 78–9) similarly critiques the separation of means and ends, which Jackson (1989, p. 432) summarizes as follows: 'All ends can be seen as means to other ends and all means have normative implications of their own.' Ulrich thus proposes critical interaction as a model of rational discourse to 'guarantee an adequate translation of practical needs into technical questions, and of technical answers into practical decisions' (Ulrich, 1983, p. 78; also quoted in Jackson, 1989, p. 432, our emphasis).

We relate this to our description of a systems analyst's expertise in conceptual triangulation as follows: a surveyor or a navigator can make reasonably accurate measurements for proposed action by taking two known points as givens insofar as trees and stars are considered to be discrete physical objects. However, analysts work not just with physical artefacts but with concepts whose meaning cannot necessarily be ‘guaranteed’. Lyytinen (1987, p. 14) explains this as follows:

The language context is necessary in systems development because an information system always has a symbolic function. Use of information systems is not just the physical transfer of material objects and their efficient manipulation. It is based on users' ability to see these transfers as having a common significance (language expressions) that goes beyond their peculiar physical properties (our emphasis).

Working with symbols or concepts therefore has an important practical consequence for systems analysts. Even two known conceptual points of inquiry – theory and practice – are not absolute; instead, they are tentative since their potential relationships are governed by the significance of contextual meaning.

In working with such emergent structures – both physical and conceptual – a systems analyst's dilemma in conducting active conversations that build information systems centres on negotiating each stakeholder's freedom to communicate within existing organizational constraints, both physical and conceptual (Flood and Jackson, 1991).

As reflected in a methodology's theory and practice and in Lyytinen's taxonomic perspective, inquiry carries with it the potential of disrupting hierarchical relationships by creating unanticipated contexts in ISD. We discuss this below and conclude by proposing post-structuralist approaches as areas for further research.

## Discussion and conclusion

When analysts make distinctions between what is of central and marginal relevance, they interpret contexts in each situation. In considering certain aspects of a situation more relevant than others, resulting perspectives impose a degree of general structure such as hierarchy to order how interventions are conducted. In ISD, for example, 'hard' or technical views have dominated 'soft' or socially based perspectives (Miles, 1989, p. 59). However, methodologies associated with these perspectives interpret contexts in ways that can be both revealing and limiting. For instance, Lyytinen (1987, p. 35) explains how technical design oriented methodologies neglect language and organizational contexts thereby strengthening failure patterns in some cases.

A potential limitation of our analysis is that the language context may be in danger of overwhelming Lyytinen's other two contexts: the technical and the organizational. However, the boundaries between such categories are themselves problematic. This was suggested, generally, by our argument concerning context-bound meaning and boundless contexts. Additionally, Bloomfield (1992, p. 191), for example, questions the dichotomy between social and technical arguing that criticisms should focus not so much on the way technological perspectives have ignored social aspects of organizations, as the way they have 'ignored the social nature of the technical aspects of systems development' (his emphasis). Dahlbom (1992) also argues how perspectives are represented in physical forms, so, for example, technology may itself represent an organizational perspective. To this we add that regardless of whether we are interested in technical, organizational or language contexts, in communicating such concerns to other people, we nonetheless often rely on some form of language.

A second potential criticism of a deconstructive perspective is that it does not adhere to any given set of values. While this supports Boland's (1989) assertion that knowledge is not guaranteed by a method because it is based in practice, deconstruction could be coupled with perspectives that are interested in adhering to certain value systems. For example, Giddens' (1984) structuration theory could be linked with a deconstructive perspective to reflect on the material and historical circumstances in which values systems are embedded. Similarly, a deconstructive strategy could inform value systems based on democratic principles by drawing attention to the ways these are suppressed or considered supplementary. For a considered discussion of the way deconstruction can be understood as an ethical demand, readers are referred to Critchley (1992).

In relation to Burrell and Morgan's (1979) four paradigms, like Rosenhead (1984, p. 82) we emphasize that methodologies carry 'a pantechnicon of ideological baggage' whether they are reactionary or emancipatory. As such, a deconstructive perspective may be helpful in exposing universal claims insofar as it is a way of recognizing that methodologies are partial and biased perspectives. With this end in mind, we have adapted some post-structuralist approaches from Culler (1983) to information systems since these might be helpful in raising critical issues about our methodologies for future research:

(1) Consider ways a methodology differs from itself. Our analysis of the way Lyytinen's (1987) taxonomy of three exhaustive hierarchical descriptions undermines itself, suggests that what may define the identity of a particular methodology will entail perspectives that cause it to differ from itself.

(2) Attend to conflicts within methodologies that are reproduced as conflicts between them. Critical disputes can often be identified as displaced reenactments of conflicts already admitted within a methodology. In this way we can learn to question the assumptions and decisions that transform a complex pattern of internal differences into alternative perspectives.

(3) Think about how descriptions of another methodology can be read as a self-description. When a methodology describes how another approach works, can this be read as a description of its own methods? This often indicates the return in a disguised or displaced form of a procedure that it criticizes in others.

(4) Watch out for asymmetrical oppositions in value-laden hierarchies. Often in hierarchies the first term is more highly valued than the second. For example, in considering theory and practice, is the second term treated as the marginal version of the first, and does this prove to be the condition or possibility of the first?

(5) Observe what methodologies marginalize. This could help to identify exclusions on which hierarchies such as theory and practice depend and by which their preferred readings may be disrupted.

(6) Examine points of condensation. Is there a single term, such as 'context' that integrates different lines or value systems?

We propose that such approaches have the potential of changing how we view contexts by working with existing conceptual frameworks, not to destroy, but to reconstruct them in new ways. As such, deconstruction suggests that even if we cannot get out of existing conceptual frameworks, resulting inquiry does have the potential of producing changes in assumptions and practices in how systems analysts interpret situations.

We argued this using the notion of taxonomy and methodology as examples of conceptual frameworks. In considering the presuppositions on which taxonomy is based, we suggested that critical categories are not merely tools used in interpreting contexts. Rather, they are problems to be explored. We considered this further in relation to methodology through the notions of theory and practice. By disrupting oppositions and hierarchical relations on which our approaches to information systems development depend, we intend to prevent these from being taken for granted. This supports Boland's (1989) criticism of the temptation to regard language and metaphor as tools. That is, language and metaphor are a human condition, and as such, they are not merely physical objects that we can put down and abandon like we might do a hammer. Perhaps then, our research approach may be seen as an example of how to work critically with that condition.

To conclude, a deconstructive perspective considers what is entailed in criticizing a position. One adopts at least some of the language and assumptions of that position to present arguments against it. Ironically, one may therefore gradually assume many of its implications in developing an ‘alternative’ critical position (MacCormac, 1985, p. 27). As we have argued above, to deconstruct a perspective is to consider how it potentially undermines the preferred reading it claims to assert. This is not to announce exhaustively the discovery of a general principle – that we can never know anything for certain. Here deconstruction differs from post-modern perspectives, and for a discussion of this readers are referred to Norris (1992).

To sum up these matters, while deconstruction has no ‘guarantee’, it works much in the way that a bumble bee flies: despite structures that simultaneously assert this to be a technical impossibility, we proceed, nonetheless, to explore changing contexts.
