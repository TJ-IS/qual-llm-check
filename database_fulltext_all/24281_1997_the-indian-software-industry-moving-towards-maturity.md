# The Indian software industry: moving towards maturity

S. C. BHATNAGAR

Indian Institute of Management, Ahmedabad 380015, India

SHIRIN MADON

London School of Economics and Political Science, Houghton Street, London WC2A 2AE, UK

Over the last decade, India has made a conscious effort to participate in the global software industry by providing software development services to client companies in the West. We see the Indian software industry as sustaining its competitive advantage and having reasonable growth prospects. In some ways this is a counter-point from many of the earlier predictions in the literature that suggested that the growth of the software industry in India was a temporary phenomenon which exploited an existing opportunity of shortage of software engineers in Western countries and benefited from the consequent moves towards outsourcing.

## Introduction

The global software industry has been growing exponentially since the late 1970s (Schware, 1992; Correa, 1995). The constituent segments of this industry are systems software, applications tools or packaged software, application solutions or systems integration in which the vendor provides a custom-developed solution for the key business processes in an industry and professional services. Since the 1970s, the industry has witnessed a trend in outsourcing software development activity by organizations due to the huge increase in software costs, the increasing demand for complex information systems applications, the rapid obsolescence rates of the information technology (IT) infrastructure and the inadequate supply of IT personnel. Cost-cutting has been high on the agendas of most organizations. For example, according to one European survey, cost-effectiveness and economic pressures are the most important reasons for outsourcing (Mitchell, 1994). At the same time, the growing demand for information services within an organization is occurring at a time when the supply of human resources adequately trained in the skills associated with new IT technology is declining (Kirkpatrick, 1991).

In a survey conducted by Sobol and Apte (1995) of 100 US companies, a significant proportion of the firms (77%) were engaged in domestic outsourcing of one or more of their information systems (IS) functions, while global software outsourcing was adopted by a far smaller percentage of companies (16.7%).

However, this practice is likely to change since the survey revealed that two-thirds of the companies interviewed were willing to consider global outsourcing as a valid option.

What makes this trend interesting from the point of view of developing countries is the fact that there is an increasing tendency among Western companies to outsource certain activities in software development to software factories in developing countries in which it is inexpensive to develop software. A catalyst for outsourcing activity to developing countries has been the improvements in satellite communications in countries where labour is cheap. The size of such outsourced software development work which is sometimes labelled as ‘foreign opportunity’ is growing very rapidly (Heeks, 1996).

The greatest conscious effort by any single country to provide external vendor services to client companies in the West is India with a 12% share of the total foreign opportunity and a 20% share of the professional services segment of this opportunity (Taylor, 1996). In recent years, much attention has been focused in the literature on the growth of the Indian software industry over the last decade. Much of the success of Indian software companies can be attributed to their entrepreneurship and trading abilities. They were able to spot a need for trained programmers in the US and Europe and were able to quickly organize the supply of reasonably good quality programmers at low prices on a man-month charge basis. These contracts, representing 70–80% of the industry's export revenue, were generally short-term, low in risk and investment and low in value added in terms of revenue and skills. This window of advantage is, however, narrowing gradually as other countries such as China, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and the Philippines are offering cheaper labour (Heeks, 1996). Moreover, the automation of software development is resulting in a reduction in the labour intensity of this activity while at the same time making the quality of skilled manpower more important than ever before. Schware (1992) argued that the Indian software export industry cannot really take off unless there exists a good domestic industry. However, the export industry remains vibrant with a compound growth rate of 55.48 (National Association of Software and Services Companies, 1996). Other authors continue to characterize exports from India as 'body shopping' and being 'highly concentrated in a few firms'. By implication, all these authors are not recognizing any future for Indian exports in higher value-added services and products (Correa, 1995; Sen, 1995).

This paper presents a counter-view and argues that the Indian software industry has matured and is likely to play a significant role in the higher value-added services and product markets. We first comment on the trends experienced by the Indian software industry. We then define some indicators by which the maturity of the industry can be judged and present data from the recent past to assess its future direction.

## Is the Indian software industry moving towards maturity?

Much has been written about the Indian software industry in recent years. However, with the exception of some substantial research efforts, usually as doctoral programmes, publications in this area are limited both in terms of data and conceptual analysis. A few analytical studies on the Indian software industry have been carried out. One view put forward by Heeks (1996) is that the rapid growth of software exports stifles the domestic market. Heeks (1996) presumed supply constraints – that the domestic software demand could not be met because it was more attractive to deploy scarce programming resources for higher earning export projects. However, in the last 2 years, domestic software growth has outpaced software exports. Domestic software demand has been spurred on by economic liberalization and by the consequent increase in competition. In 1996–1997, Indian industry spent US\$2.5 billion (up 22% from 1995–1996) on IT – triple the amount that was spent 4 years ago (Dataquest, 1997). The fact of the matter is that earlier Indian companies preferred to do all the software development in-house. It is in recent years that they have begun to spend on software and outsource development work. As domestic demand improves, small and medium-sized companies may no longer look to export software, but will begin to service Indian companies exclusively.

One way of evaluating the degree of maturity in the software export industry in India is to use Yourdon's (1992) 'stages of development' model which is reproduced in Table 2. By the mid-1980s, India's software export industry had reached the first stage of maturity – that of reputation building through low value-added, body-shopping activity. Foreign firms increasingly turned their attention towards India's low-cost, skilled software professionals in order to meet their software development needs. By the end of the 1980s, on-site development work accounted for as much as 95% of the total Indian software exports. This involved sending programmers to work abroad – a practice named body shopping. India provided a pool of cheap labour for Silicon Valley when and if required. While the margins in exporting cheap programmers were low by international standards, low manpower costs meant that Indian exporters could still achieve returns on the capital employed of over 25%. Moreover, this strategy enabled Indian software companies to build credibility with potential clients.

## The Indian software industry

As can be seen in Table 3, in the last 5 years the offshore content of the software exported from India has risen steadily. Since the onset of offshore software development, Indian software exports have grown from US\$164 million in 1991–1992 to US\$1.1 billion in 1996–1997 representing an annual growth rate of 55%. Several companies are now developing offshore packages in the area of internetworking tools, tools for handling the year 2000 problem and customizable solutions for warehousing management, port management, games management, etc. While today most Indian software exporters are probably at the second stage of maturity, they are already beginning to explore the third state – that of building software products for export overseas. Moreover, some of the more aggressive companies have even begun to move towards the fourth stage of maturity developing niche products for the global market. It can therefore be surmised that the Indian software industry is at the beginning of stage 3. What is encouraging for the industry is that, while it took all of 10 years to move from stage 1 to stage 2, the transition to stage 3 appears to be much quicker.

Yourdon's (1992) stages of development for the software industry indicate the direction in which the industry is likely to move. However, to predict whether such a movement will take place we need to understand the conditions that must be fulfilled at each stage. After stage 1, when reputation is built through low value-added body shopping, the movement to offshore activities will be facilitated by the existence of a reliable telecom infrastructure. There has been substantial growth in data communications and network facilities in India. There are already two commercial services for packet-switched public data networks covering the large cities in a X.25 network and small towns through a satellite-based network. A large number of organizations are now installing very small aperture terminals (VSATs) to connect different parts of their organizations in wide area networks. International data communications facilities are also available with Indian organizations able to hook into international data networks via equipment which supports data traffic speeds of 9.6–64 KBPS (kilobytes per second). Links for videoconferencing of up to 2 MB (megabytes) have also been installed in some organizations (Mehta, 1996). The movement to offshore activity would also require the widespread use of standard system design and development methodologies, so that clients have the confidence that projects will be done in time and with requisite quality. Project management skills need to be developed for managing projects which, in the first stage, were often done under the guidance of client's project managers.

For movement to the third stage of offshore package development, a greater understanding is required of the operations of the industry for which packages are being developed. This means developing skills of organizational analysis. Such package development also entails greater risks and larger expenditure on research and development (R&D). A greater presence in international markets through offices, joint ventures and subsidiaries is required to be able to negotiate and sell such packages. This means that units must achieve a minimum critical size before they can undertake the development of offshore packages. Finally, the development of 'shrink wrapped' products requires a much greater understanding of emerging IT platforms and technologies. A large expenditure on marketing needs to be incurred and a brand image needs to be created. A much greater degree of risk is involved in package development.

Another viewpoint in assessing the stage of development of an industry comes through the frameworks for achieving national competitive advantage proposed by Porter (1990) and subsequently by Nagy (1994), specifically in the context of the IT industry. Both authors identified endowments in terms of skilled labour and infrastructure. In the discussion above we also identified technical competence and capability building as important factors. The presence of a strong domestic market has been identified by both authors as being an important factor. We have indicated the importance of the size of individual units whereas Porter (1990) mentioned industry structure and managerial practices. Hanna (1994) recognized the role of government support. We therefore believe that an analysis along the following dimensions will allow us to judge the level of maturity as these are indicative of the new strengths required by the industry to move from one stage to the next.

(1) The level of acceptance of products and services in international markets.

(2) Endowments in terms of technical competence and capability building.

(3) The software industry structure and managerial practices.

(4) The extent of support provided by the government.

(5) The growth pattern of domestic demand.

## Some indicators of the maturity of the Indian software export in industry

## The level of acceptance of products and services in international markets

We judge the acceptance of Indian services in international markets by the growth of software exports, particularly the growth pattern of recently established companies. If new ventures can grow to a reasonable size in a short period, it suggests that the services offered by such companies are accepted in the international market because the Indian software industry has acquired a reasonable positive image. The fact that many of these units are able to obtain offshore work without having to go through long years of reputation building is also suggestive of the positive brand image of the industry. Table 4 indicates that out of the top 50 companies in 1996, there were 19 companies which were established in the previous 5 years. These companies are highly export oriented and account for 20% of the total Indian software exports.

Another indication of the acceptance of Indian companies is the fact that an increasing proportion of the export industry's revenue is being derived from the execution of large turnkey projects undertaken in India by foreign clients, particularly for financial institutions. The man-month rates for such contracts are significantly higher than for programming services, involving around 200–300 man years. For example, TCS has recently designed and developed a securities clearing and settlement system for the Swiss Corporation for Securities Settlement. This system was needed to meet the demands arising from the integration of the European Union (EU) in 1992. The bulk of the software development work for the project was done offshore in Madras using satellite links to the host machine in Zurich. The project involved hundreds of hours of programming code, underscoring the industry's technical and managerial strength. Another example of a mission-critical project concerns the liaison between five top Indian companies and BNR – the Canadian-based research wing of Bell South and Northern Telecom. These companies operate a special development centre for BNR for ongoing software development and enhancements to BNR telecom technology (Soota, 1994). This liaison marks a fundamental change in the nature of outsourcing relationships between Western clients and Indian companies. The transfer of ownership of the system for long-term maintenance and enhancement suggests a trend towards Indian companies becoming partners rather than mere suppliers of software services.

There is another way in which this acceptance is manifesting itself. Multinationals operating in various sectors in India have now begun to outsource software development work to the IT department of their subsidiary in India. For example, Phillips Holland sources some of its IT services from Phillips India.

In an internationally competitive market, firms must continuously innovate in the products and services being offered. In the software industry the major resource is manpower. A sign of growing technical capability amongst companies is reflected in their usage of state-of-the-art software development tools. Over the past few years, a number of trends have emerged in the global software industry, including the use of computer-aided software engineering (CASE) and fourth generation languages (4gls) in software development, the adoption of graphical user interfaces (GUIs), the move towards open systems, the use of object orientated programming languages (OOPLs), the emergence of client-server computing, the growth in packaged software and the use of formal software development methodologies. Several Indian exporters have adopted these emerging technologies, as indicated in Table 5.

Indian software companies are trying to move away from being low-price, low-quality producers to being competitive-price, high-quality producers. Since 1993, market leaders such as TCS and Infosys Technologies have adopted the ISO 9000 international standard for quality management and assurance. To date, 43 software companies have acquired ISO 9000 certifications and another 52 are in the process of acquiring this certificate. The Indian software industry will soon have the maximum number of ISO 9000-certificated companies in any industry, anywhere in the world. The Indian operations of Motorola have achieved the highest level of international standard with only one other project having done so earlier – IBM's project at NASA (National Association of Software and Services Companies, 1996).

The perception of improved quality has resulted in better value for money for the services of Indian programmers. According to the Department of Electronics (1992), India is no longer rated above other countries on its low labour cost since other countries have similar or lower costs, but on its productivity rates of manpower (Bhatnagar, 1994). Major Indian software houses are also beginning to invest in the research and development of their own products. ## The software industry structure and managerial practices

In terms of the structure of the industry and growth pattern, we have tried to analyse the rate of new entrants as well as the distribution of units according to their size. If the industry continues to attract new players at a significant rate, it is suggestive that it is on a high growth path. This growth should be seen in the context of increasing competition from other countries such as China which are lower cost producers as compared to India. Similarly, a good performance of software companies on the Indian capital market is suggestive that both the foreign investors as well as the domestic investors are 'bullish' about the future growth and sustainability of the industry.

The growth of the industry will depend on the growth of the existing players and the ability of the industry to attract new players. The rapid proliferation in the number of small and medium-sized companies prepared to invest capital by entering the software export business is a sure indicator of the growth that lies ahead. In 1995, there were 410 companies (excluding one-man operations) in the export segment employing a total of 60 000 people (National Association of Software and Services Companies, 1996). Two years later, in 1997, this number is reported to be around 1000 (Nanda, 1997). While the top 20 exporters account for almost 70% of the total software export revenue, the figures in Table 7 reveal that there is a large presence of small and medium-sized companies in the export industry. Over three-quarters of the companies involved have fewer than 39 personnel as shown in Table 8.

Companies established a decade back could not tap the equity market and therefore started with a low capital base. Their growth came through the plough back of earnings. Today the potential of earning profits in the software industry is well established and new companies are able to tap financial institutions and public for capital. Such companies can acquire a critical size in a matter of 1–2 years.

Many of these recent start ups have achieved a remarkably good performance in the share market. For example, Table 9 shows a comparison between the current market price and book value of five software companies. It can be seen that all of these companies are doing rather well – the current prices are three to five times the book value. Most of these companies went public in the last 5 years. Therefore, this increase, which has taken place in the last 5 years, is quite impressive. The biggest jump in the rankings amongst the top 50 companies was achieved by Square D Software Ltd. which jumped ten ranks from last year to close at 30 this year. Square D Software typifies the new aggressive type of software company that has emerged in the last 2 years. With a heavy investment backing them, these companies are willing to take the associated risks to strike gold in the software arena.

Growth for existing companies is also coming through horizontal expansion. Many of these companies are considering locating their offices in secondary cities such as Madras, Hyderabad, Calcutta and Pune. For example, Infosys Technologies has recently set up another three software factories similar to the one in Bangalore, in Bhubaneshwar, Mangalore and in Pune.

Another noticeable trend in the nature of companies has been the lateral movement of non-software companies into software exports which offers greater profits than the domestic market. For example, Siemens, a hardware company, earned more from software exports last year than from hardware. NIIT, a training company, currently earns 40% of its revenue from software exports. Similarly Indian companies not connected with IT are moving into software exports. For example, during the late 1980s, a tobacco company, a chemical company, a textile company and an engineering consultancy company moved into the software industry by setting up software divisions/companies (ITC, Sonata, Mafatlal Consultancy Services, and MN Dastur & Company). All of these companies have been quite successful in the domestic market and are counted amongst the top 20 domestic players. More recently, new software companies such as Twinstar Software have been floated with large capital outlays by promoters totally unconnected with IT (Economic Times, 1996a). The public sector trading house State Trading Corporation, which has no experience in IT products/services, has just announced a joint venture with Datamatics, an Indian software company (Economic Times, 1996b).

The structure of the Indian software export industry in terms of the types of companies represented in the top 50 exporters is analysed in Table 10. The data on software export performance was taken from Dataquest (1992, 1993, 1994, 1995) and profiles of the companies have been studied to identify their category. The recent growth in the number of joint venture companies is a direct result of the liberalization of government policies and the growing stature of the Indian software industry.

## The extent of support provided by the government

The liberalization reforms of the Indian economy which began in 1991 resulted in a wave of new foreign and domestic direct investment into India and a growing recognition that investment in IT was necessary if India was to compete in open, global markets. A new policy was announced in 1986 (Government of India, 1986) to allow the latest foreign designs and software development tools in order to enable the Indian industry to produce world class software for exports. In terms of infrastructure development, the government launched its Software Technology Park (STP) scheme in 1990. The scheme, operated by an autonomous organization under the Department of Electronics, was meant to promote the development and export of software using data communications links or by exporting professional services. At present, around 225 software units have been given approval by the government to operate under this scheme. The parks are established at Bangalore, Pune, Bhubaneswar, Trivandrum, Hyderabad, Noida, Gandhinagar, Jaipur and Calcutta. They offer general infrastructural facilities such as power, office space, centralized computing facilities and high-speed data communication facilities. By March 1995, there were 124 high-speed leased datalines providing 64 KBPS links with overseas customers. STP also provides a single window interface for member units dealing with the issuance of import certificates, export declarations, etc., in order that they may expedite paper work with minimum delay (Taylor, 1996).

Economic liberalization resulted in a marked shift towards offshore projects with the percentage of offsite work rising from 9% in 1991 when the reforms were put into place to approximately 40% (National Association of Software and Services Companies, 1996). The reputation for sound programming skills and improved data links between India and the client organization led to a growing ‘comfort level’ among Western companies with outsourcing IT work to Indian software firms. As a result, foreign companies sought a variety of linkages with Indian software firms. These linkages involved multinational hardware vendors entering joint ventures with Indian companies with equity participation and technology transfer agreement. Examples of such companies include HP-HCL, Digital Equipment (India) Ltd, PSE Bull, Tata Unisys Ltd and Tata Information Systems Ltd. Apart from the low cost of developing software in India, such companies have been attracted to India because of the growing domestic market for hardware and software products. ## The growth pattern of domestic demand

A major lacunae in India's software export effort has been the lack of domestic demand although experience has shown that this lack of demand has not affected the growth of exports. However, the scenario with respect to domestic demand is changing rapidly. In the past 8 years, the domestic software industry has started to show high growth rates of 40–50%. In 1994–1995, the domestic software sales saw a 54% increase to US\$330 million as against US\$230 million in 1993–1994 outpacing software exports which grew by 51% to US\$470 million. During 1995–1996, the software industry grew by 61% over the previous year and the domestic component was 40%, up from the 33% in the previous year (Economic Times, 1996c). These figures do not include the large volume of in-house software development done in the country and also systems software. The National Association of Software and Services Companies (1995) estimated that there were approximately 390 domestic software companies employing over 32 000 technical people. As Table 11 reveals, over 67 companies could claim such a distinction. Unlike software exports, the structure of the domestic software players is less top heavy with the top 20 players accounting for only 43% of the total domestic software industry revenue.

The break-up of software activity of the domestic industry shows that turnkey assignments top the list with a share of 40.7% and products and packages 43.7%, indicating an upsurge in value-added activities in the domestic sector. In contrast, in the export industry, professional services still command a share of almost 50% (National Association of Software and Services Companies, 1996). Table 12 shows the segment-wise break-up of the industry for 1993–1994. The experience acquired in turnkey projects will help Indian software companies in developing a cadre of systems analysts who could later be deployed in turnkey projects in other countries.

In the package segment, over 100 new foreign software products were launched into the Indian market last year and imported software sales jumped by almost 160% to a new high of US\$0.09 billion, triggered off by the decrease in duty on software from 65% to 10% (Taylor, 1996). In the area of off the shelf software packages, imported software governs the majority market share. Despite the fact that the Indian software industry has had over one decade of exposure to the global market, India is still unable to compete with the giants. During the early 1980s, venture capital was unavailable for Indian companies which meant that entrepreneurs could not invest in the sophisticated hardware needed for the development and marketing of software packages. However, fund raising became easier in the late 1980s when the financial community began to recognize the potential of the software industry.

In recent years, the domestic market has moved to higher levels of microcomputing from simple personal computers (PCs) to workstations (Chopra, 1993). The growth in domestic IT spending in these key sectors has been fuelled in part by a steady reduction in import tariffs on computers, peripherals, software and components as part of the liberalization programme. By the turn of the century, an estimated 1 000 000 PCs are expected to be sold in India, up from 468 000 in 1996–1997 (Dataquest, 1997). For the purposes of remaining competitive, there is already a tendency for user organizations to outsource software development work to external vendors within India. Most of the top 20 vendors of hardware and software made their 'big bucks' bagging large orders from the government and corporate sector. Sales to the manufacturing sector continue to account for one-sixth of the total revenue as entrepreneurs begin to use technology to transform the nation's ageing industry infrastructure. Similarly, India's service section, including the financial services industry, has begun to use IT to help modernize its antiquated and labour-intensive infrastructure. Overall, the Indian services sector, comprising banking, telecommunications, energy and other services, accounts for approximately 405 of the revenues of the top 20 Indian IT companies (Dataquest, 1997). The implications of the recent growth of the domestic software industry for exports of software are many. As Indian companies implement sophisticated IT applications such as enterprise-wide solutions, they acquire new skills of systems analysis which will give them the confidence of quoting for similar projects overseas. Many Indian companies are partnering with international suppliers of such software to service larger markets in South East Asia. The larger installed PC base will provide a minimum critical size of market to those companies which plan to launch their products.

## Looking at the future

To summarize, from leveraging per-hour productivity, Indian software companies are now poised to leverage intellectual property rights (IPRs)-based productivity, moving towards the third and fourth stages of maturity in software development. Some of this growth is due to an increasing demand amongst local organizations for more sophisticated software systems.

The Indian IT industry, particularly software, has an impact far beyond the sector. It has enabled India to participate in the global technology revolution. The software industry has promoted India's image abroad in terms of entrepreneurial and technological capabilities. For example, within software exports, a new type of market is emerging. Large multinationals such as Phillips and Mico-Bosch, in a variety of industries, are beginning to outsource software development to the IT departments of the subsidiaries in India. Others such as Unilever are recognizing the software capability of Indians and are allowing their subsidiaries to undertake large IT applications suited for the local context rather than copying the standard IT application packages operated by them globally in all their subsidiaries. The software industry has also provided demonstration effects for other industries, in terms of export orientation, strategic alliances and foreign investment. For example, the success of software export has opened up opportunities for exports in other areas. Many multi-national corporations (MNCs) in other industries such as the pharmaceutical industry are setting up design and R&D centres in India to benefit from the inexpensive, technical manpower available in India. Finally, the Indian software industry continues to provide a strong domestic voice for liberalization and competition. And it is likely to be a major channel for modernization and technological change throughout the economy (Hanna, 1994).

However, despite these encouraging trends, there is plenty of room for internal growth and improvement. For example, what has prevented multinational software houses outsourcing to India? One of the main worries of client companies is security. Indian companies should be more conscious of this and demonstrate that they have a stable environment for certain applications and a legal framework to set out what happens when someone leaves their job. The issue of intellectual property rights (IPRs) has been a critical determinant for client companies contemplating offshore software development within India. It is estimated that software piracy has resulted in losses of approximately US\$140 million, amounting to nearly 20% of the revenue earned by the software industry in 1996 (Computers Today, 1997). The necessary laws to protect IPRs were originally contained in India's 1984

Copyright Act, but were not enforced strictly. Progress is now being made with India's recently amended copyright law of May 1994 empowering the government to impose fines for offenders. The issue of software quality continues to be of concern to the industry. As discussed earlier, many Indian software houses have adapted to international quality standards. However, new and higher standards being applied worldwide by offshore software factories continue to pressurize Indian companies to aim for even higher standards. For example, Citimax is a leading company dedicated to providing specialist software solutions to international clients in the financial services community through its offshore factory in Cairo. The company operates to the rigour of the latest standards in software quality such as ISO 9001, ISO 9000-3 and TickIT.

After 5 years of high growth in software exports, India's strong presence in the foreign opportunity market is acknowledged. However, the inadequate supply of qualified, skilled and experienced personnel is threatening to be a major constraint in the expansion of the software sector. Approximately 60 000 professionals are currently engaged in software development for export. In order to achieve projected exports of US\$3.6 billion around the turn of the century, an estimated 18 000 software developers will have to be added over the next 2 years even if the revenue per employee can be doubled (Mehta, 1996). As per current estimates, the number of software professionals available in the country is 140 000 of which 60 000 are employed in the software industry. If additional sources of supply of software personnel are not identified there will be a shortfall of software professionals to the extent of nearly 250 000 during the next 5 years (Planning Commission, 1997). Educational curricula will need to be revised to produce software engineers who are immediately employable. A major gap today is the skills of project management, particularly in view of the distinct shift towards offshore work. New institutions will have to be set up to impart multidisciplinary skills for project management (Bhatnagar, 1997). Large companies therefore need to have extensive training programmes to impart basic training in software engineering. Companies also need to think of innovative human resource development policies to retain quality staff. Some companies such as Infosys Technologies have come out with innovative ideas to attract and keep quality staff such as the idea of an employee stock option plan.

However, the availability, quality and price of universal telecommunication services in India continues to be out of norm with international services making Indian offshore work less competitive. In terms of the domestic market, despite the growing demand for IT services and products in the public sector, procurement practices stifle competition. Since two public sector agencies, CMC and NIC, still enjoy preferential access to execute software development and service projects for public agencies discouraging independent software houses from looking to the public services market for sales and experience in large systems projects. If the industry can continue to climb the value-added ladder while addressing the customers' concerns, Indian software developers will play an increasingly important role in one of the most dynamic global markets.
