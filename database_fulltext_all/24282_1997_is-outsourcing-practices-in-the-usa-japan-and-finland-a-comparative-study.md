With increasing cost-reduction pressures, outsourcing of information systems (IS) functions has emerged as an important option for managers in companies around the world. In this paper we present the results of our exploratory, empirical research comparing practices of domestic and global IS outsourcing in the USA, Japan and Finland. The research methodology of a mail survey with similar questionnaires was used in all three countries. The paper provides new empirical evidence comparing these countries on the types of functions being commonly outsourced, the extent to which these functions are outsourced, views of Chief Information Of® cers (CIOs) on the desired cost savings and the comparative ratings of the advantages and disadvantages of IS outsourcing. Also compared are the roles played by different executives in making outsourcing decisions. The implications of these results for IS managers and directions for future research are presented in the conclusions.

## Introduction

Tracing its roots to the time-sharing and professional services of the 1960s, outsourcing has become an important option today for information systems (IS) and corporate executives. Outsourcing means selectively turning over to a vendor some or all of the IS functions, ranging from simple data entry to software development and maintenance, data centre operations and full system integration. Announcements and anecdotal reports on outsourcing arrangements have by now become routine items in the IS trade literature. However, research into the phenomenon, although growing rapidly, is still limited. One early research study applied the transaction cost economics framework (Williamson, 1986) in the context of the make versus buy decision (Walker, 1985). More recently, several researchers have used empirical methodologies to understand the determinants of outsourcing decisions (Loh and Venkatraman, 1992a; Arnett and Jones, 1994; Lacity et al., 1994; Hanaoka and Shimada, 1995; Saarinen et al., 1995; Sobol and Apte, 1995) while others have employed analytical approaches such as diffusion theory (Loh and Venkatraman, 1992b) to prescribe managerial actions. A comprehensive review of the theory and practice of outsourcing can be found in Willcocks and Lacity (1995).

Today, several issues related to domestic and global outsourcing ± the extent of outsourcing being practiced, the advantages and disadvantages of outsourcing, the activities that are best to outsource and a comparison of all of the above for different countries in the world ± remain far from settled. Providing answers to some of these issues through questionnaire-based, empirical research that was carried out in 1993± 1994 in three countries ± the USA, Japan, and Finland ± is the focus of the research presented in this paper. The choice of our research sites was in¯ uenced by several considerations. The USA is a large industrialized country with an advanced information technology (IT) infrastructure, a large number of companies and a large market for IS services. Japan is also an equally advanced industrialized country with an excellent IS infrastructure, a large number of companies and a sizeable market of IS services. However, there are some well known differences. The Japanese companies are noted for their unique practices such as lifetime employment and the Keiretsu system involving a network of companies having interlocking customer± supplier and ownership arrangements. They are also distinguished by the use of their own language, a different character set and a tradition to rely on both. Finland, in contrast is a small European country with a well developed IS infrastructure and use of modern technology. With the small number and sizes of the companies in Finland, the market for IS services is relatively small. It also has its own language. With these interesting sets of similarities and differences, we believed that comparing the practices and views of IS outsourcing in these three developed countries in three different sectors of the world would provide interesting and important insights into the phenomenon of IS outsourcing.

We asked three sets of research questions, the answers to which can bene® t both Management information systems (MIS) researchers and practitioners. The answers to these questions are compared across different countries to seek underlying causes for the differences and similarities in IS outsourcing practices in the USA, Japan and Finland. The three research topics are as follows.

(1) The extent of outsourcing. What IS functions are being outsourced? To what extent are these functions outsourced? The functions we consider include software development and maintenance, data communications network, support operations, disaster recovery, data centre operations, integrated system development, training and back-of® ce clerical tasks including data entry.

(2) Outsourcing decision. What are the reasons for and against outsourcing of IS functions? What monetary rewards (i.e. cost savings) are desired for outsourcing? Who usually initiates the efforts to outsource? Who makes the ® nal decision?

(3) A comparison of global versus domestic outsourcing. It would seem that while companies may readily outsource domestically (i.e. sourced from within one’s country), they may not be as eager to outsource globally (i.e. sourced from an entity that is outside one’ s country or \`offshore’ ) due to some of the inherent dif® culties and risks. Therefore, it is important to study the views of IS executives on global outsourcing and to compare these views to their views on domestic outsourcing.

The research reported in this paper is exploratory in nature. The results of any prior research in inter country comparisons of IS outsourcing practices and views were not available and, hence, the principal goals of this research were to understand the similarities and differences and to develop plausible hypotheses regarding the underlying causes. The scope of this research was limited to the study of IS outsourcing practices. Measuring the perceived success or failure of outsourcing decisions or testing a theory explaining the outsourcing phenomenon (such as the transaction cost economics theory) were not the goals of this research.

## IS outsourcing: an overview and review of the literature

A number of factors have led to the recent growth of the outsourcing phenomenon (Apte, 1992). IS departments are no longer experiencing the generous budget increases that occurred in the 1970s and 1980s. In fact, cost-reduction pressures and the need for better ef® - ciency often trigger companies to consider outsourcing of IS (Lacity et al., 1994). As found in a survey of outsourcing practices of America’ s most effective IS users (Sobol and Apte, 1995), cost containment and a reduced need to hire IS professionals are the two most important reasons for outsourcing. Other important reasons behind outsourcing include improved cost predictability, an improved ability to focus on the strategic use of IS and increased accessibility of vendors.

Based on Williamson’ s (1980) transaction cost theory (TCT), Saarinen and Vepsalainen (1994) proposed a general framework of procurement strategies for IS development, while Klepper (1993) suggested a contingency framework for analysing economically ef® cient relationships after the outsourcing decision has already been made. However, the question of whether the TCT can explain the IS outsourcing phenomenon is yet to be conclusively resolved. In an empirical research, Lacity and Willcocks (1995) found that in a signi® cant number of instances the TCT either failed to explain the outsourcing experiences or the experiences contradicted the TCT.

The risks and costs of outsourcing are sometimes lost amid the rhetoric about outsourcing’ s bene® ts (Meyer, 1994). Loss of control over the quality of the software and the project’ s timetable (Foxman, 1994), reduced ¯ exibility and loss of strategic alignment (Walker, 1985) are often expressed as the drawbacks of outsourcing. With in-depth case studies and interviews of 36 executives in 13 American companies, Lacity and Hirschheim (1993) analysed the pitfalls and potential risks of outsourcing to give practical advice for drawing a successful contract between the outsourcer and the vendor. The costs of negotiating and monitoring the outsourcing contract with a vendor and the cost of reverting to the insourcing option should outsourcing fail can be substantial. Likewise, the risk of losing touch with the advances in IT and thereby losing the ability to implement strategic

applications of IT can also be signi® cant. In another empirical research, Clark et al. (1995) carried out 63 in-depth interviews with executives to understand the forces that drive outsourcing decisions and how these decisions are arrived at.

The signi® cant disparity of the salary levels of personnel between developed and underdeveloped countries has made global outsourcing a small but rapidly growing segment of the overall outsourcing market (Apte and Mason, 1995a). Global outsourcing can lead to substantial bene® ts: signi® cant cost savings, a faster cycle time, help in developing and operating global IS and access to foreign markets and a skilled labour pool. However, with the advantages come certain drawbacks: problems of communication and coordination, potential violation of intellectual property rights, unclear governmental rules and regulations concerning transborder data ¯ ow and trade in services, and an inadequate infrastructure within the vendor’s home country. The outsourcer should, therefore, approach the global outsourcing option with suf® cient care. Patane and Jurison (1994) surveyed ® rms with an IS base of larger than \$25 million and found that strategic applications are not outsourced presently and are not likely to be outsourced to foreign countries in the future.

A signi® cant part of the empirical research reported in the above-mentioned papers has been carried out in the context of the USA and a few European countries. To our knowledge, however, little work has been done in understanding the outsourcing trends in Japan and, in particular, in gaining further understanding of IS outsourcing as a global phenomenon through making crosscountry comparison of outsourcing practices. The empirical research we present in this paper was carried out to meet these research needs. The US-based survey (Sobol and Apte, 1995) was used as a starting point for the current research. This research surveyed large samples of ® rms in Japan and Finland and used a similar questionnaire which allowed a cross-country comparison of both domestic and global outsourcing practices.

## The empirical study

The research methodology of a questionnaire-based mail survey was used for this study. The original mail questionnaire was developed and deployed in the USA. This questionnaire was subsequently shared with colleagues in Finland and Japan who translated the questionnaire into the appropriate languages and administered it in their individual countries. While a vast majority of the questions were kept identical in the translated versions, minor modi® cations were made to the questionnaire to suit the particular business environments, terminology and practices in individual countries. For example, a few questions were added in the Japanese version while a few questions pertaining to global outsourcing were removed from the Finnish version. We discuss these modi® cations later.

The sample for the USA study was obtained by mailing questionnaires in late 1993 to the CIOs of companies listed in 1991 and/or 1992 Computerworld Premier 100 issues (Sobol and Apte, 1995). Since there was some overlap in the two lists, a total of 149 companies were contained in this sample frame. Typically, these were large companies with median annual revenues of \$3500 million and a median annual IS budget of \$65 million. The median IS department employed 350 workers (see Table 1). Forty-eight companies out of 149 responded to the survey. This response rate of 32% is quite high for a mail questionnaire. Indeed, given that approximately one-third of the companies had responded and that the data were to be used to obtain overall percentages, the sample size was considered to be adequate for tests of statistical signi® cance. Moreover, the statistics for the companies who did and did not respond showed no signi® cant differences with respect to the company characteristics and industrial sector representation of the companies. A comparison of late and early responders also showed no differences in response. (This test is often used to study the effects of non-response assuming that late responders are more like the nonresponders.)

The sample in Japan was selected by mailing questionnaires in 1994 to the 483 largest companies in Japan ranked in order of decreasing asset size by the Diamond Corporation, Tokyo (Hanaoka and Shimada, 1995). This sample consisted of 340 manufacturing companies, ten ® nancial services companies and 133 other non-manufacturing companies. As seen in Table 1, the companies sampled in Japan were comparable in size to the American companies sampled; however the response rate in Japan was 18%.

In Finland the questionnaire was mailed in 1994 to the CIOs of 444 Finnish companies ± 400 companies selected from the list of 500 large industrial companies reported in Talouselama magazine, plus the 23 largest ® nancial institutions and 21 public organizations (Saarinen et al., 1995). With 141 responses, a response rate of 32% was achieved. As reported in Table 1, the Finnish companies were on the average smaller than their US and Japanese counterparts. The CIOs of these companies were surveyed to determine (1) to what extent they outsourced their IS functions both domestically and globally and (2) what their views on outsourcing were.

The questionnaire used in our study (see Appendix I) was divided into six sections containing both closedended and open-ended questions. The ® rst section asked whether the ® rm was engaged in outsourcing, what cost saving is desired for outsourcing and the type of IS function/s it outsourced. The next two sections investigated the reasons for and against outsourcing. The respondents were asked to rate each statement, in a series of statements capturing the generally accepted advantages and disadvantages of outsourcing, on a nine-point, Likert-type scale representing the statement’s importance as they perceived it.

The fourth and the ® fth sections of the questionnaire dealt with the global outsourcing. These sections included questions similar to the ones asked earlier in the context of domestic outsourcing. The sixth and ® nal section gathered information concerning the IS department, such as the size of the budget, the number of IS professionals, and the extent of centralization of IS functions. Information concerning the executive/s (chief executive of® cer (CEO), CIO or user department head) who initiated the investigation into the outsourcing option and the executive/s who made the ® nal decision was also elicited in the ® nal section of the questionnaire.

Each questionnaire was accompanied by a short cover letter explaining the purpose of the study. Considering the need to limit the length of the questionnaire and given that the terms used in the questionnaire were commonly used terms in the industry (none were technical terms that needed explanation), a glossary of terms de® ning each was not included. This means that there is always a possibility that some respondents may have had to de® ne and interpret some terms (such as a disaster recovery function or an initiator or a decision maker of outsourcing decision, etc.) for themselves.

## Survey results

## Background on IS outsourcing in the USA, Japan and Finland

The use of IT in each of the three countries has a long history spanning three to four decades. The application of IT is widespread and has reached a stage of maturity in all three countries. However, the unique history and characteristics of each has lent a different character to this use in each country. For example, being a sparsely populated country, Finland has become a quick adopter of modern technologies such as mobile telecommunications ± one in ® ve persons has a mobile telephone in Finland. Outsourcing has been practiced in the form of time-sharing and packaged software since the 1960s in all three countries, but this practice has evolved along different patterns in different countries. Kodak’s decision in 1989 to outsource its entire IS function to three parties ± IBM, DEC and Business Land ± is considered a landmark event in the USA (Loh and Venkatraman, 1992b). Since then, outsourcing has been viewed as a credible and important alternative by the IS community. In Finland, IS outsourcing received more attention in the mid-1980s due to the common practice of forming a captive company to whom all IS functions of a parent company were outsourced. Labelled as \`total outsourcing’ , the practice involved large sums of money and risk and, hence, these outsourcing decisions were often made by the CEOs of companies in Finland. With subsequent failures experienced by several captive companies (see Saaksjarvi et al., 1991; Saarinen and Saaksjarvi, 1993), the practice of total outsourcing fell into disrepute. According to our questionnaires, the practice of \`selective outsourcing’ of IS function has gained more popularity in Finland.

As the country of origin of many technological innovations (such as semiconductors) and IT applications, the USA is generally considered to be at the forefront of many developments and managerial practices related to IT. The use of IT is also widespread in Japan, but as observed by Shimada (1991), the use of IS in the USA is on a much wider scale than that in Japan. For example, as of 1994, the number of personal computers (PCs) per 1000 employees was approximately 550 in the USA, 500 in Finland and 146 in Japan (see White Paper, 1995). The level of connectivity of computers is also much higher in Finland and the USA than in Japan. In companies, the percentage of PCs connected to a local area network (LAN) is approximately 53% in the USA, is as high as 80% in Finland and is only approximately 9% in Japan. Our survey found that both Japanese and Finnish companies spend a signi® cantly smaller

## Extent of domestic and global IS outsourcing

Given the level of use of IT, it is not surprising to ® nd in Table 2, that companies in the USA have embraced the practice of outsourcing more widely (77.1%) than other countries. However, IS outsourcing is almost equally large in Japan (73.3%) and is only a little less so in Finland (60.3%). In fact, a test of the differences between the proportions in the Table 2 data shows that the differences in the extent of outsourcing are not signi® cant at the 0.05 level. It should be noted that in Japan and, to a certain extent, in Finland outsourcing relationships are primarily with a subsidiary of the outsourcing company, whereas in the USA the outsourcing partners are typically independent vendors.

We can see in Table 2, that in line with domestic outsourcing, 16.7% of companies in the USA make use of global outsourcing as compared to 12.8% of companies in Japan and 11.3% of companies in Finland. As expected, tests of the differences between the proportions show that the differences in global outsourcing levels are not signi® cant at the 0.05 level. ## Outsourced IS functions

As we see in Table 3, on domestic outsourcing levels for different IS functions, software development is more frequently outsourced in Japan (61.6%) and Finland (47.5%) than in the USA (33.3%). The same is true of domestic outsourcing of software maintenance function. Data centre operations (running of corporate computers) are more frequently outsourced domestically in Japan (44.2%) and Finland (32.4%) than in the USA (12.5%).

The companies in the US sample are larger and have a larger IS staff, in particular when compared to companies in the Finnish sample. The US sample also consisted of companies rated to be the most effective users of IS by the peer companies. It is highly likely that these companies develop and maintain unique IS applications in-house to attain a competitive edge. This may explain why companies in the USA prefer to develop and maintain systems in-house.

The companies in the Japanese sample are also large and have similarly unique IS and computer needs. However, one peculiar practice raises the outsourcing frequency in Japan. Due to the use of the Keiretsu system in Japan, subsidiary companies play an important role as outsourcing \`vendors’ in software development/ maintenance and in data centre operations. As reported by Hanaoka and Shimada (1995), in 46.3% of domestic outsourcing cases, the vendor was a subsidiary in the same Keiretsu. Managers and senior IS staff are assigned to these subsidiaries on a rotation basis and the subsidiaries practically act as divisions of the parent company. This is perhaps the reason why outsourcing percentages for these functions are so high in Japan. In Finland, the companies sampled are small in size and therefore may not be able to justify the cost of hiring of many IS specialists. This may explain the high rate of outsourcing in system development and maintenance in Finland. It is also observed that many Finnish companies rely on mini-computers and servers for their computing needs. Not having the need for dedicated main-frames, the Finnish companies tend to frequently outsource the data centre function.

In the domestic outsourcing data of Table 3, we observe that disaster recovery is infrequently outsourced in Japan (9.3%) as compared to the USA (39.6%) and Finland (30.5%). Given the size of the USA, it is highly unlikely that the same disaster (such as an earthquake or a ¯ ood) can affect all its areas at the same time. This factor has led the companies located, say, on the east coast of the USA to use the disaster recovery services of a vendor located in the west or the south (or vice versa). In contrast, the Japanese companies face a much higher risk from earthquake-related disasters, although even in Japan the likelihood of any single earthquake affecting the entire country is extremely remote. Faced with a much higher risk of an earthquake, large Japanese companies may routinely choose to absorb this risk in-house, since a vendor is in no better position to offer a more reliable disaster recovery capability than what the company can offer itself. For example, a large moneycentre Japanese Bank deals with this risk by locating one data centre in Tokyo and another one in Osaka. This factor may explain the low outsourcing of disaster recovery function in Japan. In Finland, the standard practice is to outsource data centre operation together with the disaster recovery function. This explains the high outsourcing percentages for disaster recovery function in Finland.

We also ® nd that training and education is more frequently outsourced domestically in the USA (47.9%) and Finland (37.6%) as compared to Japan (12.8%). Much is written about the lifetime employment and the attention paid by Japanese companies to the nurturing of their human resources through inhouse career development programmes. It is also customary for Japanese companies to develop applications to suit their unique needs as opposed to buying software packages from a vendor and customizing them further. This uniqueness of IT applications in Japanese companies makes it harder for outside vendors to offer training courses useful to the employees of Japanese companies. The training courses being offered by vendors, therefore, deal mainly with the operational aspects of hardware and the intricacies of standard operating systems and data communications software. The number of vendors offering training services is also small. Together, all these factors explain why the companies in Japan often choose to provide IS training in-house as opposed to through a vendor. In the USA there has been a large growth in the number of companies which specialize in computer training. The competitive nature and pricing structure of these services have made the outsourcing of training and education economically attractive for companies in the USA. The companies in Finland, on the other hand, are smaller and hence cannot justify the cost of offering in-house training courses. This perhaps is the reason for the higher percentage of outsourcing of training and education in Finland.

Finally, the data on the domestic outsourcing of IS functions shows that telephone support of customers function is more frequently outsourced in Finland (22%) than in the USA (10.4%) and Japan (10.5%). As mentioned earlier, it is customary in Finland to bundle several functions, including the telephone support function, together with the outsourcing of data centre operations. This explains the relatively higher percentage for outsourcing of telephone support function in Finland.

Commensurate with the differences in the overall levels of global and domestic outsourcing, the percentages of global outsourcing of individual functions are smaller than for domestic outsourcing of the same functions for all three countries. Under the framework proposed by Apte and Mason (1995b), programmingrelated activities of software development and maintenance are more suitable for global outsourcing than the other IS functions. They are ideal candidates for global outsourcing, since programming is characterized by high information intensity, low customer contact need and low physical presence need. In contrast, other activities such as data centre operations and data communications require a physical presence and, hence, these activities have a reduced potential for global outsourcing as indicated by the abovementioned framework.

The data on global outsourcing of individual IS functions mostly supports these conclusions. Among the IS functions globally outsourced, the development and maintenance of software are the most frequently outsourced functions, particularly in Japan and Finland. The incidence of global outsourcing of these functions in the USA, however, is much lower. The large size of the ® rms present in the US sample, as argued before, may explain why the relative percentages for global outsourcing of the same functions are low in the USA or perhaps it was simply due to the small number of ® rms who engaged in global outsourcing practice. However, as we noted earlier, on the whole, a higher percent of US companies outsource domestically and globally than in Japan or Finland.

## Desired cost savings to outsource

We can see in Table 4, that companies in the US and Japan, respectively, desire mean savings of 23.2 and 25.8% to outsource domestically. However, companies in Finland desire a mean saving of 30.7% to outsource IS functions domestically. This difference is found to be signi® cant at the 0.05 level using the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ test.

The culture in Finland does not favour as much risk taking as in the USA. The earlier disappointment with total outsourcing practice may also partially explain the desire for a higher saving in Finland. Furthermore, companies in Finland more often outsource high-risk functions such as software development and maintenance as opposed to the low-risk functions such as training and data entry functions which are more frequently outsourced in the USA. All these factors together may explain why the companies in Finland desire higher savings to compensate for the risks of outsourcing.

We also see in Table 4, that for global outsourcing, the level of desired saving is signi® cantly higher than that for domestic outsourcing. For example, the US companies desire mean cost savings of 30.9% to outsource globally as compared to the 23.2% cost savings desired to outsource domestically. It appears that companies in both the USA and Japan consider global outsourcing to be a more risky option than domestic outsourcing. This explains why the desired level of savings is higher for global outsourcing as compared to domestic outsourcing.

## Advantages and disadvantages of domestic outsourcing

The companies sampled were asked to rate, using a scale of 1 (unimportant) to 9 (very important), the advantages or disadvantages of domestic outsourcing on a speci® ed list. The median rating for all of the advantages and disadvantages for all three companies are reported in Table 5.

The data in Table 5 mostly con® rms what we know as the advantages and disadvantages of domestic outsourcing. Some interesting observations, however, stand out.

(1) Cost reduction was the most important advantage recognized by managers in all countries. An improved opportunity to focus on the strategic use of IS was considered the next most important advantage.

(2) The reduced need to hire IS professionals and improved cost predictability were considered signi® cant advantages in the USA and Finland but not Japan.

(3) Dif® culty in monitoring the performance of a vendor and in explaining the business needs and system speci® cations to a vendor were considered to be the most important disadvantages of outsourcing in all countries.

(4) Loss of control over quality and the timetable was considered a more important disadvantage in the USA but not in Japan or Finland.

(5) The potential for the violation of intellectual property rights and loss of company secrets was considered a more signi® cant risk in the USA and Japan than in Finland.

As mentioned earlier, the life time employment system in large companies is dominant in Japan, although in recent years the practice appears to have decreased. Hence, the use of outsourcing to deal ¯ exibly with human resources is not a real option or a consideration in Japan. This explains the low ranking given to the advantage of \`reduced need to hire IS professional’ in Japan. As discussed earlier, outsourcing contracts in Japan are often given to subsidiaries in the same family of companies. Hence, the concern for loss of control over quality and the timetable is also possibly much less in Japan.

Concerning the disadvantage of an increased potential for the violation of intellectual property rights, the low ranking it received in Finland is possibly rooted again in its culture which is considered to be very open and trusting. Data security and the violation of intellectual property have never been perceived as major issues in Finland. However, as Finnish companies become more globalized in the future, this factor may receive more attention as it does in the USA and Japan.

## Overview, conclusions and future research potential

This exploratory empirical study comparing IS outsourcing practices in the USA, Japan and Finland has led to several important conclusions; some con® rming our initial understanding and others being counter-intuitive. The survey showed that, in spite of all the differences between the three countries, the extent of both domestic and global outsourcing is equally large and is far wider than what might be expected. The percentage of companies domestically outsourcing one or more IS functions ranged from 60.3% in Finland, to 73.3% in Japan and to as high as 77.1% in the USA. Global outsourcing is carried out at the smaller although signi® cant rates of 11.3% in Finland, 12.8% in Japan and 16.7% in the USA. As expected, the Keiretsu system has in¯ uenced outsourcing practices in important ways in Japan. For example, the partner in an outsourcing relationship in almost half of the domestic outsourcing cases in Japan was another subsidiary in the same family of companies. In Finland, on the other hand, the phenomenon of total outsourcing, which was common in the mid-1980s, has shaped the outsourcing practices in that country towards more selective arrangements today.

Although the extent of outsourcing is high in all three countries, striking differences are observed in the levels of outsourcing of individual IS functions. For example, software development and maintenance and data centre operations are outsourced in Japan and Finland at much higher rates than in the USA. On the other hand, training and education and disaster recovery functions are frequently outsourced in the USA while these functions are mostly insourced in Japan. The practice in Finland in this regard is somewhere in between. The principal causes of these differences are conjectured to be the unique information needs of large companies in the USA, the practice of the Keiretsu system and lifetime employment in Japan, the unique history of outsourcing in Finland and the availability of a large number of vendors devoted to computer education and training in the USA.

The study provided interesting and useful information on the desired level of cost saving at which companies are willing to outsource. Companies in both the USA and Japan desire mean cost savings of approximately 23.2 and 25.8%, respectively, for domestic outsourcing and 30.9 and 30.5%, respectively, for global outsourcing. The companies in Finland desire a mean saving of approximately 30% to outsource either domestically or globally. These country differences are possibly explained by the risk-averse culture and different mix of outsourced functions in Finland. The desired cost saving information can also be interpreted to estimate the ultimate potential of the outsourcing phenomenon. Assuming that a desired level of saving is available to each company in the USA, approximately 90% of companies will be willing to outsource domestically and approximately 67% of companies will be willing to outsource globally. This portends, subject to other caveats, a tremendous opportunity for growth in the global outsourcing of many IS functions in the future.

Companies in all three countries considered the signi® cant potential for cost reduction and the opportunity to focus on the strategic use of IS as the most important advantages of domestic outsourcing. The most important disadvantages are the dif® culties in monitoring the performance and in explaining the business needs and speci® cations to an outsourcing vendor. The reduced need to hire IS professionals was considered a signi® cant advantage in the USA and Finland, but it was not considered as important in Japan. The potential for intellectual property rights violation was considered an important risk in the USA and Japan, but was not given as much importance in Finland due possibly to its open and trusting culture.

For global outsourcing, improved access to a much larger pool of highly skilled professionals and reduced cycle time were considered to be the most important advantages, while dif® culties in verbal and data communications were rated to be the most critical disadvantage and the US companies rated the uncertainty surrounding governmental rules and regulations as the most important obstacle to global outsourcing.

Contrary to the commonly held belief that CEOs initiate and drive outsourcing decisions, IS executives are observed to play a dominant role as initiators and decision makers. They are responsible for more than 80% of outsourcing decisions in both the USA and Japan. Even in Finland, where CEOs still play a dominant role, IS executives are assuming increasing responsibility as the practice of total outsourcing is giving way to selective outsourcing of individual IS functions. This exploratory study has provided new and interesting empirical evidence on the similarities and differences in IS outsourcing practices in the USA, Japan and Finland.
