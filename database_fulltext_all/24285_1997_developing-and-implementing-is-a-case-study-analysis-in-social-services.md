# Developing and implementing IS: a case study analysis in social services

LESLEY RILEY

6 Arnold Villas, Rugby, CV21 3AX, UK

GILL SMITH

30 Sycamore Rise, Bracknell, Berks RG12 9BU

Many organizations fail to implement or reap the benefits of information systems (IS) projects. The social care sector is not yet a great user of information technology (IT) and the thinking about IS is generally less well-developed than, for instance, in the health care sector. Research in health informatics indicates that there are special problems in creating integrated solutions and achieving the uptake of systems. This project was therefore undertaken with the benefit of experience of previous projects in health and social services and in the expectation that attention to participative design and people factors would be the key determinants of success. The paper describes the outcomes and draws conclusions about the special characteristics of IS projects in social services.

## Introduction

The literature on human-centred computer design and change management points to the problems associated with introducing computer systems, such as developing appropriate mental models, creating buy in and sustaining commitment to new methods of working. These problems are particularly evident in social care environments such as community health, education and social services, where there is less enthusiasm for computers and less opportunity for ‘quick win’ automation projects than in many commercial sectors. However, the issues encountered in the implementation of a comprehensive social services package went beyond the problems of introducing computers per se and touched on fundamental questions of social work practice and how technology can be assimilated into an environment where professional autonomy is jealously guarded.

The first part to this paper seeks to explain the nature of the project and the research themes which were to be evaluated. We then examine what happened and analyse the findings. Finally, the paper attempts to draw conclusions, building on the research themes, for how to achieve the ‘institutionalization’ of the system.

The authors were involved with the project from the outset: one in the role of business lead/project manager and the other as an outside consultant. The project brief was concerned with the implementation of an information systems (IS) strategy and, hence, the issue of ensuring the system made a difference to the way in which the organization performed was always paramount. This provided an opportunity to manage the project in such a way that project staff, end users and sponsoring managers could be interviewed at intervals about the perceptions of the project. Other material was obtained from meeting minutes and project reviews and from the recorded experiences of key events in the project. The timescales are shown in Table 1. The key part of the project on which this paper is based took place between May 1994 and April 1996.

## The social services project

## Background

Social services play an important part in the Welfare State in the UK. The principles laid down in 1948 by Beveridge are still largely intact in that all citizens have access to health care which is free at the point of need, there is a state pension and benefits system and social services fulfil statutory duties to protect the vulnerable (under a variety of legislation concerning children, the disabled, those with mental health problems, etc.). However, the spirit of the Beveridge (1948) reforms is less well preserved. For instance, many turn to private health to avoid the waiting lists and depressing conditions in many parts of the National Health Service. Eligibility for state benefits is increasingly complex and new schemes have been introduced which have effectively eroded the state's role as a safety net (e.g. the removal of benefits from 16–18 year olds and curtailing of unemployment benefit under the Job Seekers Allowance). Social services has also seen its role changed in recent years as funding is progressively reduced. It is now only able to help those in greatest need and is rarely able to provide the preventative and community services which were an important part of social work in the 1970s when social services departments were first set up under Seebohm.

The work from which this study arose consisted of implementing a software package to support social services in Borsetshire. The pseudonym has been adopted since any frank exchange about how social services deploys its funds is likely to attract unwelcome political attention.

The logistics of the project were daunting, as Borsetshire had 2000 staff working in 34 locations and needed to replace their existing system in a short time frame (3 months) to save on further leasing costs. It was accepted that they would then spend 18 months consolidating the initial implementation and introducing new functionality which would provide the real benefits of the project, by transforming methods of working.

This gave it the capability to track everything happening to a client from one place and to analyse the performance of the various aspects of the service, as well as control budgets.

Experience of being involved in a number of procurement exercises with social services suggests that authorities apply certain criteria to the purchase of computer systems.

In evaluating usage impacts, an organization would have to ask the following.

(1) Are we intending to use this system to change methods of working?

(2) How would we envisage it working and what benefits would we expect?

(3) How will the people affected react? What do we need to do to manage the change?

Borsetshire was moving from a simple computer system, predominantly used by clerical staff to record 'after the event', to a more sophisticated product which required practitioners to interact with the system as an integral part of their work. A comparison was made between the product characteristics and usage impacts of simple and sophisticated computer systems (Table 2).

The comparison highlights certain risks in introducing sophisticated systems.

(1) The stakes are high (high investment and potential benefits).

(2) The systems may well be seen as intrusive by professionals – permeating the way they work, demanding cognitive and manual skills they may feel unwilling or unable to acquire.

(3) The organization may be ‘betting’ a large part of its operation (scope and functionality) on new methods of working, without due cognizance of the impacts (possible resistance and increased dependence on systems).

With these risks in mind, Borsetshire appointed an assistant director to manage the information strategy, including the implementation of the new computer package. She had the practitioner background, the management overview and the authority to command cooperation from all parties.

Borsetshire also agreed to a participative approach to the implementation (see Figure 3) to maximize learning opportunities and focus on delivering the concept of a practitioner tool.

The key points about this approach were as follows.

(1) The launch involved roadshows for all staff, explaining the broad nature of the information strategy and its likely impact on working methods (rather than just talking about computers) and introducing the idea of managing change.

(2) The team had practitioners seconded to look specifically at how the processes and practice could be optimized with the use of the new system tool.

(3) As each new area of functionality was addressed, staff were involved to review the system component, agree tailoring and align practice.

## Research themes

## Organizational learning

Participative approaches to introducing technology are not new. Revans (1982) worked from the 1940s through to the 1970s developing his ideas on the learning cycle of organizations and the value of action research. For instance in 'Worker Participation as Action Learning' (item 41) he described management challenges for the 1970s: 'The qualities to be called for will be temperamental and emotive rather than intellectual and cognitive; they must be involving rather than detached, questioning the self no less than quantifying the situation'. Similar ideas were being taken forward by the 'learning organization' school of researchers, such as Bateson (1973), Gardner (1963), Lippitt (1969), Lewin (1947), Schon and Argyris (1978) and Argyris (1991).

A key aspect of approaching new and challenging situations is ‘double-loop learning’, in which the participants solve a problem but also gain insights into the nature of the problem and how to prepare for future instances. Pedlar et al. quoted an example (1991):

## Developing and implementing IS

The Director of a large Social Services Department in a Midlands town said that after three reorganisations in the last ten years, she had learned that you had to do two things:

'You must give clear direction and then keep up unrelenting pressure for change from the top and you must provide resources, facilities and time to support people whose jobs and lives are affected by the changes. Everyone deserves help and time to work through the individual implications and consequences of organisational change'.

Now there's a useful bit of single-loop learning – next time, she'll do it better . . . (but) could we say the Social Services department has an increased capacity to learn, adapt and transform itself as a result of its experience?

Schein (1987) developed the theme of intervention as a facilitation mode designed to help people understand their own problems and find their own answers. Weisbord (1987) added a moral dimension:

Dignity, meaning, and community in the workplace are for me the anchor points for economic success in democratic societies. . . . Quality of worklife . . . can be seen as a serious effort to conserve our cultures deepest values against erosion by narrow economic and technocratic thinking.

## Sociotechnical approaches

Winograd and Flores (1986) described the problems of programming rationalistic instructions based on the technician's reductive view of a given user situation: 'Computers have a particularly powerful impact, because they are machines for acting in language, in using them, we engage in a discourse generated within the distinctions set down by their programmers'. The book points to the disturbing evidence that patients 'conversing' with ELIZA (an early attempt at using artificial intelligence to collect information for medical diagnosis) believed the machine was making sense of what they said, where it was only responding with stock phrases cued from particular language constructs that it detected from the patient.

The importance of approaching language and meaning with respect when creating computer systems takes on a new emphasis when we look at prototyping as a means of developing the system in the context of the workplace, Greenbaum and Kyng (1991) described how an exercise to computerize administrative records in an office elicited the following questions:

(1) What does it mean to have the lists of incoming and outgoing mail computerised?

(2) What information needs to be entered and by whom?

(3) Which lists should be available to which caseworker?

(4) How should the lists be structured?

(5) Who 'owns' the lists?

Zuboff (1988) described numerous instances of the unintended effects of ‘automating’ tasks in the workplace (such as deskilling, alienating people by separating them from the ‘hands-on’ approach that created their job skills and understanding). She introduced the idea of an informed workplace as ‘an arena through which information circulates, information to which intellective effort is applied. The quality, rather than the quantity, of effort will be the source from which added value is derived’ (Zuboff, 1988). This model has the possibility of overturning conventional hierarchies and empowering people to act, but it demands a new skill of whole system thinking. ‘If the informing process is to be exploited for its competitive opportunities, then widely distributed mastery over the intellectual grounds of knowledge becomes an acute demand. For this to occur, organizational innovation is required’ (Zuboff, 1988).

Gerstein (1987) looked at the advantages of computerization. For instance, professional groups can have tools which increase their personal productivity, leverage the intellectual capital they generate and enable them to do things which were previously impossible.

## Sustaining commitment to results

Investment in computer systems creates dilemmas for social care organizations. It is money that is taken away from service users, for purposes which are difficult to articulate, in the hope that machines can help to make sense of the vast array of events, transactions, costs and outcomes which characterize large service organizations. Computers are not a panacea, so it is important to try and articulate the purposes of introducing systems, Strassman (1985) talked of distinguishing between the value added in operations and management:

The justification of equipment as a timesaving device is valid only for people in operations. Management should acquire IT as a means of improving the effectiveness of the entire management process, such as by making it possible to change organisational design, IT itself may not save any time: it may actually require more time to arrive at a small number of really important decisions.

He went on to point out that most organizations appear reluctant to take these messages on board and continue to treat information technology (IT) investment as if it were a commodity for which one haggles on price and demands instant (usually time-saving) returns.

These warnings were summed up by Walton (1989): 'IT systems usually fall short of their potential – and the fault invariably lies in the failure to understand and manage the mutual influences of technology and organisation throughout the extended implementation process'. In particular, there is a need to align expectations of the system with operational needs, build user commitment and foster 'mastery' (competence in using technology for the operational task in hand).

## History of technology in social care

Social care organizations came to the point of using IT fairly late in the era of the 'white heat of technology', with client indexes (and the equivalent Patient Administration Systems (PAS) in hospitals) appearing in the 1970s, a boom in 'stand alone' specialist applications for particular services in the 1980s (including child protection systems, radiology, etc.) and a gradual move from the late 1980s into more comprehensive systems which attempted to integrate applications and show something of the transactions, decisions and costing consequences of the organization's total service portfolio. For social services, the prompting to make more comprehensive use of technology was in large part due to the recommendations of the Griffiths report on community care and the government sanctioning of supplementary credit approval (SCA) loans for buying hardware (which came to an end in 1996).

Most social services departments now have a system, but many are only now tackling the migration from first generation client indexes run on mainframes, whilst consolidating and replacing additional homegrown or packaged personal computer (PC) applications. Community health is also in a transition period as the new community trusts wrestle to adapt inherited systems from the acute sector or acquire community-based systems which still have a strong medical bias and are weaker on holistic support for community patients. In education, systems are dominated by the administration needs of schools and the logistics of local education authority (LEA) planning and admissions. There are few client-centred systems.

A report by the European Network for Information Technology in the Human Services (Steyaert et al.) brought together the findings from 17 countries. The general conclusion was that there were substantial ‘drag factors’ to the effective take-up of technology in human services: not only does it take investment in infrastructure, education in computer literacy and agreement on coding, etc., but it depends on establishing relationships between agencies and imposing ‘greater control and accountability on previously autonomous individuals’.

The history of systems in health has incited much analysis. For instance, Willcocks and Mark (1989) described a system called PROMIS (Problemoriented Medical Information System) which even in the 1970s managed administration functions and also provided a full clinical record to nurses (who could check on treatment), pharmacists (who could review a patient's medical history and check the appropriateness of prescriptions) and radiologists (who could get involved in the diagnosis). The physicians did not like it and the Department of Health eventually rejected it as too expensive. Was it because it threatened the doctors' power base? Arguably we are still trying to find patient-centred systems that adopt a team approach to health care in the 1990s and still finding the same opposition. Keen (1984) made the point that a system is only as good as its acceptance by the people who use it: 'Implementation is not just installation of a technical system in an organisation, but the institutionalisation of its use.... Installation does not guarantee institutionalisation'.

Based on extensive work in the public sector, Willcocks (1990) pointed to the need for analysing the organisation's power base and anticipating resistance where an IT project threatens vested interests: 'Resistance should not be seen merely as a problem to be solved so that the original system can be installed as intended. Resistance can be used more positively (as) ... a clue to what is going wrong and what can be done about it. The narrow determination to see a certain system's design up and running will inhibit useful analysis of resistance'.

Other modules were provided according to business priorities and an attempt was also made to balance the cross the board functionality (which supported a common care cycle in both adult and children's services) with requirements that were particular to each service. Home care (part of the service delivery process) was included because of the importance to adult services, being the biggest provider unit and central to creating a detailed view of the spending relating to the special transitional grant.

Table 1 highlights certain pressure points in the project.

(1) The initial cross-over from the old system involved transferring the data and training 400 staff in a 3 month period. Although considerable effort was made to 'clean up' the data in the old system this was not entirely successful. Pressure from staff who had 'ownership' of the old system led to the decision being made to transfer data which probably should have been scrapped. The consequence of this decision was to have repercussions over the whole period of implementation.

(2) The ‘holding action’ for the next 5 months, during which there was no new functionality but intensive support for the 400 staff ‘thrown in at the deep end’ and concurrent activity to prepare for the introduction of care management and the possibility of using the inspection and registration module in the ‘arm’s length’ inspection unit.

(3) A further 6 months of dual activity – extending the training and dealing with the emerging challenge of an event-driven practitioner system, whilst trying to bring children's services 'on board'. At the same time, work was beginning on negotiating how the system would fit with the organization's plans to acquire a new corporate finance system.

(4) A complicating factor at this time was that inspection and registration decided they would not implement their module: the main project was too stretched to respond.

(5) The final stretch of phase 2 in which the deferred decision on corporate finance meant that the project plan had to be revised and substantial development effort put in to support financial management from within the system. This also involved changes to other parts of the system and extensive training for care managers in how to use commitment accounting.

(6) An additional stress, characterized by tension between the technical IT unit and the user-led project, resulted in the authority instigating a broad review of project conduct and results in late 1995.

The results can be measured in terms of increased functionality, extended user base and impact on the methods of working. The amount of functionality which was taken on across all operational teams and units over the 2 years was impressive. The previous systems provided a basic client index and was supplemented by a number of in-house systems, none of which related to each other or provided opportunities for 'case recording' (the social work task of documenting client needs, planned interventions, the services provided and outcomes). The previous user base was approximately 15 and almost all of the operators were clerks. The concurrence rate was negligible.

The user base for the new system has reached over 800 and the concurrence rate has reached 114. It enables a much wider view of 'what goes on' in the department, covering the care cycle, care packaging and costing and provider functions. What is more, this view is built up dynamically as events occur, instead of waiting until pieces of paper arrive at in-trays. The move from passive recording systems to active practitioner tools is one of the biggest challenges of the project. The patterns of usage vary widely between teams, but it is worth noting that committed teams have streamlined their methods of working, reduced their paperwork and achieved significant improvements in the consistency of practice and the accuracy of recording. It is now possible for these team leaders to assess bombardment rates, deploy resources to meet anticipated demand, review workloads and examine outcomes of care planning decisions.

The findings of the project confirmed the sort of obstacles arising from introducing sophisticated computer systems. In particular, the desired changes in working practice were very difficult to bring about. The overriding impression was that in relation to the planned approach (see Figure 3), the technical tasks absorbed much of the team's time and attention (and could not be ignored since they resulted in the tangible implementation), but the challenge of 'institutionalizing' the system depended on the 'organization preparation' tasks – particularly process mapping, practice alignment and training. It is worth noting that the big bang approach to crossing over from the old system (a business decision, based on savings of leasing costs, but with substantial technical implications) probably contributed to many of the 'people problems' that occurred later. In particular, it may have sensitized the central IT unit and made them feel their advice was being ignored, thus contributing to the complaints about the conduct of the project which led to the 'uncomfortable' review in 1995.

In assessing the success of the project, we reviewed the objectives originally set for the information strategy (of which the system was a part) in 1993. These concerned the following.

(1) The need for efficient recording, collation, presentation and interpretation of data.

(2) The need for comprehensive case recording.

(3) The importance of informing people about policy, strategy and good practice through provision of clear procedures.

(4) The recognition that organizational ‘intelligence’ consists of a combination of hard data and softer information, accumulated (and shared) through experience.

Against these criteria, the project was successful in raising the profile of data management and case recording and the increase in the user base (from 15 to over 800) was phenomenal. As noted earlier, some teams have also benefitted from the emphasis on a process-driven method of working to streamline their procedures. However, productive utilization of the data (such as would create true organizational intelligence) remains elusive.

## Developing and implementing IS

A stakeholder analysis of the success of the project is represented in Table 4. It is noteworthy that the project was part of the wider remit of delivering an information strategy, which had specific goals to which only some stakeholders make reference.

It can be seen that the stakeholders were divided into three groups.

(1) Those who were involved in the project, appreciated the challenge and were largely satisfied that they achieved something innovative and valuable (the project leader and technical and practitioner members of the team).

(2) Those who felt outside the team, had doubts about the project from the outset and declared that the basis of the project was unsound, the conduct unconventional, the exposure too great, but did not generally dispute that the results were good (for example, trainers, social services finance and central IT).

(3) The users, who varied in their response: each set (clerks, team managers and practitioners) had initial hopes or fears, but those who believed in the possibility of systems as a tool for good practice and client benefit generally 'made it work', whilst others found reasons to object or avoid involvement.

## Intractable problems

It is worth describing some of the aspects of the project which did not go according to plan and which make good learning points. The following subsections include quotes from the 1995 project review.

## Confidentiality

## Training

The intention was to integrate technical training and practice, so that staff would learn how the system would be able to change working practices. However, there was insufficient practitioner resource for more than ‘guest appearances’ on the training sessions (which were therefore largely technical). The project was also largely ignored by other management initiatives: ‘For a considerable length of time training has continued in other areas of work with barely reference to the project let alone full integration’.

## Project management

A deliberate decision was made to set and achieve ‘milestones’, leaving a degree of latitude about how tasks and problems were approached. This has contributed to the anxieties of some stakeholders who prefer a more regulated regime:

This approach was successful in that there was management buy-in for the plan, and the project has achieved the broad thrust of what it set out to do. However, the down side of milestone planning is that it subordinates the tasks to the goal, and may underplay some important risks or areas of uncertainty.... It necessitated pragmatic decisions where goals were in jeopardy, and documentation and formality were sometimes sacrificed to move the obstacles faster.

## Practice issues

The project attempted to map the system to processes as it progressed, but was hindered by debate between teams who all worked differently. Where the project attempted to rule on methods of working, it was open to accusations of 'technology driving practice'. However, replication of the existing complex, variable processes or the multi-form procedures being used with countless variations would not have achieved quality improvement and certainly would not have allowed for eventual cost reduction or consistency.

## Management support

Despite every attempt to involve senior management in the project and emphasize their role in setting the strategy, defining information requirements and leading change, they were still generally ‘bystanders’:

It is difficult to make comment on the results of all this activity. There were real problems with ‘follow through’ of any of these initiatives. It was a very difficult time for the department with all the other issues surrounding the introduction of Community Care.

## Exposing business problems

In mapping the system to the way people worked, it became apparent that the introduction of community care was creating pressures, particularly in terms of the volume of invoices from spot purchasing. The purchasing strategy was unclear, audit were unsure about the required controls and the split of responsibilities between purchasers, commissioning officers, clerical staff and finance was causing duplication of work. However, there was not recognition that the project had highlighted a real business issue or that management attention should be immediately applied:

As it is, arrangements with consumers and suppliers vary from Team to Team. It is extremely difficult to systemise this activity and the number of ‘exceptions’ proliferate. The impression gained is that Team Managers have been very much left ‘to get on with it’ and not surprisingly they have taken various approaches.

## Differences between adult and children's services

Despite a determination not to let the department divide and adopt a two-tier approach to implementation, it was very difficult to keep the children's division 'on board'. This problem has occurred in many other authorities, regardless of what system they were trying to implement. It appears the children's social workers see themselves and their work as 'different':

A considerable amount of work with Children Teams during 1994 did not generally result in a great improvement in system uptake. It was hard to find ‘product champions’ in Children’s services in the way they were beginning to emerge for Adult services.

## Social work principles

It has been difficult to get social workers to accept that accurate recording on a system can help to ensure that client issues are known and addressed, that resources are appropriately deployed and that aggregate data is available for planning services:

We have experienced and continue to experience serious difficulties in relation to quality of data. This is not a system problem but arises from an historical indifference to data precision. Social work has been seen and to some extent still is seen as a creative process which is most importantly the interaction between the worker and the consumer.

## An independent analysis

Towards the end of the 2 year project, it was independently reviewed through staff interviews carried out by Vickers (1996). Certain themes emerged. One was that insufficient training and hardware contributed to workers' resistance to the system, whereas a pilot site which had more resources had not experienced the problem.

There were not sufficient terminals for one each and team managers had to ensure equitable access. Sharing was difficult given the design of buildings and was exacerbated by other demands (e.g. if clerical staff never had sufficient access to word processing). The role of the team manager was also critical in determining whether the system was used productively. There were polarized views, with some participants ‘absolutely refusing’ to make any changes to their practice or style of work because they considered it affected their professional autonomy, whilst others welcomed the system because it ‘professionalized’ their work.

The majority of people participating in the research expressed concern about confidentiality. There were additional concerns about sensitive information sharing. However, nine out of 11 of the participants interviewed felt that the computerized system was potentially a ‘better’ system than any of the previous systems, partly because it is an integrated system but also because it provides a consistent structure.

The review by Vickers (1996) concluded that there is resistance to the introduction of new technology in relation to social work recording. The pattern identified is feelings of no choice, technology leading practice, no worker involvement, no benefit from training, not enough hardware and inconsistent use by individuals and teams. All of this has the potential to turn workers away from technology, returning to paper records and gradually eroding the progress made.

## The research themes revisited

In this section, we look at the relevance of the research themes to our findings on the project.

## Organizational learning

The members of the project team were prepared to 'step into the unknown' and displayed a willingness to increase their ‘capacity to learn, adapt and transform ... as a result of experience’ (double-loop learning). Outside the project the results were variable and this is perhaps predictable. The project team had set up for themselves what was essentially a learning environment – they were entirely focused upon the implementation. Users learning to use the system were working in an environment where they were constantly bombarded by demands for change. Many were seeing their roles transformed almost beyond recognition as the shift began from the traditional social work service to care management and the delivery of resources.

## Sociotechnical approaches

The project team members described going through a process of discovering that the new system was not ‘set in concrete’ but provided a set of tools that could be moulded to the organization’s purpose. What appeared a reductive interpretation of social work actually allowed considerable flexibility within the framework of ‘concepts’. It was necessary to understand the mental model behind the concepts (e.g. the assumptions behind workload management). Language was important (e.g. distinguishing between allocating case accountability for a client and assigning a piece of work).

The process mapping workshops elicited questions about ‘what we do and why’ which are unfamiliar in a procedurally oriented organization. The challenge of ‘informating the organization’ and developing the analytical skills of reading and acting on new sources of information which the system makes available requires time and training. Additional training was provided for team managers in how to frame the new sorts of questions that the system could illuminate and how to be innovative and approach the system with a new ‘spirit of enquiry’.

## Sustaining commitment to results

Strassman (1985) came to mind on several occasions during the project: 'What are the reasons for an attitude that lacks both short- and long-term realism? It is true that management look for quick remedies to redeem exaggerated promises. It is also true that there is a strong institutional bias against disturbing established relationships. This discourages unconventional thought about the long-term effects of IT'.

One of the most frustrating elements of the project was that there was so much to gain and yet so many obstacles in the short-term. The stakeholder analysis (Table 4) explains some of the conflicting pressures. Would it have helped if the goals were qualified by specific measures. For instance, standards to reach for case recording, targets to achieve for streamlining the client entry process and questions for management to seek to answer in relation to resource management. Being able to demonstrate new or improved capabilities in these areas might have been an additional incentive to 'stick with the vision'.

## History of technology in social care

The experience of the project confirmed the impression that social services is a difficult environment for introducing practitioner systems, because of the required investment in infrastructure and training and the resistance against ‘imposing greater control and accountability on previously autonomous individuals’. Every attempt was made to see resistance as ‘a clue to what was wrong’ and apply an understanding of power bases and the political forces at work within an organization. The impression remains, however, that it is an anomaly for ‘caring organizations’ to be so intractable in the face of introducing technology which would appear to benefit its consumers.

## A deeper analysis: learning from the project

If we review the three dimensions within which we analysed the project the findings, in relation to the identified characteristic of sophisticated computer systems (see Table 2 and Figure 4), the judgement of the various stakeholders about the success of the project and the relevance of the research themes in explaining the observed phenomena, then the most salient factor appears to be that if practitioners enthusiastically embrace a system, it is less likely that other obstacles (e.g. funding for infrastructure, system performance problems, the reservations of other stakeholders, skills shortages, practice issues, the need to demonstrate results, etc.) will become a critical factor. To put it another way, unrest amongst the user population will render a project vulnerable to a second-order criticism about the feasibility, the management style, the value for money and so on.

However, this explanation – ‘if people want the system, it is more likely to be a success’ – merely poses another question. What affects the degree of support given to those (IS) projects which, by their nature, involve transforming some aspect of the organization?

## Deriving a new model to explain resistance to IS-facilitated change

We looked again at the learning points derived from the project and characterized each in terms of a reaction against something or a refusal to tackle something:

(1) Confidentiality: there was disquiet from foster parents and staff who are relatives of clients – reaction against opening up the ‘private’ relationship between a social worker and their client.

(2) Training: there needed to be closer liaison between trainers and project staff (who knew the desired culture and practice which needed to be embodied in how the system was used) – reaction against redefining the training role.

(3) Project management: there was disquiet about the informal approach – reaction against redefining the nature of control and introducing a more collaborative approach.

(4) Practice issues: work on organizational design (e.g. agreeing consistent practice across teams) should have happened earlier; it was too much to handle at the same time as implementation – refusal to tackle inconsistent practice.

(5) Management support: despite having an IS strategy steering group and spending time briefing senior managers, it is doubtful whether they were really ‘up to speed’ – refusal to engage challenges of major change.

(6) Exposing business problems: the volume of invoices being processed as a response to the commitment to mixed provision was paralysing the social work teams, who had been very much left 'to get on with it'. There was ambivalence within the department about the notion of business planning and its applicability to social care organizations – refusal to tackle business issues.

(7) Differences between adult and children's services: children's teams have not taken advantage of what appears to offer a partial answer to some of the findings of enquiries, that recording practices and the inability to link information is often a crucial factor when things go wrong – reaction against ‘demystifying’ child care practice.

(8) Social work principles: social workers do not accept that case recording is a core business activity of social services – reaction against assuming ‘managerial responsibilities’.

How are these ‘reactions and refusals’ related? They can be arranged in a matrix (Table 5) which suggests four types of defensive behaviour. Two (professional dilemma and behavioural defence) are about what an individual thinks they may lose if a change goes ahead and typically involve a ‘reaction against’ something. The other two (political distaste and boundary defence) are about the lack of gain (in political terms) perceived in a course of action and typically involve a ‘refusal to tackle’ something.

## Moving ahead - researching the model

At first sight, the model merely elaborates on the well-known theme of resistance to change. However, we believe reactions and refusals represent two quite different forms of behaviour and that the ‘arena of threat’ in each case (professional values, roles, political will and territory) involves quite different mind sets.

Having narrowed the focus of the study to these behavioural aspects of response to an organizational project, we returned to the literature to see what research could be applied.

## Research on professional dilemmas

The key theme under this category appeared to be case recording. It was evinced by a reluctance to ‘expose’ what had been recorded (confidentiality), to record comprehensively (differences between adults and children’s services) and to do so in a ‘business-like way’ (assuming managerial responsibility).

Prince (1996) provided an investigation into the central role of record keeping in social work. Her work confirms the finding that case recording is seen by social workers as boring, a chore and subsidiary to the main work of ‘interacting’ with the client. She suggested that ‘Records actually occupied a “hot seat” in the power relations between social workers, their managers and consultants, and functioned not only as an index of power but also as a bearer of meanings, code, resources and emotions’ (Prince, 1996). She went on to point out that social workers are increasingly required to communicate effectively with other agencies in the course of coordinating complex cases and ‘It is clear from enquiries into cases which have gone badly wrong that communication failures were largely to blame’ (Prince, 1996).

Keeping records has always been a difficult and contentious issue and the quality of recording has nearly always been unsatisfactory. The difficulty lies in two areas: ownership of the record and transparency of the intervention and decision making.

The Access to Personal Files Act (HMSO, 1987) presented opportunities for a partnership between clients and social workers in both the maintaining of records and possibly their ownership. In fact, the evidence seems to suggest that few people access their files and the sometimes bureaucratic requirements often discourage this. Social workers are under enormous pressure and, unlike colleagues in other arms of the public sector, are rarely thought to be doing a good job or representing society's interests. They are more likely to be the scapegoats who support and encourage fecklessness, lack of responsibility, reliance on the state and even criminality. Little wonder they become 'precious' about what they actually do.

Sophisticated technology makes social work transparent and allows easy access to record keeping, decision making and money spent. Practitioners may well resist the potential benefits a system could offer to them and in this they may well be aided by others: clerical staff who fear the loss of their jobs, finance personnel who may be less interested in individual expenditure than in broad bottom lines over which they have great controls and even some managers who may also perceive that their decision making has greater exposure.

Resistance to more explicit case recording can also be seen in non-IT projects. A report into the implementation of the new Look After Children (LAC) forms promoted by the Department of Health, suggests 'it was perceived as trying to reinforce or establish good practice in a bureaucratic manner and it was time-consuming. To some professionals, this was seen as an insult . . .' (Wilcox, 1996).

In making arguments to professionals, it is all too easy to get caught up in the tortuous debates about the professional know-how and lose the 'big picture' of why the professional has been entrusted with the work in the first place. Social workers are not here to develop professional practice but to help clients solve problems. So what works for the client? An example from a totally different workplace illustrates the potency of arguing from direct observations about the work. Lacity et al. (forthcoming) reported on a manager of an insurance company trying to persuade claims officers that their 'professional approach' of recording claims was counter-productive: 'It is more likely that the customer will paint an accurate picture of the claim on the telephone. If they are filling out a claim form, they can start dreaming up what they want to claim. The reason we want to do this is good customer service. We want to handle a claim promptly and swiftly. The second reason – if they do it on the phone, it is more accurate, easier to determine what is actual'.

This manager successfully argued against the way professionals did the job on the grounds that changing the practice would lead to a more direct relationship with the customer and better results. Presumably he could prove the point by showing that the size of claims became more realistic. Measures of 'better' are not so easy to find in social care.

This brings us to the second category of behavioural response: management refusal to tackle political issues.

## Research on political distaste

According to Walsh (1996), social work practice and management skills are not adequately integrated in social services. This often appears to be ‘excused’ on the grounds of the enormous changes since the NHS & Community Care Act or because of the strictures of central government funding. ‘Beliefs in the unmanageability of social service, or the uniqueness of the problems it has to deal with, as blockages to the development of managerial competence and problem-solving techniques when confronted by apparent contradictions’ (Walsh, 1996).

To ‘boldly go’ and take up the challenges of confronting unpopular change requires some thought and organization. There is a great deal of research in the IT industry about the problems of trying to change the technology of work (and, hence, the mind sets, relationships, environment and career prospects of workers). Beath (1991) described two roles that senior managers can play in introducing new systems:

(1) Project champion: managers who vigorously promote their personal vision for using IT, pushing the project over or around approval or implementation hurdles. They often risk their reputations in order to ensure the innovation's success.

(2) Project sponsor: managers who have the funds and authority to accomplish their goals.

Jarvenpaa et al. (1991) described two required behaviours for the success of IT:

(1) Senior management participation: activities or substantive personal interventions in the management of IT.

(2) Senior management involvement: the psychological state, reflecting the degree of importance placed on IT.

We can paraphrase this by saying that senior managers have to embrace new systems enthusiastically in what they say and what they do.

Not all senior managers embrace the difficult areas of change management with equal zeal. Argyris (1990) described how managers are wary of challenging the social norms of organizational life and rely on a paternal set of values he calls model 1 which encourages managers to be upbeat and avoid difficult issues.

(1) Craft messages that contain inconsistencies.

(2) Act as if the messages are not inconsistent.

(3) Make the ambiguity and inconsistency undiscussable.

(4) Make the undiscussability of the undiscussable also undiscussable.

Of course, staff are painfully aware of these undercurrents in organizational life. The authors are reminded of the issue of unmet need, which must be identified (according to the Department of Health), but not in such a way that would expose the department to the charge of not fulfilling identified needs (judicial review). Hence, staff in one department would refer cynically to the ‘unmet unmentionables’.

There are examples of this type of inauthentic behaviour and defensive routines in the implementation project. Managers would say ‘the new system is vital’ and forget to include it in a range of other initiatives which were ongoing in the department (e.g. management development and quality standards). Similarly, practitioners would say that managers did not understand the pressures they were under in implementing the system, but would be unwilling to explain how they knew this or how they would like managers to behave differently.

The collusion between management and staff, where both sides make broad unsubstantiated statements rather than explore potentially embarrassing or damaging issues, leads us to the third area of research.

## Behavioural defence

In the project, several groups of staff said 'the project is not doing this right – they do not understand'. The most dramatic instance was when the central IT department questioned whether sufficient 'professionalism' was available in running the project and triggered a project review which caused delays at a critical stage in the 2 year cycle. Other examples involved clerical staff questioning whether social workers could learn to use computer systems, finance staying ‘in their corner’ rather than coming out to help tackle the messy problem of supporting managers in controlling budgets and certain practitioners arguing that their particular service area was ‘different’ and not amenable to the system’s approach.

The common theme here is a fear of losing position, and seeing a cherished role downgraded in the stampede towards something 'out there' called progress. The authors are reminded of some recent work in a department which was considering empowering small groups of home care assistants to take more responsibility for scheduling their own work. The supervisor level (home care organizers) initially opposed this idea as 'it would get out of control'. The clerical staff pointed out that they would be unable to monitor holiday and sickness accurately if field staff arranged their own work. Then the organizers suggested a compromise: the empowered care assistants would have a 'senior' who spent time in the office, collecting instructions from the organizer and delivering paperwork to administration. This was a seductive solution which would have provided some autonomy for the home care assistants whilst preserving territory in the office, so nobody would be upset. The head of home case said 'no – I will not have any more office staff'.

The resistance to breaking established roles and pecking orders is often couched in terms of warnings about loss of control. Part of the problem is the expectation that any project will proceed in an orderly fashion, despite the fact that the plans were made at the outset without the benefit of the further knowledge accruing as the project proceeds. A similar fascination with orderliness can be seen where many social services committees insist that budgets are spent to the nearest halfpenny, despite evidence of the ‘disorderly’ pattern of demand through the year.

If mind sets are to change, there are a number of preparatory tasks. Many organizations produce documentation and give training but neglect to spell out the full set of resultant working practices, e.g. what remains the same, what actions at the periphery are affected and whose role changes. In a Northumbria University (1996) study, training was given 'but the problems were back at work where the groundwork was not done to receive the system.... The rewriting of procedures is an essential task to allow the new system to be fully integrated'. Another point is that such changes will tend to slip back if not monitored.

The nature of monitoring is important: the data quality achieved in the Borsetshire project was monitored by the project team, but the monitoring of conformance (policing) is not as effective as monitoring outcomes, which can become a source of pride and legitimization of the project. However, those outcomes have to be agreed from the outset. Interestingly, one of the reasons why this did not happen was that there was little interest from the department as a whole in agreeing those outcomes and the project team failed to fill this void.

The resistance of staff to changing working methods is more accentuated when the change crosses organizational boundaries. This brings us to the final area of research.

## Boundary defences

In the project, there was a reluctance by the children's teams, particularly those in the 'purchasing arm' of the department, to 'lose their identity' by taking on a system which they perceived as driven by community care. Other boundary issues occurred over the ownership of records, e.g. between social workers who traditionally hold the 'main record' and 'providers' in the department, for example the home care services or residential services, who manage day to day issues for the client.

Similar struggles are emerging where social services and health are trying to negotiate shared responsibilities. A Department of Health (1994) publication discussed the cultural differences between the two agencies (see Table 6).

Ackroyd et al. (1996) researching continuing care, found that despite extensive negotiations and apparent agreement, time-scale pressures to publish agreed eligibility criteria caused a souring of relations. The issues included the following.

(1) Whether clinical judgement should be the sole basis for deciding when to offer continuing care to a patient.

(2) Whether the notion of beds as belonging to a doctor or consultant was at odds with eligibility criteria and common discharge procedures.

(3) That GPs showed little interest in continuing care.

(4) That health authorities concentrated on in-patient discharge and only slowly realized there was a responsibility for people in the community.

These ‘territory’ issues take us into the realms of business process redesign (BPR), which advocates breaking down the functional ‘fiefdoms’ and ‘us and them’ mentalities which interrupt the meaningful flow of a process. Majchrzak et al. (1996) described the difficulties of breaking ‘functional mind sets’. They suggested that many projects ‘underestimate the actions required to transform the way employees behave and work with one another. They assume that simply changing the organisational structure from functional units into process-complete departments will cause people to shed their functional mind-sets and will forge them instantly into a team intent on achieving common goals’ (Majchrzak et al., 1996).

Divisional rivalries are relatively common in social services, arising in part from ‘turf wars’ at the management team level (e.g. over allocation of the budget) and in part from stand-offs between functional units. For instance, foster link workers may not communicate well with children’s social workers (even though they share responsibility for the successful placement of a child). Similarly, specialist teams providing input to the joint care of a client may not share information. Such polarizations will only exacerbate the difficulties inherent in trying to reframe methods of working.

## Conclusions

We can see that each of the defensive behaviours (professional dilemma, political distaste, behavioural defences and boundary defences) were present in the project and contributed to the uphill task of winning over practitioners, rationalizing processes and providing clear leadership on the way ahead.

It is apparent that groundwork was needed for the following.

(1) To educate managers as to the extent of cultural change that would be involved in introducing a practitioner system.

(2) To review the whole issue of case recording – the duty to record, the obligation to communicate and coordinate care and the ownership of records.

(3) To think through role changes (for clerks, finance and practitioners).

(4) To rewrite procedures comprehensively, providing a single point of reference on methods of working (perhaps backed up by a pilot project to test the theory in practice).

(5) To win support for running the project in whatever style best suited the challenges to hand.

(6) To have managers ‘walking the talk’ and using their skills in navigating the organization to address issues and promote confidence in the way ahead.

This study builds on the broad understanding which already exists about how to introduce technology in such a way that it is taken up and used productively to good effect. The findings emphasize the additional problems of introducing sophisticated systems and of addressing environments where there is a strong professional lobby which is somewhat resistant to technology, suspicious of management intentions and concerned with preserving its autonomy. The key success criteria are to manage the defensive phenomena associated with professional dilemmas, political distaste, behavioural defence and boundary defence.

In this context, the original research themes (organizational learning, sociotechnical approaches, sustaining commitment to results and the history of technology in social care organizations) provided apposite but largely background assistance. They were like 'hygiene factors' – if neglected, they could make a project fail, but in themselves they were insufficient to assure success. The findings of the project were found to be consistent with other research into the problems associated with innovative projects and analyses of cultural problems in social services.

There is a significant danger that innovative projects will be undermined by the (often unconscious) actions of people who react against or refuse to tackle something. A sure sign that these behaviours are endemic in an organization is when people relate how they have 'seen it all before'. Argyris (1990) described the tendency to collude with and cover up defensive

There appears to be a challenge here to social services to use their own expertise in one to one working to see and understand the nature of dysfunctional processes in their own organization, and to promote the managerial expertise to act upon the diagnosis.

## Biographical notes

Lesley Riley has worked in health and social services since 1961. She has practitioner experience in many service areas (e.g. residential care, day care, mental health services, fieldwork, hospital social work and inspection) and has served in various senior management roles before becoming assistant director in a county social services department, responsible for introducing new systems and practices to support community care. She now works as an independent consultant in the public sector.

Gill Smith is a consultant working in the social care sector. She has a background in IT but has developed a particular interest in strategy, process innovation and human factors. Gill has co-authored chapters in two business books (Re-discovering Public Sector Management and Business Process Change and produces a quarterly bulletin Management Issues in Social Care. She is currently working on ways in which organizations can effectively hold the mirror up to what they do and liberate themselves from past constraints.
