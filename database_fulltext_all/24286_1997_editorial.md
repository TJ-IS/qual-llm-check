## This issue

There are, in this issue, two papers on client/server computing. In the ® rst Mary Lacity and Ashok Subramanian investigate how three client/server implementations were managed. The paper is an important empirical counter to the heavy selling of client/server as a new paradigm of computing. It ® nds, in all three companies, that client/server has indeed represented a radical change for the better in terms of technology, but that, in terms of its management challenges, practitioners need not discard the lessons from the past. The pertinent lessons are discussed in some detail.

In the second paper Graham Low and Richard Looi focus on client/server application development methodologies. The authors ® rst contribute a description of client/server computing and its bene® ts. They then use structured interviews and questionnaires to investigate ten organizations’ use of development methodologies for C/S, and the emerging issues. They ® nd an interesting diversity of approach, with some organizations using their normal development methodologies, others tailored methodologies, while others rely upon the experience of their project development teams rather than on formal methodologies. The authors provide details on how these different approaches work out in practice as well as the dif® culties faced.

Our third paper is by Pelly Periasamy and David Feeny. It is concerned with Information Architecture practice ± an issue that continues to maintain its position amongst the top issues registered in IS management surveys. The authors make several distinctive contributions. A strength of the paper is its clear delineations between Information Architecture, Corporate Data Model and Application Architecture. Secondly, the authors report ® ndings from both a large-scale postal survey and six detailed case studies. Thirdly, they provide a range of ® ndings from which they derive a number of recommendations for future practice. The paper is an important contribution to a neglected ® eld of study.

Julika Siemer and Marios Angelides focus on integrating an intelligent tutoring facility into a gaming simulation environment. Essentially the paper argues that the pedagogical effectiveness of gaming simulations can be increased by utilizing an intelligent tutoring facility. The bulk of the paper is taken up by a description of how an intelligent tutoring facility can be used in a business simulation game called Metal Box Company.

Our ® nal paper in this issue covers much ground in two areas of high practitioner interest, namely the characteristics of external IT service vendors and how to select an effective vendor. This has been strangely neglected in academic study, and once again, as in Information Architecture practice, raises questions about the degree to which IS academic studies are, and should be, leaders or laggards in their relationship to IS practice. Vaughan Michell and Guy Fitzgerald provide a very pertinent analysis of types of vendors, their strategic positionings and the dynamics of the vendor market. They then provide detail on the client perspective and reveal interesting discrepancies between what vendors, on the one hand, and clients, on the other, regard as important in the vendor selection process. A related review of a new text on outsourcing appears in the Book Review section of this issue of the Journal.
