# The IT outsourcing market-place: vendors and their selection

VAUGHAN MICHELL

Templeton College, University of Oxford, Oxford, OX1 5NY, UK

GUY FITZGERALD

Department of Computer Science, Birkbeck College, University of London, London WC1E 7HX, UK

This paper focuses on outsourcing vendors, their characteristics and the vendor selection process. It draws on current research and two research studies, one specifically examining outsourcing vendors and the other examining vendor-client issues. We first outline the development of the market for the outsourcing of information technology/information systems services and activities, then detail the characteristics of different types of vendor companies and their competitive positions, before providing a client perspective to the issue of vendor selection.

## Introduction: development of the vendor market-place

To understand the position and approach of vendors and clients in the outsourcing market we first need to define what we understand by outsourcing of information technology (IT) or information systems (IS) and services. Many definitions of outsourcing have been proposed (see CCTA, 1993; Lacity and Hirschheim, 1993a,b; Willcocks and Fitzgerald, 1993). For our purposes the Computing Services Association's (CSA's) (1993) definition of IT outsourcing describes its extent: 'Outsourcing is the delegation to a third party of the continuous management responsibility for the provision of an IT service under a contract that includes a service level agreement'.

The third party in the above definition is the service provider or vendor that is the subject of the discussions within this paper. The discussion will be based mainly on the UK outsourcing market, with occasional references to North American and European practices. Our discussion in what follows is based on two major research streams. The vendor study involved a series of face-to-face interviews with 16 outsourcing vendors and structured interviews from 671 business directors and 667 IT directors across the finance, manufacturing and retail sectors (Michell, 1994). The client study (Willcocks and Fitzgerald, 1994; Willcocks et al., 1996) was based on a postal questionnaire with over 150 respondent senior managers/directors, in both medium- and large-sized organizations in the UK and a small proportion of European countries as well, together with 25 client case studies.

## Developments

The UK IT outsourcing market has only developed significantly in the last 7 years. (CSA, 1993; Michell, 1994) Historically, outsourcing took off in North America and spread rapidly as a result of the 1989 Kodak-IBM outsourcing agreement (Loh and Venkatraman, 1992). However, IT outsourcing was preceded by IT facilities management (FM) which concentrated on outsourcing data processing activities which had developed from bureau services and the 'renting' of computer time in the 1960s. The evolution of outsourcing has been influenced by both IT service vendors opportunism and rising costs, complexity and the risks associated with information systems.

In the period 1970–1987 the IT services market was defined by three different types of IT service provider: hardware suppliers, systems houses supplying and developing software and IT consultancies. IT service providers extended their offerings to include outsourcing of systems project management and systems developments in the 1970s and then the contracting out of the entire data processing function and the computer that supported it in the 1980s. The late 1980s saw a stream of new entrants entering the outsourcing market-place from these traditional IT service providers and new sources, driven, for example, in the UK by the government contracting out many public services, economic recessionary pressures and developments in the North American market (Lacity and Hirschheim, 1993a; Michell, 1994). In addition, as Clark et al. (1995) noted, few barriers to vendor entry exist and there is a rising demand for third parties to shoulder the technological risk and high costs associated with information systems. This has led to five identifiable trends in the vendor market.

(1) Global, vertically integrated, IT solutions providers developed out of international consultancies specializing in IT and project management (for example, EDS and Andersen Consulting) and moved into the outsourcing market in the late 1980s to take advantage of the then lucrative margins and long-term nature of contracts. This international element has led to the import of mainly North American approaches to outsourcing in the developed economies, for example large, encompassing deals such as Kodak and large financially stable vendors achieved sizeable contracts by leveraging their North American outsourcing track record, experience and skills.

(2) Traditional hardware manufacturers also moved into the market (for example, IBM, DEC and Seimens), often through hardware maintenance outsourcing as hardware sales declined and the development of separate outsourcing units. Thus, IBM set up a separate outsourcing arm, ISSC, that has enjoyed massive growth (Caldwell and Krass, 1992).

(3) Ex-IT departments of large companies also entered the market (for example, ITNet and Barclays Computer Operations), as their parent organizations cut costs and disposed of expensive internal specialists (Martin, 1991).

(4) Traditional providers of building and facilities services, for example suppliers of security and catering services (generic outsourcers) also moved into IT outsourcing by offering desktop PC support and cabling services.

(5) As of early 1997 some of the largest vendors had already moved into business process outsourcing, that is outsourcing of both the IT services and specific business units such as accounting functions.

The recession and reducing margins during the early 1990s had forced IT services vendors into seeking closer and longer term relationships with their clients in order to guarantee future work and ensure on-site position to take full advantage of any ‘change’ opportunities. Outsourcing contracts provide the opportunity for close and continuous contact with a client that can threaten traditional IT product and services vendor relationships, by gaining control of a prime marketing channel to the customer. Outsourcing can therefore be a key to IT services marketing channel control (Michell, 1994) and is influential in driving more IT services vendors into offering outsourcing capabilities in an effort to maintain control over the channel.

## Vendor types

## How vendors position outsourcing

The way in which vendors propose and sell outsourcing services depends both on their background and the context in which their IT outsourcing services have developed as well as market changes. There has been evidence of vendors beginning to position and differentiate their services clearly according to lucrative trends. Although in the late 1980s and early 1990s FM was seen as the growing opportunity, it has been replaced in terms of growth by higher value types of outsourcing. New entrants to the outsourcing marketplace (in 1 year alone there were over 130 UK major vendors according to Kavanagh (1993)) have led to increased rivalry and a growing saturation of the FM sector of the market. This in turn has led to vendors attempting to distinguish outsourcing of higher value services from FM. FM is seen as the management of the operational elements of information service provision (Michell, 1994), involving responsibility for the infrastructure (hardware, networks, and peripheral facilities) and existing software (Mill, 1991). FM is concerned with ‘running the IT infrastructure management activities’ (CCTA, 1993) and refers to the operational level of outsourcing (Earl, 1990).

In contrast Michell (1994) found that vendor definitions of outsourcing varied according to their business needs and positions in the market, that is outsourcing was seen as an opportunity to sell any kind of IT service according to customer need. In many cases what was defined as being sold by the vendor was at variance with the vendor capability of providing the services according to the vendor background. This clearly illustrates the 'bandwagon' effect and vendor strategies of keeping their options open to a variety of business opportunities. However, by early 1997 a long UK recession had receded and there was evidence that some vendors were clearly positioning themselves in the market-place and were more specific about their capabilities.

The differences between vendor approaches to outsourcing can be described by dividing vendors into 5 main categories.

## The IT outsourcing market-place

## IT consultancies and IT solutions providers

IT consultancies exist as part of operational management consultancy firms or are very large/global systems houses which cover both traditional software development services and IT consultancy and systems integration roles (for example, EDS). Their main characteristic is large numbers of highly trained/high cost staff and traditional high margin and diverse consultancy services covering IT solutions from the strategic to operational levels, but driven from a business not technology focus. Interestingly some IT consultancy vendors described IT outsourcing as a subset of business process outsourcing, which includes outsourcing specific business functions and associated IT, which suggests the development of business process as well as IT skills, probably through staff acquisition from clients. Many vendors with consultancy backgrounds stressed the importance of the business-based relationship and the provision of strategic information systems advice. These vendors have the market advantage of extensive experience of business issues (often through accountancy arms), high level business contacts and global reach through their consultancy role. They see IT outsourcing encompassing all the IT functions from IT planning through to operations and maintenance. In contrast many saw FM as a low margin, high risk business, based on cost leadership, which most consultancies are reluctant to undertake because of the poor margins. On average the consultancy-based IT outsourcing vendors questioned suggested that only between 20 and 30% of their revenue came from operational outsourcing and the majority came from more tactical and strategic levels (systems design, development and consulting activities). However, the research findings suggest that IT consultancies have moved from providing strategic and tactical advice into operational IS projects, software development and IT outsourcing, that is a downward integration of IT service offerings.

## Systems houses

These are defined by their traditional background in custom systems design, development and implementation. They are often medium sized and have less global reach compared with the consultancies. As outsourcing vendors they are under pressure from above and below their traditional applications development activities. The invasion of their traditional markets downwards from the IT consultancies and upwards from the hardware vendors are forcing them to move into both operational IT management and planning as well as IT outsourcing. Interestingly, outsourcing vendors evolving from systems applications design and development businesses often made a clear distinction between outsourcing and consultancy, perhaps more aware of their core skills in this area as opposed to strategic and hardware areas. For example, customized systems development and IT project management was not termed outsourcing as in many of their projects the IT function remained under the control and management of the client when the project ended. Many systems houses are increasingly involved in the long-term provision and management responsibility for custom or package software and its maintenance (application management) (International Data Corporation, 1993), concentrating on IT technical skills. This is clearly a development to integrate above operational outsourcing vertically to include client investment in more innovative and higher value systems development services and information provision.

## Hardware vendors

These have been undergoing the most change, due to pressure from falling margins forcing diversification into services and outsourcing. Many hardware vendors have moved upwards from hardware and infrastructure provision to operational activities, software development and IT consultancy and outsourcing (see Figure 1). Without exception the hardware vendors interviewed saw outsourcing as the provision of services and the solution of business problems at all IT function levels. The outsourcing services were defined widely to include all potential information services and to distance themselves from the hardware. Sample hardware vendor definitions included ‘outsourcing is the contracting out of any service in advanced IT’ and ‘outsourcing is anything that is not core business, FM is hardware based and involves actual transfer of assets’.

This is an attempt to diversify from the increasing commoditization of the hardware market and provide vertically integrated, technically focused IT services. However, hardware vendors have the problem of differentiating their hardware- and product-based activities from their IT service/outsourcing activities. Many feel that a hardware brand image is a double edged sword with clients impressed by the brand but doubting their independence as an outsourcing services and hardware provider. Software and hardware product independence implies a concentration on service and objectivity that also acts as a differentiator from software and hardware vendors. Clearly, demonstrating objectivity is a key problem for hardware vendors with outsourcing arms, particularly as competitors such as generic outsourcers see their hardware and software independence as a key differentiator.

Client concern over a lack of independence is reflected in client research (see later) and in the

International Data Corporation (1993) user survey, which, in the early 1990s at least, placed hardware vendors at the bottom of the list of preferred suppliers. Hardware vendors have the benefit of a traditional close relationship with the IT department and its operational activities aiding movement into FM. However, there is less contact with the business management and pure service skills (unlike the consultancies) that are needed to develop the higher value, design, planning and even functional outsourcing deals. Hardware vendors are also making use of their technology and manufacturing knowledge by stressing the benefit of innovative technical solutions that improve the efficiency of the outsourced activity and act as a differentiator.

## Ex-IT departments

A number of outsourcing vendors have developed from the ex-IT departments of large companies (for example, ITNet and Barclays Computer Operations) in the early 1990s spate of industry restructuring. Their focus is mainly on FM and operational expertise through the provision of system management (hardware, software and networks). Some ex-IT department-based vendors stated that they were only concerned with operational systems management more akin to FM, for example to quote one vendor, 'FM is running the shop'.

These vendors often have specific technical expertise within vertical industry groups at the operational level. There is a clear leverage of these industry-specific skills and the trend towards specialization in a vertical industry market, for example financial operations or a specific type of outsourcing, such as networking/telecomms. Although some ex-IT function vendors recognized their operational service skills many also emphasized their interest in the provision of vertically integrated services up to and including IT consultancy, although often limited by the narrow vertical industry sector of their parent company and in general medium size.

## Generic outsourcers

Another vendor type, the generic outsourcing vendor that developed in the late 1980s, provides a range of outsourcing services, not just information systems but also ‘managing the working environment and business services’ which includes buildings support services, people management, (for example, catering, cleaning and business infrastructure provision) and cabling equipment and office services. The trend towards package software and simpler user friendly PC applications has enabled new generic outsourcers to enter the market. An example is Granada Computer Services whose focus is the building infrastructure and PC services. Vendors often enter the market by outsourcing low value commercial activities (for example, cleaning/catering services) then build up a portfolio of 'bundled' outsourcing services including outsourcing of cabling, IT hardware and desktop operational IT on the basis of economies of scale, low cost and service level synergy. Lower costs than IT specialist vendors and the capability to 'bundle' IT (PCs and software) along with other services under one business-focused contract provide a competitive advantage.

## Vendor market dynamics

Porter's (1979) 'five forces' industry model will be used to identify the market pressures.

## New entrants

The entry barriers for outsourcing are currently low, requiring only skilled IT functional or system expertise. Many vendors have complained of being 'undercut' on price by new entrant vendors keen to gain a market share, at low margins. In addition, outsourcing is not only confined to local geographic areas and established vendors. For example, outsourcing of IT development to skilled and low cost new entrant vendors overseas is growing. For example, software development costs in India are estimated at only 30–40% of UK costs (Michell, 1994).

## Supplier pressure

Supplier pressure from IT skills shortages was not significant as the recession and widespread unemployment reduced skills costs in the early 1990s, but from 1996 salaries rose and skills shortages were increasing. In addition, further pressure was placed on labour issues from the EEC by Transfer of Undertaking, Protection of Employment (TUPE) regulations applicable to the large and growing public sector markets in much of Europe. In particular, the need to retain excellent staff implies retraining and investment in skills set against the need for vendors to reduce costs, which is likely to be a growing burden on the vendor, particularly in the light of EU court cases and evidence of industrial unrest over public sector market testing programmes, particularly in the UK (Independent, 1993).

## Substitutes

Throughout the 1994–1997 period outsourcing substitutes were developing as consultants moved in to the lucrative market to advise private companies and government departments on market testing or efficiency improvements and business process re-engineering. Some companies have been selecting efficiency improvement programmes to gain more value from IT activities in preference to outsourcing. For example Stevenage Borough Council abandoned outsourcing for internal efficiency improvements (Michell, 1994). Many client companies have been re-evaluating their business objectives and operations as a result of competitive pressures and have developed an interest in business process re-engineering, which suggests an emphasis on internal analysis. Business process consultancy is likely to invoke detailed analysis of efficiency improvements and delay and possibly remove outsourcing decisions particularly where the re-engineering makes them unnecessary through large-scale technology and business focus change. On the other hand, some vendors have responded by enlarging the offering to include ‘partnership’ deals covering both re-engineering and IT activities and have also pushed for the outsourcing of business processes, including the IT activities subsumed therein.

## Increased rivalry

Many vendors appear to be re-defining their activities to suit the growing market popularity of outsourcing, differentiating their outsourcing activities and moving onward from FM which is fast becoming a low margin cost leadership and maturing market segment.

## Vendor services - what is provided?

The range of services offered by vendors is large and growing. Based on previous surveys (International Data Corporation, 1993), five broad types of IT service categories can be defined covering high value IT planning and design to operations and maintenance activities. These are strategic IT planning outsourcing, systems design outsourcing, systems development outsourcing, IT operations/management outsourcing and IT infrastructure outsourcing.

In our own research a sample survey of ten major outsourcing vendors was taken, covering the main vendor types and their percentage revenue from their outsourcing. ## Service trends

The outsourcing market is, in the view of many of the vendors interviewed, producing a number of expected trends which can be classified into three types: (1) desktop systems services or outsourcing system building, (2) growth in complete functional outsourcing and (3) 'one stop' design to implementation services or end to end systems ownership.

The vendor responses suggest a trend towards outsourcing complete IT services, including the design and operating and maintenance functions. The implication is that either the outsourcing of the bulk of the IT department and its services is becoming more prevalent or we are seeing the second stages of repeat business from existing outsourcing deals as client confidence grows and vendors sell additional services to existing clients to cover skills shortfalls. (For an example of outsourcing to support skills/capacity management in the health service see Michell (1994).) These types of outsourcing clearly involve more than just outsourcing the IT ‘facility’ and may include the design and innovation elements of IT provision. This suggests that outsourcing activities have been moving up the value chain to higher added value levels, such as development outsourcing and the provision of business as well as technology support in response to client criteria. Outsourcing service packages appear to be developed to support this trend and provide added ‘value’. Those identified in the research were as follows.

## Desktop information provision

This offers a complete information service, incorporating the management of the desktop PC, applications and maintenance as well as the purchase and implementation of new applications. There is little applications development in this form as ‘shrink wrapped’ applications are purchased on the basis of economies of scale (Computing, 1991, Computer Weekly, 1993).

## Applications management

A form of managed services and systems, that is an extension of the desktop approach in which a complete applications function is outsourced in a contract that includes development of the application, its operation and maintenance (Ringshaw, 1993). This form of outsourcing differs from desktop information provision mainly by the inclusion of a development role, but the services offered have been further subdivided by vendors into applications operations and maintenance services as individual packages to suit varying client needs. This provides integrated software management and often does not include the hardware, therefore separating the service from traditional FM deals.

## Partnership outsourcing

We have used the term ‘partnership; outsourcing to refer to the outsourcing of complete IT functions at levels from 1 right up to 5, that is the trend to outsourcing end-to-end systems ownership. This is also called ‘partnering’ (Oltman, 1990) and often involves long-term flexible contracts up to 10 years in length. Often complete responsibility is given to the vendor for providing IT services from design to maintenance. This differs from traditional outsourcing deals as it often involves the client in a very close and long-term partnering relationship in which there is mutual investment in infrastructure, applications, process and even business level activities. The vendor invests heavily in specific knowledge of the client business. For example, EDS have developed the term Co-sourcing™ to refer to their partnership approach. The term has been regis-

## Vendor capabilities

Our research suggests that the vendor ability to offer a vertically integrated range of services depends on their core IT background (that is, software, hardware and consultancy), developed track record and growth. The highest level of channel ‘penetration’ of the customer (in terms of vendor contact level) is also likely to be an influencing factor in the characteristic type, value and margin of the contract and the extent to which services can be ‘cascaded’ from the point of client contact. For example, Co-sourcing $^{TM}$ , which is likely to involve the control of core IT activities such as planning and development in addition to knowledge of the business process, will require high level executive contact points who have budgetary and decision-making power to influence this type of outsourcing. In addition, the vendor must have a track record and capability in the areas in which they expect to ‘cascade’ their services into. We can conclude that many vendors are moving towards offering vertically integrated outsourcing services in varying degrees and that steps are being taken to differentiate services from traditional FM and low value outsourcing packages.

## Emerging vendor segments

Given the findings detailed above on the influence of vendor background on outsourcing capabilities, the increasing competition and lack of market entry barriers, we can now propose likely outsourcing segments that vendors may develop to protect their marketing channels. Vendors responses to questions of their core competences suggested six main areas that vendors concentrate on: track record, price, technical expertise, industry knowledge, relationships and objectivity. If we extrapolate these trends with the vendor backgrounds and perspectives on outsourcing we can identify the likely evolution of IT outsourcing market segments.

## The one stop shop segment

The central government market and large private clients, such as British Aerospace and Kodak, have shown a preference for large vendors with financial stability and a track record. Given the existence of large IT consultancy and solutions vendors, a potential outsourcing segment is likely to develop for vertically integrated vendors able to provide a range of IT outsourcing services on a 'one stop shop' basis. An outsourcer providing total management of all IT requirements appeals to clients who see IT as non-core and who require a more cost-effective and versatile IT provision. The vertically integrated outsourcing vendor can also act as a prime contractor for the 'pyramid' of subcontracted and more specialist outsourcing vendors. This is the preserve of the global player, that can provide integrated outsourcing services from systems, planning and design to implementation, operation and support, hardware and software procurement. The particular qualities required of the one stop shop vendor are (1) global presence, (2) brand image, (3) high level chief executive officer (CEO) contacts, (4) in-depth range of outsourcing services, (5) business process knowledge and technical knowledge and (6) large and integrated contract experience. Currently, the best equipped for this approach are large hardware vendors or consultancies and IT solutions providers.

## The business process outsourcing segment

Business process outsourcing involves management responsibility for a complete business process or activity. This appeals to clients who see particular business functions, including IT, as non-core, for example local government or the 1993–1994 example of British Petroleum outsourcing an accounting function. This requires vendors with business-specific business capabilities rather than specific IT expertise and it is unclear whether IT will play a significant part. In the few current examples IT has been excluded from the deal or has been a small part, but it can be difficult to separate IT from the business process unless the IT acts only in a support role. It is important to discuss it here because of its effect on the IT outsourcing market and it raises particularly complex questions. For example, if the IT is already outsourced how can the function be outsourced separately? Can the business function management be separated from the IT function and is it wise to do so? It is difficult to estimate what is required to be successful at business process outsourcing because it is a very young and hardly developed market, but discussions with vendors interested in this market suggest that the vendor requirements are likely to be as follows.

(1) Business process knowledge.

(2) CEO and executive level credibility and contacts.

(3) Financial security and business process expertise track record.

(4) Cultural match (business process developments are long-term).

To operate in this market-place vendors must have business process as well as IT skills. It is therefore unlikely that many IT companies will be seen as capable unless the process is very IT dependent, they are vertically integrated service providers (consultancy, IT and outsourcing) or they have acquired the business process expertise by merger or acquisition. Consultancies or IT solutions providers are ideally placed for this segment, having the critical size, track record and combination of business and technical credibility. Generic outsourcers may also be tempted into this segment at a less sophisticated level through horizontal service synergy and diversification.

## The technology segment

Technical expertise (for example, telecommunication and security) was seen by vendors as an important characteristic for winning bids). Hardware vendors and systems houses already have technology/development expertise. Our research suggests that clients were beginning to use outsourcing to obtain additional advice and competitive advantage. Clients with particular non-core competencies in technology or who wish to remove the 'burden' and risk of IT (Michell, 1994) may be particularly inclined to selection of vendors with technical expertise and a track record. For example, clients who have no telecommunications or network expertise but require a network to do business or clients who require software application expertise. The client is essentially purchasing technical management expertise and may be concerned with removing the risk of IT investment and seeking technology proofing through expertise.

Technology change is an important driver of outsourcing (Michell, 1994) enabling specialization in specific technology areas for vendors with backgrounds that have (1) technical expertise (for example, networking and telecommunications), (2) software application expertise (for example, specific packaged software), (3) client credibility and (4) a track record in technology solutions.

This form of outsourcing depends on satisfying the client's needs for specific technical management and skills that are lacking in the organization. Network, telecommunications and integrated systems outsourcing is one example of a niche that has been increasingly developed, with a host of international and national entrants. Manufacturing process management outsourcing is another area that is already developing and demands technological experience as a manufacturer and process systems developer or user.

## The industry specialist segment

Industry-specific expertise as a differentiator is suggested by ex-IT functions and hardware vendors. Experience as both a user and an experienced provider of solutions within a particular industry is clearly an advantage, particularly if a vertical industry market is being targeted and vendor understanding of the business area is also a client selection criteria. For example, ex-IT function outsourcers such as ITNet are well equipped with industry-specific expertise as mentioned above. Such outsourcers have developed from successful businesses with sophisticated IT departments. Industry specialist vendors are likely to require the following.

(1) The ability to satisfy industry-specific requirements (value for money IT, technology, etc.).

(2) Past experience/good relationship.

(3) Reputation for industry expertise and knowledge.

Their clients are likely to see IT as non-core and so be concerned with the strategic use of outsourcing to make up for a lack of core competence or remove the burden of IT. This suggests that industry image and a track record combined with user experience are vital ingredients. For example, hardware manufacturers with recognized experience of the manufacturing industry and technology knowledge specified their interest in the development of a manufacturing industry outsourcing niche.

## The IT function segment

The lack of perceived capability of clients in the development and maintenance areas suggests specialization in the software applications development area. Similarly, user concern and a lack of knowledge of networking, integration and IT infrastructure activities is a growing segment. Examples of limited use or short-term use of outsourcing suggest that many clients are uncertain of the merits of outsourcing yet see advantages of using IT tactically or wish to outsource operations to make significant cost savings on a commodity service.

## Vendor relationships

If these segments develop how will the market work? If the trend towards clients outsourcing higher level services continues vendors will be forced to choose between becoming vertically integrated outsourcing providers or differentiating and becoming niche industry or technology specialist outsourcers. The existence of large and lucrative contracts, for example from market testing is altering the shape of the market, both by taking up vendor capacity and by limiting the expansion of medium-sized vendors. Some vendors have suggested this distortion may be moderated by the development of consortia in which large global outsourcing vendors may act as prime outsourcing contractors for a pyramid of second and third level specialist outsourcers, as in Figure 3. The base of the pyramid is focused on technical skills and the apex on business skills. This is a way for small- and medium-sized vendors to share large scale contracts with large vendors without the need to develop vast vertically integrated IT expertise. For example, a large global outsourcing vendor may subcontract network and desktop services to a specialist network outsourcing vendor and an FM vendor. This approach may also be supported by client pressure to spread the risk of outsourcing through more than one vendor, but to reduce the management complexity by using a prime contractor approach. The outsourcing at British Petroleum (Michell, 1994; Cross, 1995) is a good example of the use of a specialist network vendor in conjunction with two other outsourcing vendors to provide a complete solution that reduces the risk of reliance on a single vendor.

This model is based on the general ‘visions’ of outsourcing and vendor responses from the research interviews. This categorization is of course a generalization, but helps to provide a basis for clients to understand the relative core competencies, strengths and weaknesses of the vendor during the vendor selection process, as discussed further below.

## The demand side: the client perspective

We now turn to the findings from the client-based study and examine some of the issues from the perspective of the clients and the selection of vendors. Firstly, the survey revealed outsourcing of IT as a dynamic process. Against general projections of a rising trend in outsourcing, nearly one-third of the organizations who had outsourced had, in the previous 5 years (1989–1994), cancelled contracts as opposed to straightforwardly renewing them. In half of these cases the contract was subsequently renegotiated, but a further 28% of the cases involved a change of vendor, while the remaining 22% saw the IT work brought back in-house. The survey also sought to examine problems that were experienced over the progress of the contract. The respondents indicated many problem areas that emerged but the top five ranked as ‘very difficult’ were, in order, (1) defining service levels, (2) managing the contract and its details, (3) getting different contractors and vendors to work together, (4) vendors’ lack of flexibility and (5) vendors’ lack of responsiveness.

Thus, the major problems actually experienced related to contracts and vendors. Given that difficulties with the vendor were a prominent problem area we explored whether these difficulties turned into disputes and found that half of the organizations that outsourced IT had experienced disputes with their vendor(s). The disputes centred around one or more of: (1) a failure of the vendor to meet service levels (37% of the cases mentioned), (2) unclear contractual issues (23%), (3) changing requirements (21%) and (4) unforeseen charges (18%).

Of the organizations experiencing disputes, 69% had encountered two or more of these issues. When asked if they would have done anything differently when they outsourced, most (over four-fifths) of these organizations referred explicitly to tightening service contracts, more detailed measurements and making clearer what was covered by the contract. Thus, the survey suggests that the relationships that clients are adopting with vendors are becoming increasingly contractually based and this was borne out from answers to other questions.

However, even though there is a trend towards more contractually based relationships there is strong evidence that the contracts and their definition could be significantly improved. Client companies were not particularly adept at writing good, effective contracts. In this area the vendors were much more experienced and it showed. Thus, many of the problems that client companies experience are closely related to contractual issues and relationships with vendors (see Willcocks and Fitzgerald (1994) for further details).

## Client selection criteria

The demand side of the selection of the vendor equation reflects, of course, the requirements of the client companies. It emerged from the client-based study that what client companies are looking for when selecting a vendor is not always the obvious. They firstly clearly require the vendor to be able to handle the area or application being outsourced but this did not appear to be the differentiator when selecting the vendor. That is what has been described above as order qualifiers, the things that are necessary to get vendors onto the short lists. The aspects of importance for client companies when selecting between vendors are a series of much softer and perhaps more perceptual criteria, much more difficult to define and quantify.

The process of selecting and evaluating vendors will be described from the perspective of the client companies. Figure 4 provides an overview of the process.

## The evaluation and decision-making process

Firstly, the process of selecting and evaluating vendors is viewed as important and treated seriously by client companies. It is also a resource- and time-consuming process. In the majority of cases companies had a real and rigorous selection process, rather than simply choosing some preordained supplier, that is the majority of selection processes seemed to be genuinely open. This was particularly true for significant new contracts rather than enhancements or renewals to existing contracts, where the existing vendor, providing they had performed well, had a significant advantage. One indication of this openness was that in quite a number of cases we were told that the vendor selected was probably not the one that might have been considered to be the prime candidate prior to the selection process and occasionally was even a rank outsider.

## The IT outsourcing market-place

## Selecting a short list of vendors

Starting at the point where the business case for outsourcing has been established and agreed by senior management in the client company, the next stage was to create a short list of relevant vendors. (It should be noted that by this point some companies we researched had not yet made the business case for outsourcing nor achieved a management sign off. In such cases they often used the vendor selection process as part of the making of the internal case.) Typically there were two types of process for establishing a short list of vendors – open or closed. An open process was one where the client companies would openly trawl or advertise for vendors. A closed process was one where the client companies kept their interest in outsourcing secret and approached vendors for the short list directly.

The main influence on whether the process was open or closed was whether the client company wished the outsourcing to become public knowledge. Some organizations liked to keep the outsourcing deal closed and then announce it as a fait accompli (particularly to the staff) after all the details had been agreed, including the selected supplier. An important factor leading to open processes was the influence of culture or the established rules of the client organization, irrespective of whether it involved IT or not. If the organization had well-established standards and norms for procurement these usually specified that an open process had to be conducted.

## The request for information

The process of searching for relevant vendors to create the short list sometimes first includes a request for information (RFI) from vendors. The RFI generally outlines the client company's present position and objectives, the operations, services and functions it is seeking to outsource, its staffing position and anything else of relevance. It then requests certain information from the vendor as to their likely approach to addressing the proposal, its capabilities and abilities, the company's background and experience, customer references, etc. There may also be some questions that attempt to reveal their views on partnership, flexibility, contracts, contingency, security, etc. The client might also ask the vendor what benefits they could expect and how they feel they compare with other vendors, highlighting their particular competitive advantage. Finally, the RFI would usually seek an indication of costs and a formal expression of interest from the vendor. The RFI process might involve visits and presentations as well as the exchange of documentation.

The purpose of the RFI is for the client companies to obtain a preliminary feel for the vendors and to perhaps identify some vendors that would not have been considered for the short list without an RFI process. For many companies in our research, however, the RFI process was either thought to be unnecessary or was rather limited in scope. Most of the client companies felt they already had a good feel for the vendor market to be able to construct their short list without a great deal of effort. This may possibly be explained by the fact that the number of major vendors in the UK is relatively small and that the niche players that specialize in particular industry sectors are likely to be well known by the client companies within their sector. Our general perception however was that client companies were not very knowledgeable or sophisticated in their analysis of vendors, for example there was little talk or understanding of the vendor types outlined in earlier sections of this paper.

The number of vendors that should be on a short list was thought to be approximately four and certainly no more than six. Too few vendors was thought to be dangerous in case some dropped out and left an inadequate field or because it might be construed as a 'fix' by various people.

## The invitation to tender

Whichever way the short list has been established, it then leads to the real business of selection. This stage is usually characterized by the invitation to tender (ITT) document. The ITT differed considerably from company to company, particularly in the level of detail involved. It would be a much shorter document if a full RFI process had already been conducted. Normally the client would send information about the requirements to the vendors on the short list asking them to reply with details, ideas, approaches, cost etc. Again there were two main approaches. For some, the ITT would be at the outline or conceptual level only. This approach has the objective of selecting a vendor with which the client feels happy to work in principle and then the details would be discussed and negotiated with a single vendor once selected. This approach is characterized as an informal ITT. The other approach (a formal ITT) was to enter into a very detailed dialogue and exchange of information with each vendor on the short list and then, on the basis of the replies and quotes, make a selection.

The problem with this second approach is that it is very time-consuming and involves the duplication of documentation and negotiation for each vendor on the short list. One organization that had six vendors on their short list received 30–40 queries from each vendor as a result of their ITT and found themselves in a position (dictated by their corporate procurement policy) of having to provide any information they provided to one vendor to all the vendors. The workload proved large and well above their expectations. The problem with the first approach is that it tends to restrict the client company's negotiating potential when the details are discussed because the vendor has already been selected and the leverage of playing one vendor off against another is rescued.

Those companies that adopted a formal ITT approach then tended to adopt a formal evaluation process, with scores and weightings against a check list of features, described by one interviewee as the use of a 'sausage machine' and another as collecting all the inputs and 'turning the handle', that is a very mechanistic and formal process. This process could be used to eliminate vendors but it was clear, even where a formal evaluation process was undertaken, that this was essentially not the real decision-making process; at most it served as a background to the decision.

The real selection was based on a set of factors that were much softer and more perceptual than those that featured in the formal evaluation matrices and check lists. They are more difficult to evaluate and cannot be easily quantified.

## Selection criteria

One of the most important or at least frequently quoted factors was what was described as the cultural fit or cultural match between the client and the vendor. This was not well defined or articulated by the client companies but it was partly a feeling that the client could work happily and feel comfortable with the vendor, including notions of shared corporate values and working practices. The importance of this factor is underlined by Klepper (1994). This cultural fit was often used as a summary term for a number of other factors, as follows.

Client companies were often looking particularly for the vendor to be able to contribute to the business above and beyond the boundaries of the actual service or activity that was being outsourced. Many companies indicated that they did not want simple 'machine minding' or traditional FM. They wanted a more sophisticated package of IT service and support which added value to the organization. They were looking for vendors to participate in the management of the activities that were outsourced and to take on some of the business responsibility and even ideas for developing the business. Although this was what companies frequently said they were looking for, in practice this did not often appear to materialize and a number of companies expressed this as an area of disappointment.

The companies were also disappointed by the lack of effort that the vendor made to find out about the company and their requirements. They felt the vendors were more interesting in fitting them into a predefined category rather than seriously evaluating what they required as an individual company.

This supports the view expressed earlier that many vendors are still selling their outsourcing services according to their background skills and, despite the wish of many to offer vertically integrated services, many do not have the business capability. This stresses the need for a careful analysis of vendor capabilities as demonstrated by Figure 4.

The client companies selected vendors on the basis that the vendor was felt to be able to add value to the existing in-house capabilities and skills and they were not looking for vendors simply to reproduce approaches and solutions that they already possessed. Again there was some disappointment in this area in practice. This is complicated by the fact that in many cases the client company was actually providing much of the expertise by the transfer of existing staff to the vendor but they expected additional technical and managerial skills which they did not often feel they received.

In situations which involved the transfer of staff, the perception of how those staff would be treated, above and beyond the contract, emerged as of critical importance to the client companies in selecting a vendor, not only the perception of the client management but also the perception of the staff to be transferred themselves (and/or representatives). Staff sometimes effectively had a veto over the selection of a particular vendor. Not only was this factor of critical importance but in retrospect the perception of the client company as to whether the outsourcing was a success or not often hinged on whether the vendor was felt to have handled the staff transfer issues well or not. This focus on staff as a key influence on the selection of the vendor was not always just for altruistic reasons; it was also recognized that they had a key role to play for the client company even after they had been transferred, particularly in the early stages of the contract.

The general perception of the vendor as a quality organization, with a quality culture and quality people was felt to be very important. Perhaps surprisingly, given this, only a couple of client companies mentioned any of the formal process quality measures, for example BS5750, as being important. Others were not much impressed by BS5750 and, in general, it did not appear to count for much in their evaluations, although some vendors appeared to believe it is becomingly increasingly important and have already acquired it or are seeking to do so.

## The IT outsourcing market-place

There were a couple of more specific and traditional areas that client companies specified as important in the selection of a vendor, although these were often added somewhat as afterthoughts. Firstly, the vendor had to be seen to have a track record in the relevant area and to have proved itself in some way. Despite this, the assessment of track records sometimes seemed somewhat arbitrary and based more on anecdotal evidence and information supplied by the vendors themselves rather than independent assessment. For example, reference visits to existing clients of vendors to see and hear about their performance and capabilities appeared not to be universal practice. One organization felt that reference visits would not increase their knowledge of the vendor much because the vendor would select ‘suitable’ sites and that most companies would not criticize their vendor externally.

Another area mentioned was the financial stability (and potential for long-term survival) of the vendor. It is obviously very unhelpful if the vendor ceases to be able to provide the necessary service halfway through the contract. However, in practice it was again really just a prerequisite for getting on the short list. In some cases where small companies (and therefore possibly financially more risky) were in contention their rejection (and they generally were rejected, large companies were much more favoured) was always said to be for other reasons.

A factor that was rarely mentioned as being important in the context of vendor selection was cost, despite the fact that in many cases one of the driving forces for outsourcing is cost reductions. Obviously if prices differed by orders of magnitude then it became an issue but providing it was within an acceptable range other factors were more important and frequently the cheapest vendor was not selected. This corresponds with the findings from some other studies, for example CTC (1990) where it was suggested that five of the top ten critical selection factors were not related to price. Interestingly a price that was felt to be too low was often viewed suspiciously. It was often assumed that this would result in additional costs and disputes over the duration of the project. Many companies felt it was better and safer to allow the vendor to make a fair profit to ensure good service and avoid disputes.

One negative factor that turned clients against a vendor was any notion that the vendor was not independent, particularly if it was felt that the vendor might be using the deal as a way of selling hardware. This obviously puts some of the traditional hardware manufacturers who have moved into outsourcing (see above) in a difficult position.

The above findings concerning the selection criteria were surprisingly consistent and somewhat different from the requirements as perceived by the vendors.

## Discussion and conclusions

An analysis of the findings from the two studies finds a number of similarities but a significant number of differences. This is not altogether surprising as we would expect vendors and clients to have some different perceptions concerning outsourcing but nevertheless there are some areas where the mismatch is quite large and indicates that perhaps both sides could learn from each other.

The first significant issue concerns the levels of problems and disputes between the vendors and clients during outsourcing. Clearly the view from the vendor side is that although there are a number of areas where vendor-client conflict does occur, overall the situation is one where disputes are hardly mentioned or put down to client inexperience or lack of detailed specification of needs. The findings from the client study are very different and paint a picture of obvious problems and disputes. This does not necessarily mean that outsourcing cannot be successful but it does mean that the process can be fraught with difficulties. Vendors might do better to recognize this and attempt to address some of the problems rather than hide them.

One response of client companies to these experiences is to try and specify an increasingly detailed and highly specified contractual relationship with vendors in order to minimize contractual disagreements and ensure service levels are met. This demonstrates the wish for a sound understanding, that is the notion of partnership, for example as embodied by 'partnering outsourcing', is not unrestrained and if it goes wrong the client has to recourse an effective and specific contract.

Despite the increasing focus on the contract and its specification the actual selection of vendors by clients appears to be based more on qualitative rather than quantitative criteria. This appears to be driven by the vendors offering a wide range of similar services, particularly at the operational or FM level and the services are increasingly seen as commodities. Even at the higher level, where client companies are seeking vendors to add value in their business and managerial offerings, there appears to be little differentiation as almost all vendors claim to be able to offer all services across the spectrum of requirements. However, it is clear that not all vendor capabilities are equal and the analysis of vendor backgrounds in Figure 4 suggests significant variations in capabilities. Not all client companies appear to have recognized this and perhaps their evaluation processes need to be improved and made more quantitative and formal, with more time and effort devoted to identifying vendors that can deliver their particular needs. In particular, clients need to understand better the skills, backgrounds and track records of vendors in order to identify those most likely to provide for their needs.

Despite some similar vendor and client perceptions, there are some specific gaps between vendor provision and client expectation. The focus of client companies on softer, cultural issues in the vendor selection process appears to have been missed by some vendors and they might do well to focus on these issues. For example, clients attach great importance to the way vendors treat outsourced staff, but only one vendor mentioned this as a core skill or benefit that could be provided, although vendors clearly see staff skills as an important vendor value. Similarly, few vendors admitted to having quality systems in place to monitor service level agreements and manage the client account. In addition, the demand for independence suggests problems for hardware vendors. Some vendors need to be aware of the emphasis on value and other factors and not low price, which further emphasizes the need for vendors to differentiate their services in preference to achieving cost leadership and for clients to understand what value is being added by a vendor.

Although many clients were seeking to base their outsourcing relationships with vendors on an increasingly tight contractual arrangement, some clients were moving in the other direction and looking to establish partnerships and seeking a cultural match with vendors. Even those moving to tighter contractual arrangements listed close relationships and understanding the client business as important and a desired objective. However, many vendors were found not to be committed to such close relationships and were not willing to follow client business changes and some gave the perception of being arrogant about the superior service they provided without understanding the individual needs of the client (Michell, 1994). Clearly there is a gap between many vendor and client perceptions of relationships and partnerships.

Consultancy-based vendors have particularly recognized this by developing partnership forms of outsourcing which rely on adding value and shared risk. This also supports the findings that vendors see partnerships as a way of controlling the IT services marketing channel to a vendor. Clients therefore need to choose such partnerships carefully to ensure added value and shared risk, but not at the expense of inflexibility and 'lock in' to specific vendors. Elsewhere Fitzgerald and Willcocks (1994) argued that clients have misunderstood these issues and that, in situations of greater business and technical certainty concerning the area being outsourced, then a tight contractually based arrangement is sensible and desirable but in situations of greater uncertainty a tight contract can be counter-productive. In these situations, partnership relations based on a more flexible contract that shares the risks and rewards with the vendor is a better way to proceed. This reflects the Co-sourcing $^{TM}$ approach that some vendors have been attempting to adopt, although, as noted above, this is relatively rare.

The identification and perceptions of the market are also different between clients and vendors or at least they way that they were expressed. The clients saw it as being a 'sellers' market where the vendor could pick and choose, while the vendors often saw it the other way round. The client companies regularly stated that some vendors would not quote or accept an ITT implying that it was a sellers market, whereas the vendors talked about 'intelligent' customers meaning that they are prepared to deal only with those that are serious about outsourcing and not indulging in 'tyre kicking'. As to whether it is a sellers or buyers market, the truth probably depends on the type of outsourcing required. For example, the traditional operational FM area would now seem to be a buyers' market, though cost advantages may be eroding. However, for very large outsourcing contracts, for example at Sears in the UK in 1996 and the Xerox global outsourcing deal, there would seem to be only a limited number of vendors able to provide services on this scale. This may change with continuing new entrants due to relatively low entry barriers but with the client push towards higher level outsourcing and expectations a credibility factor may come into play which only the larger more experienced vendors are seen to provide.

In summary this paper has identified vendor and client approaches to outsourcing that underlie the market dynamics and outlined a model of the variation in vendor approaches and their skills. The current findings suggest that the low entry barriers of the outsourcing market combined with the client demand for greater added value, managerial and technical skills appear to be contributing to a buyers market. However, vendors are clearly not all alike and the vendor selection process must match not only 'hard' track record, financial stability, quality and capability requirements, but also understand the 'softer' issues of vendor vision, culture, background and human resource management issues. The increasing vendor rivalry and needs of clients are driving the market to segmentation with a range of general and specialist vendors. The pressure for vendors to segment and identify clearly the added value of outsourcing will only make vendor selection harder as the range of outsourcing services and packages expands, demanding from the client a good understanding of vendor approach and core skills and compatibility with their outsourcing requirements.
