# Programme evaluation of a government information technology programme for small businesses

CHEE-SING YAP

Department of Information Systems and Computer Science, National University of Singapore, Lower Kent Ridge Road, Singapore 119260

JAMES Y. L. THONG

Department of Information and Systems Management, School of Business and Management, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, Clear Water Bay, Kowloon, Hong Kong

The Small Enterprise Computerization Programme (SECP) was introduced by the Singapore Government to encourage and aid computerization in small businesses. The programme incentives include information technology (IT) seminars, the provision of technical expertise in the forms of advisers and consultants and various financial subsidies and loans. This paper presents the results of a formal evaluation of the impact of the SECP based on the views of various participants in the programme, including chief executive officers (CEOs) of small businesses, technical advisers, consultants, IT vendors and programme administrators. A programme evaluation methodology is utilized in this study. The main finding is that the government programme has lowered the barriers to computerization and aided small businesses that lack the resources to computerize successfully. More efforts need to be expended in promoting this programme to other small businesses that suffer from resource poverty. There is a lack of knowledge of the existence of the computerization programme and a lack of appreciation of the programme.

## Introduction

Many countries have formulated national information technology (IT) policies and plans to promote the use and production of IT (Matley and McDannold, 1987). Within these policies and plans are various programmes designed to train IT personnel, mobilize the population, encourage the diffusion of IT in all sectors of the economy and promote the growth of the IT industry (King et al., 1994). Many of these programmes have been introduced based on a priori belief that they will be effective in achieving the objectives. There is limited published information about the effectiveness of these programmes which can be used by policy planners. In fact, armchair speculation and partial solutions seem to be the norm (Schneider et al., 1982). Yet, there is considerable evidence that the findings of evaluations often influence policies, programme planning and implementation and the ways programmes are administered – sometimes in the short term and at other times in the long term (Rossi and Freeman, 1989).

The phenomena of technological change are measurable and amenable to empirical research, although this approach has been rare (Tornatzky and Luria, 1992). This paper presents the findings of a formal evaluation of the effectiveness of the Small Enterprise Computerization Programme (SECP) introduced by the Singapore Government. The Singapore Government considers the application of IT in all sectors of the economy essential in achieving a competitive position in the global market and sustaining national economic growth (Gurbaxani et al., 1991; Sisodia, 1992; Wong, 1992). It has also recognized the important role of small businesses in the national economy (SME Committee, 1989). The SECP was introduced to encourage and assist small local enterprises in becoming more productive and efficient and, hence, more competitive through the exploitation of IT. The incentives offered by the SECP are outlined in the next section.

Small businesses generally lag behind their larger counterparts in the use of technology (Niedleman, 1979; DeLone, 1981; Yap, 1990). In the use of IT, previous studies in the US and the UK have found that the diffusion of IT in small businesses has been much slower than in large businesses (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978; DeLone, 1981; Gray, 1988; Yap, 1990). In Singapore, a National Computer Board's (1990) survey reported that only 60% of businesses with less than 50 employees were using computers, as compared to 85% of businesses with 50 or more employees. Several reasons have been put forward to explain this phenomenon. Small businesses lack financial resources and internal technical expertise and generally have a poor appreciation of the benefits from utilizing computers (Delone, 1981; Massey 1986; Lees, 1987; Lees and Lees, 1987; DeLone, 1988; Montazemi, 1988; Gable, 1991). In addition, small businesses face difficulties in recruiting and retaining internal IT experts due to a scarcity of qualified IT experts and limited career advancement prospects (Simon, 1990).

The lower diffusion rate of IT in small business is, however, a concern from a national economic perspective, particularly in developing and newly industrializing countries. It is a concern because small businesses play a key role in national economies, accounting for a significant part of employment and gross national product (GNP). In the US, they account for approximately 98% of all business, employ 56% of the nation's workforce and contribute 38% of the GNP (Pickle and Abrahamson, 1990). In the European community, small businesses represent 95% of the enterprises and contribute some 50% of the total industrial employment (Dyson, 1990). In Singapore, small businesses account for 90% of all registered businesses, 44% of the workforce, 24% of value-added exports, and 16% of direct exports (SME Committee, 1989). If these businesses are slow to innovate and respond to technological change, they may become less competitive vis-à-vis their counterparts in more advanced countries. Small businesses that are not computerized will have difficulty doing business with large businesses, as electronic data interchange and interorganizational information systems become an integral part of business operations (Keen, 1991; Bergeron and Raymond, 1992). This is because many small businesses in these countries are acting as subcontractors to multinational corporations which are much more advanced in the use of IT. Thus, from a national economic perspective, a government has an interest in encouraging a wider and faster adoption of IT among small business.

A formal programme evaluation of the SECP was carried out by the authors, as independent evaluators, at the request of the SECP administrators. The programme evaluation was undertaken for both management and administrative purposes to assess the appropriateness of the programme incentives for computerization. It consisted of two parts. The first part focused on the programme effectiveness, i.e. the impact or outcome evaluation, which is a retrospective effort aimed at determining the consequences, outcomes or measurable results of a programme or policy (Patton, 1982; Rist, 1990). Outcome evaluation requires comparing, with as much rigour as possible, the conditions of a target group that has experienced a programme with those of another group that has not experienced it (Rossi and Freeman, 1989). It may also involve comparisons between impact measures and conjectures about what would have happened in the absence of the programme. The second part is an evaluation of the effectiveness of the various services and incentives provided by the SECP. It is based on the views of various participants in the programme including chief executive officers (CEOs) of small businesses, technical advisers, consultants, IT vendors and programme administrators.

The objective of this paper is to examine the impact of a government programme promoting computerization among small businesses in Singapore using a programme evaluation methodology. Such a study will provide empirical data to help policy planners evaluate the effectiveness of government incentives on computerization (Tornatzky and Luria, 1992), specifically in the context of small businesses. The conceptual framework involving the services and incentives under the SECP are briefly described in the next section. Next, we describe the programme evaluation methodology utilized. The results of the first and second parts of the programme evaluation are then presented.

## Conceptual model

The conceptual model for this study is shown in Figure 1. The outcome variable is called computerization success. The conceptual model shows that the effects of the SECP incentives on computerization success are indirect, mediated by variables identified in the literature as key factors of computerization success. We will discuss each component of the model below.

## The Small Enterprise Computerization Programme

The SECP is administered by three separate government agencies: the National Computer Board (NCB), the Economic Development Board (EDB) and the Institute of Systems Science (ISS). The objectives of the SECP are to motivate the small businesses to exploit IT in their operations, to handhold the small businesses in their computerization processes and to help develop the computer consultancy and software development industry in Singapore. The SECP is open to local small businesses which have at least 30% local equity, have fixed productive assets (defined as factory building, machinery and equipment) of not more than S\$12 million (S\$1.00 = US\$0.60 approximately) or have less than 100 employees, except for businesses in the manufacturing sector.

The six main phases of a typical computerization project under the SECP are (1) screening, orientation and preliminary study, (2) consultant selection, (3) system study, (4) reviewing hardware and software solutions and selecting the preferred solution, (5) implementation and (6) post-implementation. Table 1 shows the six main phases, the major tasks within each phase and the people responsible for the tasks. In an earlier study, Gable and Raman (1992) focused primarily on the computer system selection stage which includes the phases covering system study (phase 3) and review alternatives (phase 4). In this study, the focus is primarily on businesses that have completed all six phases of the SECP. In addition, we surveyed various types of participants and participants in various stages of completion. Hence, this study provides a more complete evaluation of the SECP.

Government programmes need to conform to certain principles; they must be targeted and they need to be comprehensive (Tornatzky and Luria, 1992). No programme ‘silver bullet’ is possible as the phenomena of technological change, computerization included, are complex. In the case of the SECP, it is a computerization programme targeted specifically at small businesses. It also offers a variety of services and incentives to help small businesses in their computerization. These services and incentives are briefly described below.

## Requirements Assessment Service

The Requirements Assessment Service (RAS) was introduced as the front line of the SECP in the middle of 1989. It is conducted by an officer from the NCB and is made up of two parts, namely, the RAS interview and the RAS report. The RAS interview is a halfday discussion whereby the NCB officer and the CEO of the small business meet face-to-face to cover a requirements assessment, a discussion on all the government support schemes available that are beneficial to the particular small business and brainstorming for any potential areas of application of IT in the small business. The RAS report is then prepared and submitted to the CEO of the small business for review after 2–3 weeks. The report would make one of three possible recommendations: (1) there is no need to computerize, (2) register with and computerize under the SECP or (3) proceed to computerize on their own.

## Education and IT awareness seminars

A series of 3-hour seminars are presented on a monthly basis. The subjects covered range from general computerization topics to specific software. These seminars are targeted at CEOs and the senior executives of small businesses who are not sure how to go about computerizing their operations and would like to learn more. The seminars are coordinated by the ISS and lecturers are drawn from the local academic institutions, the NCB and also registered consultancy firms and IT vendors. The seminars are highly subsidized and participants attending the seminars are only required to pay a nominal fee.

The adviser's role is (1) to conduct an initial study of the potential of the business for computerization, (2) to assist in the selection of a consulting firm to carry out detailed feasibility and systems studies and (3) to advise the CEO throughout the system development and implementation process. An informal role of the adviser is to help the small businesses gain a realistic expectation of the potential benefits of their computerization. The adviser also acts in the capacity of an arbiter to resolve disputes between the small business and the external experts such as the consultant and the IT vendor.

## Technical expertise and support

A consulting firm is engaged (1) to conduct detailed feasibility and system studies, (2) to develop system specifications, (3) to evaluate and select a vendor company and (4) to (optionally) supervise the implementation by the vendor company. As a measure to ensure high-quality consultants, the SECP administrator evaluates the potential consultants and maintains a register of experienced consultants who are qualified to offer consulting services to small businesses that joined the SECP. This mechanism helps to control the quality of the consulting services provided to the small businesses under the programme.

## Financial assistance

As a computerization project is not cheap and small businesses tend to suffer from cash flow problems, the SECP provides various types of financial assistance to lessen the financial burden on the small businesses. There are four types of financial assistance: (1) up to 70% subsidy of the fee of the SECP adviser, (2) up to 70% subsidy of the fee of the consultant to conduct the feasibility study, (3) up to 50% subsidy of the cost of retaining the consultant to supervise the systems development and implementation by the IT vendor and (4) loans at low interest rates and favourable repayment terms to meet the costs of the hardware and software.

## Key factors of success

Since Ein-Dor and Segev (1978) identified organizational context as a determinant of computerization effectiveness, researchers have studied the influence of organizational variables on computerization success in small business. Based on a review of previous research on small business computerization, four variables were identified to be key factors: (1) CEO support, (2) user involvement, (3) consultant effectiveness and (4) vendor support (Raymond, 1985; Lees, 1987; Lees and Lees, 1987; DeLone, 1988; Montazemi, 1988; Gable, 1991). Being the most influential person in a small business, the CEO is in a position to identify critical business applications and has the ability to ensure sufficient resources for the projects (Lees, 1987; DeLone, 1988; Gable, 1991). Having a high user involvement is also beneficial to IT projects as it can result in a better fit of the computer system with the business requirements. Involving the potential users in an information requirements analysis can also inculcate a feeling of ownership and reduce resistance to change (Lees and Lees, 1987; Montazemi, 1988). Drawing upon prior experience, a consultant can contribute to a successful computerization (Simon, 1990; Gable, 1991). The consultant can increase the chances of successful computerization by utilizing proper project management techniques and system development methodologies. Similarly, the quality of the vendor support will determine whether the computer system is useful to the small business (Lees, 1987; Gable, 1991). If the system does not fulfil the business requirements or is not maintained properly, it will fall into disuse.

## Computerization success

Computerization success can be defined as the extent to which a computer system actually contributes to achieving organizational goals, i.e. its effect on organizational performance. Two popular surrogates for computerization success in the small business literature are user satisfaction and organizational impact (DeLone, 1983; Raymond, 1985; Lees, 1987; Montazemi, 1988). User satisfaction is based on the notion that if the computer system meets the requirements of the users, the users' satisfaction with the system will increase. Conversely, if the computer system does not prove to be useful to their jobs, the users will become dissatisfied. Organizational impact refers to the impact of the computer system on the performance of the business or its employees. The performance measures may include profits, sales, competitive advantage, staff productivity and the quality of decision making. In this study, perceptual measures of user satisfaction and various organization impacts are used as the surrogates of computerization success.

## The criteria of programme success

The computerization projects of the SECP businesses are systematic, structured and under the guidance of the external experts. They can be characterized as having a ‘normative’ approach that incorporates the key factors for successful computerization prescribed by the literature. The common factors for successful computerization, such as CEO support, user involvement, an effective consultant and good vendor support, are present in all the SECP projects. The same approach is adopted for all the projects; each has an independent adviser, an approved consultant and a vendor. The process of computerization is well structured: (1) the information requirements of an SECP business are determined by the consultant, (2) requests for a proposal are sent to the potential vendors, (3) the proposals from the vendors are evaluated by the consultant, (4) representatives from the SECP business together with the consultant and the adviser attend demonstrations of short-listed system solutions by the vendors, (5) the consultant recommends a particular solution and (6) the selected vendor implements the solution. To increase the level of CEO support and user involvement, the SECP stipulates that a steering committee consisting of the CEO and other representatives of the business, the adviser and the consultant must be formed at the onset of the project. This committee reviews the progress of the project regularly. Similarly, the level of user involvement is increased by involving the users during the information requirements analysis and demonstration of software packages by the vendors.

In contrast, the computerization projects of non-SECP businesses are not controlled in any systematic manner. Here, computerization may be considered as a purely business decision, driven by the vision of the CEOs concerned. While some have engaged consultants to carry out an information requirements analysis before approaching vendors, others rely on the vendors completely. There is also no formal mechanism to increase the level of CEO support and user involvement in the project. However, these non-SECP businesses recognize the benefits of computerization and have the determination to go ahead on their own, rather than wait to get onto the government programme. Hence, these innovative businesses are very committed to their computerization projects.

The preceding discussion of the research model forms the basis for deriving the criteria of programme success. In order to conclude that the SECP is successful, the SECP businesses must achieve a high level of computerization success in terms of user satisfaction and various organizational impacts. Further, the SECP businesses should achieve high scores for the key factors of computerization success such as CEO support, user involvement, consultant effectiveness and vendor support. In addition, the performance of SECP businesses that have government assistance should be at least as good as non-SECP businesses that have adequate resources to computerize on their own.

## Research method

## Research design

In this study, a programme evaluation methodology is utilized. Researchers of programme evaluation have identified numerous methods of evaluation (Franklin and Thrasher, 1976; Patton, 1980; Posavac and Carey, 1980; Rossi and Freeman, 1989; Fricke, 1991). These methods include randomized 'true' experiments, quasi-experiments, regression discontinuity studies, before-and-after studies, panel studies, time-series analysis and judgmental assessments. The choice of method to use depends on whether the programme is being delivered to all members of a target population or a subset, i.e. whether the programme is a full-coverage or partial-coverage programme.

A good research design for outcome evaluation is the non-equivalent control group design, which makes use of the pre-test and post-test design for two groups. In this approach, the evaluator expects to observe a larger improvement between pre-test and post-test for the programme group than for the comparison group. However, this approach could not be used in this study because no pre-test data were available and it is impossible to reconstruct the test post hoc. Instead, another quasi-experimental approach was used. A group of small businesses, of comparable size and in similar business sectors, that have computerized on their own without government assistance was chosen as the comparison group. This comparison group is referred to as the non-SECP businesses. The SECP is essentially a partial-coverage programme with the SECP businesses being the participants and the non-SECP businesses being the non-participants.

The method used in the first part of the programme evaluation was the cross-sectional survey using a sample survey on quasi-experimental groups. The SECP businesses and the non-SECP businesses constitute the experimental and the control groups, respectively. The two groups are compared on their computerization characteristics, levels of key success factors and computerization success. For the second part of the evaluation, feedback was solicited from the perspectives of the SECP advisers, consultants and IT vendors. Having views from multiple respondents helps to ensure a more complete and balanced understanding of the impact of the SECP and the effectiveness of the SECP services and incentives. Beside gathering data to evaluate the computerization success of the SECP, we wanted to assess the progress of SECP businesses that are undergoing the programme, the reasons for SECP businesses to leave the programme and the effectiveness of the RAS.

## Survey instruments

Eight questionnaires were designed for this study. These questionnaires were directed at eight groups of respondents. They were (1) small businesses that have computerized under the SECP (SECP businesses), (2) small businesses that have computerized on their own (non-SECP businesses), (3) small businesses that are currently undergoing computerization under the SECP, (4) small businesses that have dropped out of the SECP, (5) small businesses that have completed the RAS (RAS businesses), (6) SECP advisers, (7) consultants and (8) IT vendors.

The questionnaires were developed with the help of an expert panel of three SECP advisers and two SECP

The questionnaires for both the SECP and non-SECP businesses were designed to gather data on the computerization characteristics including hardware and software, the key factors of computerization success including CEO support, user involvement, consultant effectiveness and vendor support and computerization success including user satisfaction and various performance impacts. They also included questions on the effectiveness of the SECP and its various components such as the provision of external experts, IT seminars and subsidies and the reasons for computerizing and their future plans for computerization. For the non-SECP businesses, we included questions to determine the reasons why they did not computerize under the government assistance scheme. For the SECP businesses that dropped out of the programme, we included questions to elicit their reasons for leaving the programme. The SECP advisers, consultants and vendors were solicited for their views on the common problems faced by these small businesses in computerization. They were also asked for suggestions to improve the programme. The effectiveness of the SECP administrator and the usefulness of the RAS report were the focus of the questionnaire targeted at the small businesses that have undergone the RAS. All the questionnaires were pilot tested on respondents from each of the eight groups and were found to be comprehensive and easy to understand. This suggests that the questionnaires satisfied content validity.

## Data collection procedure

The data used in this study were collected by mailing the relevant questionnaires to the small businesses, advisers, consultants and IT vendors based on mailing lists provided by the SECP administrators. The questionnaires were sent together with an explanatory letter and a stamped return envelope. The respondents were assured of the confidentiality of their responses. Interviews were also conducted with a subset of the respondents from all the groups. Additional data on the business sector, number of employees, annual sales revenue, fixed assets and government subsidy and loans granted, of the target respondents were collected from the archives of the SECP administrators.

## Data analysis procedure

As the data were mainly categorical, the non-parametric $\chi^{2}$ test was used to determine whether there is any difference between the SECP and non-SECP businesses. A condition for applying the $\chi^{2}$ test is that no more than 20% of the expected observations of each frequency table are less than five (Conover, 1980). Because the expected observations of some cells in these analyses were less than five, the expected observations were increased by combining adjacent categories into a single pooled category (Siegal and Castellan, 1989). The results of the first part of the programme evaluation, i.e. the outcome evaluation, are presented in the following section.

Having confirmed that the samples of the SECP and non-SECP businesses are comparable in terms of their organizational and computerization characteristics, we proceeded to evaluate both the key factors of computerization success and their computerization success.

The computerization successes of the SECP and non-SECP businesses are summarized in Table 4. The IT seminars organized by the SECP have helped the small businesses to understand the importance of CEO support and user involvement in the computerization projects. The stringent selection process for the IT experts who are allowed to participate in the SECP has increased the probability of securing high quality consultants and IT vendors, hence increasing the possibility of successful computerization.

On comparing the levels of CEO support, user involvement, consultant effectiveness and vendor effectiveness between the SECP and non-SECP businesses, we found no significant differences except in consultant effectiveness. The non-SECP businesses have a wider range of differences in consultant effectiveness than the SECP businesses. This finding reflects the wide variation in the quality of consultants in the market. In the case of the SECP, because of the stringent consultant selection process there is less variance in the quality of the consultants. Hence, the results show that the SECP businesses can achieve the same levels of performance for each key factor of computerization success as the non-SECP businesses.

The data show that computerization has a greater impact in the intangible benefits that the tangible benefits for the SECP businesses. The main intangible benefits secured by the SECP businesses are in the areas of user satisfaction, staff productivity, the quality of decision making and the quality of service. The majority of the SECP businesses observed only average impacts on the tangible benefits such as sales and profit. The pattern is similar for the non-SECP businesses. This finding suggests that the use of computers has to be assimilated into the businesses for a longer period before more tangible outcomes can be observed (Brynjolfson, 1993).

There is no significant difference in user satisfaction between the SECP and non-SECP businesses. Both groups of businesses have high percentages of satisfied users. There are also no significant differences between the two groups of businesses in terms of the impact of computerization on sales revenue, pre-tax profit, staff productivity, quality of decision making and quality of service. These results are not surprising as government services and incentives should not discourage small businesses from computerizing on their own, if they can do it themselves.

## Evaluation of the SECP services and incentives

This section reviews the results of part two of the programme evaluation. For management and administrative purposes, it is important to assess the views of the participants on the specific services and incentives provided by the programme. In their respective questionnaires and during the interviews, the participants were asked to evaluate the effectiveness of the SECP, the RAS, the IT seminars, the external IT experts and the financial assistance provided.

## The SECP success

The SECP businesses were asked to evaluate the effectiveness of the SECP and whether their computerization would have been as successful without the SECP. The results are summarized in Table 5. The data show that 70% of the respondents felt that the SECP has been beneficial to them, while 80% felt that the SECP is an effective programme. Further, 77% of the respondents said that the computerization would not have been as successful without the SECP. In addition, 75% of the SECP businesses that were undergoing the programme said that they were confident that their computerization would be successful; 18% were neutral while only 7% were uncertain. These findings, together with the earlier results of high levels of computerization success in the SECP businesses, show that the SECP services and incentives have reduced the risk of computerization failure for small businesses that do not have adequate financial resources and technical expertise and encouraged them to computerize. The SECP has also helped these small businesses to achieve a high level of computerization success which might not have been possible otherwise.

## Participation in the SECP

The most popular reason for businesses to computerize is time savings. These small businesses recognize the need to increase the productivity of the workers in view of the tight labour market. Hence, the majority of the computerized applications are of the transaction processing nature. However, not all the businesses computerized under the SECP. Of the 67 non-SECP businesses, 39% indicated that they have not heard of the SECP. Even among those non-SECP businesses that had heard of the SECP, 25% did not really know what the SECP is all about; 21% felt that they have sufficient knowledge of IT and were confident of computerizing on their own while 8% thought the SECP to be too bureaucratic. Among the SECP businesses that dropped out of the programme, the most common reason for leaving was staff turnover. As the labour market is very competitive, it is difficult to retain good staff, particularly those with IT knowledge. Hence, when the key staff in charge of computerization leave, the small business is left in limbo and cannot continue with the SECP project.

## Evaluation of the RAS

The SECP had a high drop-out rate in its earlier years. However, since the introduction of the RAS, there has been a significant reduction. Previously, some of the small businesses that joined the SECP did so because of the perceived free hand-outs from the government. As such, they were not as committed to the computerization project as the non-SECP businesses. Over 80% of the RAS businesses felt that the technical knowledge and communication skills of the NCB officials who conducted the interviews were above average. Nearly 70% of the respondents felt that the RAS reports were above average in identifying business operations to be computerized, hardware and software needs and the costs and benefits of computerization. Hence, we can conclude that the RAS has been an effective programme in increasing the chances of successful computerization by SECP businesses.

## Evaluation of the IT seminars

Over 70% of the small businesses felt that the subsidized IT seminars were useful with only 4% saying otherwise. The SECP advisers, consultants and IT vendors were also asked to identify the problems encountered by the SECP businesses in their computerization. The two most commonly faced problems were a lack of knowledge about IT and the computerization process and financial and manpower shortages. The SECP businesses were uncertain about the responsibilities of the adviser, the consultant and the IT vendor in the computerization project. They were also reluctant to commit resources to the project.

The SECP advisers, consultants and IT vendors had a number of suggestions on how they and the government could help the small businesses address the problems identified. The adviser, consultant and IT vendor could help to educate the small businesses about IT and provide the technical assistance which the small businesses lacked. The government through the SECP could alleviate some of the concerns of small businesses by providing more detailed and a variety of IT seminars and be more flexible in granting subsidies and grants for advisory and consultancy services and for hardware and software purchase. The publicity of the SECP, particularly of successfully computerized businesses, should be increased. This could be accomplished by organizing site visits to successfully computerized SECP businesses or forums to improve the communication between the SECP administrators and small businesses.

## Evaluation of the external IT experts

At the start of the programme, there were 57 active advisers in the SECP. However, this number dropped dramatically to only nine due to the shortage of qualified and experienced advisers. This was despite the fact that a majority of the SECP businesses were satisfied with the SECP adviser assigned to them. Fifty-seven per cent of the respondents said that they would not be as successful without an adviser. Only 6% felt that they could do better on their own. A plausible reason for the high attrition rate of the advisers is that the remuneration for their services was not compatible with the time they had to spend on these projects. On further examination, we found that 70% of the respondents rated the adviser's experience and effectiveness in defining the project scope, selecting the consultant and reviewing j119 the consultant's recommendations as satisfactory. However, only slightly more than half of the respondents were satisfied with the effectiveness of the adviser in resolving differences between the parties involved in the project.

Over 62% of the respondents felt that they could not have done better without engaging the consultant to perform a feasibility study and implementation management while less than 18% felt that they could have done better. On further examination, the characteristics of the consultant that the respondents were most satisfied with were the consultant's experience and technical competence. This suggests that the SECP, through a formal screening process, has been successful in providing a quality consultancy service to the small businesses.

In comparison with the SECP adviser and consultant, there was a higher number of respondents who rated the vendors as ineffective (10%). This is, however, still lower than the 23% of the non-SECP respondents who rated the effectiveness of the vendors as poor. By joining the SECP, small businesses can ensure a higher quality of external IT expertise for computerization. On closer examination, we found that the characteristics of the vendor which the respondents were most dissatisfied with were the quality of the hardware and software documentation and the training provided by the vendor.

## Evaluation of the financial assistance

Among all the services provided by the SECP, the low-interest loan scheme for purchasing hardware and software was ranked the most useful by the small businesses. The next most useful financial assistance was the subsidies for the consultancy and advisory services. More than 80% of the responding SECP businesses found these subsidies to be useful. This finding is consistent with the view that small businesses generally suffer from tight financial constraints, particularly cash flow (Welsh and White, 1981).

## Conclusion

In summary, like many other government programmes, particularly social programmes aimed at the under privileged, the SECP has helped small businesses that lack financial resources and internal IT expertise and have poor IT knowledge to catch up with other small businesses that possess these resources. Due to the government incentives and assistance programme, the SECP businesses are able to achieve a similar level of computerization success as the non-SECP businesses. The SECP has reduced the risk of computerization failure and makes computerization more attractive to resource-poor small businesses that would not have done so without government services and incentives.

Based on the views of the various participants in the programme, the SECP may be considered to be an effective computerization programme. Its portfolio of comprehensive services and incentives were all rated highly by the participants. The participants also felt that the small businesses could not have done better without the SECP. The SECP businesses have highly satisfied users and experienced a positive impact on their performance. The levels of key success factors such as CEO support, user involvement, consultant effectiveness and vendor effectiveness were also high.

Further evidence of the programme's success is provided by the computerization plans of the SECP businesses over the next 3 years. Now that these small businesses have attained a basic level of IT expertise, they want to try out new IT innovations to improve their competitiveness. Many SECP businesses are moving to implement local area networks (LANs) to link up their computer systems. Another major application the SECP businesses are looking at is electronic data exchange (EDI) systems, e.g. TradeNet (Cash et al., 1992). This trend suggests that not only has the SECP been successful in helping small businesses to computerize, it has also encouraged organizational learning within the businesses. These small businesses are now more confident of their capability and are willing to conduct other computerization projects that will be beneficial to them. By utilizing more sophisticated systems, the level of computerization success in a small business is likely to increase (Raymond, 1985).

A recommendation from this study is that the SECP administrators need to promote this programme to small businesses that want to computerize to increase their competitiveness, but could not do so due to a lack of financial resources and IT expertise. The study has found that a majority of non-SECP businesses have not heard of the programme. Others who have heard of the programme do not really know what it is all about. Hence, there is a need for greater communication between the SECP administrators and the small businesses on the types of government incentives and assistance available for computerization. To improve information dissemination, the SECP administrators are now working with trade associations to promote the SECP among their members.

The findings also have implications for programme evaluation research. In this study, a quasi-experiment technique was used to conduct the programme evaluation. As it was impractical to conduct a randomized experiment in the real world, the small businesses in both the experimental and control groups were selected based on their prior computerization experience. By setting up a control group among small businesses that have computerized on their own without government incentives, we were able to check for differences against the small businesses that have undergone the SECP. This allowed us to evaluate the performance of the SECP businesses relative to the non-SECP businesses. In addition, multiple viewpoints on the effectiveness of the programme were solicited from various participants. By using data from multiple sources, there is more confidence in the interpretation of the programme evaluation results. Hence, a combination of a

quasi-experiment technique using a comparison group and the use of multiple viewpoints is an effective practical approach in conducting programme evaluation.

Chee Sing Yap is Head of Information Systems Division, Department of Information Systems and Computer Science, National University of Singapore. Previously he worked at the National Electricity Board of Malaysia. His research interests include IT in small businesses, national IT policy and telework. His publications have appeared in international refereed journals. He serves on the editorial boards of Information & Management and Information Technology for Development.

James Y. L. Thong is an assistant professor in the Department of Information and Systems Management, Hong Kong University of Science and Technology.
