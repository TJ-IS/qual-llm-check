# Journal of Information Technology (1997) 12, 121–129

# The SIMA: a practical approach to information technology management

RICK KLOMPÉ and LEON POTHUIZEN
InterProm b.v., PO Box 8003, 3900 CA Veenendaal, The Netherlands

The management of information technology (IT) must be organized efficiently and most of all effectively in order to support the organization's business processes optimally. The IT department must focus more on its customers and the services provided to support their business processes, thus enabling customers to gain competitive advantages from IT. Therefore the IT department should be treated as a separate business unit with its own characteristic services and business processes. The complexity of the IT infrastructure must be reduced by breaking it down into pieces according to the type of function its components perform. Each part must be treated as a separate unit, with services, relations and responsibilities in its own right. All this requires a major reorganization of the IT department. This paper presents the first results in the development of a practical method for organizing the IT management function and for effecting the inevitable organizational changes.

## Introduction

## Background

As enterprises become more dependent upon information processing systems to support them meeting their corporate objectives, the need for system management grows (Langsford and Moffett, 1993). However, a large part of the research on computerized data processing is focused mainly on the development of new technology, i.e. adoption, innovation, engineering and implementation (Kling, 1987). Maintenance is a subject apparently lacking in glamour (Swanson and Beath, 1989). However, the development costs of new systems make up only a small part of the total investment costs for information systems. Most of the expenses of information technology (IT) consist of the maintenance and exploitation costs such as service and support, downtime and management (Kay et al., 1995; Pisello, 1996), taking up 70–80% of the initial investments in hardware and software.

This paper presents a starting point for an IT management (re)organization method based on existing theories and models as well as practical experiences.

## IT management

Information system management includes all the tasks necessary to keep implemented information systems running, from operator tasks and maintenance to end-user support and watching the users' demands regarding, for example, the functionality, availability or performance. An information system consists of hardware, software, data, people and the procedures they follow. It is often defined as a more temporary component making use of more permanent infrastructure components such as network and server facilities to perform its function. We prefer to look at the infrastructure from a business perspective, making an information system part of the infrastructure as well. From that point of view the infrastructure consists of all the information system components, including people and procedures, supporting the organizations business processes.\* However, in most organizations information systems management concerns only the design and management of the technical components of the systems, while the business unit using the information system is responsible for the management of its employees including the procedures they follow. Therefore, this paper will regard IT as all the hardware, software and data supporting the organization's business processes and IT infrastructures and IT management (ITM).

The ITM function, being the organizational unit performing the management of IT, is positioned between two influential forces: the time to market and new IT technologies. First the ITM function has to meet the business needs of its customers. $^{†}$ As these needs change in time, flexibility and the ability to adjust to changed user demands is a must. Second, the ITM function is confronted with new technologies providing new opportunities to support the business processes of its customers.

In addition to customers and suppliers, the overall organization can be defined as a third party of interest determining the overall IT budget and strategy.

## ITM issues

ITM concerns the management of all the IT components supporting the organization's business processes. The set of IT components to be managed can be very complex and dynamic. In this section we will illustrate this by describing some of the most important technological and organizational movements and problems regarding the ITM function.

The main movements in IT influencing the ITM function as we see them both in the literature (Langsford and Moffett, 1993; Sprague and McNurlin, 1993; Tschichholz et al., 1996) as in practice are as follows.

(1) The rapid evolution of technology.

(2) The distribution of processing power.

(3) The diversification of products and suppliers.

(4) The outsourcing of IT tasks.

(5) Integration.

(6) The increasing maturity of end-users.

(7) Increasing costs.

(8) The increasing customer orientation.

IT is a rapidly evolving branch. Hardware prices are dropping continuously whereas the complexity and influence of IT is increasing rapidly. The processing power is distributed from mainframes to networked computers with cooperative processing functions. Systems can stretch over several organizations and components and services can be physically distributed. The size and complexity of these distributed systems introduce problems due to the number of components. Corporations are moving to a combination of centralized and decentralized modes of work both existing at one time, as downsizing and in particular client/server techniques do not meet the users' requirements in all situations. Since this movement is like a pendulum continuously swinging between centralization and decentralization, the IT infrastructure will remain dynamic and continuously changing (Sprague and McNurlin, 1993). For both modes various types of components from various suppliers are used. Organizations are confronted more and more with different suppliers specializing in fewer and more specific products or services. Equally, Stajano (in Langsford and Moffett, 1993) stated that there is a movement away from proprietary systems towards open systems and standards and away from reliance on single-vendor solutions towards open, multivendor, heterogeneous systems.

The outsourcing of both development and ITM tasks is increasing and will become a matter of interest as more and more organizations turn back to their core business. As a result of outsourcing the number of parties involved in the development and management of the organization's IT infrastructure is growing, which is also influencing relations with external suppliers.

Despite the growing decentralization and diversification of products and suppliers, organizations want to integrate all their IT components into an IT infrastructure, making all the available facilities accessible to every point in the organization. Although the IT components can differ considerably from one another and may provide very diverse facilitates, they can all interact.

End-users are maturing and becoming more aware of the possibilities of IT, resulting in growing demands for functionality, availability and performance. Due to a considerable decrease in the costs and an increase in the performance of hardware and software, the range of applications of IT has become far greater in the last few years. This has led to higher user demands resulting in larger and more complex information systems and increasing instead of decreasing IT costs. In anticipation of these changed demands, the customer and thus the organization's business objectives become central, characterizing the latest innovation wave: reaching the customer.

Another characteristic feature of (most) IT infrastructures, apart from their complexity and dynamic nature, is the evolution of systems and working methods over the years. Many networks and systems have been developed in an ad hoc manner and many distributed systems have often evolved by implementing existing services on new equipment (Langsford and Moffett, 1993). Despite the fact that information systems are often outdated, they are hard to replace as they perform a critical function in the organization's main business processes.

Many organizations are confronted with these issues, putting a heavy load on the ITM function. However, most organizations only experience two main consequences, i.e. too high IT costs and disappointing contributions of IT to their business processes. This can be explained by the fact that most organizations are managed mainly on input and output, these being financial aspects such as the number of staff, the budget, turnover and profit and the range and level of services provided to the customers. IT costs increase disproportionately when compared to, for instance, the growth of turnover. As the contribution of IT to business processes is hard to quantify, the costs are easily considered to be too high. However, the costs can only be cut down safely and effectively when their cause and impact are fully known.

To solve these and other problems and to enable the ITM function to respond easily to changed user demands in the future, the ITM function must change. Sprague and McNurlin (1993) foresaw a dramatically revised job description for the ITM function arising in the next few years, ensuring that information services are tied to business needs.

## Organizing the ITM function

The major lesson of the 1980s is that technology alone does not yield success and that strategy, people, business operations and technology should be aligned (Sprague and McNurlin, 1993). As 60% of the effectiveness of ITM can be attributed to the organizational alignment of people and processes (Gartner Group, 1994), improvements in ITM should start from an organizational point of view. The necessary savings on operating expenses mean that traditional methods no longer work and old rules and assumptions must be challenged (Sprague and McNurlin, 1993). Broadhead (1996) stated that one should search for a way of IT management that is linked up closely to how the organization and the business are managed. However, in reality we see that most organizations still believe they can organize their ITM function in the old way just by implementing one or more tools for network and systems management. This idea might be reinforced by the absence of practically applicable methods for ITM and the wide variety of tools, e.g. automating operator tasks or monitoring the performance or capacity of the IT components. In spite of a wide range of ITM tools, the maturity of the tools is far from a satisfactory level, making process and organizational alignment the most critical factors in ITM effectiveness (Gartner Group, 1994).

Today's solution has to recognize and accept diversity, turning it to the users' advantage while presenting an integrated and completed picture to managers taking into account technology as well as environmental factors, business objectives and the external business environment (Langsford and Moffet, 1993). One should organize around outcomes, not tasks, treat geographically dispersed resources as if they were centralized, link parallel activities rather than integrate them and put decisions and responsibilities where the work is done (Sprague and McNurlin, 1993). The ITM function should be treated as a separate business unit with its own products and services and the business processes to provide them. This, however, requires major changes in both the organizational structure of the ITM function, the way of working and thinking of its employees and the position of the ITM function in the overall IT department.

For many years the search for savings on IT was focused on structuring system development processes, resulting in numerous software development methods. Not until recently have organizations become aware of the importance of organizing their ITM function effectively and efficiently. However, due to the complexity of today's technical infrastructures, no practically applicable methods have been found for solving the overall problems. Most publications are focused only on one or two parts of the IT infrastructure, such as network or database management or on just one single aspect such as performance, capacity or availability management. A practical and applicable overall view on ITM describing the relations between these various areas is still missing.

Our starting point to fill this gap is based on the following statements of Deming (Bladergroan et al., 1995).

(1) Customers are the most important link in the production chain.

(2) Increasing the quality of services does not necessarily lead to high costs.

(3) Decreasing variation is the key to quality.

## The standard interprom management approach

The standard interprom management approach (SIMA) was developed to support project mangers and consultants in (re)organizing the ITM function. The SIMA has been developed from practical experience in addition to a variety of methods, techniques and standards of ITM.

The SIMA is divided into two main parts: a management structure and an approach to (re)organizing the ITM function. The SIMA management structure includes a visualization of the ideas about how the ITM function should be organized and how the problems described above should be tackled. The SIMA approach gives five areas of interest to be considered in (re)organizing the ITM function and includes an overview of the methods and techniques that can be of help.

## Management structure

The SIMA management structure presents an overall solution to organizing the ITM function, by presenting a global demarcation of the tasks and responsibilities. This structure is not intended to be strictly prescriptive or static. Based on the situational factors and characteristics this structure should be customized to the specific situation of each organization and further detailed to create a complete picture of how the ITM function should be organized. The SIMA management structure is based on the following principles.

(1) The ITM function must be customer driven and service oriented.

(2) The contribution of IT must be made clear by specifying the services delivered to the customer.

(3) The complexity of the infrastructure must be reduced by dividing it into separate pieces.

(4) The ITM function should be organized around its own services and business processes.

Based on these principles, in combination with practical experiences of existing management models, a preferred management structure has been defined. This SIMA management structure, which is shown in Figure 1, is based on three existing models as we will describe below.

First, the complexity of the IT infrastructure is reduced by dividing all its components into distinct layers by applying a layer model. This model, based on the DUneT-Management model (van Daalen et al., 1995) and the Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) Reference Model (Tanenbaum, 1988; ISO, 1994), divides the IT infrastructure into six layers. The top of this model represents the service as delivered to the customer. In a number of cases there will be a oneto-one mapping to an application, as services usually consist of just one application. The application layer represents the actual application visible to the end-user. The data layer contains both the application databases and the database management system, whereas the system layer represents the operating systems for running the application (e.g. DOS, UNIX, or Windows). The communication protocols are positioned in the network layer and, finally, the physical network components (e.g. cables, routers and gateways) are represented in the cableware layer.

According to the layer model, as presented in Figure 2, the IT infrastructure can be broken down into smaller pieces and for each layer the responsibilities can be assigned to separate organizations or business units. It is recommended considering each layer as a separate business unit providing services to a higher level and making use of the services from lower levels in the form of semi-manufactured products. For instance, for providing the service of making an application available, facilities from the lower levels such as databases, operating systems and hardware platforms are needed. An example of a service breakdown according to the layer model is presented in Figure 3. The basic idea behind the application of this model is that each layer should be responsible for providing certain services and that each layer should be organized according to this as if it were a separate business unit. These units are now confronted with less complex parts of the IT infrastructure and less variety in the IT components requiring less knowledge.

Figure 3 The service breakdown according to the layer model in figure 1

The second important model incorporated in the SIMA is the service management business process model of the Network Management Forum (NMF) (1995), as shown in Figure 4. This model defines several layers with management processes which provide network services to customers. The customer is positioned at the top of the model and the physical network and IT technology at the bottom. Between these two are four levels for delivering the required service to the customer. First, the customer interface management process provides a single point of contact to each customer. Customer problems are taken care of in the customer care layer, whereas the management of services is positioned in the service/product development and maintenance layer. Finally, the network and systems management layer covers the processes for managing the physical network and IT technology. The management processes needed to deliver the network service are spread over these layers, according to the goal and subject of the layers. A separate information systems management function is defined to watch over the correct and consistent processing of the defined tasks at each level.

![](/api/attachments/3PMSARWB/fulltext/images/68cd2d8281248cace4712ab0089b709fd4f5fe270ff2ca23de80b53df1ab9ea5.jpg)  
Figure 4 A simplified view of the business process model of the Network Management Forum (1995)

The most important features of this model are a customer-driven attitude resulting in a single point of contact, organizing the ITM function in the business processes focused on the provision of services to the customers and a separate function to manage all the business processes, e.g. the quality assurance function.

Although this business process model spreads the management processes over several parts of the organization, the complexity of the infrastructure is still the same, as it concerns all the network components building the service. As mentioned earlier, when organizing the ITM function the complexity of the IT infrastructure must be reduced. Therefore this model is combined with the layer model in Figure 1. Incorporating this process model in each level of the layer model leads to six accumulated models, one for each layer. In this compound model each layer should include all the defined business processes. If activities in several layers are performed within the same organization, the related customer interface processes can best be positioned in a single service layer, whereas the information system management processes can be merged into one overall service management function.

Communication is very important between the various parties described in the SIMA management structure and their environment, in particular when various layers of the management structure are managed by various parties. Communication at the provided service levels should be performed in terms relevant to the customer. A customer asking for a certain application is only interested in the functional terms relevant for the business process it should support, e.g. the number of dossiers to be registered, processed or updated and the availability and performance of these functions. However, when network services are provided, the terminology is completely different. At each interface between two layers, agreements should be made describing the provided service levels. The customer and provider should meet periodically discussing both the realized service levels and the required service levels for the next period. At each level, a single point of contact should be provided to each customer. When the customer and provider are both part of the same organization, it is worth considering whether formal communication and contracts are really necessary.

The IT Infrastructure Library (ITIL) (Central Computer and Telecommunications Agency, 1989) of the British government Centre for Information Systems (CCTA) is a first attempt to provide a method of organizing the ITM function. The ITIL described an ITM function organized in business processes, providing guidelines to ITM organizations. The processes are described by various authors based on the best practices within the private and public sectors. All the processes are divided into nine sets of books, of which the service support set and the service delivery set (see Figure 5) are used most in practice. The current success of the ITIL in a number of Western European countries has lead to a uniform use of the terminology and the consideration of the ITM function in terms of business processes.

Combining the ITIL with the layer model in Figure 2, all the processes should be applicable at each layer as each layer performs the same function, that being the provision of services to customers. As the ITIL is very popular in The Netherlands, many companies ask for it. Therefore, a selection of the ITIL processes have been added to the SIMA management structure as shown in Figure 1.

## Approach

The SIMA approach provides a guide to consultants and project managers performing projects for reorganizing the ITM function. As long as the SIMA is not a complete method of describing the phases and steps in ITM (re)design projects, it should be incorporated in common project management methods.

The SIMA approach consists of five areas of interest for projects (re)organizing the ITM function according to the SIMA management structure: responsibilities, services, processes, techniques and implementation. These five areas can be applied in order of appearance, but mutual dependencies justify a simultaneous and iterative use. First, the current ITM organization must be studied, the IT services provided defined and mapped, the processes of delivering these services designed and the techniques supporting these processes must be selected. Then, the new ITM organization must be implemented applying a method for organizational change. In order to be able to handle the mentioned mutual dependencies, an iterative approach is recommended.

The existing methods, techniques and standards are incorporated in each area providing guidelines to attain the defined objectives. The descriptions and lists of methods and theories given below are not intended to be complete. New methods, models and techniques that might be useful in practice will be added in the future.

## Responsibilities

When starting a project, one must create a clear picture of the current ITM organization. This area provides tools for analysing the current situation and for supporting communication with the organization about both the current and desired situation. For this purpose, a simple matrix has been developed listing the relevant IT objects on the vertical axis and the ITM tasks or processes on the horizontal axis. By asking several persons within the existing organization to fill in the matrix for the current situation, one can easily obtain a good picture of the situation. This same matrix can also be used to analyse current ideas within the organizations working towards the desired situation. For larger projects it might be necessary to repeat this several times and analyse the changes before coming to a final picture of the desired situation. In a later stage of the project it is also possible to use this matrix to draw up an inventory of the current ITM supporting tools available in the organization and to make a blueprint of the ITM supporting tools for the new situation.

## Services

When designing a customer-oriented ITM function, one has to study the services provided to the end-user supporting the customers' business processes. All the services must then be broken down according to the defined layer model as presented in Figure 2. Thus, for each layer the necessary output and input is known and the consequent activities can be defined. By describing the services provided to the customer and how these services are composed of several IT components, agreements can be made with suppliers about meeting the defined service levels. When all the services are mapped, the costs needed to provide the service to the customer can be determined.

## Processes

In many branches, the production lines are commonly organized around the business processes. Although this appeared to be a much more practical approach than a functional or organizational point of view, it took quite some time before IT organizations started describing their activities in business processes. Nowadays, there are some commonly accepted models describing the ITM function in business processes, such as the OSI Reference Model, the Telecommunications Management Network (TMN) (1992), the NMF's business process model and the ITIL. Since the ITIL is very popular among a large number of organizations, it is used in this first description of the SIMA management structure. However, as the ITIL is just a set of best practices describing each process in view of a specific situation, it has to be customized to the situation concerned. However, other process models can be incorporated in the SIMA management structure as well.

## Technology

To carry out the designed ITM processes and tasks one must have all the relevant information available. To perform the ITM function correctly one must be informed about the services provided at each level. Nowadays, there are numerous tools to measure, monitor or manage certain aspects of applications and the technical infrastructure. Platforms such as Open View or Tivoli make it possible to perform the ITM function centrally.

Making use of the available tools optimally, one should describe in detail what information is needed and analyse what operator tasks can be best automated. Within each organization or independent business unit, one must strive for an integrated ITM information system with, if necessary, a link to similar systems of suppliers and customers. The main purpose of this integrated system is supporting the ITM function in providing the services defined according to the service levels recorded in the agreements with the customers. Before buying tools this information system must be designed carefully. The most important issue in this design process is that one selects tools that fit, not tools that can do most.

## Implementation

The ITM function can only be improved when the processes and procedures designed are actually implemented in the organization, making sure that every process owner adopts the new way of working. Changing someone's working methods is very hard and could lead to a lot of resistance. It is very important that both the management and employees adopt the new way of thinking and accept changes in their way of working. In most of our projects we make use of workgroups in which various persons from within the organization participate during the design of the organization and processes. Ensuring that there is communication between these persons and their colleagues, the process of change can be started up even before the actual implementation starts. This area can be illustrated by methods for organizational change, organizational learning and business process redesign/re-engineering.

## First experiences

The first experiences with the SIMA have been very positive. Organizations have been pleased to see that their ITM function is organized on the basis of a well-considered view and policy on ITM. They find the SIMA to be very pragmatic and foreseeing for a solution that is appropriate to their specific situation. The SIMA management structure, for instance, can as easily be customized into a model with four or five layers as one with seven or eight, whereas the processes defined at each layer can just as easily be based on other models than the ITIL. During the detailed designing of the desired situation according to the SIMA management structure, the serviceability and applicability of the processes and procedures is leading to the prevention of the designing of a bureaucratic and inflexible ITM function.

Organizations have also been pleased to obtain a clear picture of the project plan and goals and obtain an understanding of why certain choices were made. The theoretical models included in this SIMA supported discussions with the various parties involved. Making certain discussion and choices visible in these models was illuminating and improved communication.

Restructuring the ITM function according to the SIMA management structure has a major impact in the IT department. Therefore, such a business process redesign can be performed better in small incremental steps, in which the SIMA approach is applied for each step.

The optimization of the ITM function should be a quality improvement process, continuously monitoring and evaluating the performance of the ITM function and keeping it in line with new developments and changing requirements. Regular reconsideration of the ITM function in periodic quality improvement cycles is therefore recommended. The evaluation of the changes made and recommendations for further improvements should be input for the next cycle, as well as expectations for future developments.

## Further research

For the (re)organization of the ITM function numerous other issues than the ones mentioned above are also important. Decisions concerning the management of the IT infrastructure must also be made on issues such as standardization, (de)centralization and security. When changing the ITM function, the relations with its environment change as well and should therefore be considered. Further research on the SIMA will focus on these items as well. Through time, the SIMA will be extended with theories, models and views on these items.

The standardization of hardware and software reduces the diversity in IT components and therefore the knowledge necessary to manage the IT infrastructure. Standardization will not necessarily affect the flexibility of the IT infrastructure negatively. When defining flexibility in terms of how fast the IT infrastructure can be adjusted to new technologies or new demands from the business processes to support IT, even the opposite can be true. Standardizing the interfaces between the systems makes it possible to replace the current IT components easily without influencing other components in the IT infrastructure. Standardization is necessary to reduce the complexity of ITM to increase the flexibility.

By managing ITM objects centrally, scale benefits can be obtained provided that the intended degree of centralization is practical and in line with the more common policies of the organization. Today, ITM supporting tools make it possible to manage geographically dispersed objects centrally.

The security of IT and the information processed and stored within the IT infrastructure is a very important item not mentioned explicitly in the SIMA. Although the ITIL for example does not include security, the SIMA considers security to be a separate IT management process. Security should take place at each level of the SIMA management structure. Theories and models on the security of IT will therefore be added to the SIMA.

The reorganization of the ITM function affects not only relationships with the customers and suppliers, but also relations within the overall IT department. The software development function, for example, now has to hand over products that meet certain requirements regarding documentation and maintainability. The software has to meet certain standards for design and implementation before it can move from the test phase into the production phase. For many years software development was considered very important in supporting the organizations business processes with IT. Nowadays, the ITM function should be at least seen as equally important, as it is responsible for the availability, capacity and performance of the operational applications. New applications should be considered as a new functionality to be added to the existing IT infrastructure, making each new application a change in the current situation. Therefore, the ITM function should have a position within the organization it is part of, giving it the authority necessary to take on these new responsibilities. The ITM function must be involved in the specification of provided service levels and participate in the development of new applications or changes. In many organizations, the ITM function has a lot of responsibilities but hardly any authority enabling it to meet the organization's demands. Short-term goals such as the time to market of new products are put above more long-term goals like the costs which are affected by the efficient and effective management of the IT supporting these new products.

The issues mentioned are not incorporated in the SIMA yet because no generic and practical methods or models on this matter exist at this moment. However, the theoretical models incorporated in the SIMA make it possible to visualize and discuss these issues with all parties involved, e.g. using the layer model in Figure 1. New experiences in ITM projects with the SIMA and related issues, will lead to more comprehensive and more detailed descriptions of the SIMA, finally resulting in a complete method for (re)organizing the whole ITM function. As this is expected to be a long way off, this is definitely not our first objective at the present time since we believe in pragmatic and directly applicable solutions.

## Conclusions

This paper describes the SIMA developed to support project managers (re)organizing the ITM function. Based on current experiences in combination with existing theories and models, the SIMA described an ITM function that is customer driven, organized in business processes and that divides the IT infrastructure into separate parts managed by more or less independent business units. The SIMA breaks down the complexity of the IT infrastructure and the services provided. This makes it possible to trace IT costs and to assign them to various ITM tasks, giving IT managers a better view of the costs related to the services provided. The service levels are made explicit, providing an insight into the relation between costs and the contribution of IT to the customer's business processes and business products. Only then can the cost be charged to the customers and the IT costs be cut down sensibly. A systematic and proactive ITM function will improve the service levels and reduce ad hoc activities. End-user support will be improved by providing a single point of contact and a customer-driven attitude at each level in the IT infrastructure. The importance of managing the ITM function at each level within the IT organization is expressed in a separate service management function.

## The SIMA

The reorganization of the ITM function should be realized in small incremental steps, whereas the new ITM organization should be treated as a quality assurance system. Both the SIMA management structure and approach are not prescriptive and must be adjusted to the organization's specific situation. Above all, the SIMA must be applied in a pragmatic manner, to prevent a bureaucratic and inflexible ITM function.

When searching for a practical and directly applicable method for the management of IT, the SIMA will be further developed based on new experiences. ITM issues not yet included in the SIMA will be studied in more detail in order to come to more generic statements, models or theories completing the SIMA.
