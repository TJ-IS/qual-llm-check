# Information technology investment evaluation: evidence and interpretations

MALCOLM KING and LAURIE MCAULAY

Loughborough University, Business School, Ashby Road, Loughborough, Leicester LE11 3TU, UK

Structures are important to the practice of information technology (IT) evaluation. Structures, as evidenced by 14 case studies and the words of six financial directors, are defined here as organizational responses, including user committees and responsibility accounting, quantitative assessments of IT projects, such as financial appraisals, and qualitative assessments. Structures in practice seem to allow for a changing world in which projects are accepted according to business needs and subsequent actions lead accepted projects to be adapted and managed as time passes. In order to manage IT technology in this way, structures alone are important but not sufficient. Active individuals have an important role to play and credibility becomes an important factor in evaluation and implementation. The credibility of IT management is created by its selling of the project in the first place and then by its ability to manage the project in a responsive way. Credible strategists are at least as important as a strategy to the evaluation of IT.

## Introduction

A number of reasons have been offered for the importance of information technology (IT) evaluation. These are, briefly, (1) that evaluating IT is an important problem on the agenda of practitioners (e.g. Brenbaum and Lincoln, 1990; Hochstrasser and Griffiths, 1990; Farbey et al., 1992), (2) expenditure on IT is substantial and needs to be assessed in terms of its value for money (Symons, 1991), (3) there are difficulties in identifying and measuring the costs and benefits, even where appropriate evaluation techniques are available (Powell, 1992) and (4) there is a need for benchmarks to assess the performance of projects and to create learning which improves future evaluations (Farbey et al., 1992). Given the importance of this topic, it is perhaps not surprising that there is a substantial and growing literature which considers the issue of how an evaluation should proceed in practice.

The available literature can be categorized into a reasonably small number of different schools of thought, one of which might be characterized as the 'one best way' approach to IT evaluation. This one best way is taken from a range of economic and accounting measures, sometimes grouped under the heading of 'return on investment' (ROI), which includes the net present value, the internal rate of return, the payback and the accounting rate of return. According to economic and accounting theory, the net present value is the optimum technique to be applied in all situations (Emmanuel et al., 1990; Northcott, 1992; Drury, 1996). It is axiomatic that accepting projects which show a positive net present value leads to an increase in shareholders' wealth (Drury, 1996, pp. 85–9). The present value is increasingly seen to be a theoretical treatment which may not be relevant to practice (Northcott, 1992). Survey evidence has consistently shown that the net present value is the least popular of the economic and accounting techniques in practice (Drury, 1996, p. 401).

The IT and broader management literature has been particularly critical of the approach and has raised a number of objections. These include the following.

(1) The costs and benefits, which are necessary to measure in advance of calculating a net present value, are difficult to quantify (e.g. Powell, 1992).

(2) There is a need to understand cause-effect relationships and such an understanding is missing where leading-edge technologies are involved (Keen, 1995).

(3) The wealth-maximizing model underestimates the complexity of IT technology (Nixon, 1995).

(4) The aims and perspectives of internal and external stakeholders are neglected (Nixon, 1995).

Following from this critique, the IT literature is notable for its creation of a wide variety of suggestions for technologies which might be used and which might overcome the declared deficiencies. Taxonomies of objective methods have been suggested (e.g. Powell, 1992; Keen, 1995) and the role of measurement in evaluation has been considered (e.g. Powell, 1992). These contributions have constructively broadened understandings about the range of available techniques, but the resulting variety poses a new challenge for practice. From the many techniques available, how might practitioners make a choice which ensures that appropriate projects are accepted and inappropriate projects rejected?

A response to this question is provided by contingency theory, the association of particular techniques with particular contexts, so that the contingent variables to be found in a particular situation will determine an appropriate technique. Farbey et al. (1992, 1994), for instance, included the level of certainty about the cause and effect relationships, the nature of the system and the environment as contingent variables. According to their analysis, ROI measures are the most suitable where the outcomes are certain, the system is the support rather than the core and quantitative rather than qualitative benefits are typical of the environment within which the evaluation is undertaken.

No objections have yet been raised to a contingency theory of IT evaluation. This is perhaps not surprising. There has been little opportunity for researchers to develop the empirical testing which is necessary once contingency theory develops beyond a conceptual level, because the relevant literature has emerged only recently. Accounting research has developed a contingency theory of capital investment appraisal but this goes little further than testing the relationship between the choice and technique and size of a company (e.g. Sangster, 1993). This seems to be a curiously underdeveloped line of enquiry, in particular because there have been contingency theory developments and empirical testing in other accounting areas. Unfortunately, the results do not appear to be encouraging. For instance, despite considerable testing of a variety of models, no definitive conclusions have been drawn for transfer pricing (Emmanuel et al., 1990).

The accounting literature has preferred to accept the one best way solution, without delving into alternatives in the way that the IT literature appears to have done. The accounting literature has taken an alternative path, by considering process models and from process models has linked evaluation with the importance of context in a different way to that proposed by contingency theory. King (1975) provided an early challenge to the accounting wisdom of the one best way by suggesting a process model as a better way of looking at investment opportunities. King's (1975) model suggested stages comprising project triggering, screening, definition (the analysis and generation of feasible alternatives), evaluation and transmission. It is interesting to note that evaluation is presented as only one stage in the overall management of capital investment. The early model has been severally adapted and has generally come to be represented by the feedback cycle which embraces the identification of potential investments, project definition and screening, analysis and acceptance, implementation and the post-implementation review (Northcott, 1992, p. 10). Each of these stages have become topics for research in their own right.

King's (1975) analysis included a concern for the ‘external influence’ and ‘organizational influence’. These factors are also evident in the IT literature. Symons (1991, p.211), for instance, used Pettigrew's (1985) distinction between the outer and inner context, essentially the same fundamental constructs introduced by King (1975), to argue that ‘evaluation must be based on a thorough understanding of content, context and process’. Process model approaches to IT evaluation have thus come to share an interest in context with contingency theory, although the respective schools of thought have different ways of progressing this interest.

A feature of these schools of thought is the prominence which is often given to determinism. For the advocates of the one best way approach, success in IT evaluation is determined by the acceptance of projects which show a positive net present value. For those who suggest alternative approaches, whether quantitative or qualitative in nature, there remains an implicit assumption that selecting an appropriate evaluation technique will secure a successful choice of projects. For contingency theory, success is given by applying a technique which is determined by the context within which the evaluation takes place. For process models, success follows from adhering to an appropriate procedure.

This deterministic interpretation of the reality of evaluation has a particular feature. It relies upon a structural response to the problem of evaluating IT. The term ‘structural’ is used here to denote the mechanisms, tools, techniques or systems designed by people or resulting from interactions between people, but which are intended to operate independently of any single, unique individual. Structure implies a pattern and regularization which operates primarily at the level of the totality of human experience and not at the level of the individual. According to this definition, a net present value measurement is a structural response to IT evaluation because the net present value is presumed to provide a solution which works irrespective of the individual character, values or personality of the human agents involved. The net present value is presumed by those who promote the one best way to be appropriate, for example within Shell or Motorola or amongst Western Civilization or countries in the Pacific rim.

A structural response, such as the calculation of the net present value, appears to give rise directly to the decision to accept or to reject a project. This in turn is driven by space and time considerations. The space which determines the structural response defines the variables which are taken into account in a net present value calculation or the contingent variables which might dictate the selection of an appropriate financial or non-financial technique. The existing literature shows that such a deterministic approach to evaluation does not fully explain practice. Farbey et al. (1992) found seven projects which were not justified beyond the belief that the project was necessary. Nixon (1995) briefly referred to the role of senior management, including their 'beliefs, assumptions and biases' (p. 277) and of influential others, including financial markets. Powell et al. (1992) found that the most frequently cited justification for the development of decision support systems was the corporate image, pointing to the influence of external parties. Farbey et al. (1992) included leader or follower motives in their analysis, thus implying a degree of voluntarism. In all of these cases the individual agent plays an active role in determining that a project should be accepted. The element of individual agent suggests that structure does not entirely dictate evaluation. There is support for this position in Powell's (1992) linking of the accounting literature with Awad's (1988) assertion for IT that evaluation techniques are a tool to aid judgement and not a replacement for judgement. There is also support to be found in Weill and

Olson's (1989) suggestion that, in practice, top management commitment and the organization's previous experience with IT influence the effectiveness with which successfully evaluated projects are implemented, thus creating key evaluation criteria. Further, the suggestion that an individual agent may be more important than structure is suggested by Brunsson's (1985) more radical position of arguing that 'the organization's main problem is not choosing; it is taking organized action' (p. 31). The desirability of managerial judgement and the need to secure a commitment to action when all evaluations are linked to future and uncertain benefits may be more important than the application of an appropriate evaluation technique.

The remainder of this paper develops the implications of this interaction between structure and individual agency. The ways in which structures depend upon individuals and the ways in which individuals need structures will be explored through the words of influential evaluators and through descriptions of the practice of evaluation. The next section outlines the research from which the evidence is drawn. This is followed by sections which explore the alternative forms of structure encountered in the research. The final two sections consider the role of structure in a changing world and the implications of creating an active role for individuals in the evaluation of IT.

## A case study of IT evaluation structures and a protocol analysis study of the judgement formation of financial directors

Our case study research (Benbasat et al., 1987; Scapens, 1990; Yin, 1994) was based on an investigation of 16 companies. Table 1 provides summary details which show the industry, sales, employees and ownership of each of the companies. The period of the study covered the late 1980s and early 1990s but, because of the kinds of stories which emerged, the study effectively covers the period from the early 1960s to the early 1990s. Managers from throughout the companies were interviewed. The interviews were unstructured (Smith (1995) provided a good summary of the issues and stances relevant to the present study) to allow the company managers to establish their own discussions about the ways in which IT changes take place. The case studies were written up and first draft case studies were presented to the companies. The comments from the companies which were received following the submission of the first drafts were incorporated into the final case study write ups. Finally, the authors of this paper analysed the working papers and revisited the case study write ups and report (King et al., 1991 a,b) with the specific topic of IT evaluation as the focus.

All the case studies shared the characteristic that their IT systems had evolved to broadly the same state of development. The major accounting functions had been computerized and comprised all ledger work concerned with profit and loss and balance sheet transactions and the budgeting data sufficient to produce monthly financial statements. Some aspects of development differed because of the nature of each company's business or because of particular investment choices made by senior managers; for instance, company D had a fully computerized warehouse which used bar coding scanners whereas company K's concern was with an electronic point of sale (EPOS) system and company M was developing an electronic data interchange (EDI) system. In all cases the accounting function had traditionally controlled the development of IT. All the companies perceived that the next major challenge would be to integrate their existing systems. The case studies raised a number of questions for which answers could not be found from the case study data. These questions centred on the detailed ways in which judgements about IT evaluation were formed. We pursued these questions in the mid-1990s through a research method which is appropriate to answering questions about judgement formation – the protocol analysis method. The origin of protocol analysis is often attributed to Newell and Simon (1972) and has been used in a wide variety of research, including the development of theories of expertise in accounting (e.g. Bouwman et al., 1987) and the development of an explanation for the relative non-use of expert systems in the financial sector (Birts et al., 1993). Using this method entails the interviewee talking aloud as a problem is solved. For the present study, a two page case study was developed which provided brief details of four projects, together with the results of calculations for the net present value, the internal rate of return, the payback and the accounting rate of return. The financial director therefore functions to assess the value of requests by non-accounting departments. The company firmly believes that the financial director should be fully aware of all developments within the company, not only from the point of view of financial control, but also to locate authority and responsibility in one senior person.

There is a certain amount of resentment about the significant role of the financial director. As a check against bias, the information systems department manager has no allegiance to any function and is considered to be in a position to give an independent judgement. ## The use of ROI techniques

An awareness that ROI techniques may be used by a company provides evidence that this structural approach to IT evaluation is to be found in practice but the implication that applying a technique provides a direct recommendation to action proves to be misleading. The use of more than one technique, which is known to survey research (Drury et al., 1993; Sangster, 1993), is evident in the words of one of the financial directors, designated S4 in Table 2.

We would use net present value. We would use internal rate of return. And we would use payback. We would also worry a hell of a lot about how big a hole we were going to have to dig in the first year because we have to worry about reporting results externally.... With certain kinds of expectations out there as to what our profits are going to be, it can be quite difficult if [reported profits do not meet expectations]. ... (S4).

This raises a number of questions. If the net present value is known to produce the one best way of dealing with capital investment appraisal, why should a financial director calculate the alternative ROI measures?

Apart from other concerns, different methods are likely to lead to different results, with the net present value possibly recommending the acceptance of an IT project while the payback possibly recommends rejection. This situation was evident in the case study presented to the financial directors. The calculation of more than one measure may lead to conflicting signals.

This financial director explains that his company uses the traditional ROI approaches of the net present value, internal rate of return and payback, but also considers the impact on profit. The reason for including the absolute impact on profit comes from ‘expectations out there’, or the influence of the financial markets. This shows that techniques may be adopted because of influential stakeholders. This may provide one reason for the curious fact that more than one technique might be used in practice. Multiple techniques might provide a response to the requisite variety (Weick, 1995) occasioned by diverse stakeholders.

A second reason for the use of multiple techniques might be that techniques are ranked, so that a dominant technique must deliver an accept judgement before further techniques are applied (Sangster, 1983). The concept of triangulation might provide a further explanation. Financial directors are aware of the limitations of cost and benefit measurements. It is possible that they calculate alternative measures in order to compare and contrast the results of different approaches to learn more about the underlying nature of the individual projects, rather than to find one absolute truth. Everyone gets very excited about these mathematical models. . . . I don' think in my experience that they're a very significant factor in terms of capital [expenditure] decisions. The [techniques] bit is just a part of the . . . sort of . . . the tool kit now. I think that things are very much more seen in the strategic sense. Daft and Lengel (1990) provided a justification for this purpose in their distinction between high and low variety languages. Low variety languages, of which the lowest is argued by Daft and Lengel (1990) to be numeric and formal and which seem to describe the kind of financial analysis which typifies a standard technique (Farbey et al., 1992), communicate effectively about well-understood, unambiguous topics which IT projects arguably are not. High variety languages, such as face-to-face interactions (Daft and Lengel, 1990, p.247–8), are appropriate for communicating about difficult, ephemeral and social phenomena which IT projects arguably are. Face-to-face meetings are encouraged by user groups, 'away days' and board meetings which provide alternative formal methods for bringing people together. The next section considers these.

## Formal interactions

One of the financial directors talked about an away days approach to board meetings.

The annual strategy conference ... is [where] the board go away for 2 days ... [a] sort of think process ... which is started off with, 'This is what the Corporate Plan looks like. ... This is our earnings gap. ...' And then we get into all the issues by area and one of those areas would be systems. As it happens, systems is a priority this year. In fact, I spent last night on it and I'm spending all day Thursday on it. Because we'd done a systems review 4 years ago; where we thought we were going in the next 5 years. That's coming round for renewal (S2).

By referring to ‘systems’, the financial director is referring to the fact that IT is on the agenda for this particular meeting. The prospect of a formal meeting requires the financial director to devote attention to IT and structure becomes a prompt for thinking through the specific issue. The meeting is a venue at which IT can then be talked about in relation to the organization’s strategy and its formalized financial plans. Meetings bring together people and ideas. Structures provide frameworks within which individuals make sense of their projects, compare their sense making with others and form judgements.

Sense making takes place in teams.

One of the strengths that any company has ... [is a] management team that has been around for quite a long while and worked with each other ... you actually get to know each other and how you actually go about making decisions. One of the dangers of ... you know ... management teams that are put together quickly.... or, we bring in people from the outside ... is that they tend to depend rather a lot on what is written down and what the financial projections are. The challenge in all of these things is that the real world doesn't usually work out the way in which you put in the projections. And, if it does, you ask ... you worry! Something could be better than the figures but it might be bad news. Something could be worse than the figures but it's good news. I think one has to recognize that true advantage takes time to create. Therefore you should be trying to do difficult things and doing them well. If the real opportunity is there, the more difficult it is, the better.... The reason that we are such a profitable company and we make good returns is because a lot of the things that we do and a lot of the position that we've developed is very difficult to replicate very quickly. Because we're in an industry where things move very quickly and you therefore have to be constantly changing, modernizing your product and have to be using the latest technologies you're capable of, continuously differentiating yourself, rather than allowing that differentiation to be eroded. I think when one looks at projects, one has to see them in the context of where you're trying to get to and what sort of input you're bringing to it (S3).

Thus, structures are limited because, in their search for order and regularity, they cannot capture what is happening in the chaos of the ‘real world’. Capturing competitive advantages comes from knowing the competencies, strengths, vision and wishes of a stable management team where mutual understanding is important.

Understanding extends to the projects themselves. The financial director needs to

make damn sure that when people do these projects that they are comprehensive, so that you think through all of the issues associated with them. Part of the test of the finance director these days is to actually really understand what people are trying to do and challenge it; to check whether they've included this and this. What happens if this doesn't work? Increasingly, we're all involved in international projects so this whole human aspect becomes more complex. You're dealing with cross-cultural things; the Americans are very different to the Brits in terms of many little things that they do as a whole attitude. You have to understand these things in comparing projects; you have to understand these cultural factors. You spend a lot of time travelling. You've got to. 'Thinking through' implies understanding IT projects in terms of the business and in terms of the people who comprise that business, even where this requires extensive travel. Three out of the six financial directors in the protocol analysis study refused to form a final judgement because the available information did not give sufficient details about the business, its story and its people. The three who did offer judgements made very little use of the financial measurements which were provided. It is one thing to know that a ROI measure is calculated, it is entirely different to known how such a measure is used as part of the formation of judgements in an uncertain world. The numbers appears to be less important than ensuring that IT projects meet the needs of the business.

The challenging in the previous quotation, which was part of determining that all project issues have been considered, is effected through questioning. The questioning appears to have a psychological basis.

It's not accurate questions or having to answer [which] is important. It's the fact that if they know they will have to answer questions about [a project], they will do thinking that they wouldn't otherwise have done. And that thinking may lead them to a different decision that they wouldn't have reached otherwise. Instead of just spending money, and thinking whether it was worth it later, they'll think very carefully through, 'What money do I think I'll need to spend?', 'Why?', 'What would I want for it?', 'What will the benefits be?' So it imposes some discipline on thought and action (S1).

Evaluation therefore seems to operate at a human level, not at a disembodied level of objective measurements. It works by prompting individuals to ask themselves questions about IT projects; to almost mindfully simulate the project in advance.

## Allocating responsibility

In addition to filling out some of the details of the structural responses to IT evaluation, the case study of company M, as summarized in Figure 2, also introduces some aspects of IT evaluation which appear to rely primarily on the designation of responsibility to particular individuals. This account reveals the personal forces which drive investment decisions. The third paragraph of Figure 2 shows that some decisions can be handled personally, outside the scope of the strategic plan, but within a structure defined by responsibility this does not necessarily imply that such decisions are less well thought through, merely that the role of the individual is paramount in such cases. This observation is consistent with Ballantine et al.'s (1996) finding that evaluation need not rely upon formally defined procedures. The final paragraph raises issues of power and vested interests. Financial directors have vested interests in IT and therefore may or may not be appropriate people to take responsibility. An awareness of this drives the financial director's attitude and potential judgement as regards IT investments. Judgement and personal position are inextricably linked.

## IT investment evaluation

Responsibility accounting systems imply sets of procedures and attitudes. A major system for the management of responsibilities is budgetary planning and control. One of the financial directors addresses the issue surrounding the budgeting of IT as follows.

I think computers are more and more getting a bit like marketing spend in the company. You know, the information systems function spends as much, within reason, as you're given to spend, as long as they've got user support. And it's very much the case of having to decide how much you're willing to spend. Otherwise . . . there are . . . problems with computers. Either you end up that you've got so [many projects] that you are incapable of managing and you can waste an awful lot of money on systems analysts and programmers and everything producing extremely sophisticated systems on last year's technology. . . . So, I'm not saying, 'Ah, I agree totally with information technology. . . .' I'm very conscious [that] you can spend an awful lot of money getting very little out in performance. And what is important is at the beginning of the project you agree a specification in detail. . . . You have benchmarks . . . along the way to make sure that what you said you were going to achieve at this point you have done. And you don't allow the project to go awry (S2).

The designation of responsibility is seen to be complex. There is an expectation that IT will consume as much resources as demanded. IT competes with marketing and other functions for scarce resources and the implication is that managers have a responsibility to seek funding for their ideas. The financial director explains elsewhere that IT expenditure is allocated in precisely the same way as marketing – as a percentage of the total sales value. The twin dangers to be avoided in the evaluation process are taking on more commitments than can be managed and failing to keep up with a rapidly changing technology. Following on from this final point, monitoring is naturally carried out against time ‘benchmarks’. The financial director is seen to be selling IT as an issue, whilst revealing an awareness of the dangers through the juxtaposition of ‘I agree totally with information technology’ with ‘I’m very conscious [that] you can spend an awful lot of money getting very little out in performance’. IT is an accepted part of the modern world but needs to be managed carefully as part of the total business.

## The role of structure in an uncertain world

Company M provides us with evidence of fluid approaches to IT evaluation which links evaluation to an uncertain world and to an understanding that evaluation is merely one aspect of the implementation of IT. One advantage of gaining access to longitudinal studies is that we have been able to collect evidence that individual companies change the relative emphasis on structural approaches to IT investment over time. For case B, for which a summary is provided in Figure 3, it appears that structure was important early in the introduction of IT. As experience of IT grew, so a greater reliance was placed on key individuals and some structural elements ceased to be effective. This is similar to the Dreyfus and Dreyfus (1986) argument that novices require rules whilst experts do what they know works. An experienced management team may down play the importance of structure and seem to replace structure with intuitive responses to a changing world. Future research might perhaps consider the ways in which some management teams exercise experience and intuition so as to develop a track record of success, whilst others seem to fail. The point of the present paper is to suggest that such research needs to take place at the level of both individual agency and structure and not entirely at the level of structure.

A failure to deliver expectations undermines credibility and leads to scepticism. In such an approach to the evaluations of IT, structural elements can be limited and simplified.

## Discussion and implications

The previous sections have shown how individual agency and structure interact. Case B shows that while structure might change over time, some structure is always evident. Even in case C, which provided an example of minimum structure and an emphasis on individual agency, some structure was evident in the form of the use of conventional financial evaluation measurements. Some duality of individual agency and structure seems to be necessary. The duality of individual agency and structure is the point of structuration theory (Giddens, 1984). Structure is constantly re-created by active individual agents. In turn, structure constrains and enables individual action. From the point of view of constraint, an objective measurement leads to some projects being rejected, so that the projects which remain are manageable within the existing limitations of management resource. Structure is enabling because it prompts a thinking through of a project in advance of its acceptance. This is not the mechanical world of choosing projects which are objectively the best; it is a world of choice where individuals are free to make a case and to sustain their own personal credibility through the quality of their own actions within the constraining and enabling forces of structure. It is to these lessons that a structuration theory of IT evaluation directs us.

An initial reading of the literature of IT evaluation suggested that structure is often stressed and implicit in this is the assumption that improving evaluation results from improving structural responses. Co-emphasizing structure and individuals provides a complementary challenge and role for the researcher and consultants to that of providing 'better solutions'. The consultant might become a facilitator, encouraging companies to evaluate their own thinking and professional practice, perhaps using approaches which have already been suggested by researchers and proved in practical settings, such as cognitive mapping (Eden, 1989; Huff, 1990) or cooperative inquiry (Reason, 1994; Reason and Heron, 1995).

Cognitive mapping is a technique which seeks to challenge the mental models of managers, a process which is attributed by Senge (1990) to the success of Shell throughout the 1970s and 1980s and which, through Senge (1990) has come to be associated with the 'learning organization'. For Senge (1990), mental models are one of the five 'disciplines' which are necessary to the learning organization. The remaining disciplines, systems thinking, personal mastery, building shared vision and team learning, are each more or less reflected in the words of the financial director given above. The concept of the learning organization as it has appeared in the early literature (e.g. Garratt, 1987; Senge, 1990; Pedler et al., 1991) might now be seen to have its limitations (Cooper, 1995) but this concept offers encouragement to IT directors and managers to act much more creatively and proactively than the image of meeting evaluation criteria might imply. The IT director and manager in the learning organization is expected to take a positive role in the positive development of the business as a whole. This is a far broader concept of learning than that which simply addresses the potential for post-implementation reviews of IT investment to act as a learning device for the benefit of future evaluations.

Shifting the emphasis towards a duality of structure and individual action implies a shift away from strategy alone towards a dual emphasis on strategy and strategists. The notion of strategist can be linked to Torbert's (1991) developmental model which included technician, achiever and strategist stages. Under this model, technicians see the world in terms of analysis and craft and this seems to be similar to the position of the one best way approach to evaluation. Achievers permit themselves to develop an explicit, unique and theoretical position which seems to be similar to that of contingency theory. The strategist's world is one which is relatively fluid, political and structureless. We see in the quotes of the financial directors that they are the ones who use techniques to establish structure, but then interpret the stories and the personalities which the structures help to regulate. The financial directors know the techniques well and take them for granted, seeking to look beyond the technique to the business itself.

The divergence we met in the case studies might now be explained by individual companies taking steps to find their own way to provide a structure within which individuals may (or may not) take part as strategists and where structure constrains and enables strategists who, in turn, can act to change both structure and their businesses. In this respect, the structures which were adopted seemed to meet the kinds of needs which might be associated with the challenging of individual's mind sets, collaborative inquiry and the learning organization. We realize that some companies clearly had situations where individuals' voices were not heard and willingly acknowledge the need for research which looks at how individuals affect the organizational agenda. The financial directors, however, appeared to be committed to setting up ways in which individual voices could be heard and there appeared to be opportunities for individuals to build their credibility through the ways in which they managed their projects.

Thus, IT needs strategies and IT needs strategists. We need people with ambition and vision and imagination to balance experience: people with peripheral vision in a world where ‘decision makers begin with preferences and select actions based on imperfect expectations about the effect of these actions upon future preferences’ (O'Reilly, 1990, p. 95). It would appear, based on the evidence presented here, that evaluation is at least as much about such people as it is about IT projects themselves.
