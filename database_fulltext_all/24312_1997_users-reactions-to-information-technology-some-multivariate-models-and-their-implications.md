# Users' reactions to information technology: some multivariate models and their implications

CHRIS CLEGG, NEIL CAREY, GRAHAM DEAN, PAT HORNBY and RICHARD BOLDEN

Institute of Work Psychology, University of Sheffield, Sheffield, S10 2TN, UK

We summarize empirical work examining the impact on users of information technology (IT) and identify some gaps in current knowledge. We outline some alternative multivariate models of users' reactions to IT, describe an initial empirical test of this work and argue the need for the specification of multivariate models to guide further empirical, theoretical and practical developments. In particular we argue that models of this kind can be used as aids in managing the development, implementation and use of IT.

## Introduction

There is a growing empirical literature examining the impacts on users of new systems of information technology (IT) as they are introduced into work organizations. Against that background, the primary goals of this paper are:

\- to review existing empirical literature in the fields of applied psychology and organizational behaviour;

\- to develop alternative models linking the principal variables influencing users' reactions to the new technologies;

\- to describe an initial empirical test of these models; and

\- to identify the theoretical, methodological and practical implications of this work.

The paper is organized in several sections. First, we present a systematic review of empirical investigations of users' reactions to new technology in a number of the leading international refereed journals in the fields of applied psychology and organizational behaviour. This sample of literature is surprisingly small and most empirical studies are partial in their selection of variables, reflecting an absence of underlying multivariate models. Second, we describe some alternative models of the factors influencing users' reactions to IT. The models are derived in part from the literature review; they identify the main classes of variables and specify alternative plausible links between them. In these fields there has been very little work of this kind. Third, we describe a pilot study testing our measures and methods, followed by a cross-sectional empirical study providing a preliminary test of these models. The results confirm the need for multivariate modelling of this kind. And finally we discuss the theoretical, methodological and practical implications of this work.

Within the fields of applied psychology and organizational behaviour, it is apparent that there is an absence of specified, multivariate models within which existing empirical studies can be interpreted and new investigations designed. The work described here attempts to make such a contribution by developing and testing alternative models of users' reactions. The empirical tests are cross-sectional and we recognize such designs do not provide full tests of the models. However, such work is seen as a necessary precursor to more comprehensive longitudinal investigations which are vital for testing predictor models.

## A critique of the literature

Our literature review focuses on empirical studies of users' reactions to the introduction of IT applications at work, for example in the form of manufacturing or office systems. Leisure or home applications are excluded from the review, as are experimental investigations and studies of general social attitudes to IT. Included in our review are empirical studies examining:

\- attitudes towards, expectations of, and aspirations for specific applications;

\- user participation in the development of systems;

● the usability and functionality of systems;

\- job demands and job characteristics associated with new systems;

\- user reactions to systems of IT, such as satisfaction with the system, commitment to the system, usage of the system, performance on the system, competences in using the system, and psychological well-being.

Systematic searches were undertaken in 19 refereed academic journals for the period 1980 until 1995 inclusive. These journals were selected as being the most relevant, international and prestigious in the fields of applied psychology and organizational behaviour, and ones in which we could reasonably expect to find detailed empirical studies. The selected journals are listed in Appendix 1.

Other material was examined following up appropriate references and reviews. We do not claim to have located all reported studies that meet our criteria and are aware that our review does not include journals from the information systems community. We are, however, confident that we have found the majority of material published in our selected fields of interest. These searches enabled us to identify 68 studies which met our criteria (Appendix 2).

Several points can be made about the outcomes of these searches, although clearly these comments are restricted to the scope of our review. First, we note that there have been surprisingly few reported empirical studies of the impact of IT in these leading journals, especially considering the enormous and accelerating uptake of IT throughout the industrialized world (Edquist and Jacobson, 1988; Kelly 1990; Pinella, 1990; Van Cott, 1991).

Second, it is apparent that many of the studies (around 50%) address the question, 'what was the impact of a new system on its users?', that is, taking the new system as the predictor variable (for example, Liff, 1990). Studies of this kind make it difficult to identify what may have been the sources of any user reactions. For example, if the new system is associated with low user satisfaction, why is this the case? Does it arise from hostile attitudes to new technology generally? Or from a reduction in job quality? Or perhaps from frustration arising from poor functionality?

Third, we note that even where there is more differentiation in the choice of predictor variables, their selection remains partial. Most of these studies are focused on specific questions or issues. For example, several studies investigate the role of job content in predicting user reactions (for example, Aronsson, 1989; Sainfort and Lim, 1989; Allingham and O'Connor, 1992). But they rarely compare a range of other plausible explanations or control for possible extraneous factors. This makes it difficult to compare results across studies. It also makes it hard to assess whether any relationships are real, mediated by another variable, or spurious, resulting from covariation with a third factor.

Fourth, it is apparent that there is some overall consistency across these studies in the selection of critical variables. For example, the most common outcome variables are: user satisfaction; some index of stress, strain, mood or psychological state; job characteristics; various indicators of performance; user attitudes towards the new system; workload demands; and health outcomes. Similarly, the most common predictor and moderator variables are: the implementation of a new system; job characteristics; VDU usage; demographic variables; experience with computers; job position; and involvement in system design. Taken together, these represent a mix of variables across the following four categories: biographical and situational; prior experiences and expectations; job impacts; and user reactions. These different categories of variables are incorporated in the models described later in this paper.

Fifth, we can see from this review that some predictors of user reactions that are often talked about within the IT community are barely represented in this sample of the empirical literature. Consider for example, the functionality of a new system and its usability, each of which is widely held to influence users' reactions. These are surprising omissions.

Sixth, many of the studies can be interpreted as being based on relatively simple underlying models. For example, user reactions to IT are primarily influenced by the impact of the new system on job content (for example, Aronsson, 1989; Majchrzak, 1990); or by prior experiences with computers (for example, Robinson, 1989; Igbaria and Parasuraman, 1991); or by job position and various demographic variables (for example, Yaverbaum and Culpan, 1990; Long, 1993). But these are rarely set up as rival hypotheses for comparative testing (for an exception see Majchrzak and Cotton, 1988). Similarly, they are seldom put together in the form of alternative multivariate models (but see Parsons et al., 1991). Testing alternative multivariate models forms the focus of this paper.

Seventh, there are some emerging patterns of evidence on which one could base further empirical work. For example, the proposition that new technologies invariably de-skill work is disconfirmed (Kemp and Clegg, 1987; Zicklin, 1987; Lindtrom and Leino, 1989; Seppala et al., 1989; Long, 1993). With computerization, job content may become more complex (Huuhtanen and Leino, 1989; Yaverbaum and Culpan, 1990) or less complex (Hodson and Hagen, 1988, Liff, 1990). Job level, group or grade can be important moderators (Zoltan and Chapanis, 1982; Stellman et al., 1987; Aronsson, 1989; Allingham and O'Connor, 1992; Henderson et al., 1995). System usage for some is seen as a predictor of user attitudes (Rafaeli, 1986), whilst for others attitudes predict usage (Grantham and Vaske, 1985; Davis, 1993). Usage may also vary with job content (Smith et al., 1981; Anderson et al., 1985). A range of other variables have been used successfully to predict user reactions, for example: age (Huuhtanen and Leino, 1989; Igbaria and Parasuraman, 1991; Marquie et al., 1994); prior experiences and attitudes (Zoltan and Chapanis, 1982; Grantham and Vaske, 1985; Mueller et al., 1986; Dolan and Tziner, 1988); user participation (Buchanan and Boddy, 1982; Baroudi et al., 1986; Allingham and O'Connor, 1992; Hawk, 1993; Korunka et al., 1995); system characteristics (Rafaeli and Sutton, 1986; Kaye, 1990; Igbaria et al., 1994); and cognitive demand (Smith et al., 1989). These variables require further study.

Above all, this review reveals an absence of underlying multivariate models in the fields of applied psychology and organizational behaviour within which individual studies and findings can be understood, and through which new investigations can be designed. We believe this represents an essential next step for progress within these fields.

## Models

The above review reveals the need to develop models which identify the principal variables which may influence users' reactions to new technologies, and which specify the major relationships between them. Delineation of rival models allows their comparative testing. In this section we describe several models which incorporate a range of predictor and outcome variables. These models were developed in part from our understanding of the relevant psychological literature (see for example Long et al., 1983; Frese, 1987; and the review above) and in part from our practical experiences of working with many organizations in this field.

These models share two components: a specification of the variables which are hypothesized as the principal potential influences upon users' reactions to new technology; and a specification of the major links between them. We expect that models of this kind will be revised and improved by further empirical and theoretical work. Following the distinctions evident in the literature, four classes of variables are differentiated within the models.

## A. Biographical and situational variables

These include variables such as age, sex, length of service in the company and in the job, job type and grade. These are important both for interest in their own right (e.g. do users' reactions to new technology vary with their age?), and also for control purposes (e.g. are other apparent effects spurious, resulting from covariation?).

## B. Prior experiences and expectations

These include variables such as general attitudes to computers, expectations of the impact of computers so far as the organization is concerned (e.g. will the new system meet its objectives?), expectations of the impact of computers so far as users are concerned (e.g. will the new system make their jobs better or worse?), and extent of influence over the design of the system. These variables are included because they may be powerful influences on users' reactions.

## C. Job impacts

These include variables such as the usability of the system, its functionality, its impact upon job content, demands and skill utilization, and the level of support given to the user. These variables are included because they may have a direct impact on users' reactions to the technology.

## D. Users' reaction

Critical outcome variables include the extent of usage of the system, the user's commitment to it, competence, mood when using the system, and satisfaction with the system and with the way in which it was introduced.

## Alternative models

The models each incorporate the three classes of variables (A, B and C above) which may influence users' reactions (D). However, these variables may be linked in different ways and some plausible alternatives are specified below in the form of different models. In the empirical tests described later we examine six rival models. These are summarized in Figure 1.

Thus user reactions (D) are primarily a function of: biographical and situational variables (A, as in model 1); prior experiences and expectations (B, as in model 2); job impacts (C, as in model 3); a mix of all three classes of variables (A, B and C, as in model 4); or job impacts (C), which in turn are influenced by prior expectations (B), and such that biographical and situational variables (A) influence both classes B and C (as in model 5); or of all three classes of variables (A, B and C), such that biographical and situational variables (A) influence both prior experiences and expectations (B) and job impacts (C), and that prior experiences and expectations (B) also influence job impacts (C, as in model 6). Model 6 represents a 'block recursive model' (Blalock, 1969) in which all the different classes of variables are linked. Each of these models are regarded as plausible alternatives. Other possible permutations of variables A, B and C (e.g. A and B, but not C, influence D) are included under model 4 in order to simplify the discussion.

It is clear that versions of models 3 and 4 currently dominate the studies in our review. Our a priori preference, however, is for model 5. Thus we predict that user reactions to new technologies are primarily correlated with their direct job impacts. However the experiences of job impacts themselves are associated with biographical and situational variables (such as job grade and job level) and also with users' prior expectations and experiences. Furthermore, the biographical and situational variables are correlated with expectations and experiences. This model is more comprehensive than models 1, 2 and 3; more specific and differentiated than model 4; and more parsimonious and specific than model 6.

We have chosen to test each of these models in part because of the absence of such differentiation and specificity in the literature reviewed.

## Empirical studies

## Settings

Two studies were undertaken, the first in the form of a small pilot investigation testing out our measures and methods, the second involving a larger sample of computer users. The pilot study was undertaken in one department within a large multinational company located in the UK. The pilot took place a few months after the introduction of a management information system for use by a group of administrators.

The second study was undertaken in a large public sector organization located in the UK. Data were collected less than one year after the implementation of a large bespoke (tailor made) office-based management information and recording system for use by all levels of staff.

## Participants

## Pilot study

The participants were users of the new system, largely using the system to check and update various records. The system was used for clerical purposes by administrators working in the same job at the same job grade. Thirty-five users were asked to complete a questionnaire about their work, resulting in 28 completed responses; a response rate of 80%.

## Study 2

The participants were all users of the new system, working at different levels of the organization, but all located at the main office site. There were two main classes of user: administrators who used it for clerical and record keeping purposes, and technical experts who used the system for technical enquiries, databases and other specialist purposes. Approximately 450 users were asked to complete a questionnaire resulting in 171 completed responses, an estimated response rate of 38%.

## Methods

All users were asked to complete a questionnaire covering the variables described below. The pilot study was used to test the questionnaire, and minor amendments were made for its application in study 2. Both studies were cross-sectional in design.

## Measures

Variables from each of the four classes identified in the literature review were measured; these are described separately below. Individual items and response formats for variables in classes B, C and D (as used in study 2) are given in Appendix 3. Sample characteristics, means, standard deviations and alpha coefficients are presented later in Table 1.

## A. Biographical and situational variables

Data were collected about each person's age, sex, length of service in the company and in the job (in months), and job grade.

## B. Prior experiences and expectations

Four variables were examined: general attitudes to computers; expectations about the impact of the new system for the user; expectations about the impact of the new system for the company; and user influence over system design. Expectations were measured by asking for the respondent's views of the likely future impact of the system. User influence was used to identify two groups of respondents: those recording a large gap between the influence they experienced and that they desired, and those who recorded either a small or no gap.

## C. Job impacts

Ten measures were used to assess the impact of the new system on the user's job: method control; timing control; overall control; attentional demands; effort demands; error demands; skill utilization; satisfaction with training and support; usability of the system; and functionality of the system. Measures of method control, timing control and attentional demands were based on scales developed by Jackson et al. (1993).

## D. User reactions

Eight separate outcomes were measured covering six categories: system usage (self-reported in terms of frequency of use per week and of number of hours of use per week); user commitment to the new system; user competence at using the new system; mood when using the new system (measured using scales developed by Warr (1990) which allow for the separation of anxiety and depression); user satisfaction with aspects of the new system; and feelings of ownership of the system.

## Alternative models

Six rival models were formulated incorporating different links between the various classes of variables (as described in Figure 1). To reiterate, these are:

\- Model 1: Users' reactions are correlated with biographical and situational variables.

\- Model 2: Users' reactions are correlated with their prior experiences and expectations.

\- Model 3: Users' reactions are correlated with the impact of the new system on their jobs.

\- Model 4: Users' reactions are a mix of these factors.

\- Model 5: Users' reactions are correlated with the impacts of the new system on their jobs, which in turn are associated with their prior experiences and expectations; biographical and situational variables correlate with both prior experiences and expectations as well as perceptions of job impacts.

\- Model 6: Users' reactions are correlated with the impact of the new system on their jobs, with prior experiences and expectations and with biographical and situational variables; job impacts are associated with prior experiences and expectations and with biographical and situational variables; prior experiences and expectations are correlated with biographical and situational variables.

## Analyses

In each study, in addition to reliability coefficients and summary statistics, zero-order correlation coefficients were computed to examine the overall pattern of relationships between the variables and as a preliminary test of the models. In view of the small sample size, no further analyses were conducted on data from the pilot study.

In study 2, stepwise regression and path analytic techniques were used to achieve statistical control of variables and to provide more stringent tests of the six models specified above. The variable for user influence (influence gap) was excluded from the regression analyses because of a low response rate.

To test model 1 (that A is associated with D), variables in category A were fed into regression equations predicting each outcome variable (in D). This however ignores any possible covariance between variables in classes A, B and C. To achieve better control of potential spurious effects, two equivalent procedures were used. In the first, variables in classes B and C were fed into each regression equation predicting each outcome variable, and only then were variables in A added. In the second procedure, all the variables in categories A, B and C were fed into a regression equation predicting each outcome variable; this effectively identifies those variables which predict the outcome holding the other variables constant. Equivalent regression procedures were used to test models 2 and 3.

The test for model 4, that a mix of all three factors, A, B and C are associated with D, involves the same regression procedure whereby all three sets of variables are used to predict each dependent variable.

Models 5 and 6 were tested competitively using path analytic techniques. The two competing models were compared for each dependent variable. Thus model 5, which specifies the presence of certain causal pathways, was compared with model 6, which allows for causal links between all the classes of variables. The amount of variance accounted for within each of these models is compared using a likelihood ratio statistic (LRS) (see, for example, Wall et al., 1978). If model 5 is valid, there should be no additional variance explained by the addition of further causal pathways from model 6 - that is, the LRS comparing the two models should be non-significant.

## Results

Summary statistics and zero-order correlations are presented in Table 1, the pilot study above the diagonal, study 2 below.

## Reliability

It is apparent that the levels of reliability (coefficients alpha) are satisfactory. In the pilot study they range from 0.69 to 0.93, with a modal point of 0.84. In study 2 the range is from 0.68 to 0.93, with the modal point between 0.80 and 0.82.

## Correlations

Examining the zero-order correlations from the pilot study, there is little evidence in support of model 1 (that A correlates with D). Only length of service with the company is significantly related to any of the outcome variables. There is some support for model 2 (that B correlates with D), with a pattern of strong associations between expectations (for self and for the company) and several of the outcome variables. Looking at model 3 (that C correlates with D), five variables show a consistent pattern of significant associations, namely control, effort and error demands, usability and functionality. Accordingly model 4 (that D is associated with a mix of variables A, B and C) receives some confirmation, the principal implicated variables being, as above, length of service with the company, expectations (for self and for the company), control, effort and error demands, usability and functionality. Model 5 (that A correlates with B, that A and B correlate with C, and that C correlates with D) has only partial support. There are no significant associations (beyond p < 0.01) between variables in A and those in B, and none between A and C. However expectations for self and for the company (in B) are significantly related to usability (in C), as is the latter with functionality (in C), both of which are highly correlated with several outcomes (in D). Similarly model 6 (that A correlates with B, C and D, that B correlates with C and D, and that C is associated with D) receives only partial support. As above, there is no evidence of significant association between variables in A and B, or A and C. There is some evidence of links between B and C, and some strong associations between variables B and D, and C and D.

In study 2, examination of the correlation matrix also reveals some interesting findings. Looking at evidence in favour of model 1 (that A correlates with D), there is some support; each of the biographical and situational variables is significantly correlated with several of the outcome variables. However the mood variables (anxiety and depression) are unrelated to the biographical and situational variables.

Turning to model 2 (that B is associated with D), there is some supporting evidence, especially for the variables measuring expectations of the new system (either for self or for the company) which are consistently highly correlated with most of the outcome variables. Prior influence over design is also highly correlated with some of the outcome attitudinal variables. General attitudes towards computers are not highly correlated with most of the outcome variables.

Model 3 (that C is associated with D) also receives some confirmation. The five variables most consistently significantly related to a range of outcome variables are overall control, skill utilization, levels of support and training, usability and functionality. Attentional and effort demands are also strongly associated with some dependent variables, most notably anxiety and depression.

There is also support for model 4 (that D correlates with a mix of A, B and C). As noted above, job grade and length of service (with company or job, from A), expectations of the new system (either for self or for the company, from B), and overall control, skill utilization, levels of support and training, usability and functionality (from C) are all consistently related to many of the outcome variables.

Model 5 (that A correlates with B, that A and B are associated with C, and that C correlates with D) also has some support. Thus age, job grade and length of service variables (from A) are highly correlated with the expectations variables (for self and company, from B) and also with overall control, skill use, usability and functionality (from C). Expectations variables (for self and company, from B) are also highly related to overall control, skill use, usability and functionality (from C), which in turn are highly correlated with many of the outcome variables (from D). On the other hand, there is also evidence of significant relationships between classes of variables not specified by this hypothesis. For example, variables in A and B are both highly correlated with outcome variables (in D). This lends support to model 6 (that all the variables are inter-linked).

From the correlational evidence one would conclude that length of service, job grade, expectations for self and for the company, overall control, skill utilization, usability and functionality are most strongly associated with the outcomes. Given this overall pattern, models 4, 5 and 6 appear better candidates than do models 1, 2 and 3. However, these variables themselves are highly intercorrelated and greater statistical control is required to provide stricter tests of the hypotheses.

## Regressions

Further analyses using stepwise regression and path analytic techniques were undertaken to test the six hypothesized models on responses from study 2. These are presented in Table 2 for each of the outcome variables.

Table 2 shows results for each of the eight dependent variables organized in columns. The rows show the results for different analyses. Row 1 gives the results from regressions predicting each dependent variable, including only biographical and situational variables (category A) as predictors. Thus row 1 equates to a test of model 1. Rows 2 and 3 show equivalent results for B and C respectively (testing models 2 and 3 respectively). Row 4 shows the significant predictors for each outcome when variables from A, B and C are all added into the regression equation (testing model 4). Row 5 presents a comparison of hypotheses 5 and 6, giving for each dependent variable a value of chi-square which represents a likelihood ratio statistic comparing hypothesized model 5 with hypothesized model 6. A significant value of chi-square means that the addition of the new pathways from model 6 adds significantly to the amount of variance accounted for in comparison with model 5. A non-significant value means that model 5 represents a more parsimonious explanation than model 6. Recall that model 5 is our preferred model.

Looking at column 1 in Table 2 we can see that frequency of use of the new system is best predicted independently by job grade (see rows 1 and 4). An apparent effect that error demands and overall control (from C, see row 3) predict frequency of use is statistically spurious (see row 4). This substantiates the need for the use of models of this kind. From row 5 we can see that model 6 provides a significantly better prediction of frequency of use than does model 5.

Examining Table 2 there is little evidence to substantiate model 1, that variables in category A are the principal predictors of outcomes on their own. Grade, age and length of service in the job are implicated for several of the outcomes (see row 1), but most of these effects disappear when variables in B and C are also entered into the equations (see row 4). Exceptions (also from row 4) are: job grade which predicts frequency and hours of use (see columns 1 and 2); age which predicts depression (column 6); and sex which predicts anxiety (column 5). Interestingly, neither the age nor the sex findings are apparent by inspection of the correlation matrix (see Table 1), again substantiating the need for this kind of multivariate approach.

Turning to model 2, that prior experiences and expectations (factors in B) are the principal independent predictors of outcomes – there is considerable support, especially regarding expectations for the company and for oneself (see row 2). However, with statistical control, many of these relationships turn out to be spurious (see row 4). Nevertheless expectations about the impact for the company are significant predictors of several of the outcomes, most significantly for commitment to the new system (column 3).

Model 3 (that job impacts, variables in C, are the principal predictors of outcomes) receives widespread support (see row 3). Furthermore, many of these relationships maintain significance levels even when other variables are controlled (see row 4). In particular, functionality predicts hours of use (column 2), commitment to the system (column 3), and satisfaction with the system (column 7); usability predicts competence at using the system (column 4), satisfaction with the system (column 7), and ownership of it (column 8); perceived skill utilization predicts satisfaction (column 7) and ownership (column 8); and various job demands also predict outcomes, for example effort demands predict competence (column 4) and depression (column 6), whilst attentional demands predict anxiety (column 5).

From the above we can see that model 4 (that the outcomes are best predicted by a mix of variables from categories A, B and C) receives some support (see row 4). The variables implicated are as described above.

Adopting a more differentiated view of the dependent variables, we can also discern some patterns of differences in Table 2. For example, the best predictor of behaviour (frequency and hours of use) is job grade; this is because of the greater usage by administrators in comparison with technical specialists in this sample. Looking at mood when using the system (anxiety and depression) we can see that job demands, age and sex are the main predictors. Considering attitudes (satisfaction, perceived ownership and commitment) the most significant associations are with functionality, usability, skill utilization and expectations about the impact for the company.

Models 5 and 6 were tested competitively for each dependent variable by assessing the amount of additional variance explained by the addition of further pathways from model 6 using a likelihood ratio statistic. These results are presented in row 5. We can see that the likelihood ratio statistics are significant for each outcome. Thus the additional paths identified in model 6 add significantly to the variance explained for these variables.

## Implications

We have found that user reactions to IT are associated with a wide range of biographical and situational variables, with prior expectations and experiences, and with job impacts. Furthermore, the variable groups themselves are all interrelated as described in model 6.

We stress that we recognize the acute limitations of this work, in particular that it represents a cross-sectional study in a single situation. But our overriding point is a more general one. We believe that the findings from this study validate our approach and confirm the need for specified multivariate models whose purpose is to guide further empirical, theoretical and practical work. Because of the interdependence amongst these variables, partial tests of relationships of the kind that dominate the existing empirical literature in applied psychology and organizational behaviour may be misleading. This general point can be substantiated by returning to Table 2. For each of the outcome variables (see columns 1–8) it is potentially misleading to examine rows 1 or 2 or 3 alone. For example, looking at column 1 row 3 one might conclude that levels of perceived control and error demands predict frequency of system use; and yet this effect disappears with a more multivariate approach (see column 1 row 4). More generally within Table 2, many significant predictors of outcomes are rendered insignificant with greater statistical control. This is unsurprising, and yet a partial approach has been common within this field as evidenced in the literature review described earlier in this paper. Put another way, these findings validate the need for multivariate, model based approaches within which competing hypotheses are tested. We recognize that longitudinal studies are needed in this area, but not of the kind that simply test partial models, replicating existing weaknesses in the literature. Our argument is that better designed studies are now required to develop and test multivariate models of the kind described in this study.

We now consider some theoretical, methodological and practical issues arising from these studies and from our approach. Theoretically, we believe there are five principal needs in this area.

First, there are other variables as candidates for inclusion in our model, for example user performance as an outcome variable.

Second, there is a need for more specificity and differentiation linking the predictors and outcomes. For example, psychological mood when using the system may be heavily influenced by the demands placed on the user, whereas extent of usage of the system may be associated with job role and grade.

Third, we also need to try to be more differentiated with respect to the timing of hypothesized effects. Thus it may be that users' first reactions to new technology are influenced by their attitudes towards computers in general. Later their specific expectations about the impact of their particular system may become most salient. And when the new technology becomes operational, they may initially be heavily influenced by the usability of the new system. With time and experience their reactions may, however, be more strongly influenced by the emerging functionality of the new system and by the impact it has on the user's job content and skill utilization. Phased models of this kind are rare in this field (and indeed in applied research more generally) but this model offers opportunities to undertake such developments.

Fourth, it may be that the salience of particular variables may vary across situations. For example in one case, user satisfaction with a new system may be primarily a function of its usability and this may persist, particularly if usability is poor; in another, the critical issue may be job content. Part of the task then is to identify the contingencies under which particular variables are salient.

And fifth, we need to undertake much more work investigating underlying psychological processes and mechanisms which may help explain our findings. For example, if demands are found to be associated with mood when using a new system, why is this the case? What is happening, for example, cognitively? This requires a finer grain of analysis.

We believe that the development and use of models of this kind help promote such theoretical differentiation.

Methodologically too, there are issues to address. By adopting and adapting models of this kind, it becomes possible for researchers to attempt more consistency in their selection of variables for inclusion. It also encourages and allows a more comparative approach, testing rival explanations, thereby enabling better controlled studies such that we can have more confidence that we are not identifying spurious effects.

Furthermore, our approach makes it clear that it is appropriate and timely to develop and test phased models using repeated measures and longitudinal research designs. These models help identify variables for inclusion and enable consideration of appropriate timings of their measurement.

We believe such models also have some potential for practical application. In part they can be seen as a delineation of some necessary but not sufficient factors predicting successful outcomes. For example, if you are managing the development and introduction of a new system, these results imply that functionality, usability, skill utilization and job demands are of critical importance; without active consideration of these, the new system may fail. The importance of functionality is of no surprise and is well recognized by the IT community. The role of usability however has only come to be seen as significant over the last few years, and the acceptance of its importance is still patchy. On the other hand, the levels of skill utilization and the nature of job demands experienced by users are rarely explicitly addressed as positive design and use goals. Rather, new technologies are often used to reduce skill use and to increase job demands. The implication for practice is that managers implementing new technologies should explicitly consider the impact on the job designs and work organization of the users. The evidence is that this is undertaken only rarely and that this represents a major reason for the failure of many new systems to meet their objectives (see Clegg et al., 1996).

Furthermore, prior expectations and attitudes towards computers in general are important, as are a range of biographical and situational factors. For managers introducing new technology, a further implication is that they should invest time and effort discussing with users what they need to undertake their work, and ensuring that they understand what the new system will do and what impact it will have on them. In this perspective, user participation, education and training are all central aspects of technological change.

Our argument is that multivariate models of this kind can be used to provide advice and checklists against which managers and others may consider their new technology developments and their plans for design, implementation and use.
