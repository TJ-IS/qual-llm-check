Journal of Information Technology (1997) 12, 61–71

# Power, knowledge and social process in technology analysis: the case of CAD/CAM

MARTIN HARRIS

Department of Management Studies, Brunel University, Uxbridge, Middlesex UB8 3PH, UK

'Social process' analyses of technology implementation have had a major influence on research carried out over the last 15 years. Technology implementation is now widely regarded as a 'socially negotiated' process the outcome of which is likely to be shaped by different interests and constituencies within the innovating firm. However, the explanatory status accorded to questions of power and political process remains problematic. This paper combines an empirical investigation of the political processes associated with the implementation of CAD/CAM with a cross-disciplinary treatment of the underlying theoretical issues. The concluding part of the paper focuses on 'political' and 'learning' aspects of technical change. 'Power', 'knowledge' and 'control' rationalities are included in a revised theorization of the technical change process.

## Introduction

A large body of academic research has examined the changes which take place at the conjunction of technology and organization in both the service and manufacturing contexts. In the latter sphere, new production technologies have been associated with wholesale changes in the organization of production, with the emergence of new organizational forms and with enhanced competitiveness. However, the organizational dimension has retained its ‘problematic’ status. (Clark and Staunton, 1989; Loveridge and Pitt, 1990; Scarbrough and Corbett, 1992; McLoughlin and Harris, 1997). The analytic relevance of the social process view has been maintained precisely because organizational outcomes are inherently difficult to predict and because many aspects of technical change are mediated by inherently complex social choices.

The advent of integrated manufacturing technologies such as computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM), computer-aided production management (CAPM) and computer-integrated manufacturing (CIM) in the early 1980s was accompanied by a range of widely-predicted benefits. CAD/CAM has been identified as a key building block technology with wide ranging ‘strategic’ advantages, including reductions in work in progress (WIP), an enhanced tender response, lead time reduction, improvements in design quality and reconfigured work organization (Voss, 1986; Senker, 1994). However, a broad range of contextual and organizational factors will typically influence the assimilation of these technologies into the operations of the firm. $^{1}$

This paper combines a theoretical analysis with an empirical investigation of the power mechanisms and organizational processes associated with the implementation of CAD/CAM. The research draws on literature from industrial sociology, management and innovation theory. The paper examines (1) the part played by power resources and organizational interests within the technical change process and (2) the ways in which political resources technical expertise and managerial control are combined in the management of technical change. Case study evidence is used to show the ways in which senior management 'readings' of the sectoral and corporate contexts (Weick, 1969; Child, 1972; Child and Smith, 1987) exercised an 'agenda setting' influence on both resource allocation and the scope of the changes considered. The case study analysis indicates a move from an ad hoc approach to implementation to a more planned strategic approach. Comparative 'case in sector' evidence is used to show the ways in which managerial assumptions about the implementation of CAD/CAM were informed by the 'technical and organizational remit' ceded to project engineers. The concluding part of the paper argues that social process accounts can be reframed around the idea that the 'power', 'knowledge' and 'control' rationalities are juxtaposed within the technical change process.

## A note on method

The paper is based on a case in sector study of CAD in the UK traction engineering industry, where CAD had become a key issue in the restructuring programmes of the 1980s. The sector is comprised of the following firms.

(1) Traction A is a division of a multinational power engineering and electronics corporation. The company is regarded as the technical leader in propulsion and control equipment. The company adopted turnkey CAD in 1977, 5 years prior to the first adoption elsewhere in the sector. At the time of the research the company had adopted much more advanced CAD and was in the process of implementing CAD/CAM.

(2) Traction B is the main competitor to Traction A in the supply of propulsion and control equipment to the overseas and domestic markets. This company adopted CAD in 1984, 7 years after the earliest investment at the rival firm. There was little or no in-house experience with CAD. This meant that there were technical uncertainties surrounding the issues of system configuration and the first CAD applications.

(3) Locobuild 1 is a large design and manufacturing operation which until 1987 was owned by the British Railways Board (BRB). The company was investing something in the order of £20 million per annum on new production technologies. Investments in CAD and CAD/CAM formed part of the corporate restructuring prior to privatization.

(4) Locobuild 2 is a private sector supplier of railway vehicles. The company contracts with Traction A and B for the design and manufacture of power electronics. The introduction of an integrated CAD/MRP system was closely associated with a company survival crisis and with a financial rescue package, which allowed the company to restructure its design-engineering capacity.

CAD systems offer potentially significant benefits to firms in the sector insofar as engineering and production costs can be cut and the lead and tender times reduced, in the face of the need for shorter and more specialized production runs. The first part of this paper presents empirical findings taken from a detailed case study analysis of Traction A. The second part of the paper elaborates on the empirical findings and draws out some theoretical implications. Appendices 1 and 2 contain case in sector comparisons of the four firms within the traction engineering industry. These highlight the ways in which political resources, technical learning and managerial control are combined in the management of technical change.

## Traction A: the management of technical change

## The implementation of the first CAD system

Traction A is a leading manufacturer of propulsion and control equipment in the UK traction engineering industry. The company is regarded as the technical leader in propulsion and control equipment for high-speed trains and other railway vehicles. The company adopted turnkey CAD for both design and drafting applications in 1977, 5 years prior to its first adoption elsewhere in the sector. At the time of the research the company was in the process of introducing a range of more advanced 2-d and 3-d CAD and CAD/CAM applications.

The implementation of the first CAD system was subject to a number of technical and organizational influences. The company was the first in this sector to invest in CAD and experience was scarce. The equipment was introduced in a context of rising turnover and work-load. Senior management wanted the system to become operational as soon as possible. The financial justification was carried out on a purely formal ‘rubber stamping’ basis. The cost of the investment was justified according to the 4 year payback time used to justify more conventional plant and capital equipment spending.

There was, in effect, no implementation plan. Negotiations with unionized CAD operators on the productivity and shift utilization of the system began after the system was introduced. $^{2}$ The existing grading and pay arrangements remained intact and payment for the introduction of the technology was made automatically. The union negotiators successfully defended the right to an ‘automatic reward for change’ and the right of all members to be trained in the use of CAD equipment. $^{3}$ These arrangements did not change when the system was extended in 1979–1980.

These difficulties notwithstanding, technical expertise was gradually accrued and a number of technical uncertainties (for example, developing software for specialized electrical schematics and avoiding processor downtime) were substantially reduced over a period of 5 years. The use of CAD produced a number of operational benefits including enhanced accuracy and replicability of the production drawings and productivity gains in the drawing office. $^{4}$ These benefits were confined mainly to the engineering department.

The company operates in a science-based product market in which electronic control and propulsion equipment has been subject to constant technical advance. $^{5}$ By the mid-1980s, the failure on the part of the British Government to invest in new rolling stock had produced a shortage of domestic orders. The export markets were affected by severe price competition which made tendering conditions difficult. Senior management took the view that engineering costs were a relatively high proportion of the overall costs. The perception was that the company was faced with the need to compete on both the price and 'non-price' performance criteria within these markets. $^{6}$

The first CAD system was one of the many investments in computer-aided equipment which had been made incrementally and without regard to the question of integrating different systems. The engineers had come to regard the first system as obsolete. They argued that CAD was part of an 'island' pattern of automation. They argued that establishing a CAD/CAM link would allow the direct transfer of production data from the engineering department drawing office to a remote factory site.

## The adoption of CAD/CAM

Moves towards CAD/CAM were announced in early 1985, against a background of profound and long-reaching changes in the composition of the European power electronics industry and changes in company manufacturing policies. $^{7}$ The project formed a key element in plans for the restructuring of the manufacturing capacity and it was seen as the first step in moves towards establishing the capability for CIM. The project was directed by a committee which was made up of senior engineering and production managers. The research indicated that control was exercised via ‘steering devices’ – mechanisms deployed at different levels within the corporation. Thus, controls operating at one particular level were only loosely coupled to those operating at another. For example, the corporate controls and communications exercised in respect of the company restructuring, plant closure and merger activity were distinct from those used within cost-benefit analyses or those attaching to the implementation of CAD/CAM. ## The implementation of CAD/CAM

The CAD/CAM project was conceived and launched as a 'top-down' initiative and it proceeded on a company-wide basis. The initiative required extensive managerial effort to maintain clarity and coordinate the various specialisms and departments. The long-term planning for CAD/CAM and CIM can be contrasted with the locally initiated and fragmented pattern of adoption which characterized the middle and late 1970s when CAD systems were first introduced.

Unlike their counterparts operating in the other three firms within the UK traction engineering industry (see above), who were by now adopting 'stand alone' CAD systems and relatively unsophisticated applications for the first time, the engineers at Traction A did not choose to focus on the costs and quantification of the benefits when they carried out the technical and financial evaluation of the integrated CAD/CAM system. The evaluation and monitoring of CAD/CAM was focused on non-price company-wide benefits.

Both the prescriptive (see, for examples, Ingersoll Engineers, 1985; Voss, 1986) and the academic literature (see Buchanan and Boddy 1986; Rhodes and Wield, 1994) suggests that the evaluation of non-price benefits is important because a broad range of issues and factors can be taken into account, even when these cannot be precisely quantified. The monitoring exercise was informed by the need to reduce lead/development times and by the need to find an appropriate balance between new product development and price competitiveness. Operational targets were set, not for individual systems components, but for the system as a whole. The CAD module and then the CAD/CAM system had to produce operational benefits before the next part of the plans for CIM was allowed to go forward.

The implementation of CAD/CAM represented the first stage in a three-phase plan, the aim of which was the integration of engineering, commercial and production systems across the company. Systems specialists from engineering and production were involved in the CAD/CAM part of the project. The project management team was relatively well resourced and it was assembled with some care. The senior project manager (a former production engineer and works manager) was a company director. A special appointment was made for a new director of computing operations. The director of computing and the two junior engineers were subordinate to the project engineer, whose role was to counterpose the detailed computer knowledge of the three specialists by maintaining an ‘overview’ perspective. It would appear that the two junior specialists were given the time and resources appropriate to the task, reducing the very considerable technical uncertainties (a lengthy and rigorous series of bench-mark tests was carried out over a 12 month period). The senior project manager continued in his own words to ‘guide, direct and challenge’ the technical specialists at every stage of the project. In the first 2 years of the project these engineers were responsible for the following.

1 Installing and implementing the first CAD/CAM module.

2 Establishing the local area network (LAN) within the engineering department.

3 Linking the CAD/CAM system to IBM and VAX mainframes via the LAN.

4 Developing the interface with the manufacturing function on a remote factory site.

5 Developing the interface between the engineering, procurement and production management (CAPM) databases.

6 Developing a range of 2-d and 3-d CAD and CAD/CAM applications within the drawing office.

## Operational benefits

The newly-established CAD/CAM applications produced a number of operational benefits. The direct transfer of engineering and production data between the drawing office and the remote factory site was achieved. The system allowed production drawings and machine-readable files for three-dimensional assemblies to be generated and transferred to the factory in 5 days as opposed to a previous approximation of 3 weeks. The bills of material generated on the system could also be fed into a computer-aided production control system, allowing the lead times and WIP to be reduced. The system produced benefits in PCB (printed circuit board) design and drafting work. The management were able to demonstrate that the PCB design times could be reduced from 40 days using subcontractors to 15 days following the introduction of specially adapted software. The management were confident that the costs could be substantially reduced and a number of quality control problems eliminated if these benefits could be fully routinized and the CAD/CAM applications fully assimilated into the organizational work within the drawing office.

The successful implementation of CAD/CAM meant that a number of the 'logistical bottle-necks' which had long troubled the engineering management in tendering for new orders were overcome. The operational control over the design and build process was also improved. The initial configuration of the traction and control gear has to be agreed between a number of manufacturing disciplines and the need for 'collisions' (which are apparent when two-dimensional production drawings are used in the design and building of prototypes) has to be avoided. Prior to the introduction of the CAD/CAM, the production engineers tended to accept the earliest design solutions as final. The use of three-dimensional CAD allows a wider range of design and build solutions to be advanced before the design is finalized. Prototypes are subject to modification at a much later stage in the life of a typical contract.

## Human resource management (HRM) issues

The case study evidence indicates that the financial and technical benefits noted above had been specified as part of the implementation plan. The production and personnel directors took the view that the CAD/CAM initiative would stand a greater chance of success if the management attempted to generate some commitment to organizational change within the drawing office. This part of the implementation plan hinged on an attempt to reach an agreement with the unionized operatives on the shifts and on the view that new working practices and performance criteria should be established within the drawing office. The consultation exercise began 14 months prior to the introduction of the equipment. Considerable effort was expended on developing consultative mechanisms and on generating commitment to the use of new CAD/CAM applications.

It has already been noted that the early CAD systems were introduced without a prior agreement on productivity, work organization or shift work. The personnel management were once again forced to accept single-shift working because of the threat of disruption caused by a refusal to use the new workstations. The staff remained opposed to two-shift working 3 years after the purchase of the equipment.

The personnel management were attempting to replace the collective reward for change with individually assessed rewards for using the new CAD/CAM applications on the CAD workstations. The union representatives took a generally positive line on the principle of introducing CAD/CAM within the drawing office, but argued that certain applications would require the draftsmen to accept an increased responsibility. They also argued that the subsequent moves towards CIM had organizational implications which were hard to predict in advance and that the applications not specified within the agreement might entail much more substantial responsibility at a later point. The move away from collective bargaining was emphasized in the company documentation on the new appraisal scheme which was designed to assess individual draftsmen on (1) their engineering knowledge, (2) their approach to cost, time and quality targets, (3) their readiness to take decisions and (4) individual cooperation.

The scheme can be seen as an attempt to incorporate elements of performance-related pay within a new technology agreement. However, the implementation of the scheme involved a revised approach to assessing the performance of individual draftsmen and a cultural shift from collective bargaining to a much more individualized ‘ethic of production’. The scheme encountered the following problems.

(1) The scheme was formulated by the personnel management, but the responsibility for its implementation and administration was passed on to the senior draftsman, who was also responsible for managing the CAD/CAM system itself. The senior draftsman was not trained in the negotiating skills necessary for the implementation of the scheme. The personnel director argued that the 'culture of technicism' which pervaded the Traction A engineering department tended to marginalize the HRM skills which would have been required for the successful administration of the appraisal scheme. This culture of technicism is shared by unionized technicians, graduate engineers and managers.

(2) The personnel management also argued that this culture was sustained by the fact that the management, at all levels, tended not to have experience outside of Traction A. The senior draftsman, for example, had worked with the company ‘man and boy’ (i.e. since his earliest working days). The technical director was renowned for his distinctive management style which epitomized the culture of technicism. This style of management combined technical virtuosity with admonishments and threats to individual section leaders.

(3) The individual assessments were held at irregular intervals throughout the year. The appraisal scheme was linked to the annual pay award and to increments within the existing pay structure. The line management argued that this made the scheme very difficult to administer.

(4) The senior draftsman had the responsibility of managing the throughput of work associated with tenders for highly innovative new projects such as the Channel Tunnel shuttle and Eurostar locomotives. The personnel director agreed that there was a general lack of confidence in the appraisal scheme. The senior draftsman found it difficult to find individuals willing to accept the responsibility for working on the new CAD/CAM applications. The moves towards the introduction of performance-related pay and individual rewards for using CAD/CAM applications were rejected. The technicians remained committed to the principle of collective bargaining and rewards for advanced applications. The personnel managements later created a new assistant manager grade which separated ‘technician grade’ operatives from ‘management grade’ individuals who were willing to use the new CAD/CAM applications.

## Analysis and theoretical implications

The introduction indicated that empirical research and theoretical development would be required if we are to advance our understanding of power and the political process in explaining technical change. This part of the analysis is concerned with examining more closely the way in which the political resources were deployed within the CAD/CAM initiative and with investigating the precise ways in which ‘politics’, organizational learning and managerial controls provide an explanation for the outcomes observed in the case.

## Political resources and organizational interests

It is apparent that the adoption and implementation of CAD/CAM was shaped by more or less powerful organizational interests. However, ‘political’ behaviour (and social action generally) attaches itself to different issues which obtain at different levels within organizations. It is clear, for example, that the senior management dialogue with the corporate centre exercised a powerful influence on the ways in which the investment in CAD/CAM was considered prior to its implementation. It is also clear that both the project management activities and collective bargaining had a significant bearing on the final outcome.

The negotiations over the introduction of CAD/CAM applications into the Traction A drawing office were remote from the negotiations which took place between the corporate centre and senior management. This is consistent with the social process perspective which characterizes technology implementation as a complex, inherently political activity. However, it is apparent that a range of different structural, material and symbolic resources were deployed. Thus, the controls exercised at the corporate–divisional level depended on mechanisms which bore little resemblance to those aimed at the reduction of the political or technical uncertainties within the CAD/CAM project itself.

The senior management readings of the operating environment (Weick, 1969; Child, 1972, 1988; Child and Smith 1987), particularly those associated with the broad context of sectoral restructuring, exercised a significant influence on the initiative. The identification of CAD/CAM with non-price benefits was accompanied by a rejection of the existing accounting controls. These moves were authorized and initiated at the senior divisional and corporate levels. This can be thought of as an ‘agenda setting’ form of control. $^{8}$ Managerial thinking was directed not merely at the establishment of the CAD/CAM link, but also at the strategic need to address the question of lead time reduction and the need for shorter production runs and reduced operating costs.

The early CAD systems were adopted on an autonomous and incremental basis, with little or no senior management involvement in the implementation planning or evaluation of the benefits. The CAD/CAM project was supported by a sophisticated implementation plan and organizational practices which produced significantly enhanced managerial control over the key technical and financial aspects. The implementation of CAD/CAM was managed within particular technical and political parameters. The project team operated on a quasi-autonomous 'arms length' basis vis-à-vis the dominant coalition and the steering committee which had authorized the spending on CAD/CAM. The resources allocated to the CAD project management team, the scope of the changes considered and the key assumptions about the implementation and monitoring derived support for the project from the senior management and from the 'technical-organizational remit' ceded to the project engineer. This is the sphere of devolved power and control. The comparative evidence presented in Appendix 1 suggests that the terms of this remit exercised a crucial influence on the CAD project outcomes elsewhere in the sector.

## Power, knowledge and control in the technical change process

As noted in the Introduction, the issues of power, control and organizational choice have been central to the social process school. Neo-Schumpeterian innovation theory provides an alternative and equally influential account of how technological change should be explained. According to this school of thought, technological change is driven by technological trajectories and by the acquisition of cumulative, firm-specific technological knowledge $^{9}$ (Dosi, 1982; Pavitt, 1984; Dosi et al., 1988). A number of commentators have argued that the economics and sociological analyses of technical change are converging on an emergent conceptual synthesis (see, for example, Coombs et al., 1992; Willman, 1996). $^{10}$ It has been argued, on both conceptual and substantive grounds, that such a synthesis should be bound to incorporate counterposed notions of power (associated with the political process school) and knowledge (associated with the neo-Schumpeterians). $^{11}$

The evidence presented in this paper can be used to illustrate the ways in which power and knowledge are intertwined in the technical change process. The move from two-dimensional CAD to more sophisticated CAD/CAM applications reflects the cumulative technical learning which is frequently cited in the analysis of technological trajectories (Dosi, 1982; Pavitt, 1984; Dosi et al., 1988). The move to CAD/CAM also saw decisive shifts in the heuristics and 'rules of thumb' (Nelson and Winter, 1982) which underpinned the evaluation and implementation of this technology. It has been argued that our understanding of the technical-organizational change process would be improved if the social dynamics associated with these shifts were identified (see, for example, Howells, 1995). One way of doing this is to concentrate on the power-knowledge divide or, more specifically, to examine the precise ways in which organizational interests and technological expertise are combined in the innovation process. The

comparative findings on the technical and organizational remit ceded to project managers are of particular interest in this connection. The managerial working assumptions and tacit knowledge which underlaid the evaluation/implementation of the CAD were, in all four cases, very significant influences on the technical-organizational outcomes observed (see Appendices 1 and 2). A number of commentators have singled out this type of tacit knowledge as the very stuff of technological change (see, for example, Scarbrough and Corbett, 1992; Fleck, 1993) and some have argued that innovation is dependent on the ability of technologists to engage in 'learning by doing' and 'learning by example' (Argyris and Schon, 1978; Rosenberg, 1982; Dodgson, 1989; Pavitt, 1990). The notion of 'organizational learning' would thus appear to offer a viable account of the technical change process. However, this ignores the problem of how the technical function, with its capacity for absorbing technological knowledge, is related to other functions and to key players operating at the different levels within the innovating firm. The management of technical expertise implies the ability to engage in 'imperative coordination' (Reed, 1985, 1989) as well as the ability to deploy symbolic and material resources in support of particular initiatives. Once again, this draws attention to the ways in which the categories of power and knowledge interact at different levels within the organization. The momentum for technical change on the scale seen at Traction A was not generated by the technical function in isolation from other functions. Decisions about the strategic response to the profound changes in the sector took place at the corporate–divisional level and the moves to CAD/CAM were informed by various strategic and manufacturing policy considerations. The management of technical change is a cross-disciplinary activity and it is necessarily dependent on financial and other considerations outside the technical area. It would thus seem reasonable to argue that the technical–organizational learning and tacit knowledge associated with CAD/CAM, although no doubt important qua knowledge, was nevertheless deployed and legitimized within a specific constellation of power and organizational interest. The evidence on the implementation and evaluation suggests not just that technical learning occurs in a political context, but that the exercise of agenda setting power plays a crucial role in defining what is regarded as viable knowledge within technical change projects.

The technical learning associated with CAD/CAM co-existed with the bargaining surrounding the introduction of the technology into the drawing office. At this point one must acknowledge the more overtly political and conflictual forms of social action within the technical change process. CAD/CAM presented management with the opportunity to make a number of organizational changes within the drawing office. The dominant coalition within the company (who were at one point themselves under threat of closure from the corporate centre) had to negotiate with a well-organized, technically able, 'white-collar craft' union who succeeded in resisting the changes with respect to the pay structure, shift working and work organization.

The Traction A case shows that the project engineer was able to exercise effective control over the technical and financial aspects of the CAD/CAM initiative. However, the middle manager responsible for the appraisal exercise had neither the authority nor the professional knowledge appropriate for managing the cultural and organizational aspects of the changes espoused by the personnel director. $^{12}$

At this point it may be appropriate to draw out some theoretical perspectives which may be developed in the long term. There is a rich but largely untapped body of social process work which shows that radically different rationalities and modes of action may co-exist within any change setting. $^{14}$ For example Hickson et al. (1986) identified separate political ('interest accommodating'), technical ('problem solving') and coordination ('control') aspects of social action within complex organizations. This framework is adapted and 'mapped on' to the Traction A case in Table 2. The framework allows problem-solving activities to be seen as one aspect of the technical-organizational change process, whilst supporting the view that technical change can also be seen as an inherently political 'negotiated' process. The organizational complexity and 'messiness' observed by many researchers derives from the ways in which divergent rationalities are juxtaposed and balanced within the technical change process. Problem-solving and learning activities are crucially informed by both the control and interest-accommodating rationalities.

## Appendix 1: technical and political parameters of CAD project management

Economic accounts of technical change projects are often focused on the issue of resource allocation (see, for example, Gold, 1981). A significant part of the literature on managing technical-organizational change highlights the importance of political sponsorship and 'innovation champions' (Willcocks and Harris, 1990). The case study evidence suggests that the resourcing of the CAD project management team at Traction A derived not just from the political sponsorship of the project, but from a more closely specified technical and organizational remit ceded to the senior project engineer. Comparisons with three other cases, detailed below, indicate that it was the scope of this remit which differentiated the effect implementation of CAD at Traction A from other, less successful cases within the sector (see Locobuild 1 and Locobuild 2, below). The terms of this remit also had a strong bearing on the approach taken to targeting and monitoring the benefits (see Harris (1996a) for an analysis of CAD, strategic change and the debate on 'accounting lag'.)

## Traction A

The senior manager was appointed to oversee the implementation of CAD/CAM and the eventual computer-aided integration of the engineering and production capacity in line with the revised corporate strategy and manufacturing policies.

## Traction B

The CAD committee appointed a junior engineer to oversee the evaluation and implementation of the first CAD system; the engineer was given responsibility for meeting the cost-cutting targets.

## Locobuild 1

The senior engineering management appointed systems specialist as representatives of the engineering department in the implementation of the CAD system.

## Locobuild 2

The senior management appointed a junior engineer to evaluate the CAD and to provide technical support.

At Traction B, the project engineer was given a technical-organizational remit which was tightly focused on productivity and the reduction of the subcontracting costs. The project enjoyed substantial senior management support. The resourcing of the project management teams was not well matched to the scale of the technical and organizational changes sought by the management in the two Locobuild cases. At Locobuild 1, the senior engineers did not accept the full responsibility for the CAD project and left an inexperienced and under-resourced team to manage a technically and organizationally complex project. This situation derived from the compartmentalized and hierarchical management practices which had long been a feature of the state-nationalized parent body. Operational means and ends did not always match in what was in many respects a classic ‘garbage can’ (Cohen et al., 1972) situation.

The problem of under-resourced project management was to some extent paralleled at Locobuild 2, but in this case the relatively small number of users and the high levels of skill and commitment within the organization ensured that the operational means and ends were not divorced in such an obvious way. The CAD project was underwritten by the managing director and by senior engineers whose main concern was with winning orders and creating strategic room for manoeuvre – however, the implementation and development of the system was left to the junior engineers. (See Appendix 2 for more details on the reduction of the technical and political uncertainties.)

## Appendix 2: the reduction of political and technical uncertainties

## Traction A

This company attempted to reduce the political uncertainties within the consultation exercise. The technical uncertainties were reduced by planning and scanning awareness and bench-mark tests; the transfer of knowledge was via a company teaching scheme. The 1985

CAD/CAM investment was justified according to non-price company-wide benefits.

## Traction B

The political uncertainties were reduced by long negotiations with the draftsmen's union, an eventual agreement on shift work and the gradual dissemination of CAD skills. The technical uncertainties were reduced via a rigorous technical and financial evaluation. There was an engineering and accounting consensus on two-shift utilization. The cost reduction and productivity benefits were realized.

## Locobuild 1

The retention of the existing shift work, payment and grading arrangements maintained the political status quo. The technical uncertainties associated with the CAD/CAM integration remained. Two-shift working was not achieved.

## Locobuild 2

The retention of the nucleus of design engineers during the company's survival crisis precluded the political uncertainties. The technical uncertainties were reduced by trial and error. There was informal monitoring of the productivity. Ad hoc utilization produced uncertain productivity benefits.
