# A comparative study of business to consumer electronic commerce within the Australian insurance sector

GRAHAM I. COSTELLO and J™RG H. TUCHEN Andersen Consulting, 141 Walker Street, North Sydney, NSW 2060, Australia

Electronic commerce is causing fundamental changes in the insurance sector. Inherent opportunities of this innovative sales channel are driving the development of a new customer relationship paradigm, development of new products, pursuit of low cost \`self service’ strategies, and emergence of \`virtual brokers’ . The Australian insurance sector is well positioned to take advantage of electronic commerce due to the high level of PC penetration, high Internet usage, and extensive broadband infrastructure. The perception is that the Australian insurance sector is meeting these challenges. Surprisingly, despite the emergence of electronic commerce as a \`hot topic’ in the information technology and insurance sector literature, little empirical research has been reported. Much of the extant literature can be criticized as being too generic and super® cial. It is argued that until research is focused on speci® c aspects of electronic commerce, we will fail to capture meaningful insights. The aim of this research project is to develop a research framework appropriate for electronic commerce, research and to apply it to a speci® c sector (insurance), in a speci® c geographical region (Australia), using a speci® c electronic commerce, medium (Internet), for a speci® c purpose (business to consumer sale of risk products). The research objective is to discover which Australian insurance companies are using electronic commerce for what. The survey found that of the 21 largest Australian insurance companies only 18 have web sites. These sites are mainly used for promotional purposes and not for directly generating sales. Only six companies offer customer-speci® c pricing of their products. And of these, only four companies sell any of their products over the Internet. Paradoxically, despite pressing busines drivers in the insurance sector and a favourable electronic commerce environment in Australia, these ® nd ings demonstrate a signi® cant gap between appreciation of the importance of electronic commerce and real ization of commercial potential. Whilst most Australian insurance companies are well aware of the specia importance of electronic commerce, many fail to take full advantage. Although further qualitative research is recommended to understand why this is so, it is clear that a signi® cant gap remains between the tech nical capabilities of electronic commerce and actual practice in the Australian insurance sector.

## Introduction

The emerging online economy promises to transform traditional ways of doing business, particularly in the service industry (Tapscott, 1995). New technologies facilitating communication and knowledge transfer, including e-mail, electronic data interchange, and even interactive multimedia kiosks, as well as the Internet, have huge potential to change the ways organizations create markets, deliver products and serve customers (Malone et al., 1987; Rayport and Sviokla, 1995; Hagel and Armstrong, 1997).

Electronic commerce can be broadly de® ned as the interchange of goods, services, property, ideas, or communications through an electronic medium for purposes of facilitating or conducting business. This may involve the ordering, payment, and even the actual delivery of goods or services. There are many arguments why electronic commerce will and must become the dominant form of interchange between businesses and consumers. One such reason is that it can be shown that about 40± 90% (depending on the industry) of the cost of a typical consumer good is added after the product leaves the factory; electronic commerce has the potential to signi® cantly reduce these costs. Another reason is the increased ability to service that product at marginal cost or to provide increased levels of customer service.

In moving online, Australia and its service sector have a number of advantages (Australian Coalition of Service

Industries, 1997 and Australian Coalition of Service Industries, 1996):

(1) Australia has the eighth highest PC penetration per capita in the world.

(2) The International Telecommunications Union (ITU) ranks Australia the ® fth most likely nation in the world to emerge as the leader of the information superhighway due to its high projected Internet usage rates and high density of online access technology.

(3) Most students in Australia have access to the Internet through state funded initiatives.

(4) Australia has an advanced communications infrastructure.

(5) Australia has a history of being an early adopter of new technology.

(6) Australia has recognized the symbiotic relationship between government and the commercial sector in fostering the one-line economy.

These factors in the development of eCommerce beg the critical questions when an eCommerce market will emerge and what organizations must do to capture the full value of electronic commerce. The convergence of the accelerating capabilities of computers, the expanding reach of a ubiquitous standards-based network like the Internet, and the increasing ability to capture and leverage knowledge in a digital form are primarily responsible for driving electronic commerce today.

A number of other forces are developing in parallel driving markets, especially ® nancial services markets, towards a new customer relationship paradigm:

Increasing customer Consumers’ education about sophistication: complex products is being advanced by a dramatic increase in advertising and information.

Deregulation: Boundaries between different industries (i.e. between banks, insurance companies and other ® nancial institutions) are breaking down. Government deregulation leads to lower entry barriers and increasing competition.

Service philosophy: Increasing consumer demands lead to a 24-hours-a-day, 7 days-a-week customer service. Customer orientation leads to immense information needs for companies.

This new paradigm has important implications for each stage of the customer relationship life cycle. Most notably, the increased importance of targeted marketing; product delivery on a more direct selling basis; around-the-clock customer service; and the integration of third party business into the service delivery process.

It will become crucial for the insurance industry to close the gap between the widespread expectations (nearly 72% of insurance companies expect the Internet to have an important impact on their businesses within the next ® ve years) and the realities of today (very few insurance companies, if any, yet have a coherent Internet strategy in place (Pasher, 1997)). Given the media hype and speculation about the impact of electronic commerce on the insurance sector, there is comparatively little empirical supporting evidence. On the one hand there are broad and quantitative reports developed by specialized IT research companies, which usually cover buzzwords such as \`Internet’ or \`electronic commerce’ as a whole, focus on special industry types or on special aspects of electronic commerce. Although often providing useful high-level statistics, forecasts for future trends, and recommendations, these reports fail to analyse the situation on a company level in a speci® c industry. On the other hand there are qualitative papers analysing the situation in Australia (Federal Bureau of Consumer Affairs, 1997), which summarize current knowledge about the subject but lack usable frameworks enabling comparative studies. There is no research that clearly articulates what is actually happening on the ground in speci® c companies. This research project goes some way to rectifying that need by investigating and comparing the efforts of the largest Australian insurance companies in regard to the Internet, the design and the structure of their web sites and the ef® ciency and the extent to which electronic commerce is exploited as an alternative distribution channel.

## Setting up the research framework

## Objectives and research question

The research objective is to discover which Australian insurance companies are using electronic commerce for what. Speci® c research questions are: (1) Which Australian insurance companies have an Internet presence? (2) What are those companies using the Internet for? (3) How have those companies exploited \`virtualization’ opportunities of the Internet? And (4) What observations can be drawn from actions of the Australian insurance sector?

There are a number of different mediums for electronic commerce including electronic data interchange (EDI) value added networks (VAN) (such as WIN), proprietary dial up or closed user group (CUG) networks (e.g. risk management systems such as Worldwide Insurance). The scope of this project is

## Business to consumer electronic commerce

limited to the medium of electronic commerce over the Internet. Likewise there is a variety of different types of electronic commerce: business to business, business to business to consumer (i.e. insurance sales through intermediaries such as insurance brokers), business to consumer (i.e. direct sales), and electronic markets (such as insurance malls InsWeb (http://www.insweb.com) or InsureMarket (http://www.insurmarket.com)). This study will focus on business-to-consumer applications of the Internet for various kinds of insurance such as life, health, property and casualty, and car. Typical applications comprise: (1) marketing products online, (2) allowing customers to update data online, (3) recording claims through the Internet, (4) allowing customers to generate policy quotes, and (5) selling products online.

## The research framework

To answer the research questions it was necessary to develop a research framework against which to assess each insurance company. A number of alternative frameworks were considered. The two considered most appropriate were value chain analysis (prepurchase, purchase, and postpurchase) as recently employed by Liu et al. (1997), and the \`eCommerce Opportunity Matrix’ (Andersen Consulting, 1997). The second framework was chosen, as the two key dimensions considered most relevant are the business processes that are supported, and the degree to which the \`virtualization’ opportunities of the Internet are exploited:

Business processes: The processes electronic commerce are used for can be described as generic high-level business process categories:

(i) Develop products and services:

How can electronic commerce be used to improve the development of new products and services?

(ii) Generate demand:

How will buyers and sellers ® nd each other? How will customer and market data/knowledge in real time be analysed and utilized?

(iii) Ful® l demand:

How will customers, suppliers, partners be engaged and retained?

How will purchases be executed, goods delivered and payments made?

(iv) Plan and manage enterprise: What kinds of business and technical architectures will be necessary?

Virtualization spectrum: Virtualization means the systematic elimination of time, space and form barriers. Three phases of virtualization can be distinguished:

(i) Publish:

Use of electronic channels for knowledge dissemination and one-way communication.

(ii) Interact:

Use of electronic channels for collaboration and interactive engagement.

(iii) Transact:

The formal, quanti® able exchange of value.

A sample comprising the top 21 Australian insurance companies representing more than 70% of the Australian insurance market (judged by premium income, see Figure 2) were mapped against this framework. The research approach was a combination of directly visiting each of their web sites three times over a period of six months to conduct content analysis as well as a desk survey of company information in the public domain. Given the commercial sensitivity of many of these Internet related projects, no attempt was made to discover information that was not in the public domain.

## Analysis of Australian insurance sector web sites

The largest 21 Australian insurance companies have been included in the study. A total of 18 web sites were found, meaning that only 86% of the largest Australian insurance companies have web sites. Despite the immense potential inherent in electronic commerce, there are three Australian insurance companies, which are not present on the Internet.

This section analyses the 18 web sites, which were found. At ® rst a demographic overview will be given to represent the company’s intention in creating a web site. This intention will be further investigated by mapping each site against the framework developed in the previous chapter. Some detailed and speci® c ® ndings are attached as appendices (i.e. detailed listing of web sites’ content, evaluation of web sites’ quality).

## Demographics of web sites

The sample was categorized by the product line they advertise and/or sell via their web site. It could be assumed that complex products such as life or health insurance are harder to sell online than simple insurance products such as travel insurance. Figure 3 shows the percentage frequency distribution of insurance products advertised on the 18 web sites.

The ® rst surprising result is that 72% of sites advertise ® nancial services other than insurance. Services advertised include provision of mutual funds or home loans. (From the 13 companies offering other ® nancial services, 100% offer saving and investment opportunities, 85% offer loans, 69% offer superannuation, 38% offer other retirement investments, 31% offer accounts and PC banking and 23% offer credit cards over their web sites.) These services do not usually represent the core business of the company. This suggests that the investigated insurance companies are focusing their online activities on ® nancia services traditionally offered by banks and investment funds rather than their core products and services. This re¯ ects the trend in Australia for insurance companies to offer the full range of ® nancial services (all® nanz).

The research framework developed maps the web sites against the following two dimensions: (1) core processes (vertical axis), and (2) level of interaction (horizontal axis). Shading is are used to visualize the degree of exploitation. The darker the grey shading the more insurance companies are making use of the Internet in that particular segment. The main patterns of the demographic overview of the previous chapter are again apparent. As the level of interaction increases (moving on the horizontal axis from publish to transact) fewer Australian insurance companies are to be found in the particular segments of the framework.

Eighteen Australian insurance companies (100%) are found in the segment \`Publish/Generate Demand’. (The remaining web site was in construction and contained the note that a full version would be available soon.) Thus all of the companies use the Internet in order to promote major products and to give a broad overview about the services offered by the company. This result is contrasted by two other key ® ndings:

Key ® nding 3: Only 4 companies (22%) are making use of the Internet in the segment \`Transact/Ful® Demand’. Key ® nding 4: Only seven companies are to be found in the segments \`Interact/Generate Demand’ (39%) and six in \`Interact/Ful® l Demand’ (33%), where they offer customer-speci® c prices for their products or offer online after-sales services such as claims handling.

## Discussion

In terms of product complexity the results show that the Internet can be broadly used for insurance products. Even though the life products sold over the Internet today are simple in their nature (i.e. term life or accidental death bene® t), the market does not focu entirely on simple products. This ® nding concentrates analysis of potential electronic commerce bene® ts for insurance companies on four questions:

(1) How is the sale of an insurance product over the Internet affected by that product’s complexity?

Hypothesis: All products for which the sale transaction may be completed at the point of sale with the use of automated systems may be candidates for sale on the Internet. Hypothesis: It is necessary to develop products speci® cally for direct Internet channels. Some products are too complex to explain via the Internet. Some are legally not suited to electronic commerce. It is necessary to recognize both the weaknesses as well as strengths of the Internet direct channel and to design product appropriately.

In addition the following thoughts could serve as a basis for a detailed analysis of the possibilities and opportunities for individual companies. They are examples of how the scope of electronic commerce could be broadened by introducing innovative products and/or services:

(1) Set up relationships with any relevant value provider to sell insurance on the Internet and to bundle products with added value (e.g. offer car insurance on car dealer web site).

(2) Provide customer auction sites where the customers articulate their needs and the insurance companies bid the coverage.

(3) Use data on consumer patterns through web sites or on-line demographic marketers and rede® ne de® nitions of target markets as well as develop new products and services.

(4) Differentiate between different products and develop innovative distribution channels for each type (i.e. use push-technology for commodity insurance products).

(5) Identify and directly address potential risks such as the following:

Concerns that the Internet is still an insecure medium for transmission of ® nancial information.

Clients may be reluctant to transact insurance business without human intervention. Consequently a mix of channels is most appropriate.

Although this research project has answered the research question of \`which Australian insurance companies are using the Internet for what?’ , it has been beyond the scope and methodological capabilities to investigate why? It is argued that further research is needed to address this important question. Consequently it is recommended there would be value in pursuing the following research topics:

(1) Comparison with:

(i) major insurance markets such as the US, Europe, and South East Asia.

(ii) ® nancial service providers such as banks, investment companies, and brokers.

(iii) companies in other sectors such as products (e.g. Amazon, CD Now), high tech-nology (e.g. Gateway, Dell, Cisco), and media (e.g. Reuters, Bloom® eld, Economist).

(2) Qualitative case study research of Australian insurance companies to investigate why they exhibit the behaviour reported here. In addition it would be possible to identify bene® ts realized and to examine business cases.

## Conclusion

This comparative survey of business to consumer electronic commerce within the Australian insurance sector has revealed the following insights:

Signi® cant gaps: There is a signi® cant gap between public perception and actual functionality. Whilst most Australian insurance companies have publicly declared strategies to exploit electronic commerce, only a small minority have met the challenge.

Missed opportunities: Australian insurance companies use the Internet mainly for publishing their products and services rather than as an additional sales channel. Only four Australian insurance companies are today selling any of their products over the Internet.

Vacant marketplace: Australian insurance companies are leaving the \`virtual insurance marketplace’ open to new entrants and global competitors. This new marketplace is likely to be characterized by new products and services developed speci® cally for the Internet, different forms of marketing and branding, and different customer segments with different behaviour, expectations and value propositions.

Learning organizations: Australian insurance companies are not taking advantage of existing technological capabilities to begin to learn how to operate in the new marketplace. By doing so, they place themselves at risk of being forever forced into the position of global insurance marketplace \`laggards’ .
