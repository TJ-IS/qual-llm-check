# Using multiple suppliers to mitigate the risk of IT outsourcing at ICI and Wessex Water

WENDY L. CURRIE

Shef® eld University Management School, 9 Mappin Street, Shef® eld S1 4DT, UK

The paper presents the ® ndings from empirical research on IT outsourcing in two British companies: ICI Plc and Wessex Water. Both organizations have pursued a selective sourcing approach using multiple suppliers in an attempt to minimize risks and maximize bene® ts. Whilst the majority of existing literature on selective sourcing concentrates on the \`mega-contracts’ , this research explores some of the issues and concerns of companies with small to medium sized outsourcing contracts. The ® ndings suggest that selective and competitive sourcing may offer the client company value-added bene® ts, although it is important to develop strengths in the areas of contract management and negotiation and competitive benchmarking and performance management.

## Introduction: The outsourcing marketplace

The worldwide outsourcing market has continued to grow steadily. A recent claim by the Yankee Group is that the worldwide (US and nonUS) market was US\$185.3 bn in 1998 and will rise to US\$267 bn by 2000, with the US market accounting for US\$116 bn of this ® gure. This gives a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 20% (Yankee Group, 1998) (see Figure 1). The International Data Corporation (IDC) believe the world-wide IT outsourcing services market will surpass US\$136.6 bn by 2000 (IDC, 1998a). Notwithstanding the disparity in the forecasts, IT outsourcing has increased in both the federal/public and commercial/private sectors (Willcocks and Lacity, 1997). In the United Kingdom, privatization witnessed through market testing, compulsory competitive tendering (CCT) and contracting-out have led to large outsourcing contracts in central and local government (Currie, 1996). One of the most high pro® le outsourcing contracts is between the Inland Revenue/EDS at an estimated cost of US\$1.6 bn over 10 years. The western European outsourcing market is also predicted to increase to US\$33.6 bn by 2001, a ® gure which represents approximately 26% of the global outsourcing market; with the \`outsourcing reluctant’ countries in Europe such as Germany, Belgium and Italy becoming more interested in outsourcing (IDC, 1998a).

Coupled with the burgeoning global outsourcing market place, the scope of IT services being outsourced is also growing. One reason is the increasing complexity in IT service provision, maintenance, and the business processes which support them (Currie and Willcocks, 1997). Some commentators are advocating the advantages of Business Process Outsourcing (BPO), which is a relatively new concept, and extended to functions like HRM, accounting, logistics and customer services. In essence, BPO refers to the combined outsourcing of an IT system and the business process it supports (IDC, 1998b). In another survey, Gartner Group offers a percentage breakdown of IT services over the next four years: 25% desktop/distributed; 18% applications; 15% networks; 42% datacentres. They claim the area of desktop/distributed services is becoming increasingly complex, largely because of the accelerated obsolescence of PCs. Indeed, providing support for the entire PC network poses complex problems for those organizations with a large PC facility. Yet the rapid rate at which PCs and packages become out of date offers fresh challenges to organizations wishing to outsource this activity. This is because it is dif® cult to predict what the PC marketplace will look like one year ahead, let alone in ® ve years’ time.

The range of outsourcing vendors/suppliers is large, but there are currently only about ® ve to seven vendors with the capability to provide a full suite of IT services. These vendors are spearheading the move towards business process outsourcing largely because they perceive there will be additional pro® ts from this work compared with the smaller margins from datacentre outsourcing (Currie and Willcocks, 1998a). Coupled with the market leaders, there are a range of smaller \`niche-players making inroads into the outsourcing marketplace. Both the large and smaller outsourcing vendors have

## A typology of IT outsourcing decisions

Research by Currie and Willcocks (1998a,b) identi® ed ® ve types of IT outsourcing decisions: total outsourcing; multiple supplier sourcing; selective sourcing; joint venture/strategic alliance sourcing and insourcing (retaining an in-house IT department). For the purposes of this paper, we are interested in multiple supplier/selective sourcing arrangements (Willcocks and Fitzgerald, 1993b; 1995a). This is where a company signs outsourcing contracts with more than one IT supplier. In the case of multiple supplier sourcing, a company may outsource a signi® cant amount of IT work to only one supplier who may then subcontract work to other suppliers (Willcocks and Choi, 1995). This occurred with JP Morgan (Hoffman, 1996). Alternatively, it may outsource a signi® cant proportion of IT work to many different suppliers. Two key advantages are associated with multiple sourcing arrangements. First, it allows the client organization to negotiate outsourcing contracts with a range of potential suppliers, differentiated by knowledge, experience and market position (Cross, 1995). Since the client organization is unlikely to possess all the relevant in-house expertise, the decision to negotiate with niche player suppliers may be critical for the continuing or new provision of IT services. Second, by using the services of several suppliers, the client organization is more likely to mitigate the risk of outsourcing. For example, research ® ndings demonstrate that total outsourcing deals where the client organization outsources more than 70± 80% of IT activities and services to a single supplier pose the greatest risks (Lacity and Hirschheim, 1993). They may choose to seek an arrangement with one large supplier who may then subcontract IT work to other suppliers, or may apportion IT work more equally among suppliers (Lacity et al., 1995). Similarly, companies may engage in what is termed \`selective sourcing’ and outsource selected IT work to a discrete number of approved suppliers. For example, systems development work may be contracted to one supplier and datacentre work to another (Fitzgerald and Willcocks, 1994). The company may still retain a large in-house IT department, although it may be planning to outsource more work in the future. Multiple supplier sourcing and selective sourcing are outsourcing strategies designed to mitigate the risks of relying on a single supplier, as is the case in total outsourcing deals. Joint venture/strategic alliance sourcing is a further option, but this involves a company entering into a long-term partnership with another company. Clearly, this is not appropriate for many companies who seek short to medium term IT sourcing arrangements (Currie and Willcocks, 1998b).

Whilst it is outside the scope of the present paper to discuss total outsourcing and joint venture/strategic alliance sourcing in detail, it is apparent that identi® able risks are associated with all types of IT sourcing arrangements. Since we are concerned with multiple supplier and selective sourcing arrangements, we will give a brief overview of their main risks. The primary risk appears to be the dif® culty in managing and co-ordinating the work of several suppliers (Willcocks et al., 1995). This point has been mentioned in the literature by Cross (1995) referring to the experience of BP Exploration, and the dif® culty of implementing a \`framework agreement’ with suppliers. Another problem with sourcing IT work to several suppliers is pinpointing accountability and responsibility, particularly if the business processes are interdependent (Loh and Venkatraman, 1992). In this paper, we analyse the case histories of two very different British organizations: ICI plc and Wessex Water Services. The contribution of this paper is to examine empirical work on IT outsourcing contracts with multiple suppliers, as opposed to the \`mega deals’ which generate much publicity in the media.

## Methodology

The research study was intended to elicit data/information from IT directors/managers in a variety of private and public sector organizations. Adopting a qualitative approach to the research enquiry (Silverman, 1993) the focus was on how this group interpreted the IT outsourcing process. IT directors/managers were interviewed about their participation in the various stages of the IT outsourcing from decision making to implementation. Whilst it was recognized that this group was likely to interpret the outsourcing process differently from business managers and senior executives, etc, they were interviewed because they were more likely to have dealings both internally (with business units/departments) and externally (with outsourcing suppliers). This wa not the case for other personnel.

This paper draws on two detailed case studies. The ® rst company is ICI Plc (Imperial Chemical Industry), a company with a turnover of £11 billion and 55 600 employees (The Times, 1998). The main business of ICI is the manufacture of paints, adhesives, fragrances, food additives, industrial materials and industrial chemicals. The second company is Wessex Water Plc, based in the west of England with an annual turnover of £254.3 m. The main business activities of Wessex Water Plc are water supply, sewage services, waste management, and the design and manufacture of advanced thermal drying process equipment. Both organizations have entered into multiple supplier sourcing arrangements (Currie and Willcocks, 1998a). Interviews with the IT directors in the two organizations lasted over two hours. A tape recorder was used during the interviews and the transcripts were prepared and sent to the interviewees for their comments. Whilst some editing of the transcripts was necessary (to remove material considered to be con® dential), the case studies offer a comprehensive account of the events both leading up to, and following, the outsourcing decision. Semistructured interviews sought data information on a range of topics. They included: the history leading up to outsourcing; the outsourcing strategy; the range of IT activities and services to be outsourced; the preparation of the outsourcing contract; outsourcing and IT spend; the success and failure factors of outsourcing; relationships with suppliers; skill requirements and training strategies. These issues were prioritized differently by the two IT directors at ICI and Wessex Water respectively. To this end, it is interesting to compare and contrast the histories, experiences and outcomes of IT outsourcing in two organizations, each pursuing a multiple supplier strategy.

## ICI plc

## Context and overview

ICI has gone through a major transition in the last ® fteen years which was business and technology led. This transition is still taking place facilitated, in part, by an outsourcing arrangement described as \`selective and competitive sourcing’. By tradition, ICI had a fairly active involvement with IT. It grew up originally in the more technical side of the company in the post-war years of the 1950s and 1960s as computing emerged from the engineering and production

One result was an information technology capability which grew up initially from the various separate divisions of the company, each owning and managing its own data centre, telecoms network and associated IT activities. When the company made a major thrust into continental Europe and set up ICI Europa, this situation was repeated with the new company having its own data centre, telecoms network and software facility. This was once more the situation in North America, with the acquisition of another company. By the early 1980s, fragmentation of the entire technical resource affected the whole company.

## 1980± 1990 ± a period of restructuring

Under the latter days of Sir John Harvey Jones as CEO in 1981/2 there was an effort to restructure business and technical activities. A debate was emerging about how best to organize these activities for cost effectiveness, ef® ciency and rationalization. This resulted in setting up a central monopoly based at Wilmslow in the United Kingdom, initially called Central Management Services. The monopoly was given powers in mainframe computing and wide area telecoms. What followed was a ten-year period when that central monopoly created an effective global telecom network by integrating those networks. It was still an area of monopoly telecoms suppliers (with British Telecom in the UK and others operating abroad), where raw bandwidth was purchased, i.e. leased lines. But then ICI built up its own network of switching technology to load that capacity and give it a wider range of services.

The changes initiated in the early 1980s were generally perceived in a positive light. They generated greater shareholder value. Such change was top management led. There was a degree of opposition from the businesses as they felt a marked loss of technical autonomy. Yet the board took the view that there was real bene® t in merging these activities. During this time, the businesses still retained signi® cant IT budgets and they had to pay towards this monopoly since it was not paid for centrally. Coupled with this, software was largely speci® ed and developed by the businesses even though it was run on central mainframes. There was a certain amount of shared software, i.e. pan-Continental European distribution and accounting systems, but totally different systems in the UK, including quite signi® cantly different ones between the businesses, and different ones in North America.

## The 1990s and beyond

In the early 1990s, the central group that had generated signi® cant shareholder value in the 1980s became too large and too technical in its approach. It tended to see itself as setting the IT agenda for the entire company. So by about 1992, there were about 1300 people in corporate IT (telecoms, main frame, systems development, etc). A situation had arisen in that monopoly conditions within corporate IT tended to restrict the autonomy of the various business units from making their own IT-enabled business decisions. Structural problems also faced corporate IT, particularly with the demerger plan. This period therefore suggested a radical rethink facilitated by the opportunities to source IT services from the market.

In 1992, corporate IT services were paid for by the businesses who had little choice in the matter. They were unable to go to the external market place for IT services, as this was not permitted. A situation was reached where the group had signi® cant technological excellence. However, the businesses were unhappy because they believed that corporate IT services were not responsive to their requirements. Many felt this group was simply pursuing its own technical agenda and, in doing so, passing costs onto the various businesses. A concrete example of this is where the 45 people from the 1300 strong staff in corporate IT were engaged in \`blue sky technical work’ like voice recognition. Yet questions could be asked about the feasibility of doing voice recognition development work in a chemical company, especially when this technology can be procured from the marketplace.

## Internal deregulation of IT services

The concerns about the role of corporate IT services from the businesses coincided with other wide ranging strategic changes at ICI, such as the demerger of Zeneca. In mid-1992, ICI announced it would take its pharmaceutical, crop protection and speciality chemicals businesses and create a totally separate company. So in the process of achieving this objective, ICI had to break up all those corporate groups and locate them into one of two much smaller groups ± ICI and Zeneca. The major structural changes introduced to reduce costs and rationalize business services created an opportunity to re-visit the role of corporate IT.

## Selective and competitive outsourcing

Rationalization and downsizing resulted in a loss of 400 of the 1300 jobs in corporate IT. These job losses were across the hierarchical scale from managers to technicians. Given the split between ICI and Zeneca, a strategy was formed to split the telecom network giving each company a much smaller facility. This was a critical activity within ICI, but to divide it internally would cost about £5 million. This option was considered too expensive for each of the two businesses to undertake. An outsourcing arrangement was therefore considered as an alternative and cheaper option. Senior managers at ICI went to the marketplace and offered British suppliers the opportunity to compete for the contract to split the telecoms network. At the actual time of the demerger in 1993, ICI had already signed a contract with Zeneca that stated that by the end of 1994, the split would be complete. So for the period between 1993± 94, ICI continued to sell services to Zeneca on a transitional basis. In the autumn of 1993, the outsourcing document was developed, and tested throughout the marketplace at a fairly high level.

## Selecting the suppliers

A short list of about three major suppliers was identi-® ed: Racal Network Services, a combination of BT and MCI, and a combination of Cable and Wireless and AT&T. In the following spring of 1994, they were given the bidding document. This document written by ICI with the help of its legal team clearly speci® ed what the company wanted from the supplier. The competitive bidding process was undertaken throughout the spring and summer of 1994 with the three potential suppliers. The ultimate contract was eventually won by Racal Network Services who paid ICI and Zeneca $\mathcal { L } 3$ m to take over the telecom network. Senior management at both companies considered this to be a preferable outcome than paying $\mathcal { L } 5$ million themselves to split the network. This move also cut tariffs by 20% because ICI’s telecoms network facility was merged with the larger out® t of the supplier. This was an additional bene® t from outsourcing. Twenty employees transferred to Racal under the terms of the deal.

## A world-class datacentre

As a consequence of the demerger with Zeneca, ICI was left with a world class data centre in the United Kingdom and a much smaller one in Holland. Zeneca ended up with its own data centre. The UK datacentre was a full scale, purpose built and world class, which ran with about 350 MIPS. But it had the capability of running between 8± 900 to 1000 MIPs. The Dutch datacentre by comparison was situated on the 4th ¯ oor of an of® ce building and was much smaller. ICI had a total of about 400 staff associated with those two datacentres including a large software group that was doing development and maintenance work.

When the company looked at the business use of this corporate resource, it decided that it was in decline as the individual businesses were increasingly reevaluating their own business processes. They were modernizing, streamlining and implementing new offthe-shelf software packages like SAP, which ran on midrange machines This required smaller capacity. As time went on, they were moving off the mainframe onto standalone machines. ICI was therefore faced with three possible options. The ® rst was to wait while the use of the datacentre went into marked decline, resulting in staff redundancy, redeployment or reskilling. The second was to convert the datacentre into a standalone business with its own pro® t and loss account. The third was to ® nd an external supplier to manage the datacentre for ICI. Weighing the three options carefully resulted in the decision to go out into the marketplace with a bidding document.

## An alternative supplier selection strategy

ICIs strategy was somewhat different from the tradi tional approach to outsourcing. The traditional strategy was to go to about 12± 15 suppliers and request information from each one in turn. This was believed to be a waste of time, energy and money. So, instead, the company employed the help of consultants to review the marketplace. The potential ® eld was narrowed down to a list of about four suppliers who were judged to be able to offer the required standard of service. They were then invited to present their case in December 1994. They were presented with an analysis that basically outlined the existing resource of datacentre and people. Suppliers were informed that ® nancial projections showed that the business use of this resource would reduce over time. However, the supplier would only be guaranteed a sum of money based upon ICI’s assessment of how this service would be used over time. In other words, the cash ¯ ow that was associated with the historic mainframe legacy system which was moving into decline.

## Risk and reward ± the key to success

Potential suppliers were told that any deal was incumbent on them taking over the datacentre and all the people associated with it They were not expected to make anyone redundant, nor should they close the datacentre at some point in the future. Instead, they were expected to generate new business using that resource. So as ICI’s use of the resource declined, new customers would come on stream. Should the suppliers agree to operate under these conditions, they were told they would have the right to be on the bid list every time ICI goes outside to tender. ## Summary

The approach to outsourcing at ICI was described as \`selective and competitive sourcing’. From ICI’s point of view, the most effective approach was a mixture of best of breed suppliers (i.e. you put together a portfolio of four to ® ve suppliers each with different strengths and weaknesses) and offer relatively short term contracts. It was pointed out that sourcing from the marketplace is a very different proposition from using an in-house IT facility. What was needed was a thorough understanding of the dynamics of a changing and ¯ uctuating marketplace. It was folly to assume that sourcing from the marketplace was a one-way street with the client approaching suppliers with a list of terms and conditions. Instead, the client must understand the market strategies of suppliers even before entering into negotiations about outsourcing. One example was given where a particular supplier was eager to bid for an outsourcing contract with the company until it won a major contract with another client. Within 24 hours of winning this contract, the supplier had withdrawn its intention to bid for the contract. Thus,

They had lost interest in us. Their strategic interest had been met, so they no longer needed to bid for our contract. I don’t blame them for that, because it is normal commercial behaviour. That is what you have to remember when you go out into the marketplace. It is just like any other marketplace. Supply and demand. What may be very important for a supplier to achieve this month, may not be the same in three months time.

The company tended to consider information systems/services and applications systems as being a service rather than a core competency. IT was described as an enabling technology. What was core to a business like ICI was information management tightly aligned to business objectives. The skills associated with information management strategy were vitally important to the company. The following points were highlighted for client organizations:-

(1) Understand the market place you are entering.

(2) Be absolutely clear about your objectives and work to keep all the cards in your own hand.

(3) Put the legal drafts into the bidding documents when you go to the marketplace.

(4) Handle the whole thing as a very clearly de® ned project. It should be business-led and businessmanaged.

(5) Make sure the people who are going to manage the contract are an integral part of the project team.

ICI intended to retain control over its suppliers in three important respects. First, it had clearly de® ned exactly the IT services it required from an external supplier. By understanding the IT services thoroughly, the company was able to approach suppliers from an intel ligent position. Second, it understood the market in which the various suppliers operated. This meant that it could predict the interest it would receive from various suppliers. Third, it approached suppliers with a carefully crafted legal contract, the parameters of which could not be easily altered by potential suppliers. In doing these things, the company was protecting itself from some of the more common pitfalls of IT outsourcing.

## Wessex Water

## Context and overview

Wessex Water Plc comprises three main businesses: Wessex Water Services; UK Waste and SC Technology. Wessex Water supplies 400 million litres of high quality drinking water to 1.1 million customers. It operates 137 water sources, 131 treatment plants, 11 000 kilometres of water mains, 320 pumping stations and over 300 storage reservoirs. Sewage services are provided to 2.5 million people in the Wessex region. UK Waste is jointly owned by Waste Management International and Wessex Water Plc. Having completed 35 acquisitions, it has grown from virtually nothing to be the leading waste management business in the country with a turnover of £150 million. It employs 2000 people at 46 locations. SC Technology is based near Zurich, Switzerland and designs, manufactures and operates environmentally advanced thermal drying processes for the treatment and disposal of all types of organic sludges (Wessex Water Plc, 1997).

This case study is concerned with Wessex Water Services, which is a privatized utility that has undergone major ® nancial and structural changes since the early 1990s. Like our previous company, the approach to outsourcing was based on market, business and technological factors. The ® rst outsourcing contract was commissioned in 1993 and this was for mainframe services. The rationale for it was described as \`very straightforward’ , and was essentially a cost-cutting exercise. At the time, the company had initiated the development of a major billing system. An upgrade of the mainframe was necessary but to do this in-house was considered too expensive. Instead, the company considered outsourcing the mainframe as a viable and more cost-effective option. This was described as a \`tactical decision’ rather than a strategic one. The company was also undergoing some cultural changes with the accent on commercialization. It did not wish to continue to be a provider of day-to-day IT services given that a mature external market place was available. An assessment of the market showed the company could procure mainframe services at a reduced cost with improved service delivery compared with the inhouse facility.

## Multiple supplier sourcing

Since the signing of the ® rst contract in 1993, the company now has three more outsourcing contracts. Four different suppliers provide a portfolio of IT services. Prior to outsourcing, the IT budget was about $\mathcal { L } 6$ million per annum. Since outsourcing, the budget is $\mathcal { L } 5$ million per annum. Wessex Water has pursued a strategy of multiple supplier sourcing. Initially, IT was divided into its various component parts for the purpose of evaluating the type of service required from an outsourcing supplier. The company approached the market with an open mind and did not wish to exclude any supplier from bidding for the contract. The rationale for selecting a supplier was primarily commercial. They were assessed in terms of whether they had the technical capability to provide the service as well as how they would interface and work with the client. The company said it was \`fairly cynical about how suppliers use the word partnership’. It wanted to establish an open relationship with the supplier and one that satis® ed the commercial objectives on both sides.

## Focusing on the core business

Wessex Water plc approached outsourcing in a very pragmatic way. Senior managers were aware of the common advantages and disadvantages of oursourcing and wanted to mitigate risk by thoroughly understanding and evaluating any IT service activity before it was outsourced. The advantage of outsourcing was because it focused attention on the core business. IT services had to be measured and benchmarked. The traditional charging mechanism for IT services was described as \`cumbersome’, In the words of the IT director,

Outsourcing forced us to re® ne it so when we charged someone as a direct relationship with the cost driver, we knew what the value was. So the exercise of outsourcing forced us to develop a more commercial approach to IT.

But outsourcing also meant that a different style of management was necessary. To effect the move to outsourcing, the company had to re-evaluate the roles of people in the IT department. Many of these roles had to change and the whole issue of changing the cultur from one of service delivery to managing the supplier was very dif® cult. More important, with the signing of new outsourcing contracts, the challenge of managing multiple suppliers was more dif® cult than managing a single supplier. Thus it was important to \`manage the gaps’ between suppliers.

## Managing the supplier

Prior to signing the ® rst outsourcing contract there were between 65± 70 people employed in the IT department. There are now about 15 people who now have a managerial or supervisory role. There is a Head of IT and three service managers. Within the support centre, there is one supervisor and associated support staff, and the remaining staff in admin (procurement, interna management information and strong line on performance measurement, service management).

One of the fundamental elements of the outsourcing contract is relationship management with the supplier. The IT director said this was an important issue for the company. Yet it found that skill shortages existed on both sides. Suppliers tended to pay lip service to contracts management issues, and this reduced their ability to add value to the service they provided.

By the same token, the company tended to learn by \`trial and error’ and sometimes underestimated potential changes to IT service provision. The problem of not anticipating changes to the IT service is, of course, a common problem (Currie and Willcocks, 1998c).

## Four outsourcing contracts ± learning how to manage the \`gaps’

The four contracts are for the following IT services: (1) Mainframe services, (2) Applications development, (3) Wide Area Networks (WANs) and (4) Desktop computing. The range of suppliers varies from a large organization that comprises many different business groups to a medium size supplier and two further smaller suppliers. Each organization is highly differ entiated not only by size, but also by business culture. The company said that while the issue of managing the gaps between the suppliers was anticipated from the outset of each of the four outsourcing contracts, it was underestimated. Procedures had been put in place but additional discussions were needed with the suppliers to address speci® c issues on a continuing basis. The key problem is interpreting and managing the concerns of the various suppliers. In particular, the issue of how responsibility is apportioned between suppliers and who is accountable for speci® c aspects of the IT service.

Sometimes, grey areas emerged where one supplier relied upon the client organization or another supplier to address a speci® c issue. This causes confusion and leads to a decline in service. Having learned from this experience, greater efforts were made to co-ordinate and control the activities of the four suppliers to provide a seamless service across the organization.

The issue of suppliers \`extracting more money for extra work not covered in the original outsourcing contract’ was raised by the IT director as a potential cause for con¯ ict. He asserted that it was important to prepare carefully worded contracts and administer them properly. Yet some instances had arisen where the supplier had billed the company twice for work carried out.

## Retaining IT strategy and planning

The area of strategy and planning was done in-house by senior managers from the business. The company was currently preparing its IT strategy for the next phase, having recently just completed some major IT work, such as the billing system. Part of the rationale for the IT strategy was to present IT as a service and not focus on in-house delivery as had been done in the past. A group of business people was therefore charged with the responsibility of looking at IT in terms of what it could do for the business. Technical matters were addressed by IT staff. The important thing was to discuss IT requirements within a wider business framework. The time horizon for the IT strategy and business process strategy was three years.

The above strategy and planning processes were for the same time period as the outsourcing contracts, all of which had been signed for three years’ duration. One outsourcing contract had just been extended. Longer contracts of ten years were not recommended by the company, although it would agree to setting up a framework agreement with selected suppliers for ® ve years and possibly ten.

One issue that arose was the dif® culty of separating strategy from operations. Most of the outsourcing literature discusses strategy and planning as distinct from its operationalization. Responsibility for these areas is usually divided so that internal senior management focus upon the former (strategy) whilst lower level managers or staff working internally or external suppliers concentrate on the latter area (operational izing the strategy). Asked about whether it was possible to separate strategy from operations, the IT director said,

No, I don’t think you can. There are too many bluesky strategies, which exaggerate what they can actually do, but there is a difference between what is theoretical and what is practical. I look at my peers, and I see instances of concrete strategies that have not been tied down to the realities. Strategy development is a socio-economic political exercise which needs an awareness of what technology can deliver.

In retrospect, the IT director at Wessex Water claims that outsourcing has been an \`educational experience on both sides’ (see interview with Computer Weekly, 12 March 1998). The key lessons learned are ® vefold. First, it is important to de® ne the hardware and software requirements of the business. Second, to implement a performance measurement system and agree it with the supplier. Third, not to fragment the source of supply. Accountability and responsibility of each supplier must be properly communicated. Fourth, assign the best people to a contract. Contracts management is an important area of outsourcing. Fifth, communicate realistic expectations to the rest of the business.

## Multiple supplier sourcing at ICI and Wessex Water: a comparison

Evaluating the experiences of outsourcing within ICI, both at the corporate level and in the various business areas, management believe that a wide range of expertise exists in commissioning and managing IT outsourcing contracts. But the experience of outsourcing should not be examined in isolation of the wider business imperatives (Lacity, Willcocks, and Feeny, 1996). As such, the two corporate IT outsourcing contracts were driven by very different market, business and technological conditions existing in a given time period. The demerger of ICI and Zeneca initiated the ® rst contract. This involved outsourcing the telecoms network in Europe and the UK. Here, the key issue was how to ® nd the most effective way of splitting the telecoms network. In the second outsourcing contract, the data centre and the people associated with it was part of a declining asset in terms of cost and business use. Here, the key issue was whether there was a strategic partner in the marketplace who would wish to run a large database. Given there was an active marketplace from which these services could be purchased under competitive conditions, ICI believed it was more effective to outsource this facility.

The multiple supplier approach pursued by Wessex Water was differentiated from that of ICI in a number of ways. Privatization was a major factor in forcing management to look for cost reduction in IT services. Unlike ICI who used management consultants to investigate the outsourcing, marketplace, Wessex Water engaged in a process of competitive tendering (see Table 1). Both organizations believed that IT services should be managed by the business and not by the IT department and that IT strategy should be developed in-house. At ICI, the term used was \`business led and business managed’. Similarly, at Wessex Water, the IT director said that a commercial approach was important, particularly in dealing with external suppliers. Contract management was an area where skill shortages were apparent. The transition from having a large inhouse IT department to one that outsourced a range of IT services engendered the need for new skills. Problems arose in de® ning legal contracts, particularly where IT services were undergoing changes over time. Service level agreements (SLAs) were used by both organizations, although they only addressed a smal number of key service level requirements. Issues such as cooperation, coordination, sharing of knowledge and expertise and managing the relationships with and between suppliers could not easily be put into a contract. To improve the preparation and implementation of outsourcing contracts, both organizations were attempting to achieve two objectives. First, they wished to formalize the evaluation process to gain a better understanding of the breakdown of IT services. Second, they hoped to develop closer working relationships with their suppliers.

From an analysis of the two case studies, the multiple supplier sourcing strategies in each company were differentiated according to historical background, market selection strategies (of suppliers), skill requirements and future goals. Both organizations experienced problems in managing outsourcing contracts, with one recommending that IT people should develop greater commercial awareness, and the other, learning how to manage the gaps in supplier contracts. ## Mitigating the risks of IT outsourcing

issue has been widely discussed in the IS literature, and is often referred to as a culture-gap between generalists (managers) and specialists (technologists) (Currie and Glover, 1998). The latter problem has been experienced by BP Exploration (Cross, 1995) and is one of the more common risks associated with this form of sourcing arrangement (Currie and Willcocks, 1998b). Paradoxically, the use of many suppliers as a strategy of mitigating risks (from outsourcing) resulted in additional perils as suppliers sometimes blamed each other for loss of operational performance. Yet bridging performance gaps was not easily resolved by the client organization, particularly where demands for extra services were outside the scope of supplier contracts. To some extent, suppliers bene® ted from being one of many, since their main objective was to meet the conditions of service level agreements (SLAs) without necessarily offering additional value-added service to the client. To this end, both ICI and Wessex Water were seeking improved ways of mitigating risk through encouraging competition between suppliers and formalizing the evaluation of outsourcing performance. It would appear that lessons learned from the case studies offer a useful template for other organizations negotiating multiple supplier outsourcing contracts. Yet it would appear that client organizations have some way to go before they can improve IT service provision from their suppliers without developing new skills in areas like contracts management.

## Conclusion

The above discussion of two UK companies contributes to the existing literature on IT outsourcing by offering fresh insights into multiple supplier and selective sourcing contracts. Both ICI and Wessex Water have outsourced a signi® cant proportion of their IT facility to a range of suppliers, with some contracts more successful than others. In support of the existing literature on selective sourcing (Lacity et al., 1996, Willcocks and Fitzgerald, 1993) the experiences of ICI and Wessex Water suggest that risks from outsourcing were reduced in three important respects. First, it was critical for the companies to fully understand the nature of the work being put out to tender. This meant that negotiations with potential suppliers could take place from a position of strength rather than ignorance. Second, it was important to implement short to medium term contracts with suppliers so as to avoid a \`contractual stranglehold’ where the supplier becomes all-powerful. Shorter-term contracts also enabled the client company to acquire a better understanding of transaction costs. Third, the need to develop commercial skills was critical. It was therefore important for the client company to retain and develop management capabilities and skills to ensure that contracts with suppliers achieved value added bene® ts. Whilst ICI and Wessex Water are persevering with their respective outsourcing contracts, it appears that many lessons have already been learned, sometimes from painful experience, and more lessons need to be learned. Given that these companies have acquired some considerable experience of outsourcing, the lessons that emerge are likely to be useful to others. More academic research is needed on IT outsourcing in the areas of contract management and negotiation, contract renewal, developing managerial capabilities, and competitive benchmarking and performance measurement. Finally, the experiences of ICI and Wessex Water suggest that \`selective and competitive’ sourcing offers greater opportunities to the client company for mitigating risks compared with other forms of IT sourcing, most notably, total outsourcing.
