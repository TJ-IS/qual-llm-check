# The four P’s revisited: business value assessment of the infrastructure impact of IT investments

THEO J.W. RENKEMA

Eindhoven University of Technology, Department of Technology Management, 5600 MB Eindhoven, The Netherlands

Assessing the business impacts of increased IT investment has become one of the major issues in contemporary management. Many senior managers realize that a well-structured appraisal process might make the vita difference between IT success and IT failure, both in the private and the public sectors. Senior management’s prime concern has shifted from controlling IT costs to managing and delivering IT bene® ts. Today, increased emphasis is being put on the role of infrastructure investments in order to fully exploit the pro® t potential of IT. The infrastructure impact of IT investments more and more centres around the emerging notion of an infor mation infrastructure. Assessing the role and impacts of infrastructure investments, however, has proved to be particularly dif® cult and it is not yet clear how the underlying strategic decision-making process should be managed. The purpose of this paper is to present a model that offers four control options ± paraphrasing conventional business wisdom in marketing coined the \`P4 model’ ± to manage investment appraisal and to support organizational decision-making. As a prelude to this, the paper explores the role of an information infrastructure from an IT investment perspective and examines four styles of decision-making. It also presents case study material from the ® nancial services sector, in order to illustrate the relevance and applicability of the P4 model. Much of this paper draws on a research study funded by Eindhoven University and two large ® nancia services organizations.

## Introduction

Until recently, information technology (IT) was mainly used to rationalize routine business processes in the corporate \`back of® ce’ (e.g. payroll automation or inventory control). The main thrust was to improve ef® ciency through cost savings and cost displacements. In this day and age, long-term business investments are made in the corporate \`front of® ce’ with the purpose to improve effectiveness, to gain and sustain competitive advantage and to transform entire business processes. Increased emphasis is being put on the role of infrastructure investments in order to get the full bene® ts of large scale IT deployment (see e.g. Gunton, 1989; Scott Morton, 1991; Turnbull 1991; Weill, 1993; Renkema, 1995).

Various sources point out that the infrastructure part might well be the largest part of IT capital expenditures (McKay and Brockway, 1989; Keen, 1991). Broadbent and Weill (1997) claim that infrastructure investments account for more than 58% of the total IT budget of large ® rms and about 4% of revenues. They state infrastructure investments have increased about 11% annually. Infrastructure investments closely relate to what has come to be known as the information infrastructure. There is, however, a lack of clarity with respect to the precise meaning and role of this infrastructure for pro® table IT investment strategies. Consequently, managers ® nd it dif® cult to make strategic investment decisions that are aligned with the current and future needs of their business.

The dif® culties with managerial decision-making are for a large part due to their observation that the value for money from investments is far from guaranteed. IT investments have repeatedly been the subject of disappointed expectations and their evaluations raise many questions. Organizations estimate that around 20% of their IT spending is wasted and that 30% to 40% does not contribute to business performance (Willcocks and Lester, 1993). Around 70% of all IT investment seems to give no adequate return on invest ment (Hochstrasser and Grif® ths, 1990).

In light of the many controversial ® ndings regarding the impact of IT on business performance, senior management has become highly unreceptive towards \`act of faith’ investment decisions. Today, senior management is expressing an urgent need for more concrete, sound evidence of the expected business impacts of proposed infrastructure investments. The assessment of such impacts is however easier said than done. Costs are dif® cult to estimate and often hidden, bene® ts are dif® cult to quantify and measure, and the uncertainties and risks are often substantial.

Compared to other types of IT investments, infrastructure investments are particularly dif® cult to justify, because of their long-term, indirect business bene® ts and high, often ® xed costs (Parker et al., 1988; Farbey et al., 1993; Dos Santos, 1991; Kambil et al., 1993). Many managers are convinced that creating a businessoriented infrastructure, although dif® cult to justify, does play an essential role in the achievement of their corporate strategies. Not investing might severely jeopardize their organization’s future viability. Keen (1991) accurately expresses this dilemma many senior managers face as: \`Senior executives are caught in a worrisome double bind (¼ ); economically, companies cannot afford to increase capital spending on IT; competitively, they cannot afford not to do so. The economics of information capital is ® rmly on the top management agenda, and corporate managers are clamoring for help.’

## The role of information infrastructure

## Traditional and modern views

The notion \`infrastructure’ is not entirely new in the IT/IS ® eld. In the literature the term infrastructure is being used in a variety of meanings, usually without de® ning or delineating it very narrowly. Until recently, the notion infrastructure merely had a rather narrow, technological connotation, generally referring to the centralized computing equipment. In this \`traditional’ view an infrastructure is considered to include all facilitative resources in the ® eld of technology, often organized in a central information systems department. This encompasses all technological means such as mainframe computers, data communications equipment and operating systems. Also the personnel (e.g. operators and system designers) and organizational procedures in the IS department are seen as part of the infrastructure. Maes (1990) contends that through this primarily technological view the notion infrastructure has entered the IS ® eld and therefore still has a rather technological connotation. A recent de® - nition of an infrastructure that falls within this view is the one by Weill (1993): \`the base foundation of IT capability budgeted for and provided by the information systems function and shared across multiple business units or functional units. The IT capability budgeted for included both the technical and managerial expertise required to provide reliable services.’

More modern views use the notion of an infrastructure in a broader sense, often referred to as an information infrastructure. This information infrastructure has been drawn to attention from notably two directions. First of all, the information infrastructure has emerged as a focal point of national or even international IT policies. From this perspective, infrastructure investments are seen to provide the telecommunicationsbased \`information super highway’ . The notion of an information infrastructure has also gained prominence as a key element in the strategic management of information technology. From a corporate organizational level perspective, it is advocated that organizations should direct their strategies towards building and maintaining an information infrastructure (see e.g. Gunton, 1989; Turnbull, 1991; Berg and Renkema, 1994).

What both perspectives on the information infrastructure have in common, is that they stress the increasingly shared, coordinated nature of today’s IT investments. A comparison can be made with public infrastructures such as roads, public transport and facilities in the ® elds of education or social services. The users of such an information infrastructure have, together with many interested persons or parties, a whole range of IT based services at their disposal. The user also has substantial degrees of freedom; the infrastructure provides the base foundation that enables the subsequent local application of IT, tailored to one’s own characteristics and preferences.

## Direct and indirect infrastructure

In view of the changing nature of the notion infrastructure we consider an information infrastructure to be the shared system of people, resources and procedures in the ® eld of IT. Essentially, the information infrastructure is a social construction, as it covers al IT elements that are to be shared by organizational agreement and therefore are part of the infrastructure. This organizational agreement amongst involved parties is the outcome of a decision-making process.

In our research we have found it to be useful to make a distinction between investments in two types of infrastructure: indirect and direct infrastructure (see Table 1). The indirect infrastructure includes the technological and organizational facilities, such as processing equipment, datacommunications tools, systems developers and operations personnel. This infrastructure enables the use of the direct infrastructure, that comprises the infrastructure of data bases, application systems and knowledge bases. The direct infrastructure is generally to a large extent integrated with the business processes and the products and services of an organization, by providing direct information processing capabilities. The bene® ts of the direct infrastructure are therefore much more related to business improvements than the bene® ts of the indirect infrastructure.

An important characteristic of the information infrastructure is that it re¯ ects the relative and contextbound character of the notion organization, leading to a layered model of the infrastructure (see Figure 1). Every organization can be part of another organization or be dependent upon the regulation of another institutional level. Consequently, when speaking about infrastructure, one has to be very explicit about the organizational level under consideration. It is possible to speak of e.g. a group infrastructure, a business unit infrastructure or a corporate-wide infrastructure that are all interdependent. Also these organizational levels will have to take account of the infrastructures of the industries and nations they are working in and even of infrastructure standards of international bodies (e.g. ISO standards).

## Four styles of investment decision-making

Although there is a plethora of methods for IT investment evaluation (Renkema and Berghout, 1997), these methods generally do not take account of important features of infrastructure investments and of the strategic decision process that underpins them (e.g. long-term and enabling nature of bene® ts, cost dynamics, organizational politics). The literature states that investments in what the previous section de® ned as the indirect infrastructure are considered as most dif® cult to evaluate and suggests that methods using ® nancial option theory might be of help (Dos Santos, 1991; Kambil et al., 1993). Option theory, however, although conceptually strong, has too many theoretical and practical drawbacks to be used in decisionmaking practice (see for a more detailed review: Sharp, 1991; Renkema, 1996).

This paper’s premise is that methods for IT investment appraisal should make better use of the knowl edge gained in studying how organizations make strategic investment decisions. This amongst others implies in our view that it should be looked upon as a largely communicative decision-making process. It is not a \`method of pinning numbers on things to prove or disprove a case’ (Farbey et al., 1993). This decision-making process involves multiple stakeholders, who are through mutual consultation trying to assess the future value to be gained from a proposed invest ment. The product of such a process provides the crucial standards against which the investment’s business value can be measured and managed across its life cycle. Theories of strategic decision-making make a distinction between a prescriptive, goal-oriented (or in our terms \`product-oriented’) and a descriptive, process-oriented view (see e.g. Mintzberg, 1994). Idenburg (1992) follows this distinction and speaks in his review of the strategy literature of the \`what’ and the \`how’ of strategy development. He pictures these dimensions in a 2 2 matrix, which results in four views on strategy formation. Figure 2 visualizes ou adoption of the Idenburg matrix to four styles of invest ment decision-making.

## Act of faith

Without any structuring on the product or process dimension, investment decisions will amount to \`act of faith’ decisions. This leads to what Shank and Govandarajan (1992) have called a \`technology roulette’. The decision to invest looks like gambling; there are no clear goals or expectations and one can only hope for a successful outcome. After an investment has been made, its also quite easy to qualify it as a success or as a failure, depending on what someone wants to prove. This situation has become unacceptable in the present IT investment climate, in which not \`acts of faith’ but more rigorous analyses are required.

## Rational planning

Too much structuring on the product dimension will lead to \`rational comprehensive planning’ (Rosenhead, 1989); i.e. trying to quantify as many aspects as possible, thereby ignoring the much more complex and uncertain organizational reality decision-makers face. This re¯ ects the classical view of the \`rational’ decisionmaker. The many ® nance-based evaluation methods are product-oriented, by concentrating on the consequences of IT investments that can be monetary valued. Also multicriteria methods, that are applied in a mechanistic way, often show the characteristics of rational planning. Logical analyses of investment criteria predominate; these generally take the form of costbene® t analyses, in which there is no room for intuition or emotion.

## Learning experience

An exclusive focus on the process dimension does, wrongly, hardly account for the essence of organizational decision-making: i.e. stating objectives and, given scarce resources, choosing between alternatives to reach these objectives. This implies that an investment decision merely is regarded as a learning experience. The prime purpose of supporting decision-making is to learn involved stakeholders to arrive at a decision (e.g. through building shared \`mental models’, see: Senge, 1990; Gregory and Jackson, 1992). Providing them with relevant decision criteria to evaluate and choose between investment alternatives, falls beyond the learning focus.

## Balanced control

The decision-making perspective taken here, advocates a more balanced approach towards the evaluation and management of infrastructure investment decisions, what Figure 2 refers to as \`balanced control’. It is aimed at structuring investment appraisal through a dynamic alignment of both the product and process dimension. At the heart of any evaluation lies the establishment of a set of investments arguments to assess the business impacts of investments. This establishment does, however, not take place in isolation of its organizational context. It is the outcome of a communicative process between involved stakeholders.

## The \`P4’ decision-making model

## Four control options as appraisal P’s

As argued in the previous section, the aim of any investment appraisal is to improve and facilitate organizational decision-making on its proposed IT investments. This has led us to believe that we need an explicit view of infrastructure investment appraisal as a decision-making and largely communicative process. To develop this view, a model of investment appraisal was designed. Four aspects are distinguished that can be used to improve the investment evaluation and to manage the underlying decision-making process. Paraphrasing the well-known four P’s of conventional business wisdom in marketing (but with a different meaning), the resulting model is referred to as the \`P4’ model. Figure 3 summarizes the four aspects, while the remainder of the section provides a further elaboration of these aspects. The P4 model is grounded in our conviction that decisions on infrastructure investments are not taken according to the classical idealized picture of the \`rational decision-maker’. It is more realistic to base decision-making support upon the practical consequences of the paradigm of \`bounded rationality’ (Cyert and March, 1963; Simon, 1977; Kickert, 1979). Building on four types of bounded rationality that we discern (i.e. rationality concerning content, procedural rationality, structural rationality and political rationality) four important control options can be identi® ed (see for a more detailed treatment: Renkema, 1996), coming together in the \`P4 model’ of investment appraisal.

## Managing the product of investment appraisal

At the heart of the P4 model lies the product of the investment appraisal, i.e. the set of arguments (evaluation or decision criteria) on the basis of which the decision whether to invest or not is made. Every investment decision is made against the background and judgement of advantages, disadvantages and risks, that can be both ® nancial and non® nancial (see Table 2). It is best to make these as explicit and debatable as possible. Every evaluation is generally subject to personal, informal and implicit judgements of involved stakeholders. By explicating different views on an investment the ® nal decision will, as a rule, have more organizational support and commitment.

Our research showed that evaluation criteria with respect to the direct infrastructure should consider impacts on the organization’s processes, its strategy, its decision-making style, its structure and its culture. The criteria with respect to the indirect infrastructure should consider impact on the content and shape of IT facilities, on the available IT capacity and on the characteristics of IT services. For both types of infrastructure an investment can also yield important learning experiences. Expenditures refer to the ® nancial efforts with respect to realization, use and maintenance of an investment and integration of the investment with existing working practices. Possible earnings stem from ef® ciency gains and additional earnings.

The assessment of non® nancial consequences in terms of what is considered positive and what negative is less trivial than that of ® nancial consequences. It is not always clear which consequences should be quali® ed as positive and which as negative due to interactions between consequences and different valuejudgements of involved stakeholders.

An investment can for instance lead to more structured business processes (which is considered as positive) and also imply a competitive disadvantage as the organization is less ¯ exible (which is considered as negative). Or increased structuring might be advantageous from a business management point of view but considered as a threat by employees because they feel their freedom of action is reduced. Important risk factors, that can be partly managed, are external risks (e.g. initiatives of government or competitors), realization risks (e.g. complexity), organizational risks (e.g. resistance to change, lack of management support) and technological risks (e.g. new tools, new skills).

Since infrastructure is a shared facility, infrastructure investment appraisal also requires an evaluation of the shared versus the individual right to dispose of IT capabilities. The value in creating infrastructure lies in the bene® ts of sharing facilities, more often referred to as synergy. In our view, the two types of infrastructure we discerned (see subsection \`Direct and indirect infrastructure’) will also be able to deliver two types of synergy: synergy concerning strategy through the direct infrastructure and synergy concerning realization through the indirect infrastructure. Synergy concerning strategy refers to the consequences of collaboration aimed at improved achievement of organizational goals (e.g. shared distribution systems or shared research systems). An important investment characteristic is the existence of network externalities; the possible added value of an investment depends on the number of users of the planned infrastructure. Synergy concerning realization refers to the consequences of collaboration aimed at the exploitation of scale effects (e.g. economies of scale, scope and specialization).

## Managing the process of investment appraisal

The second control option of the P4 model refers to the process of investment appraisal. This process considers the different phases the evaluation goes through, both prior to, and during, project execution. It is recommended to decompose investment decision-making into manageable steps, analogous to well known decision making models (Simon, 1977; Harrison, 1987): (1) Problem statement; (2) Formulation of project goals; (3) Evaluation of alternatives; (4) Choice of investment alternatives; and (5) Implementation of the chosen solution.

Since infrastructure investments provide the longterm foundation for many business-speci® c applications, decisions should be subject to a thorough preparation. This subdivision of steps is not meant as a sequential and rigid procedure, but more as a pattern of thought, with possible feedback loops (Witte, 1972; Mintzberg, et al., 1976). Another important recommendation lies in performing post-implementation reviews of the investment decision in order to monitor and control the investment across its life cycle. These reviews provide valuable information on whether the investment actually delivers value for money and to what extent there is still room for improvement.

Post-implementation reviews can also be used to establish an investment climate in which organizational learning is encouraged. Many investment decisions are made by \`jumping from one project to the other’. Explicit knowledge of prior investments and their realized value can very much contribute to improved decision-making. Regular reviews of the investment also minimize the phenomenon of \`investment entrapment’ (Dinther, 1992); a situation in which ever-greater resource commitments are made because of too much emotional involvement, without sound evaluations of increased investment.

## Managing the participation in investment appraisal

The third aspect that gets special attention in the P4 model concerns the participation of the different project appraisal stakeholders. It is advisable to involve all appropriate people in decision-making with respect to the investment. These include senior management, IT specialists, ® nancial executives and the employees whose work is affected by the investment. Organization of the collaboration between involved stakeholders is an important means to increase adherence to and support for a decision. Special attention is needed to ensure senior management support. Infrastructure decisions should be prepared and approved of at the highest hierarchical level. It has further been shown (see e.g. Farbey et al., 1993) that the likelihood of success of investment projects is considerably improved when there are one or more \`project champions’ involved. This championship refers to the special effort that is made by some involved stakeholder to make the investment a success. This stakeholder does not necessarily have a formal role that implies such an effort. The more powerful this champion’s position is in the organization the better.

## Managing the politics of investment appraisal

The previously discussed control options of the framework merely sketched a homogeneous, rational picture of an organization. This view implies for instance that the different stakeholders in the investment appraisal share the same intentions, goals and priorities (the \`sys tem model’). A more realistic view is that of an organization in which different stakeholder groups have their own wishes and preferences (the \`coalition model’). Such a view allows for the recognition of con¯ icting interests and the use of political means to safeguard one’s interests (Pfeffer, 1981; Mintzberg, 1983). As infrastructure investments are, by de® nition, for common use, their evaluation is generally subject to politics.

Managing these politics ± the fourth control option of the P4 model ± means that the intentions, wishes and preferences of stakeholders are explicitly taken into account. Experience shows that agreement on the route to follow and a common perspective among evaluation parties gives a greater chance of a successfu project. This should preferably stem from the same motives and a relationship based on equality. In order to reach such agreement, it may be necessary to use such decision-making strategies as negotiation and coalition building. Decision support with respect to the politics of the evaluation lies in what has been called \`stakeholder analysis’ . Boonstra (1991) suggests the following steps in such an analysis:

(1) Listing of stakeholders, their estimated power and impacts of the proposal investment;

(2) Assessment of possible \`winners’ and \`losers’ and their possible (political) \`pro® ts’ and \`losses’;

(3) Establishment of feasible strategies (e.g. ® nancial compensation) to in¯ uence the political account of pro® ts and losses.

## Case study application of the P4 model

## Case study approach and general ® ndings

The P4 model was used to analyse and diagnose decision-making practices in two ® nancial services organizations, an insurance ® rm and a bank. Both organizations have their headquarters in the Netherlands and are two of the most important players on national and international ® nancial markets. In total, seven investment cases were analysed, with different accents with respect to the type of infrastructure concerned. Also some cases concerned investment projects that were approved of recently, whereas others were still in their feasibility stage. All case studies resulted in case reports of about 20 pages, re¯ ecting interviews, documentary analyses, participative observations and action research. These were reviewed by key stakeholders. The case reports that examined decision-making in the insurance ® rm were also separately discussed with important appraisal stakeholders, in order to review investment decisionmaking.

The application of the P4 model in practice shows that the model is a helpful tool for the diagnosis and support of appraisal practices. A diagnosis can be used to foster organizational learning, which in turn is a prelude to improvement of decision-making practice. The use of the model as a decision-making tool usually requires adaptations to organizational characteristics and preferences, but the involved practitioners concluded it is bound to increase decision-making quality. The following two subsections further discuss the application of the P4 model in two decision-making cases, that serve as examples. The ® rst case concerns the installation of a \`session manager’; a piece of software for on-line communication in a mainframe environment. The second case concerns the investment in an infrastructure to report and analyse commercial data organization-wide. Some general conclusions with respect to the case study analysis are discussed in the remainder of this section.

Explicit, more-dimensional decision criteria to evaluate the investment decisions are hardly used, although all decisions are subject to a formal cost-bene® t appraisal. In many of the cases, solving some \`problems’ in the existing infrastructure gives suf® cient justi-® cation of a proposed investment. The actual in¯ uence of ® nancial arguments is therefore unclear. This supports the observation that (® nancial) calculations often play a \`ritualistic’ role (Currie, 1989; Symons, 1990). In our opinion, the advantage of this approach is for many stakeholders that they cannot easily be held accountable for project outcomes, since there are no performance measures that are commonly agreed upon. This, however, makes performance control very dif® cult.

The process and participation of investment appraisal are the most important factors that are used to control investment appraisal and decision-making. The general approach seems to be that a number of knowledgeable evaluation stakeholders suggest investment arguments that are considered relevant. Structuring this process with formal phases and regula reports, milestones etc. ± elements of what is seen as \`good’ project management ± offers options for control. After a decision is made, evaluation activities are generally not an integral part of project control. There are no regular post-implementation reviews performed. Although this can be partly explained by the \`ritualistic’ role of investment arguments, this is also caused by the competitive pressure to focus investment decisions on new investments instead of reviews of previous decisions.

Politics is very important in the decision-making process, ensuing from different interests of the organizational stakeholders. An important cause of politics regarding infrastructure investment evaluation is the tension between central control and local autonomy. Politics of decision-making is an aspect that is considered relevant by all stakeholders, but is discussed more covertly than overtly. This often inhibits an open discussion to improve and learn from investment decisions and project outcomes. Generally, great importance is placed on good communications between involved stakeholders and commitment to the decision and resulting infrastructure by users and senior management.

## Case study 1: an investment in the indirect infrastructure

## Managing the product

This investment had two main purposes. The ® rst concerned improved security of automated data processing in general, formally triggered by a report of the internal auditing department. The second purpose was to increase ease of use, partly through the application of standard software packages. Alternatives were the standard packages from two vendors as well as internal development. The investment evaluation and the choice between the packages and internal development, was hardly made on the basis of explicit investment arguments (® nancial and non® nancial consequences, risks). Valuation and weighting of investment alternatives was not tried. The expenditures (several ten thousands of Dutch guilders) were not considered as a problem. The wish to conform to the IBM standards and the deliverance of the needed functionality were the decisive arguments of the decision to invest in one of the standard software packages.

## Managing the process and the participation

After an explorative phase in which several policy reports were written, the project went into a phase in which the decision was prepared and a pilot project was started. A project and steering group with IT employees were in control of this. There was also a formal assignment of management with several management reports. A steering committee, responsible for implementation of the investment was established. In-between the phases of the project were several gaps, in which nothing really happened. The total lead-time of the investment, until implementation was about 4.5 years. There was no formal post-implementation review of the investment decision and no project champion. The general feeling is that the project cost much more in terms of manpower and time than was initially expected.

## Managing the politics

During the entire decision-making process, especially when implementation of the investment came into sight, there was a political tension between IT staff and user departments. This covered both the individual end-user, who had to learn new log-in procedures, and the system developer in the business who had less \`space’ to test new systems. Also the interests of the centralized IT department, better control of the supporting infrastructure, sometimes con¯ icted with the need to \`sell’ the system to the business community. Finally, there is, even until now, some politics because of the lead-time that is considered much too long by many stakeholders. The different causes of this are also the subject of different opinions.

## Case study 2: an investment in the direct infrastructure

## Managing the product

The ® rst purpose of this investment was to have more consistent and reliable commercial data. The second purpose concerned the supply of appropriate commercial management information. There were hardly any explicit investment arguments used, both in terms of ® nancial and non-® nancial consequences and risks. Explicit valuation and weighting of investment alternatives was also not attempted. Given the then prevailing development practices, the choice for in-house development was quickly made. The option of buying a standard software package was considered too late in the whole decision-making process, to be a serious decision-making alternative. Top management was, in contrast to the wish of the project committee, unwilling to label the project as \`strategic’ . Development costs were estimated using function point analysis and prior experiences. Total costs were justi® ed on the need to solve rather pressing problems in the functioning of the existing infrastructure and on projected cost savings.

## Managing the process and the participation

The investment was initiated because of an existing infrastructure that was considered inappropriate to solve the prevailing information needs. The infrastructure originated from a merger in the recent past of the case study organization. System development was coordinated by a project committee. The methodology \`System Development Methodology’ (SDM) was used, which implies several formal decision points. The project committee worked under responsibility of a permanent policy committee, responsible for the commercial IT strategy. This latter committee reported to top management. End-users were also regularly consulted. There was a \`project champion’ involved. This concerned an employee from the commercial staff. There was no formal postimplementation review of the investment decision. There are, however, regular audits of the functioning of the infrastructure. The general feeling is that the new infrastructure is superior to the old situation.

## Managing the politics

This investment in the direct infrastructure solved several problems, that were considered as such by many employees of the case study organization. This led to a decision-making atmosphere in which politics did not play a very prominent role. Some aspects, however, can be traced back to the politics of investment evaluation. In the course of the decision-making process, there was a problem of alignment of the interests of IT staff and marketing staff, vis-…-vis the interests of the organization as a whole. There were also political tensions between the sales staff and central authorities, arising from increased room for detailed checks and performance evaluations. A ® nal point that raised concerns, was the (often low) priority that was given to infrastructure realization by the several, dispersed business units.

## Conclusion

This paper argued that the emerging notion of an information infrastructure re¯ ects the increasingly shared and coordinated nature of today’ s IT investments. Infrastructure investments provide the base foundation of IT provisions that enable subsequent local IT application. The distinction between a direct and an indirect infrastructure highlights the different roles these infrastructures play in business processes and the different bene® ts to be gained from them. Appraising the infrastructure impact of IT investments involves managing the product, process, participation and politics of decision-making, as summarized in the P4 model. Decision support along the lines of these four aspects leads to a more \`balanced control’ of investment decisions. This balanced control captures both the need to arrive at a more sound, rigorous appraisal, as well as the organizational decision context in which this appraisal is taking place. Ultimately the business value of infrastructure investments can only be assessed if all stakeholders are willing to participate in a well-structured decision-making process. They should be triggered and motivated to make their personal, informal assessments explicit. An appraisal should above all be considered as an important means to control and manage investments and also to learn from investment decisions. It should not be treated as a device to hold people accountable for their supposed mismanagement. Finding ways to create an organizational climate that embraces the use of IT evaluation as a genuine, collective tool of management present a unique challenge to IT practitioners, consultants and the research community.
