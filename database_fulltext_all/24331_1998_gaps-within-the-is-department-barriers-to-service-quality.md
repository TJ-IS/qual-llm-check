# Gaps within the IS department: barriers to service quality

LEYLAND PITT, PIERRE BERTHON and NIKALA LANE Cardiff Business School, University of Wales, Aberconway Building, Colum Drive, Cardiff CF1 3EU, UK

While the external measurement of service quality, and even the measurement of the expectations and perceptions of internal customers, is well established in the services literature, less attention has been given to the assessment of the \`gaps’ which cause the customer discrepancy. This paper describes a study which ascer tained internal gaps (\`1 through 4’) in a large information systems department within a major consulting and accounting ® rm.

## Introduction

Internal service providers, such as training and information systems (IS) departments, who ignore the current concern with measuring productivity improvements are likely to ® nd it increasingly dif® cult to justify capital investments and other expenditures. In this paper we focus on service provision by the IS departments, but hopefully much of the discussion is relevant and applicable to all providers of service to internal customers, that is, the ® rm’s own employees.

Several researchers have considered the effectiveness of the IS department (see Bù rn-Andersen and Davis, (1986), but the problem of assessing has still not been resolved. User satisfaction is still the most widely used surrogate measure of IS effectiveness (Melone, 1990). Rockart (1982), however, has proposed that the quality of the IS department’s service, as perceived by its clients, or customers or users, is a key indicator of IS effectiveness (Rands, 1992). Conrath and Mignen (1990) have found that the principle reason IS departments measure user satisfaction is to improve the quality of service they provide. Rushinek and Rushinek (1986) assert that user satisfaction studies have ignored the impact of service quality on user satisfaction. As the construct of service quality has not been ignored by IS academics and researchers, it is contended that it is more appropriate to measure service quality rather than user satisfaction because this is what IS really wants to measure and could be an antecedent of user satisfaction (Pitt et al., 1995).

Here we consider the other side of the service quality coin ± the provider’s domain. In the area of services marketing, a model has been developed for describing and understanding the service quality process (Parasuramen et al., 1985), and an instrument,

SERVQUAL (Parasuraman et al., 1988), has also been constructed that assesses the quality of service from the customer’s (or user’s) perspective. The use of SERVQUAL in the measurement of service quality as a surrogate for user satisfaction of an IS department is described by Pitt et al. (1995). In this paper, we present data for the other \`Gaps’ in the model of service quality, as a useful way of understanding some of the barriers within the IS department to the provision of excellent quality service to users.

## Is the IS department a service provider?

In reality, the fundamental role of the information systems (IS) department within most organizations is that of the classical services marketer. The services it provides are no different from any other kinds of services, such as ® nancial services, professional services, or health care ± they possess the well-documented characteristics of services as opposed to physical goods (Bateson 1977; Berry 1980; Lovelock 1981; Rathmell 1966, 1974; Shostack 1977; Rands, 1992). The department provides, to other divisions, branches, functions or departments within the organization, a service which is essentially intangible, meaning that unlike physical products, its quality cannot be touched or seen, but only experienced. Intangibility relates to the dif® culty that users may have in understanding the service offerings of IS departments. While the services of the IS department may have a few tangible attributes, such as equipment and user manuals, typically called search qualities that can be viewed prior to use, the resulting user-relationship problems of intangibility are signi® cant. Furthermore, the service(s) provided by the IS department are inseparable. This is related to intangibility, and means that the services of the IS department are produced and used simultaneously, which is typical of the simultaneous production and consumption which characterizes most services. Frequently the user’s presence and participation in the production of the service is necessary. As there is high user involvement in the production and consumption of most services, the standardization and control thereof are dif® cult to maintain. The implications for service quality and the measurement and management thereof are thus obvious.

The services of the IS department are also heterogeneous. Because most services are labour intensive, they are susceptible to heterogeneity. Many, if not all of the services of the IS department services are performed by people, and people do not always perform consistently. There may be variation from one service to another within the same department, variation in the service a single individual provides from day to day, and from user to user. Thus standardization and quality of service are extremely dif® cult to control. However, this also leads to large opportunities for the customization of services in order to meet users’ speci® c needs. Because of these factors, the IS department is often faced with the dilemma of providing ef® cient standardized service at some acceptable level of quality, while simultaneously attempting to treat each user as a unique individual.

The management of user satisfaction issues has been an issue for IS managers for some time. It is our contention that IS managers can gain much from adopting a services marketing approach to the management of user satisfaction. In this paper we introduce the \`Gaps’ model of service quality (Parasuraman et al., 1985; Watson et al., 1993) to the IS community, and attempt to make it directly relevant to the management of user satisfaction issues.

## The \`Gaps’ model of service quality

The \`Gaps’ model of service quality had its origins in the marketing department of the Texas A & M University throughout the 1980s (Parasuraman et al., 1985; 1988; Zeithaml 1981; Zeithaml et al., 1988; Zeithaml et al., 1985). Following intensive exploratory work involving service customers, and the managers of service organizations, Parasuraman et al. (1985) proposed a model of service quality that can be summarized as follows:

Service Quality as perceived by a user depends on the size and direction of the gap between expected service and perceived service, which, in turn, depends on the nature of the gaps on the service provider’s side, associated with the design, marketing and delivery of the services.

A minor adaptation of the model with relevance to the IS department and its users was made by Pitt and his colleagues (Pitt et al., 1993; Watson et al., 1995). It views service quality and service quality problems as existing between user and IS department. From the user’s point of view, service quality is the difference between what he or she expects, and what he or she perceives themselves to be receiving from the IS department. If the expectation equals the perceptions, the user is technically satis® ed. When the perception exceeds the expectations, the user is more than satis-® ed and can even be excited, and delighted. When expectations exceed perceptions however, the use is dissatis® ed, and there is a service quality problem.

Expectations of users are formed by word of mouth communication; personal needs; past experiences; communications by vendors (Pitt et al., 1995); and, communication by the IS department. The user’s gap between expectations and perceptions ± Gap 5 in the model ± is caused by four other gaps, which are interna to the IS department. Gap 1 is a gap between IS manager’ s perceptions of expectations and the user’s actual expectations ± quite simply IS managers don’t always understand what users want. Gap 2 is a gap between IS manager’s perceptions of user expectations and their ability to translate these into IS service quality standards. In other words, IS managers might know what users expect, but are unable to set quality standards. Gap 3 is a gap between the IS service quality standards that are set, and the IS service quality that is actually delivered by the department. The IS department does not deliver according to the standards it has set for itself. Finally, Gap 4 is a gap between IS service delivery and external communications by the IS department ± the service that is actually delivered might not live up to promises made by the IS department. The process of measurement of the internal gaps is discussed in detail in Zeithaml et al., (1990), and with speci® c reference to the IS department in Watson et al., (1993).

## The study

A complete gaps study was conducted on behalf of the IS department of a Big 6 accounting ® rm (referred to as ISD). The Gap 5, or SERVQUAL questionnaire was administered to a random sample of 800 (from a tota of 8000) end users. Items in the questionnaire were suitably amended to re¯ ect the nature of the study. This is not discussed in detail here. Managers within the IS department were required to complete the Gaps 1 and 2 questionnaires. Anonymity was assured, and once again the questionnaires were dispatched to the population by means of the internal mail system. A total of 19 out of 35 managers responded by the cut-off date, resulting in a response rate of 54.28%. User-contact staff within the IS department were required to complete the Gaps 3 and 4 questionnaires. Anonymity was assured, and again, the questionnaires were dispatched to the population by means of the internal mail system. A total of 56 out of 190 user-contact staff responded by the cut-off date, representing a response rate of 29.47%. The instruments provided by Zeithaml et al. (1990) provided the basis for measurement of the internal gaps, and the discussion which ensues is based on their text.

The speci® c objectives of the study were to:

(1) Determine how well the IS department was meeting the user’s expectations on the service quality dimensions; determine service quality scores on each dimension; determine the relative importance of the ® ve dimensions to the users;

(2) determine an overall service quality index (SQI) for GAP 5 and overall scores for GAPs 1, 2, 3 and 4;

(3) determine management’s perceptions of user expectations; and, the extent of their ability and willingness to set standards in terms of service quality;

(4) determine the user contact employees’ perceptions of their ability to deliver service quality according to the set standards; and, to determine the extent to which the quality of service delivered matched the external communication of the IS department.

## Management’s perceptions of customer expectations

The unique characteristic of GAP 1 is that it traverses the boundary between the customer and the service provider in the conceptual model of service quality. This gap is pertinent to the simple question, \`Do managers know and understand what customers expect?’ The extent of GAP 1 is measured by determining the discrepancy between manager’s ratings and customer’s ratings on the corresponding questions on the SERVQUAL questionnaire. The GAP 1 scores are calculated along the ® ve dimensions in a similar way to the computation of GAP 5. First, the average expectation score along each dimension for the customer sample is calculated. Similarly, the average expectation score along each dimension as perceived by the manager sample is calculated. The average score for the manager sample is then subtracted from the average score for the customer sample. The resulting difference is the GAP 1 score along each dimension.

The more negative the score, the more serious the gap: a negative GAP 1 score stems from manager’s lack of understanding of customer’s expectations. However, it should be remembered that the closer the scores are to zero, the more ideal they are. A positive score actually means that managers overestimate, and are thus still out of touch with, consumer expectations. The GAP 1 scores for the study are reported in Table 1 ± the customer’s average expectation score, the manager’s average perception score, and the difference between them, more than ± 0.2 are items; 1 ± modern looking equipment; 9 ± error-free reports; 11 ± prompt service; 13 ± never too busy to respond to questions; 18 ± individual attention; and 19 ± convenient operating hours.

GAP 1 scores by dimension, and for the overall gap, are shown in Table 2. It can be seen that all the dimensional scores, except for tangibles, are negative, and that the overall GAP 1 score is ± 0.237. This means that while management enjoys a reasonably good understanding of user expectations, it does tend to underestimate the expectations of users in general. However, when one compares Table 1 with Table 2, it can be seen that in general, management has an accurate assessment of the relative importance of dimensions to users-managers having also given a relative rating of importance to users by allocating 100 points among dimensions on a constant sum scale.

The critical challenge is then one of using this knowl edge to set service-quality standards for the organization. It is all very well having a reasonable ± if slightly underestimated ± understanding of what users want, but it is more important to deliver what users expect. The fact that large negative gaps exist in the user sample con® rms that the IS department’s service quality delivery could be considerably improved.

The difference scores shown in Table 1 contribute to the score on each of the ® ve dimensions in Table 2 in the same manner as the calculations in GAP 5, namely tangibles (statements 1± 4), reliability (statements 5± 9), responsiveness (statements 10± 13), assurance (statements 14± 17) and empathy (statements 18± 22). The average difference scores on these statements represent management’s scores on each of the dimensions. Since these dimensions are part of an overall service quality construct, they are additive, and present a quantitative indication of the extent of GAP1, as evidenced by the overall GAP 1 score of ± 0.31.

While it is valuable to have a quantitative indication of the extent of GAP 1, management also needs to understand the driving forces behind it. Three conceptual factors contribute to GAP 1. These factors are (1) Lack of a marketing research orientation, evidenced by insuf® cient marketing research, inadequate use of research results, and a lack of interaction between management and customers; (2) Inadequate upward communication between customer contact personnel and management; and (3) Too many levels of management separating contact personnel from top management. These three factors can be analysed, and their relative strengths identi® ed, by measuring certain key contributing aspects, known as antecedents to GAP 1. These antecedents are listed in Table 3. The scores in Table 3 are the average scores for each antecedent on a scale of 1 to 7, on which the higher the score, the more favourable the current status of the antecedent, or situation. Antecedents 1± 4 assess the extent of marketing research orientation; antecedents 5± 8 assess the extent of upward communication; and, the ninth antecedent assesses the extent to which too many levels of management might contribute to the overall GAP 1 score.

## Translating user service quality expectations into service quality standards

Understanding what users expect from IS service quality is not enough to ensure it. This intelligence must be converted into meaningful service quality standards for the IS department. There are four chief causes of gap 2 ± or, why, despite the fact that they may know what users expect, IS managers don’t always set acceptable, appropriate service quality standards.

## Lack of commitment to IS service quality by senior IS executives

Despite the lip service paid to it, in many IS departments there is little or no real commitment by management to service quality for users. Short-term objectives such as cost reduction, capacity utilization and ef® ciency are far easier to assess, and, therefore, seem to merit focus. Frequently, IS service quality is de® ned from the IS department’s point of view, and not the user’s. Middle management and contact employees cannot be expected to commit to quality if senior levels in the IS department are not.

## A perception of infeasibility

In many IS departments, there is a perception of infeasibility ± \`It would cost too much’, \`It cannot be done’, \`Users expect too much’, \`Users are unreasonable’, \`Users do not understand’. Frequently from a conventional point of view this may be true. However, it is a contention that astute IS managers will ® nd unconventional ways of giving users exactly what they want.

## Inadequate task standardization

Inadequate task standardization as a cause of gap 2 has to do with the extent to which hard and soft technology are used to standardize service tasks. Here the IS department must ask the questions: \`Are we ourselves successfully using automation to achieve consistency in serving users? Are programs in place to improve operating procedures so that consistent service is provided to them?’

## An absence of goal setting

If service quality goals are based on IS department standards rather than on customer standards and expectations, then there really is an absence of goal setting. Frequently, IS departments will set goals in terms of capacity utilization, and similar measures instead of in terms of user satisfaction, and service quality.

Results of the IS department study for Gap 2 are reported in Table 4. From Table 4 it can be seen that Gap 2 is a serious one for the IS department concerned. Bearing in mind that 7, or as close to it as possible, is an ideal score, the overall extent of the gap is 3.27. All four antecedents to the gap are also low, with the most serious being a perception of feasibility problem ± a score of only 3.15. The antecedent items are also listed, with the individual scores. Items 10 through 13 measure service quality commitment; items 14 and 15 measure goal setting; items 16 and 17 measure task standardization; and, items 18 through 20 measure the perception of feasibility. Among the most serious scores here are those on item 11 (no internal programs for improving the quality of service to users = 2.96) and 19 (giving users the level of service they really want, would always cause overspend on budgets = 2.54).

## Gap 3: the service delivery gap

Gap 3 in the model ± the gap between service standards and service delivery ± is probably the most serious from the IS manager’s point of view. It suggests that while service quality standards may have been set, according to knowledge of user expectations, the IS department is not delivering according to these standards. Yet it is also the gap which is easiest to identify, rectify, and manage. The causes and cures of Gap 3 are discussed in more detail below.

## Role ambiguity

Role ambiguity in the IS environment exists whenever employees do not possess the information or training necessary to perform their jobs adequately. They will be uncertain about what managers and supervisors require of them, and what they should do to satisfy these expectations. What must the IS department do? Provide role clarity. This must be done by communication ± employees must have accurate information about their roles in the IS department, with speci® c frequent communication about what they are expected to do. There should of course be feedback ± they must be told constantly about how well they are performing according to standards and expectations. Thirdly, role ambiguity must be eliminated by instilling con® dence and competence, and this can only be achieved by constant attention to training, from both technical (or knowledge) and interpersonal (skills) perspectives.

## Role con¯ ict

Role con¯ ict occurs when employees within the IS department perceive that they cannot satisfy all the demands of the individuals they must serve. A simple example of this is the con¯ ict between the instruction of a manager to service as many users as possible, and the user’s desire for individual attention. This is often complicated by the reward system within the IS department, frequently geared to quantity rather than quality. To overcome this the IS department should strive to eliminate the distress of role con¯ ict by attempting to de® ne service roles and standards in terms of user expectations.

## Poor employee± technology± job ® t

Very often in IS departments there is a poor ® t between employee and job, and between technology and job. As one IS manager explained, \`At the salaries we pay, I’ m surprised anyone with skills in computers and software wants to do the job, let alone the incompetents we have doing it’ . The two causes can be cured by giving adequate attention to the type of people IS departments put into jobs, and by making sure that they have the appropriate technology needed to ensure the user’s expectations are met.

## Lack of perceived control

Lack of perceived control has to do with the extent to which employees within the IS department believe the department can actually control the standards that have been set. Perceived control involves the ability to make responses that in¯ uence threatening situations and the ability to choose outcomes or goals. The IS department employee who has no choice but to refuse a user a justi® ed request when the supervisor is at lunch perceives him ± or herself to have little control over the situation. What management within the IS department may need to do is empower employees ± give them far more capacity to make more meaningful decisions if they affect users.

## Lack of teamwork

Teamwork ± or lack thereof ± is probably one of the commonest problems facing all IS departments. From our perspective, one of the most important aspects of teamwork is the extent to which employees view other employees as users. Another is the extent to which employees feel personally involved and committed to the IS department. Teams need to be built, they generally do not just happen. Teamwork is at the heart of all service quality initiatives ± if employees cannot work together, then service cannot \`come together’ for the user.

A ® nal strategy for closing Gap 3 is the management of users. This is imperative when users play an active role in the quality of the service delivery that they receive. Accountants are reliant on clients keeping records for tax purposes, and airlines are more effective when passengers arrive promptly for check-ins. Smart service providers will be effective at actually managing users, and the role they have to play in service delivery. The implications of this for the IS department are obvious. Successful IS departments are those that enable users to understand that a great deal of the quality of what they receive is dependent on their own participation and cooperation. This does not have to be done in a dictatorial, \`we know what is best for you’ fashion ± what is needed is a subtle communication of the fact that the service quality received will be a function of user participation. Results of the ® ndings for gap 3 for the IS department are presented in Table 5.

GAP 3 scores are also calculated along the ® ve dimensions. Customer contact employees were required to indicate on a seven point scale whether they were able to meet service standards consistently, where 1 = unable to meet standards consistently, through to 7 = able to meet standards consistently. On these scales, a higher mean score thus implies a smaller gap. An overall measure of GAP 3 is obtained by averaging the scores across the ® ve dimensions pertaining to GAP 3. In other words the closer the overall GAP 3 score is to seven, the better. A perfect score of seven would simply mean, no GAP 3.

## Gap 4 service delivery and communication by the IS department

The fourth gap in the service quality model is that between what the IS department promises about service and what it actually delivers. Much of the communication by IS departments creates expectations in the minds of users. When what is delivered does not match these expectations the user is often dissatis® ed to an extent that the IS department would have been far better off had it said nothing. There are two major causes of gap 4, with speci® c reference to the IS department.

## Inadequate horizontal communication

Inadequate horizontal communication is characterized by members of the IS department, at the same hierarchical level, making promises on behalf of one another, or simply communicating badly with one another. Users are informed that a particular IS department member will address their problem, or that another section within the department will deliver the service. When the user seeks this, the \`other part’ may have been misinformed, or not have been informed at all, about the user’s problems or needs.

## A propensity to overpromise

Frequently representatives of the IS department will make promises to users which will raise expectations which can never be met. At other times, non-persona communications, such as news letters, bulletin boards or information sheets, but the IS department will raise expectations to unrealistic levels. It is important to note that the user will ordinarily not have had these expectations ± they have been created by careless communication, or sometimes, a general lack of communication. The cures for Gap 4 are simple, yet very hard to follow, because it is frequently exciting to promise the unattainable. The IS department needs to work very hard at improving horizontal communication within, and to ensure that individuals and divisions do not make promises, unthinkingly, on behalf of others, that cannot be kept. The extent of Gap 4 is reported in Table 6.

In general, Gap 4 would seem to be a problem. Both causes are relatively serious, as witnessed by the low scores of 3.22 and 4.04 respectively. Item antecedent 25 is particularly low at 2.96, where front-line employees indicate that they are not consulted in the development of communications.

## Summary and conclusions

In summary, it can be said that the overall gaps uncovered in this study give rise to some concern. Gap 5, while not discussed in detail here, is relatively large ± it is clear that ISD is not delivering the quality of service that users expect. While Gap 1 does not seem to be a particular problem, as managers have a reasonably clear understanding of what users expect, the same cannot be said for Gaps 2 and 3, and to some extent 4. Gap 2 ± the inability of management to set service quality standards ± is probably the most serious contributor to the overall service quality problem. This means that management must overcome the antecedents to this gap ± by committing to service quality, and by denying the supposed infeasibility of service quality standards. Under Gap 3, management must give particular attention to the degree of perceived control, and to supervisory control systems. The IS department concerned should also attempt to overcome the problems caused by inadequate horizontal communication, and control the propensity to overpromise to users.

This type of study provides a diagnostic tool to IS managers wishing to manage the quality of service their departments provide to users. It provides the information required if effective benchmarking and tracking is to be carried out. It can be conducted in a relatively inexpensive and ef® cient way. The results, if acted upon, can lead to enhanced IS effectiveness, and ongoing user satisfaction ± the most cogent re¯ ection of investment in this IS resource.
