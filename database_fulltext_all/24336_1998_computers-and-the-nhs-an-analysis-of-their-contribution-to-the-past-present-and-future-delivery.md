# Computers and the NHS: an analysis of their contribution to the past, present and future delivery of the National Health Service

ALAN GILLIES

Health Informatics Research Unit, Faculty of Health, University of Central Lancashire, Preston PR1 2HE, UK

This paper looks at the contribution that computers have made to the delivery of the National Health Service through a period of major reform that began in 1986, and is still ongoing. The paper starts with a retrospective analysis of the ® rst generation of NHS Reforms and the role played by computer systems. The major empirical component is a case study looking at the impact of computers on health promotion activ ities among over 1 million patients in Lancashire. Finally, the paper looks forward to the latest NHS reforms, as outlined in the 1997 White Paper The New DHS (Department of Health, 1997, HMSO, London) and outlines the information implications and a strategic framework to deliver changes required if the reforms are to succeed.

## The NHS environment

## The NHS reforms

The NHS has been the subject of constant reform and change since its inception in 1948. However, the rate of change has accelerated since the mid-1980s under what has become known as the \`NHS reforms’. These may be considered within two generations. The ® rst generation was instituted by the Conservative Government and re¯ ected the prevailing political ideology of market forces and competition. The second generation of reforms followed the Labour Government’s election in 1997 and is currently ongoing. The reforms have been implemented through a number of pieces of key legislation and Government policy documents. These are summarized in Table 1.

## First generation reforms

Prior to 1986, the role of the family doctor was seen as a provider of medicines to tackle simple diseases and a mechanism for referring more seriously ill patients to hospital. The problem with this approach was the continual rise in the cost of the NHS budget as a percentage of GDP. In the period 1953 to 1988, the cost of the NHS rose from 3% of GDP to approximately 6% in 1988 (see Gillies, 1995, for more details).

However, it should be noted that this trend was exacerbated by the recession of the early 1980s, which reduced GDP and therefore caused an unusual increase in the relative cost of health care. A similar trend may be detected in the early 1990s when similar international economic conditions prevailed. It should also be noted that international comparisons reveal that UK spending on the NHS at this time was lower than many comparative countries (Figure 1).

The ® rst generation reforms were dominated by the introduction of the \`internal market’, dividing the NHS into \`suppliers’ and \`purchasers’ with primary health care on the purchasing side and secondary health care on the supply side. As part of this, some GPs were given the option of taking partial control of their own budgets. These became known as fund holding GPs. By 1996, over half the population was registered with fund holding GPs.

The Promoting Better Health White Paper (DoH, 1987) set out the following aims:

(1) To focus upon preventive health care.

(2) To require doctors to provide better information to the Family Health Service Authority (FHSA) in order to increase accountability.

(3) To link doctors’ incomes to performance measured against speci® c targets. The ethos underpinning reforms was that better performance is achieved through competition and incentives. The overall impact of the reforms upon information management was a huge growth in information required, for managing contracts within the internal market, monitoring performance and facilitating health care. This in turn led to a large investment in computers (see for example, Gillies, 1995). The strategy for information management and technology was laid down in the NHS IM&T strategy (DoH, 1992b).

## Second generation reforms

Following the election of a Labour Government in 1997, a second generation of reforms was initiated. Although these were intended as \`evolutionary’ rather than revolutionary, they were based upon collaboration rather than competition and this has major strategic implications including the abolition of the internal market, a reduction in administration costs and a primary care led NHS based around groups of general practices. The new reforms were outlined in the 1997 White Paper (DoH, 1997). Details of the implementation are still emerging through a series of working papers. Principal amongst them from an information perspective is the new IM&T strategy (DoH, 1998b).

The ® rst generation of reforms led to a great increase in the number of computers in primary care. However, the competitive model has left a legacy, which will make the implementation of the new reforms extremely problematical; namely (i) a range of incompatible proprietary systems, (ii) variable levels of usage both within practices and between practices and (iii) a lack of consistent practice in coding patient records.

For the primary care commissioning groups that form the basis of the New NHS to be established, the practices within each group must be able to share data. This requires not only a means to exchange data, but agreed policies on how clinical conditions are coded within each patient record system. Pilot projects LANMORB (McMillan et al., 1996) and SIPS (see Ellis, 1998) suggest that the problems are consider able both in human and technical terms.

In the rest of this paper, we shall consider the impact of computers on the NHS to date and suggest a way forward to facilitate the introduction of the second generation of NHS reforms.

## The role and growth of computers

One of the key tenets of the original NHS reforms was a shift of resources towards preventive health care. This is both clinically desirable and ® nancially attractive. The Government encouraged GPs to undertake health promotion activity by giving ® nancial incentives. Computers were offered as a tool to assist in the management of such activities.

The strategy for improving health in the UK was outlined in the Health of the Nation document (DoH, 1992a). This document de® nes priority areas, performance measures and targets to be achieved by the year 2000. The Health of the nation strategy outlines ® ve key areas and targets: coronary heart disease (CHD), speci® c cancers, mental illness, sexually transmitted diseases including HIV/AIDS and accidents. Second order priorities identi® ed include childhood immunization, asthma, back pain and diabetes. These areas were identi® ed as those where existing practice was well established or where other mechanisms are being employed to make progress. The strategy was continued and re® ned in the Our Healthier Nation document (DoH, 1998a).

The impact of computers upon health promotion will be considered in the following case study, as an indicator of the impact of computers upon the NHS during the ® rst generation of reforms.

## Case study: the impact of computers upon health promotion

The case study looks at the impact of computers upon health promotion activity in Lancashire. It is concerned with three areas where GPs have a crucial role to play in the Health of the Nation (DoH, 1992a) strategy: CHD, cervical screening from the key areas and child immunizations from the second order priorities. These have been chosen as representative of the broader picture and those where computers can play a key role delivering the goals of the strategy. The activity in these areas has reached different levels of maturity. It will use an elevation framework that considers the impact of computers alongside environmental factors both internal and external.

## De® nition of bene® ts derived from computerization

Clinical bene® ts are de® ned as those which directly impact upon patient care as opposed to management bene® ts which may free up more resources for better patient care but only indirectly impact upon it. All GPs who control their own budgets (fund holders) use computer systems and therefore presumably regard them as a necessary evil at least. Interviews carried out in North America show that computer systems for billing are well developed but interviews with primary care doctors in Canada failed to show any use of computers for clinical purposes.

In the UK, where many GPs have been suspicious of the technology and the management and contractual changes (DoH, 1989), the doctors have often been persuaded through the promise of clinical bene® ts as opposed to management bene® ts. The de® nition, however, of clinical bene® ts is complex. Measurement of performance in this study, therefore, will be restricted to a simpler question: can computers signi® - cantly improve performance of general practice, when measured in terms of the indicators and goals de® ned within the Health of the Nation strategy document?

## Data collection and validity

This study was carried out by the author with the collaboration of the now defunct Lancashire Family Health Service Authority, who provided a great deal of assistance in collecting the data used for analysis. The data set is drawn from two sources. The ® rst is data collected by the FHSA under statutory requirements from 283 practices concerning health-screening work.

The population of practices available for the study is 250 of the 283 practices, 88% of the total. The data is a snapshot taken during the ® rst quarter of 1995. The second is an interview-based survey of the attitudes of 178 practices to computer systems carried out at the end of 1994 (Barret et al., 1995). The combination of information regarding clinical performance and computer systems allows for the evaluation of the impact of computers upon clinical factors. From this study, data are available on the budgetary status of 164 practices, covering 65% of the patient population (762 000).

## Evaluation framework

This study will consider the area of health screening as this is both a priority of the national health strategy (DoH, 1992a,b; 1998a,b) and an area where computers are claimed to have major impact. However, it is recognized that computerization is only one of a number of factors impacting upon clinical performance. Therefore, the evaluation will be based upon a framework illustrated in Figure 2.

The clinical effectiveness of the general practitioner may be in¯ uenced by the external or internal environment as well as the introduction of a computer system, and any attempt to consider the effect of a computer system must recognize the in¯ uence of these other factors.

## Analysis of the case study

## The impact of the computer upon delivery of health promotion targets

The impact upon delivery of health targets was evaluated through looking at screening programmes. Other bene® ts such as more reliable repeat prescribing were considered to have a less direct impact upon patient health. Three areas were considered:

(1) Cervical cancer,

(2) Child immunizations,

(3) Blood pressure screening.

The percentage of practices implementing screening programmes was compared for practices with and without computers. The results are summarized in Table 2. The results suggest that practices are more likely to set up blood pressure screening programmes if they are computerized, but that practices will set up cervical cancer and immunization programmes irrespective, probably as a consequence of the external ® nancial incentives to do so. It should also be noted that the information requirements of a blood pressure screening programme are more complicated than for the other programmes.

The success of a screening programme is re¯ ected in the percentage of the target patients reached. This may be compared for computerized and non-computerized practices to indicate not just whether the screening process is carried out more effectively in computerized practices (Table 3). The differences in performance are small, variable and not statistically signi® cant.

## Computers and the NHS

Populations exhibiting high levels of deprivation also show characteristics making health screening dif® cult, including mobility and suspicion of authority. In areas of high deprivation, GPs receive extra payments for health promotion, paid per patient. The case for these payments is that the health needs of a deprived population are greater than those without. The intention is that they should encourage practices in deprived areas to undertake additional screening programmes.

The study showed that in Lancashire, those practices serving populations with the highest deprivation levels (over 10% of the population attracting payments for the highest level of deprivation) were signi® cantly less likely to have a screening programme, 80% of practices against 90% of the remainder of practices.

The study considered the impact of external health needs upon computerization: the health needs were considered in terms of the following indicators:

(1) % raised blood pressure,

(2) % overweight,

(3) % obese,

(4) % above alcohol limit,

(5) % family history of heart disease or stroke.

The study analysed whether practices with greater health needs were more likely to computerize as a means to address the need. In each case, the interquartile ranges of the distribution of the indicators were compared to consider whether a higher level of health need was more likely to lead to computerization. The results are shown in Table 4. In general, the indicators provide higher values in those practices with computers suggesting that greater health care needs does act as an incentive to computerize the practice. However, the differences are not statistically signi® cant.

## The impact of the internal environment upon delivery of health targets

The internal environment may affect clinical effectiveness as much as the external. This study looked at the impact of (1) budgetary control, (2) group size, and (3) list size, on the provision and effectiveness of screening programmes.

Twenty-three per cent of practices in this study had partial budgetary control, known as fund holding. Much has been claimed for the opportunities for better patient care offered by fund holding. However, in this study, fund holding practices did not prove signi® - cantly more likely to engage in immunizations, cervical cancer screening or blood pressure screening than nonfund holding practices (Table 5). In addition, the differences between fund holding practices and others in terms of percentage of target reached were very small and not statistically signi® cant.

Group size was investigated for the practices involved. Single partner practices make approximately one-third of the total number of practices or 13% of the doctors. These practices are signi® cantly more likely to have screening programmes than the larger practices for cervical screening, but signi® cantly less likely to screen for blood pressure (Table 6). Screening effectiveness is also signi® cantly greater for cervical cancer, with a median of 97% against 88% in multipartner practices.

The mean list size, i.e. the number of patients per doctor, for this sample is 1929, which is about 10% above the national average. Grouping the list sizes into low medium and high, based upon the quartiles as the break points, cervical cancer and immunizations show no relationship with list size. However, blood pressure screening does exhibit a signi® cant relationship with list size (male, $\mathbf { \gamma } _ { \mathbf { \mathscr { P } } } = 0 . 0 0 4 ;$ female, ${ \it p } = 0 . 0 0 1 )$ ). A Spearmann correlation coef® cient test reveals no correlation between list size and actual performance within any screening programmes $( 0 < r < 0 . 1$ in all cases).

In order to investigate whether the likelihood of computerization is in¯ uenced by the internal environment, the following factors were considered within this study: (1) whether the practice is single doctor or a group practice, (2) the impact of list size, and (3) whether the practice is fund holding.

The hypothesis that single doctor practices were less likely to use computers was tested using a chi-squared test. The hypothesis was supported by a p-value of 10± <sup>5</sup>. In spite of this, single doctor practices perform signi® cantly more screening programmes for cervical cancer, although they carry out less screening for the more complex blood pressure screening programmes.

## Discussion: The impact of computers upon health promotion

This study found that many of the stereotypical expectations about which practices were likely to be computerized were true:

(1) Single doctor practices were signi® cantly less likely to use computers.

(2) Fund holding doctors were all computerized.

(3) There was some indication that greater health needs may act as an incentive to computerization.

However, it is more dif® cult to argue that computers have made a signi® cant difference to doctor behaviour in relation to screening or that screening is more effective because of computerization. The area where computers have had most impact is in blood pressure screening. This is the most intensive type of screening in terms of information management requiring the doctors to keep track of a range of factors.

Evidence that computers do assist in blood pressure screening includes the ® ndings that computerized practices are signi® cantly more likely to screen for blood pressure and the fact that single doctor practices who are under-computerized relative to the rest are signi® - cantly more likely to screen for cervical cancer, but less likely to screen for blood pressure.

However, the impact of computers upon genera practice in clinical terms remains limited. The reason for this may lie in doctors’ attitudes to computers. A practice may be computerized, but the doctors may not make use of the facilities. Shum (1992) highlighted the fact that computers at that time were under-utilized even where they had been installed.

One hundred and thirty one of the Lancashire practices were interviewed in an associated study to look at doctors’ attitudes to their systems. They were asked what they thought about (1) documentation for software, (2) documentation for hardware, (3) backing up patient data, and (4) training. The results are shown in Figure 3. These results indicate a signi® cant degree of dissatisfaction with key aspects of system implementation. However, it was not possible to show that those practices that are more dissatis® ed are signi® - cantly less likely to carry out screening programmes $( \phi > 0 . 0 5 )$

The growth in the use of computers in primary health care documented in a previous paper (Gillies, 1995) is not in dispute. However, what are in doubt are the bene® ts accruing within the performance targets laid down in the Health of the Nation strategy.

## The future role of information systems within the new NHS

The New NHS White Paper (DoH, 1997) places information at the heart of the new NHS. The emphasis upon collaborative working greatly increases the information management requirements for the NHS. Within the original NHS IM&T strategy, a crucial distinction was drawn between information technology and information management.

Many of the White Paper changes depend crucially upon information. The White Paper draws attention to the new technology required, e.g. the linking of every practice to the NHSNet, but does not identify how the improvements required in information can be achieved. Currently, much of the information technology within general practice remains underused due to poor information management. Although the national computerization of general practice is in excess of 90%, the use of those computers is much more variable.

Unless the use of existing technology can be improved, the proposed changes will not be successful. The author proposes a strategic framework that de® nes a systematic and structured approach to bring practice information management to the required maturity level. The emphasis is upon training and changes in working practices, not on new technology. Many of the more successful practices have very mature information management procedures built around relatively old technology. Due to the competitive ethos of the ® rst generation of reforms, most practices have developed as independent entities. External electronic links have been restricted to peripheral activities, generally of a nonclinical nature. The most common electronic links have been for registration information and for transmitting items of service information.

The complexity of the technology required for external links does not depend greatly upon the information transmitted. It is the information management procedures that become much more complex if clinical data are to be shared. Data collection and coding procedures need to be agreed and validated between collaborating partners. Historical evidence shows that many group practices found this dif® cult within their organizations, let alone within the context of a wider group.

Within the New NHS White Paper, it is proposed that every practice become externally networked through the NHS Net. However, this will only impact upon patient care if this enhanced infrastructure is utilized through better information management. This can only be achieved through greatly increased consultation between practices and other stake holding organizations.

## GPIMM: General Practice Information Maturity Model

In order to achieve the level of information management required to deliver the new NHS, a three-stage process is required:

(1) De® ne the information management requirements for the key elements of the new NHS.

(2) De® ne the current state of each practice.

(3) Provide a systematic and structured approach for each practice to move from their current state to the required state.

The author has proposed a model elsewhere for the maturity of practice information processes (Gillies, 1998). The model, known as GPIMM, draws upon experience of the author with maturity models in of® ce automation (Gillies and Baugh, 1995) and others in the defence software supply sector (CMU SEI, 1995). The model provides the means to achieve all three stages outlined above and has been piloted with health authority data. Here, we shall aim to show how the model can assist in delivery of the key components of the New NHS White Paper.

## The GPIMM model

The model is based around ® ve primary maturity levels, with an additional zeroth level for noncomputerized practices. The maturity levels are summarized in Table 7. The overall structure of the model is shown in Figure 4. Many practices are still at levels 1 and 2, even after up to ten years of computing experience. This provides a signi® - cant barrier to clinical practice developments.

## Use GPIMM to de® ne the information management requirements for the key elements of the new NHS

The New NHS White Paper highlights mainly IT innovations such as NHSNet connections. However, it is the use of that IT infrastructure to facilitate information provision and management that will be a critical success factor in the introduction of new initiatives such as primary care groups.

The GPIMM framework provides a systematic and structured route for practice development to improve information management to provide effective information solutions to support key elements of the \`New NHS’ White Paper, including: (1) primary care groups, (2) connecting practices to the NHSNet, (3) access to evidence, and (4) quality assurance.

Primary care groups are the corner stone of the new co-operative approach. Four types are de® ned within the legislation:

(1) at minimum, support the Health Authority in commissioning care for its population, acting in an advisory capacity,

(2) take devolved responsibility for managing the budget for healthcare in their area, formally as part of the Health Authority,

(3) become established as free-standing bodies accountable for the Health Authority for commissioning care,

(4) become established as free-standing bodies accountable to the Health Authority for commissioning care and with added responsibility for the provision of community health services for their population.

All the different options require data exchange. This requires practices to agree common coding and data collection policies, ® rst internally and then externally. The agreement of internal coding policies is a characteristic of practices moving from GPIMM Level 3 to GPIMM Level 4.

The agreement of coding policies with other practices and external agencies is characteristic of practices moving from GPIMM Level 4 to Level 5. This means that GPIMM Level 4 is an absolute minimum for practices within Primary care groups to function effectively. To make use of their NHSNet links to other practices, much of Level 5 is required. Access to evidence requires an information system able to provide information tailored to the needs of practices. This is typical of procedures at Level 4 of GPIMM.

The most effective way to deliver continuous quality assurance is through real time audits carried out through the patient record system. Within a practice, this is part of GPIMM level 4. However, in order to carry out comparative audits within or between primary care groups, it will be necessary to have Level 5 procedures to permit real time audit of comparable data.

In order to help with self-assessment, a computer-based tool has been devised, known as CAPA (Computer Aided Practice Assessment). The tool helps de® ne the appropriate maturity level for the practice and suggests six appropriate key tasks for improvement. A sample of the output from the tool is shown in Figure 5.

Early testing of GPIMM on health authority data drawn from 17 practices shows a mixture of a lack of development and inappropriate development that does not lead to further development. Without a phased change management approach, the necessary information management procedures will not be realized and without effective information management, the new primary care groups will not be realized. GPIMM can be used to provide a structured and systematic path to practice improvement as well as providing an assessment of the current level.

## Conclusions

This paper has looked at the impact of information systems upon the delivery of the National Health Service in terms of an analysis of the past (® rst generation reforms), present (health promotion case study) and the future (second generation reforms and the GPIMM model).

The paper has argued that the ® rst generation reforms determined that information management and technology developed along a particular route that has led to incompatible systems and information. This has led to variable effectiveness, as shown in the case study.

For the future, the IM&T strategy recognizes the need for information management as well as information technology. The New NHS White Paper has major implications for information management and information technology within general practice. The IT infrastructure needs are de® ned in the White Paper. However, the information management needs are less well de® ned. Current information management practice falls well below the level required to implement primary care groups as de® ned in the White Paper. Practices will be required to make signi® cant and often dramatic changes in working practices and information management procedures.

Without a systematic and staged approach to practice development in the area of information management, these changes will not be attainable. The GPIMM model described in this paper provides a mechanism for facilitating such changes in a structured and systematic way.
