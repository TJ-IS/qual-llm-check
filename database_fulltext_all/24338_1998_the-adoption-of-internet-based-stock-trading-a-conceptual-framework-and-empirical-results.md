# The adoption of Internet-based stock trading: a conceptual framework and empirical results

LAWRENCE LOH and YEE-SHYUAN ONG

Department of B usiness Policy, Faculty of Business Administration, National University of Singapore, Singapore 119260, Republic of Singapore

The adoption and diffusion of innovative technologies have remained critical concerns in information systems research. With the recent advent of electronic commerce, issues in system deployment and user acceptance have become even more crucial. An experimental system, Stocknet, which is a departure from normal practice in the local stock exchange which still uses conventional methods for share transactions, was developed and subjected to a pilot test through a stock trading competition using live stock data. A research framework was developed with the main emphasis on the adoption and deployment of a broad-based Internet stock-trading platform in Singapore. A logistic regression analysis was conducted to predict user acceptance of a new system from a measure of their perceptions, beliefs and attitudes and usage behaviour. The study reveals that users’ concerns, expectations, perceived ease of use and the real value added of a new system as well as their trading behaviour are critical determinants to the ultimate adoption of Internet stock trading. In fact, the probability of users accepting a new system is highly dependent on their perceptions of system evaluations and subsequently their usage behaviour. The primary focus of this paper is on users’ perspectives with a view towards a subsequent examination of policy as well as regulatory issues. Based on the results, some broad recommendations and implications are subsequently drawn with regard to the futur adoption and deployment of real-time, Internet-based stock trading.

## Introduction

Commercial activity on the Internet or, more speci® - cally, the World Wide Web has been experiencing a tremendous growth worldwide. Electronic commerce is a general term applied to the use of information technology to effect the linkages between the functions provided by participants in commerce (Halchimi et al., 1996). The rate of increase in the number of hosts being connected to the Internet has been growing exponentially. Forrester Research, a consulting ® rm in Massachusetts, predicts that Web users will grow from 5.4 million in 1996 to more than 11 million in 1998 and 22 million by the year 2000 (Rowley, 1996). Moreover, the World Trade Organization also estimates that the value of goods and services trade via the Internet will grow 20 times to US\$200 billion between 1996 and the year 2000 (The Straits Times, 1997b).

The Internet will no doubt open new vistas for trade in goods and services ± particularly ® nancial services ± possibly revolutionizing it within a short span of time. There are already some 1.5 million on-line stockbroker accounts in the US alone and this ® gure is growing by 50± 150% every year (The Straits Times, 1997b). Thus, this potential base will de® nitely encourage the use of open networks for the conduct of electronic commerce.

Advances in technology have radically changed the process of trading ® nancial securities. Only a decade ago, the primary method by which individual investors were able to obtain information and trade on the stock market was by going to the stockbroker’s of® ce or calling the broker over the phone. Today, with the growing awareness and popularity of the Internet, the ® nancial industry has started to take advantage of the vast opportunities presented by this new medium. These developments, along with changes expected in the near future, will revolutionize the stockbrokerage industry at both the retail and institutional levels. We will more likely see on-line stock trading services over the Internet as the Information Superhighway becomes increasingly popular. In the past 3 years, many investors have started using the Web to conduct their daily stock trading activities. Globalization of the ® nancial markets is not a myth but a reality that is here to stay (Arthur Andersen, 1988). In fact, this globalization is the culmination of the ® rst stage of the electronic commerce revolution, which has become one of the principal drivers of stock exchange development to operate at a level that is competitive with the world’s best.

The future of Wall Street is on-line investing, and it is no longer capital driven but rather information and technology driven (Farrell, 1996). In fact, Forrester Research estimates the number of on-line brokerage accounts will accelerate from 3 million in late 1997 to 14.4 million in 2002 and, at the same time, trading on these accounts will rise from US\$120 billion annually to US\$688 billion (The Straits Times, 1997c). Moreover, it is predicted that Web-based investing will overtake private dial-up by 1998 and that a new mid-tier broker will emerge to serve do-it-yourself investors (Gomez et al., 1996). By the year 2001, virtually all individual shareholders will be cyberspace investors, on-line and on the Internet (Farrell, 1996).

Given the real commercial potential and opportunities of Internet-based stock trading, the essence of user acceptance of new systems cannot be overemphasized. It is, therefore, the intention of the present research to examine empirically and explain user acceptance and rejection of computer-based technology. This paper ® rst reviews the literature and empirical studies on innovation and technological adoption and acceptance. This is followed by the survey background, conceptual framework and methodology. The analysis and ® ndings are then presented. The concluding section discusses some broad implications of the results for development policy to foster increased user acceptance and usage.

## Literature review

Although sophisticated applications have become economically feasible with the raw power of computer technology continuing to improve 10-fold each decade (Peled, 1987), identifying the appropriate functional and interface features to be included in end-user systems has proven to be challenging and subtle (Mitroff and Mason, 1983, March, 1987). As a matter of fact, one of the most challenging issues in information systems research is the understanding of the acceptance or rejection of computer systems (Swanson, 1988). A wide coverage of this research area has been studied by various researchers (for example, Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975; Lucas, 1975; Ginzberg, 1981; Fuerst and Cheney, 1982; DeSanctis, 1983; Ives et al., 1983; Srinivasan, 1985; Davis et al., 1989; Moore and Benbasat, 1991; Venkatesh and Davis, 1994; Taylor and Todd, 1995). Most of the studies consider users’ perceptions as critical factors that in¯ uence user acceptance of new systems and, hence, the actual use of these systems (Davis, 1986; Davis et al., 1989). In particular, the perceived usefulness and ease of use have been shown to be prominent in information technology innovation diffusion with the former being a more important factor than the latter (Davis, 1989; Adams et al., 1992; Hendrickson et al., 1993).

For information systems success, several indicators have been identi® ed, for instance pro® tability (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1981), user satisfaction (Cheney and Dickson, 1982; Bailey and Pearson, 1983; Ives et al., 1983, Baroudi and Orlikowski, 1988; Doll and Torkzadeh, 1988; Galletta and Lederer, 1989), the quality of decision making (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978, 1981, 1982; King and Rodriguez, 1978) and system usage (Lucas, 1978; Robey, 1979; DeLone, 1988; Igbaria et al., 1989). Recently, user satisfaction and user-perceived bene® ts (Guimaraes et al., 1992) were used instead. Evidently, from 1981 to 1987, of the 100 studies that measured the success of information system, 41 utilized user evaluations as the measure (Delone and McLean, 1992). According to the technology acceptance model formulated by Davis et al., (1989), user satisfaction and perceived bene® ts and problems are the two main in¯ uences that affect the adoption and usage of an automated system. Furthermore, the theory of reasoned action (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975) also provides a theoretical basis for the link between attitude and behaviour. Both models suggest that satisfaction leads to usage rather than usage stimulating satisfaction. Although the models provided insights into the user acceptance of computer technology, system usage was the only aspect of acceptance that they considered in the study. It is important to examine the other aspect, which is user satisfaction. After the adoption of new systems, this will affect the level of user satisfaction, which will subsequently in¯ uence their future perceptions and usage. Thus, the current study seeks to extend previous research by focusing on the plausibility of such a relationship.

The main objective of this study is to examine the impact of user evaluation and their beliefs and attitudes, as well as their usage behaviour, in which empirical investigation has shown that they have affected users’ adoption and acceptance of a new innovation. By going a step further, we will also explore the relationship between acceptance, user satisfaction and user evaluation.

## Stocknet

## Background

On-line stock trading is a step closer to reality following the launch of a pilot project in Singapore. The stateof-the-art, Internet-based stock transaction application prototype, codenamed \`Project Stocknet’, is the brainchild of the National Computer Board, the Faculty of Business Administration and the Internet Research and Development Unit of the National University of Singapore. Being one of the cutting-edge, on-line systems, Stocknet has its own unique features.

(1) An easy to use and friendly Web-based graphical interface with search option.

(2) Worldwide accessibility from anywhere with Internet connection.

(3) Instantaneous live update of stock prices and indices.

(4) A comprehensive database system for storing stock data and transactions.

(5) Practical portfolio management.

(6) A secured transaction mechanism.

(7) Sophisticated charting and monitoring systems.

Stocknet, with its expandability for multilingual and three-dimensional charting capabilities, can be further supplemented by the other popular analytical software packages including Metastock for Windows, Windows on Wallstreet and SuperCharts.

Even in its pre-release form, Stocknet has generated a great deal of interest and drawn encouraging remarks from Internet users throughout the world. In fact, it was listed as one of the interesting investment simulations in the authoritative Investor’s Guide to the Net (Farrell, 1996). It was placed alongside a few other state-of-theart stock trading simulation applications such as E\*TRADE Internet Stock Market Game, PAWWS Portfolio Management Challenge and AudiTrack.

After undergoing extensive testing, debugging and optimization, a Stocknet competition involving 151 contestants was launched in January 1997. The experimental system allows the participants to carry out simulated stock transactions in a network environment with an easy to use graphical interface based on real prices in real time, using information supplied by the Stock Exchange of Singapore (SES). To overcome security concerns, all transactions were accessible only via a password-protected, encrypted form on the Web site.

## Conceptual framework

This research attempts to study the feasibility of an electronic stock-trading platform through users’ perspectives in the trial of Stocknet. This will have implications for the eventual adoption and deployment of Internet stock trading. A conceptual model was developed based on the notion that perception is a critical determinant of individual behaviour, which, in turn, leads to a certain outcome and performance. The eventual outcome, which in this case is the user acceptance of electronic stock trading, is dependent on their perceptions of system evaluation and concerns as well as usage behaviour. These will undoubtedly in¯ uence user satisfaction, which will also affect their future perceptions and behaviour.

As shown in Figure 1, we hypothesize that the viability of Stocknet (the outcome domain) is determined by user evaluation and concerns (the perception domain) and usage patterns (the behaviour domain). The three domains are, no doubt, intricately interrelated to one another and they affect, to a large extent, user satisfaction (the performance domain), which is essential to further in¯ uence users’ perceptions of the system bene® ts and problems as well as to promote continuous system utilization.

## Methodology

Data were collected through an on-line questionnaire posted at Stocknet’s Web site, where participants could easily have access to it at all times. The questionnaire, which comprehensively covered Stocknet’s feasibility and user requirements, their usage patterns and behaviour and various key concerns about the Internet such as security, privacy and payment issues as well as users’ demographics was organized into ® ve main sections: (1) World Wide Web usage, (2) trading behaviour, (3) security and privacy issues, (4) technical and functional evaluation and (5) general information.

## Operationalization of the variables

## Perceptions

The related literature views users’ perceptions as an important factor which in¯ uences user acceptance of a new system (Fishbein and Ajzen, 1975; Davis, 1986; Davis et al., 1989). In this study, two subdomains are used, namely (1) user evaluations and (2) user-oriented concerns.

## User evaluations

(1) Perceived usefulness. The signi® cance of this variable derives from the technology acceptance model (Davis et al., 1989), which states that perceived usefulness is a major determinant of usage behaviour and intentions. Since it is a very subjective concept, different individuals may apply slightly different meanings to it. In this research, the respondents were asked to rate the usefulness of the system in terms of its overall technicality and functionality, based on a sevenpoint Likert-type scale, ranging from not at all useful (1) to extremely useful (7).

(2) Perceived ease of use. This refers to the degree to which a user believes that using a particular system will be free of effort (Davis, 1989). Effort is a ® nite resource that a person may allocate to the various activities for which he or she is responsible (Radner and Rothschild, 1975). In this case, the variable includes attributes such as ease of navigation, speed of moving around, ease of locating information and presentation format. The response options were also anchored on a seven-point scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7).

(3) Perceived real value added. This variable measures the perceived worthiness generated by using a new system. The respondents were asked to make an overall assessment about whether the new system contributes a profound impact based on their hands-on experience. It was measured on a ® ve-point scale ranging from do not know (1) to revolutionary (5).

(4) User expectation. This refers to the degree to which how well a user perceives the performance of a particular system before using it relative to how well it actually performs. The larger the discrepancy between the perceived and actual performance, the higher the expectations of users. This variable was measured on a ® ve-point scale ranging from below expectations (1) to above expectations (5).

## User-oriented concerns

This is the other subdomain of users’ perceptions. It refers to the degree of perceived uncertainty, uneasiness and fear that users experience towards the use of a new system. The measurement of this variable involves several aspects of the issues which surfaced such as security in terms of the Internet’s content regulation, payment, privacy and other threats.

## Usage behaviour

Based on previous research on the management of information systems usage (Lee, 1986; DeLone, 1988; Pavri, 1988), there are several dimensions used to measure system usage. In our study, the variables that we have included are primary uses, the duration of use, the level of sophistication of usage and the trading behaviour of users when on-line transactions are involved.

(1) Primary uses. The users were asked to indicate the extent to which they used the Web browser for a speci® c set of tasks, including academic research, browsing, entertainment, shopping and others. The response options ranged from do not use (1) to use a lot (7).

(2) Duration of use. This variable has been widely used in the management of information system studies. In fact, Mittman and Moore (1984) and others used actual daily usage as a measurement for the duration of use. It is noteworthy that this provides a slightly different perspective than the frequency of use, which was suggested by Raymond (1985) and DeLone (1988). In this study, however, the duration of use was measured on a six-point scale, based on a weekly basis, ranging from 0± 10 h (1) to over 50 h (6).

(3) Level of sophistication of usage. This variable, which was suggested by Maish (1979), serves to measure the pro® ciency of use of the computer (Igbaria et al., 1989). The respondents were asked to rate their computing experience based on a four-point scale ranging from no computing experience at all (1) to expert (4).

(4) Trading behaviour. This variable was used to capture the changes in the respondents’ investment decision making in the absence of a human element in an Internet-based environment. The respondents were asked to indicate whether their investment decisions, in terms of numbers of lots or shares, bidding and offering prices, trading frequency, time taken in decision making and selection of types of stocks, would be affected if they were to use the conventional way of trading stocks (i.e. through brokers) instead of using Stocknet. Each item was measured on a threepoint scale and the measure was the sum of the changes indicated for each item.

## User satisfaction

This variable, which is de® ned as the attitudes of users using the system relative to how well their needs are being satis® ed, focuses on several attributes, including user perceptions of service quality, support service, the delivery of service and response to problems. The users were asked to rate their level of satisfaction with the speci® c attributes of the system based on a seven-point scale, ranging from extremely dissatis® ed (1) to extremely satis® ed (7).

To investigate the relationship between the study variables, a correlation analysis was conducted. The correlation matrix between the variables is presented in Table 2. Conceptually, these make sense as, all things being equal, the more useful and easy a system is to interact with, the less effort one can allocate to other activities (Radner and Rothschild, 1975), thereby contributing to a higher level of satisfaction among users.

## User oriented concerns

Although commercial activities on the Web have been experiencing tremendous growth, there are still a number of limitations and misconceptions that must be overcome before it can be deemed suitable for commercial purposes. More than 50% of the respondents were sceptical about the resolution of problems such as security, privacy and payment in the near future. Table 4 shows the mean scores and standard deviations of the respondents’ perceived threats when doing on-line transactions on the Internet.

(1) Payment issues. Electronic payment has often been cited as one of the major reasons the Internet has been slow to expand commercially. The respondents’ perceptions of insecure payment systems had discouraged them from doing on-line trading. Conversely, some seemed not to mind if payments were of small value and if systems have proven to be safe and secure. These observations can be made from Table 4.

(2) Privacy issues. Similarly, the relentless expansion of the digital economy is causing growing concerns about the issue of privacy. Seventy percent of the respondents who perceived lack of privacy as a threat of the Internet placed great emphasis on vis iting Web sites anonymously. However, when they were asked about their willingness to give demographic information if the purpose of using the information was stated and con® dentiality was ensured, more than 50% of them were willing to give their information to a Web site.

(3) Content regulation. Apparently, most of them did not respond in favour of the government’s role in deciding what users can or cannot be exposed to (78%). (4) Other threats. For Stocknet in particular, the lack of secure systems to prevent unauthorized access (65%) and the absence of a secure electronic payment system (63%) surfaced as the top concerns. Nonetheless, most respondents did not perceive the absence of a human element in an Internet-based environment as a major threat.

## Factor analysis

To identify the underlying constructs of the various issues and concerns of Internet usage among users, a principal components factor analysis using the varimax rotation method was again conducted. As shown in Table 5, the ten initial observable variables of Internet issues and concerns are then reduced to four optimal factors, which will be used for subsequent analyses, explaining approximately 72% of the overall variance. According to the guidelines for identifying signi® cant factor loadings, a minimum factor loading of 0.60 is required for signi® cance with a sample size of 85 (Hair et al., 1995, p. 385). Thus, the variable (limitations of currently available technologies) with the factor loading of only 0.5735 should be excluded in this case. As can be seen, factor 1, which has three signi® cant loadings, is related to security and privacy issues. Factor 2, on the other hand, is more related to the Internet’s content regulation, while factors 3 and 4 are associated with human± computer interactions and behavioural change, respectively.

## Usage patterns and behaviour

(1) Primary uses. Since the respondents come from a pool of university undergraduates, entertainment (82%) and academic research (70%) were cited as their primary uses of the Web browser. Their other primary uses, which mainly do not involve payments, are the checking and updating of news, downloading of information and electronic mailing.

(2) Duration of use. When performing a speci® c set of tasks or activities using the Web browser, on average 78% of the respondents use it for not more than 10 h per week.

(3) Level of sophistication of usage. As far as their computing skills were concerned, most of the respondents were intermediate users (62%) and were able to perform most tasks competently. Of the total respondents, 24% and 13% considered themselves as experts and novices respectively.

(4) Trading behaviour. Users’ trading behaviour when using a new system is a good proxy for estimating their eventual adoption. ## Logistic regression analysis

In predicting the viability of a Web-based stock transaction and information application, the dependent variable was coded 0 for non-feasibility and 1 for otherwise. Given the dichotomous nature of the dependent variable, a logistic regression analysis was conducted to determine the best model for predicting the ultimate adoption of Stocknet. The various factor scores, which we obtained from our previous factor analyses, were used in our prediction. A priori, the estimated coef® - cients of user evaluations in terms of perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use and perceived real value added are expected to be positive while user expectation is expected to be negative. Similarly, users’ concerns are expected to be negative while usage behaviour can be either positive or negative.

## User satisfaction

As mentioned, user satisfaction is the key measure of system success and is important in promoting continuous system utilization. Those who expressed a relatively higher level of satisfaction in using Stocknet were more likely to return to the site and were also more optimistic about its feasibility. In fact, there is a very signi® cant positive correlation $( r = 0 . 4 0$ and ${ \it p } = 0 . 0 0 0 )$ between the overall level of user satisfaction and the probability of the ultimate adoption of Internet-based stock trading. In other words, the ultimate adoption of a Web-based stock trading mechanism is positively dependent on users’ satisfaction level.

## Correlation analysis

The various productivity attributes of the system, including perceived service quality, support services, timelessness and response to problems or feedback, were found to have a very signi® cant positive correlation with the level of satisfaction (see Table 8). The more favourable their perceptions of the productivity attributes of the system, the higher the level of satisfaction resulted.

## Implications

Based on the reduced conceptual framework, as depicted in Figure 2, some implications can be drawn with regard to the ultimate deployment of Internet-based stock trading by examining users’ perceived evaluations, concerns and their usage patterns. The implications of these ® ndings can be organized along three dimensions.

(1) Users’ evaluations on perceived ease of use, perceived real value added and expectations.

(2) Users’ perceived concerns about security, privacy and content regulation.

(3) Users’ trading behaviour.

## Users’ perceptions

Although past studies have shown the prominence of perceived usefulness over ease of use (Davis, 1989; Adams et al., 1992; Hendrickson et al., 1993) in predicting user acceptance of a new innovation, this study reveals that it is not always true. The importance of ease of use of a system should not be overlooked. When users are exposed to an unfamiliar system, which is supposed to deliver value added services, a system that requires minimum learning effort would de® nitely give users higher motivation for further exploration. On the other hand, if the dif® - culties of use cannot be overcome, users may not have the incentive to explore further even though the system can perform a useful task. This will no doubt affect user satisfaction, which will in turn in¯ uence their perceptions and future usage. In fact, this argument, which is supported empirically in our ® ndings, is consistent with the ® ndings reported by Branscomb and Thomas (1984), Chin et al. (1988), Lu and Gustafson (1994) and Shneiderman (1987).

## Users’ concerns

## Security and privacy issues

The issue of security has always been a major block to the realization of the Web’s recognized potential as a vehicle for commerce (Aldridge et al., 1997), even though the development of various competing and complementary speci® c electronic commerce-enabled applications to ensure secure transactions in cyberspace are already available. The total intrusions into government, business and university computers on the Internet increased by 344% between 1993 and 1995 (Keating, 1996). Based on our ® ndings, users’ concerns about security-related issues are, in fact, one of the key determinants for the widespread adoption of a new system. This makes sense intuitively as it is unlikely for users to accept a system if they have doubts and anxieties when using the system.

The issue of security mainly stems from the fact that information can be \`stolen’ if not protected suf® ciently. An effective approach to reduce credit risk is to stipulate that purchases can only be executed if suf® cient money is available in a client’s account, whereas sales are only permitted when the securities are held by the company (Singham, 1997). To alleviate investor’s fear of fraud, instead of having their bank accounts being accessed for share transactions, what could be possible is to allow investors to keep their funds with the Central Depository Pte (CDP) Ltd on behalf of their broking ® rms. To minimize further the time and risk of settlement, the existing payment system should be enhanced with the implementation of the real time gross settlement (RTGS) system, in which payments are settled the moment they are sent. The Hong Kong Stock Exchange has adopted this robust and ef® cient system since 1996 (Monetary Policy and Markets Department, 1997).

After all, it is necessary to recognize that \`secure’ is a relative term. Even in normal physical business transactions, cheques, cards and cash are regularly used in fraudulent ways. Rather, it is important to accept that there is no perfect system and it is not realistic to seek 100% security on the Internet (Rowley, 1996).

In addition, users’ perceptions of inadequate privacy have been cited as a main hurdle to the success of electronic commerce. The danger of the Internet now is the illusion of anonymity that is completely false (Miller, 1996). In reality, a user’s name, electronic mail address, time of download and access, status of request and other factors are being logged without their knowledge. Based on our ® ndings, we observed that when users were asked about their concerns over privacy, they invariably expressed a high degree of concern. However, when the question was reframed to test their willingness to give detailed demographic information, most of them expressed a much more favourable answer. In other works, users’ main concern is the loss of their personal information without their knowledge. Hence, it is very likely that privacy policies may require treating information capture as an option to be chosen by users in the near future.

## The Internet’ s content regulation

Analysis of the respondent’s perceptions towards Internet-based stock trading reveals that they are concerned with the lack of qualitative content and the rules set by Internet regulators. The implication of such a ® nding reinforces the recommendation of establishing a sound regulatory framework, which must be reviewed periodically and in accordance with the worldwide law system. The implementation of coherent and consistent regulations cannot be overemphasized, in particular in this rapidly changing environment of ® nancial markets. Nonetheless, it is noteworthy that excessive regulations can also be detrimental to the markets. In fact, in a recent report released in the US (The Straits Times, 1997a), a nonregulatory, market-oriented approach is believed to be an ideal way of easing electronic transactions across national borders. With the implementation of a \`freetrade zone’ on the Internet, where taxes and tariffs are kept to a minimum, it is forcasted that electronic commercial transactions will rise from less than US\$1 billion today to US\$70 billion by the year 2000.

For electronic stock trading in particular, to ensure the quality and reliability of information sources further, ® rms registering for Internet trading should fully disclose all information, with the help of more securities. It is often very dif® cult to control and authenticate the information being disseminated on the Internet. In addition, the setting of adequate audit trials on the Internet is important in controlling fraud. Possibly, both electronic and paper con® rmations should be sent as the latter may act as a control to detect unauthorized trading (Singham, 1997).

## Human± Computer interactions and behavioural change

Due to the advances in technological change and rapid commercialization, people are quick to adopt to those changes and this explains why the absence of a human element and the reduced face to face interaction in an Internet-based environment were not cited as major concerns to most users.

## Usage patterns and behaviour

The results also show that changes in users’ trading behaviour, in terms of investment strategies, affect their decisions as to whether to adopt a new system. In hindsight, the importance of their change in behaviour makes sense conceptually. Those respondents whose investment decisions were not affected regardless of whether they used the conventional way or Internet stock trading tended to show a higher level of willingness in adopting an unfamiliar prototype.

Interestingly, the amount of time users spend, their primary usage and the level of sophistication are found to be irrelevant in their eventual adoption of a new system. In the initial exploring stage, which may take up a lot of users’ time, most of them may encounter certain degrees of dif® culty regardless of their purpose of usage and level of expertise. Apparently, we may infer that the duration of use, level of sophistication and primary usage are not critical determinants in predicting user acceptance of a new innovation.

## Limitations and future research

Several aspects of the present study restrict the extent to which our ® ndings can be generalized. Although the respondents represented a random sample, they were chosen from a pool of university undergraduates. They are not completely representative of all users, who may be in a higher age group and computer illiterate. Thus, the generalization of the conclusions to the investing public should be treated with caution.

Moreover, as the data were gathered by a one-time questionnaire survey, the validity of the study may be affected since the adoption and diffusion of technological innovations tend to be time dependent (R.W. Zmud and L.E. Apple, unpublished). By only identifying the static forces that lead to successful information technology deployment, the dynamics of implementation and behavioural changes over time are ignored. This further raises the question of whether a 3-week exposure to a prototype system is adequate enough to permit potential users to acquire stable, well-formed beliefs.

In addition, this study has raised a number of issues that warrant further investigation. In particular, concepts such as \`usefulness’, \`ease of use’ , \`value added’ and \`satisfaction’ can vary across users. In-depth research on these variables and relationships in other settings will enhance our understanding of their generalizability. Although user evaluation measures have been commonly used as a surrogate for information systems success by many researchers, due to the weak theoretical underpinnings the measures have been strongly criticized (Melone, 1990; Treacy, M.E., unpublished). Furthermore, the empirical evidence about their ef® - cacy is mixed and contradictory (Goodhue, 1995).

Nevertheless, the overall study has yielded some invaluable insights concerning the adoption and acceptance of end-user systems in general. In fact, there is a wide variety of ways in which these measures can be applied. For instance, user feedback on different system features or design approaches can be obtained. This would no doubt be very useful for system designers in identifying and evaluating strategies for enhancing user acceptance. This is especially true at the initial design stages of a system development effort where alteration is still ¯ exible. In addition, these measures can also be used by researchers interested in understanding the diffusion of information technology and the determinants of technology adoption.

## Conclusions

An analysis of the results revealed that an application for Web-based stock trading has been feasible and successful. Technically, Stocknet was successfully implemented in a network environment involving more than 150 users. More importantly, the users’ favourable attitudes and their supporting pattern of use suggest that a wider usage of the system is feasible.

The results have also highlighted some interesting ® ndings about users’ readiness and acceptance of online transactions. Invaluable insights were gained with regard to users’ perceptions and evaluation, their usage patterns and preferences, access requirements and hardware capabilities as well as attitudes towards key issues of electronic commerce such as security, privacy and content regulation. Apparently, these are critical determinants of the eventual adoption of electronic stock trading, which will, in turn, affect user satisfaction and future usage.

Electronic commerce is not a myth. Whatever it is, this is only the beginning of things to come. The Web is truly becoming a viable medium for electronic commerce albeit slowly but surely. No doubt electronic discount brokerage is rapidly becoming a fact of life for many individual investors and traders as we march into the information era. To maintain one’s competitive advantage, electronic stock trading appears to be an inevitable action that the relevant authorities cannot avoid undertaking. It is not so much a question of whether the technology is available as whether there is a consensus to have electronic share trading. To foster the acceptability of end-user systems, user evaluation measures should be taken into consideration and their various concerns must be resolved to change their perceptions.
