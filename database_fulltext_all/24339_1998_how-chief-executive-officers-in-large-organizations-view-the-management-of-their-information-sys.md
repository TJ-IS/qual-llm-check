# How chief executive of® cers in large organizations view the management of their information systems

GRAHAM PERVAN

School of Information Systems, Curtin University of Technology, GPO Box U1987, Perth, WA 6845, Australia

As part of a research programme on key information systems (IS) management issues, a survey of Australasia’ s largest organizations was conducted to identify which issues were perceived by their chief executive of® cers (CEOs) as being important, problematic and critical over the next 3± 5 years. The results reported are based on a moderate response rate (though perhaps reasonable for the target group) but formal testing showed an absence of non-response bias. The most critical issues were revealed to be a mix of technology management issues (managing and measuring the effectiveness of the information technology (IT) infrastructure, and disaster recovery), strategic management issues (business process redesign, competitive advantage, and information architecture), people and support management issues (organizational learning, and executive and decision support) and systems development and data management issues (effective use of the data resource and effectiveness of software development). This re¯ ects their interest in a wide range of issues, but all with an organization-wide focus. The non-critical issues were mostly related to the individual technologies and the place and role of IS in the organization While the CEOs showed some agreement with their chief information of® cers (CIOs) on issues such as the IT infrastructure, competitive advantage and organizational learning, some distinct differences exist, particularly in relation to the quality and effectiveness of systems and processes and also how CEOs and CIOs perceive the IT knowledge of the CEOs

## Introduction

In recent years and for the foreseeable future, organizations have been facing rapidly changing business environments which have challenged their executives (both information systems (IS) and non-IS) to handle issues such as downsizing, outsourcing, leveraged buyouts, strategic alliances, ¯ exible manufacturing, just-in-time scheduling, globalization, business process re-engineering and total quality management. These environmental changes have placed demands on their IS departments to support product innovation, new production techniques and changing organizational designs and to provide timely, high-quality information.

Identifying and handling key issues in the management of IS is essential for executives to support and run their organizations ef® ciently and effectively. The investigation of these key issues by researchers serves to enhance the understanding of the concerns of executives and suggest relevant areas of investigation by management researchers. This particular study, which focuses on the views of chief executive of® cers (CEOs) on the key IS management issues, is part of a research programme which aims to achieve the following.

(1) Determine the IS management issues which are most important, problematic and critical to chief information of® cers (CIOs) and CEOs over the next 3± 5 years.

(2) Examine the differences between the views of CIOs and CEOs to understand their problems in relation to IS/IT (information technology) better.

(3) Examine the trends in the status of different issues over time.

(4) Inform industry (IS and non-IS) of current and future issues.

(5) Inform IS researchers of the relevant issues to investigate.

Many key issues studies have now been carried out, usually in single-round surveys or in three-round Delphi studies. These studies have usually developed or proposed lists of 20± 30 IS management issues and sought the respondents’ views on these issues by ranking or rating them. However, virtually all have been concerned with the views of IS executives only (e.g. Brancheau et al., 1996; Pervan, 1997). There have been some key issues studies which have involved data collection from non-IS executives as well as IS executives, but these have reported only aggregated results without speci® cally addressing the views of the respondent CEOs and other non-IS executives (e.g. Caudle et al., 1991; Wang and Turban, 1994). On the other hand, Moynihan (1990) reported the views of 15 CEOs (among some 49 IS and non-IS executives) and concluded that they have similar views to CIOs on organizational issues but not on technical issues. Galliers et al. (1994) found that non-IS executives focus on organization-wide issues and IT quality/effectiveness issues.

While research on the CEO view of the range of IS management issues is sparse, some research has taken place on the position of the CEO on a few speci® c IS management issues. In particular, work has been done on trying to understand better the relationship between the CEO and CIO. The success of this relationship has been variously linked with the closeness (in the organizational structure) between the CIO and CEO (Watson, 1990; Feeny et al., 1992), the participation of the CEO in IT steering committees and other IT activities (Jarvenpaa and Ives, 1991; Raghunathan, 1992) and the CEO’s personal experiences with IT (Feeny et al., 1992). Jones et al. (1995) found that the better the CEO± CIO relationship, the greater the IS in¯ uence on top-level decision making in the organization. While this research does address a few speci® c issues in the management of the organization’s IT, it mostly fails to provide an overview of the CEO’s views and of how they compare with the CIO’s views. This research aims to address the full range of IS management issues that concern the CEO in this context. Further, it may be that the nature and impact of these issues varies for different organizational types, so this study will also explore the structure, management styles, industry, focus of operations and future plans of the organizations in relation to these key issues.

## The key issues

As indicated in Table 1, there may be many different IS management issues. While most researchers accept that organizations must handle many technology management issues, there is a growing acceptance that there are many strategic management issues which are equally if not more important. Other issues can be classi® ed as either systems development and data management issues and people and support management issues. The issues in each category are listed in Table 1 and an explanation of each issue is provided in Appendix 1. These issues have changed over time, but those included in Table 1 are identical to the most recent Australian study of CIOs (Pervan, 1997).

Strategic management issues (which include strategic IS planning, developing and implementing an information architecture, business process redesign, IS organizational alignment and IS for competitive advantage) are organization-wide and relate to planning and facilitating change in relation to IS/IT and may be expected to be the primary focus of CEOs (Galliers et al., 1994; Jones et al., 1995). Conversely, technology management issues (which range from managing and measuring the effectiveness of the overall IT infrastructure to handling individual technologies such as electronic data interchange (EDI), communications, collaborative systems and of® ce automation) may be perceived by the CEO as the responsibility of the CIO. CEOs may hold a similar attitude towards systems development and data management issues such as software development (computer-aided software engi neering (CASE) tools, distributed systems, the applications portfolio and legacy systems. Finally, people and support management issues may only be of concern if they are either organization-wide, such as organizational learning ( Jones et al., 1995) or if they affect the CEO personally, such as executive support systems (Feeny et al., 1992).

## Research design

In order to provide information concerning the key IS management issues, a postal survey of the CEOs of the top 500 Australasian organizations was conducted in February± March 1997. The mailing list used was identical to that used for a similar CIO study in 1996. In the course of the development of this mailing list, some organizations were removed because they were either subsidiary to other (larger) organizations on the list or because the author was aware they had a company policy against participation in any surveys. A ® nal list of 452 CEOs was produced. Each questionnaire was identi® ed by printing the organization’s rank on each page so that the responses were not anonymous (but con® dential of course). This enabled key ® nancial indicators for each responding organization to be extracted from the original source of the Top 500 list (Jacques, 1995) and associated with the key issues ratings.

The aim of this study was to identify the key IS management issues for these organizations over the next 3± 5 years. The approach taken was similar to the most recent Australian CIO study (Pervan, 1997) where the respondents were asked to rate both how important and how problematic each issue was for their organization. A list of 31 issues with an associated paragraph of explanation for each issue was produced (the same list as for the CIO study). In addition to the brief title for each issue, a paragraph explaining the issue (its \`rationale’ ) was included to ensure that the meaning of each issue was interpreted consistently across all respondents. In order to explore the impact of other factors on the key issues, the questionnaire, provided as Appendix 1, also contained a brief set of questions seeking information on the current characteristics of the organization (ownership, business activity, structure and management style) and of future plans (in market share, staf® ng, acquisitions, diversi® - cation and IT outsourcing).

## The results of the study

Initially, valid questionnaires were received from 33 of the 452 organizations. While the response rate of 7.3% is low, it may be considered reasonable for the group targeted (namely the CEOs of Australia’s largest organizations and, hence, very busy people). The danger, however, is in non-response bias, i.e. that those not responding have substantially different views from those who have responded. A recommended strategy for overcoming this is by resampling the non-respondents (Hartman et al., 1985). Such a follow-up survey was conducted and a further 19 responses received.

In order to determine the extent of non-response bias, tests were done on all of the issues and their ranking in the list. Tests of the difference in the mean critical score ratings between the 33 respondents in the ® rst round and the 19 respondents in the second round were carried out and no signi® cant differences were found, even at the 0.1% level of signi® cance. Further, the Spearman’s rank correlation on the critical score rankings of the whole 31 issues for each group was 0.7225, which is signi® cant at the 0.1% level and so also indicates the same views in the second group as the ® rst. Therefore, no non-response bias was apparent and it was considered reasonable to combine the two samples for a total sample size of 52. All of the discussion of the results which follows applies to the complete sample of 52 responses.

The responses were spread across the ® ve major industry sectors, namely resources/mining (7.7%), retail/trading (5.8%), services (15.4%), manufacturing (46.1%) and government (25%). It should be noted that the organizations classi® ed as \`government’ were all revenue-generating organizations in communications, energy, water and insurance. Table 2 shows that these are indeed large organizations with their revenues, assets and pro® ts measured in billions of dollars. Although the sample is relatively small, a wide range of the organizations targeted (from the thirteenth to the four hundred and eighty-® fth ranked organizations) did respond and, given the evidence of a lack of non-response bias, the results are generalizable for large Australian organizations.

The representation of the industry sectors, states and company ® nances (assets, pro® ts and revenues) was similar to the corresponding groups in the CIO study and both samples were representative of these categories (based on $\chi ^ { 2 }$ goodness of ® t tests at the 5% level of signi® cance) in the target population.

The ownership of the majority of these organizations is Australian (64%) and the focus of their business activity is mostly within Australia (only 13% international). The organizational structure was mostly ¯ at (65 versus 35% hierarchical), mostly along divisional/functional lines (79 versus 21% cross-functional), centralization was fairly evenly split between centralized (45%) and decentralized (55%) and the management style varied between \`formal procedures and rules’ (18%), \`few rules, greater autonomy’ (35%) and cooperative and group oriented’ (46%). Most were planning the expansion of their sales/market share (89%), more acquisitions (80%), greater diversi® cation of their products and services (61%) and greater IT outsourcing (70%), while reducing staff numbers in the organization (54%).

## Critical issues

As indicated earlier, the respondents were asked to rate both how important an issue would be for their organization over the next 3± 5 years and how problematic the issue might be. These were both rated on a 1± 10 scale where 1 represented a lowest priority issue and 10 represented a highest priority issue. All of the respondents provided a score for both on all issues. As in the CIO study, how critical an issue was to the organization’s CEO was calculated as the arithmetic mean of the ratings for important and problematic. The views of the respondents were sought on this interpretation and 80% agreed it was reasonable. The ten most critical issues, with their rankings, means and standard deviations of critical rating, are shown in Table 3. A full list showing the means and standard deviations for all 31 issues is provided in Appendix 2.

The particular ranking of individual issues within the top ten should not be overly highlighted because their mean ratings range only by 0.49 on a ten-point scale and the average standard deviation is 1.70. However, it is interesting to note that each of the ® ve issue categories have one or more issues in this top group. While it is claimed that CEOs are not overly concerned with technology management, this is apparently contradicted by the appearance of IT infrastructure, disaster recovery and IS effectiveness/productivity measurement (all technology management issues). However, this should come as no surprise in such a rapidly changing technological environment. It is clear that the provision of a responsive IT infrastructure (ranked second) is a fundamental concern for the orga nization and that the ability of that infrastructure to respond to disaster situations is an organization-wide problem in the 1990s. a closer examination of the data revealed that a responsive IT infrastructure was rated signi® cantly more critical (in a one-way ANOVA at the 5% level of signi® cance) in more decentralized organizations. In these organizations there would be greater pressure on the IT infrastructure to support integration, communication and sharing.

Many organizations would not be able to survive more than a few days without their IT facilities (O’Brien, 1993) so disaster recovery (ranked tenth) might be expected to be a major issue to a CEO taking an organization-wide view. Measuring the effectiveness and productivity of the organization’s IS and IT (ranked equal fourth) is clearly of major concern to the CEOs given the scale of investment in this infrastructure. Clearly, the CEOs are expecting \`value fo money’ and rightly so.

Educational issues also rank highly, with organizational learning ranked eighth and executive and decision support (which involves similar motivations and contains a signi® cant user training/education component) ranked equal fourth. Organizations (and the peo ple in them) require continuous learning about ways to use their information resource better and integrate new technologies into the organization (Niederman et al., 1991). IT education is therefore critical and can lead to increased productivity and reduced applications backlogs (Brancheau and Brown, 1993). Signi® cant increases in innovation and productivity from an organization’s IT investment can be derived from an emphasis on supporting and managing organizational learning in the organization (Henderson and Lentz, 1996). The high ranking of executive and decision support may be interpreted as a \`sel® sh’ concern by CEOs for their own personal support, but these systems are also aimed at supporting other managers and decision makers in organizations, in some organizations EIS even being referred to as \`everybody’ s information system’ (Pervan and Meneely, 1995) and CEOs would see this as key to the effective operation of the organization.

The strategic management issues which ranked highly were business process redesign, IS for competitive advantage and information architecture. Business process redesign (ranked ninth) has been a top issue among CEOs and CIOs for several years (Watson et al., 1997). Many organizations are focusing on a radical redesign of their business processes in order to achieve dramatic improvements in performance and innovative and effective applications IT are seen by many as a vehicle for achieving this (Hammer and Champy, 1993). Further analysis revealed that business process redesign was seen to be signi® cantly more critical (at the 1% level) for organizations planning to concentrate their activities more, perhaps re¯ ecting that some radical redesign of existing processes may be necessary to achieve this concentration effectively. Further, through the innovative use of IT, organizations may ® nd new ways to realize competitive advantage (ranked ® rst). Further analysis revealed that this issue was more critical at the 5% signi® cance level for the revenue-generating government organizations, all of whom are facing an increasingly deregulated and competitive operating environment.

A corporate information architecture (ranked equal fourth) is a high-level map of the information requirements of an organization (Periasamy and Feeny, 1997) which is used to identify key information needs and how this information related to key business processes. Its development is necessary in order to guide the development of these innovative applications and facilitate the integration and sharing of data (Niederman et al., 1991). Further analysis revealed that this issue was signi® cantly more critical (speci® cally, more important but not more problematic) for more decentralized organizations. This indicates that this highlevel information map may be more vital in more decentralized organizations where the information needs may be more varied and sharing and integration are important.

The CEOs also perceived the effective use of the data resources (ranked third) and the effectiveness of software development (ranked seventh) as critical issues. The data resource in most organizations is becoming larger, more complex and greater in cost and value. Matheus et al. (1993) claimed that the data resources are often poorly recognized, dif® cult to access and poorly used. This issue was more signi® cant at the 5% level for those organizations planning greater acquisitions which would require the effective use and integration of data resources to operate in a more complex internal operating environment. Effective IS can provide users with timely, accurate and relevant informa tion, but the backlog in the development of these systems remains as unacceptably high levels in many organizations (Laudon and Laudon, 1994). This is exacerbated by changing hardware platforms and new software development tools arriving on the market, which the developers have to learn and use. The CEOs, understandably, are particularly concerned with maximizing the return from their substantial investment in IT and having it available on time, in budget and with the right form and content.

In summary, the most critical issues were revealed to be a mix of technology management issues, strategic management issues, people and support management issues and systems development and data management issues. This re¯ ects a broad range of concerns for the CEO in relation to IT. However, a closer examination of the issues reveals a major concern for organizationwide issues and optimizing the return from their IT investment.

## Non-critical issues

The issues which were rated the ten least critical are shown in Table 4. Overall, it may be observed that individual technology management issues and systems development and data management issues are of little concern to the CEO. Six of the ten least critical issues (including the four least critical) relate to individual IT (twenty-third collaborative technologies, equal twentyeighth data/document storage, equal twenty-eighth multivendor integration and thirty-® rst CASE technology) and speci® c systems/data management issues (twenty-fourth packaged software, and thirtieth applications portfolio). While these individual systems and technologies are part of the overall infrastructure, they may be seen to be the province of the CIO (as is the recruitment/development of their IS human resources, ranked twenty-seventh), while it is their overall responsiveness, usefulness and stability which is of greater concern to the organization as a whole. Not surpris ingly, this issue was even less critical (at the 5% level) for those organizations intending to outsource more of their IT functions, presumably because they are then reducing internal IT staff numbers. Also seen as the CIO’s responsibility for similar reasons was IT outsourcing (ranked twenty-® fth) as it may be seen as the CIO’s job to determine what is actually provided inhouse or outsourced and the CEO is interested only in the results. (Not surprisingly, further analysis revealed that IT outsourcing was seen to be a signi® cantly more critical issue at the 1% level for those organizations planning more IT outsourcing in the next 3± 5 years.) Finally, the results in Table 4 also reveal that the CEOs show little concern for understanding the IS role and contribution (ranked twenty-sixth) and determining appropriate IS funding levels (ranked twenty-second). These issues may also be seen by the CEO as the CIO’s responsibility.

## Comparison of important and problematic issues

In order to clarify the distinction between what is important and what is problematic to CEOs, the issues which rated, on average, in either the ten most important issues or the ten most problematic issues are summarized in Table 5. This also helps to clarify why some issues are more critical than others (because they are important and problematic). It should be noted that, in general, the CEOs rated issues as rather less problematic than important (with a mean difference in the rating across all issues of 1.12, which is signi® cant at the 0.1% level). This may re¯ ect the role of the CEO in recognizing and monitoring major issues (in this case the IS/IT issues) for the organization while others (in this case the CIOs) have the responsibility for actually solving the problems associated with these issues.

Table 5 indicates that some similarities and differences exist between how problematic and how important an issue is to these CEOs. A rank correlation test of the paired problematic and important rankings for the full list of 31 issues shows a correlation of 0.7032 which is signi® cantly different from zero at the 0.1% level. A number of issues (for example, effective use of the data resources, organizational learning, competitive advantage, IT infrastructure, business process redesign and senior management IT education and support) are seen to be both important and problematic and therefore appear among the issues most critical to the organization (as discussed earlier and referred to in Table 3).

The issues which are ranked as far more important than problematic include information architecture, disaster recovery, IS planning and communications networks. This may be because these issues are perceived as important to the organization (i.e. they can have a signi® cant impact on the organization), but the CIOs believe that in the future they will have them under control. The growing acceptance and quality of strategic IS planning supports the belief that developing an appropriate information architecture (a direct outcome of strategic IS planning), identifying IS for competitive advantage and properly aligning the IS organization will be facilitated more easily (Earl, 1993). The other two issues have signi® cant technology characteristics and the CEOs may believe that appropriate technologies and procedures are in place to handle them. Organizations are now more dependent on their information and the ability to protect that information is obviously important. Communications networks are becoming more reliable and mature as a technology so they may no longer be problematic, but their high importance ranking indicates the signi® cant reliance organizations do and will have on this technology which connects the parts of the organization together (through intranets and local area networks) and with the world at large (through the Internet).

## Comparison of CEOs and CIOs

In another study, the views of the most senior IS executives were sought on these same issues (Pervan, 1997). The ranking of the critical issues as perceived by the CIOs in the ® nal round of that study have been added to the ® nal column of Appendix 1. The Spearman’s rank correlation calculated on the paired (CEO and CIO) rankings was 0.6577 which is significant at the 0.1% level. This con® rms that there is substantial overall agreement between the views of the CIOs and CEOs on the key IS issues in these large organizations. That there is a fairly strong consensus on the key issues is a positive sign and may indicate the fairly advanced stage of IT growth in these large organizations and sound lines of communications between the CIOs and CEOs in these organizations. However, there are some distinct differences in the CEO and CIO rankings on a few issues and these are summarized in Table 6 below.

The ® rst ® ve issues in Table 6 are those much more critical to CEOs than to CIOs. All of these issues can be interpreted to have a focus on ensuring that the organization’s IS and IT delivers on its promises. Organization-wide and personal support and effectiveness of the data and systems are the common themes in these issues. While it is clearly appropriate for the CEOs to be concerned with these issues, it is perhaps a little alarming that the CIOs do not show quite the same concern for providing the services whose delivery is their responsibility. The IS departments and their managers in these large Australian organizations may need to be more focused on service and on perceiving the non-IS people in the organization as \`customers’ rather than \`users’ (Pitt et al., 1995).

The last ® ve issues in Table 6 are those more critical to CIOs than to CEOs. It is quite reasonable that communications networks, the key technology underpinning the IT infrastructure now and in the future, should be ranked higher by CIOs as it is their speci® c function to ensure that this technology works effectively. It is also reasonable that issues such as IS human resources, IS organizational alignment and an increasing understanding of the IS role and contribution should all be ranked higher by the CIOs as they are essentially their responsibility. However, it is interesting (perhaps even amusing) to observe that CIOs see a greater need for the IT education of senior management than do the senior managers themselves! This difference of view about the senior management’s IT knowledge may re¯ ect the long-held perception by IS professionals that a poorly educated (in relation to IT) senior management is a continuing obstacle to IT growth. Interestingly, the same difference of opinion on senior management IT education occurred in the most recent UK study (Galliers et al., 1994). On the other hand, this difference of opinion may often be observed between any specialist manager and the CEO. The managers of production, marketing, ® nance and human resources, for example, may all claim that the CEO needs to understand their area better, whereas the CEO may see his or her role as that of a generalist. Nevertheless, there is inherent danger in this apparent con¯ ict of views on the senior management’s need for greater IT education which may need to be addressed with better communication.

## Conclusions, limitations and further research

This paper has presented some results from a study of the key issues facing chief executives in Australia’s largest organizations. The most critical issues are a mix of technology management issues (IT infrastructure, measuring IS effectiveness and disaster recovery), strategic management issues (business process redesign, competitive advantage and responsive information architecture), people and support management issues (organizational learning, and executive and decision support systems) and systems development and data management issues (effective use of the data resources and effectiveness of software development). This range of IS management issues is of major concern to CEOs. However, it is quite clear that CEOs are greatly concerned with \`the bottom line’ in relation to their IT infrastructure with effectiveness in relation to the data resources, software development and the measurement of the IS function generally. CEOs want a sound return on their IT investment and it is the role of the IT professionals to ensure that they are effective and can be shown to be so (with appropriate measurement).

The issues perceived by the CEOs as non-critical were mostly related to the management of speci® c systems and several individual technologies which must be integrated and managed to ensure a responsive IT infrastructure, as well as a number of clearly CIO roles. IT outsourcing was ranked surprisingly low) considering its apparent popularity), but it did rate as a more critical issue for those organizations actually planning to implement IT outsourcing in the next few years. These same organizations considered the recruitmen and development of their IS human resources as less critical, as the outsourcing of IT would lead to fewe internal IT staff with a narrower range of functions.

The most critical issues in the study were generally perceived to be both important and problematic. These included effective use of the data resources, organizational learning, competitive advantage, IT infrastructure, business process redesign and senior management IT education and support. The issues perceived as more important than problematic related to information planning and the management of the organiza tion’s information and communications infrastructure. The CEOs apparently see that, though these issues have a major impact on the organization, they are reaching a stage where they have the technology, policies and procedures to keep them under control. On the other hand, the issues which are seen to be not so well-managed (and so more problematic than important) were mostly associated with the effectiveness and productivity to the data, systems and infrastructure.

On a positive note, the CIOs and CEOs were in broad agreement on the majority of the issues, though the CEOs had, as would be expected, a more organization-wide focus and were clearly more concerned about receiving a good return from their substantial IT investment. On the other hand, for CIOs, some of the more critical issues were directly concerned with their speci® c role in managing the organization’ s IS/IT. These differences are as would be expected according to the roles ® lled by CEOs and CIOs. However, the CIOs perceived the senior management’s IT education as a more critical issue than the CEOs themselves and this may imply a lack of communication or at least of understanding of each other.

This survey (like all surveys) has some limitations, however. The target group was limited to large organizations in Australia and New Zealand, so the results may not apply to CEOs of small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). However, the target group may be expected to be fairly homogeneous and the analysis showed a consistency of views on most issues across industries, structure, size and management style. The response rate of 11.5% was quite low, but perhaps expected for CEOs of large organizations (who are very busy people) and statistical tests showed the sample was fairly representative and was absent of non-response bias.

Nevertheless, the results were interesting and the comparisons between the CEOs and CIOs of some value. It is hoped that by providing both target groups with the results of both surveys they will see where their differences of opinion are and this may lead to better communication between them so that these differences might diminish over time. Further analysis of both surveys is being undertaken and comparisons are being followed up with recent similar studies in other countries. It is hoped that this and other follow-up studies will serve to assist CEOs, CIOs and IS researchers in identifying areas of interest in the management of IS and IT.

1. Developing and implementing an information architecture

A corporate/global information architecture is needed to identify the major information categories used within an enterprise and their relationships to business processes. It is essential for guiding applications development and facilitating the integration and sharing of data.

2. Improving information security and control

As organizations increase their dependence on IS, there is a greater risk from destruction and alteration of data, disclosure to outside sources and disruption of information services. Tight security controls and fault tolerant information delivery are becoming a necessity.

3. Facilitating organizational learning

Organizations that prosper will need to make appropriate use of IT across their entire enterprise. Business practices and organizational structures will need to be modi® ed in many cases. IS also must demonstrate its own ability to learn and use new technology.

4. Recruiting, and developing IS human resources

Current and future shortages of quali® ed IS personnel threaten the organization’s ability to make effective use of IT. Career paths need to be clari® ed. More emphasis needs to be put on developing business skills such as teamwork and leadership and staying current with new technology such as object-oriented and multimedia applications.

5. Measuring IS effectiveness and productivity

Understanding how IT use impacts on the bottom line is crucial for justifying new investment. In addition, measuring the IS organization’s performance is necessary for effective management. Measurement is becoming more important as companies attempt to reduce operating expenses to meet the competition.

6. Improving data integrity and quality assurance

Mainframe applications, time-sharing extracts and personal computer applications are all used to analyse data and prepare reports. Frequently there are discrepancies among these different data sources due to lack of controls in IS and user departments. Too often computer output is assumed to be accurate. Such assumptions can lead to faulty business decisions.

7. Improving the effectiveness of software development

The application development backlog remains at unacceptably high levels. Traditional development methods and platforms are no longer satisfactory while new methods and platforms have not yet proven themselves. Sophisticated users are getting impatient. Improved effectiveness will be essential for next-generation applica tions.

8. Facilitating/managing executive and decision support systems

Increasing the ability to exploit situations for competitive advantage depends on enhancing the ability of management to \`experiment’ with decision possibilities. Many other issues also depend on this capability. Decision support tools have long been viewed as a method for introducing modelling tools to executives to improve their decision making, however those efforts have met with mixed success.

9. Selecting and integrating packaged application software

The changing relationship between costs of hardware, packaged software and human resources makes managing this aspect of the business critical. Using purchased application software can cut costs, boost productivity and increase user satisfaction, but it can result in poor integration of systems and serious maintenance problems.

10. Aligning the IS organization within the enterprise

The IS organization’s effectiveness in supporting the enterprise’s needs is dependent on its organizational location within the enterprise. Appropriate alignment may require a combination of centralized and decentralized structures. Too often IS is not located and structured appropriately.

11. Improving IS strategic planning

It has always been important to align long-range IS plans with strategic business plants. Rapidly changing business environments, increased involvement of end-users and accelerated technological change underscore the need to continue improving strategic planning skills.

12. Making effective use of the data resource

The organization’ s data resource is growing in size, complexity and value. Despite this, it remains largely unrecognized, inaccessible and underutilized. IS must develop a climate within its department and throughout the organization which values the data resources as a corporate asset.

13. Developing and managing electronic data interchange

Electronic communication with customers and suppliers may offer competitive advantage to a company or it may be a requirement for staying in business. IS executives must develop (or adapt to) standard transaction formats, keep current on technology developments and learn to manage interorganizational projects.

14. Outsourcing selected information services

The internal information systems organization no longer has a monopoly. Outside contractors may be able to provide some services more effectively. What services should be outsourced? How should contractor relationships be managed? Fair and objective evaluation techniques are needed which assess both costs and bene® ts as well as potential risks from loss of control.

15. Planning and integrating multivendor open systems technologies

Many companies are moving away from single-vendor proprietary operating environments to vendor-neutral environments based on industry and de facto standards. Due to large investments in legacy systems, carefully planned migration paths are critical. The task is complicated by a still-maturing technology and unstable standards.

16. Determining appropriate IS funding levels

There is no generally accepted way of establishing the level of IS funding relative to the other funding need of the organization. This can put both IS and general managers at a disadvantage.

17. Facilitating and managing business process redesign

To remain competitive, many organizations are radically changing the way they do business. IT plays an increasingly important role in this change process by enabling the innovative redesign of core business processes. Much has been learned about IT implementation in general which can help facilitate and manage business process redesign projects.

18. Increasing the understanding of the IS role and contribution

IS is often viewed as an operational activity with little recognition of its strategic contribution to the organization. 19. Integrating data processing, of® ce automation and telecommunications

Information stored in both electronic and hard copy form is accumulating at a staggering pace. There is a need to provide for future data and document storage requirements.

21. Planning and managing communications networks

Communication is the lifeblood of the organization. Using IS for competitive advantage depends heavily on access to appropriate internal and external communication networks. This task is complicated by rapid advances in underlying technology and major structural changes in the communications industry.

22. Managing the existing portfolio of legacy applications

Most organizations have a large investment in their existing applications portfolio. Some \`legacy’ applications may need to be retired quickly. 23. Using IS for competitive advantage

In many businesses, long-term survival is dependent upon using IS to gain competitive advantage. Competitive advantage results from the recognition of opportunities through creativity and innovation, followed by rapid implementation. These are historical weaknesses of IS.

24. Developing and managing distributed systems

Client± server applications promise to offer a cost-effective alternative to centralized applications. 26. Facilitating and managing end-user computing

The proliferation of end-user computing through personal computers offers the promise of improved produc tivity but also the dangers of inadequate management control. IS management must balance control against the need for slack. Clari® cation of IS and end-user roles is a necessity.

27. Implementing and managing collaborative support systems

New software is needed to support the re-engineered, ¯ at, team-based organization of the future. 28. Educating senior management in relation to IT

The education of senior managers in an organization will lead to more enlightened resource allocation for IS, a more strategic view in IS planning and an understanding of IS’s role in the organization.

29. Planning and using CASE technology

Signi® cant progress has been made automating business functions within organizations; however, a vast productivity gain is possible if the automation process itself is automated. In principle, software systems can provide support for integrating the design efforts of project teams, for standardizing representation methods and fo generating code. While this technology is still being re® ned, providing support for systems development is extraordinarily complex and will require major changes within the IS function.

30. Building a responsive IT infrastructure

Building a technology infrastructure that will support existing applications while remaining responsive to change is a key to long-term enterprise productivity. This task is frustrated by the continuing rapid changes in infra structure technology and the increasing breadth and depth of applications which need to be supported.

31. Establishing effective disaster recovery capabilities

Down-side risks are increasing daily from the potential loss of business due to a disaster. Effective recovery plans must be in place and tested regularly to ensure losses are minimized. As organizational applications grow and become more integrated, the greater the risk becomes.
