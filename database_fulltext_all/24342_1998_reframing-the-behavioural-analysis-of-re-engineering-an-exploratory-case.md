# Reframing the behavioural analysis of re-engineering: an exploratory case

DANNY R. ZAIDIFARD

Strategy Competency Group, Andersen Consulting, UK

This paper examines the application of the concepts and research derived from studies of dysfunctional and addictive social behaviours to the implementation of change in an organizational arena. The majority of work on change management and, more speci® cally, re-engineering has been dominated by an emphasis on technical issues and macro-constructs that are important components in achieving successful process-change programmes. However, is it increasingly recognized that the emphasis on these is the cause of widespread disappointment with business process re-engineering. This paper argues that the management of the human and sociopolitical issues of change is a critical factor in determining the success or failure of processre-engineering projects. It further suggests a methodology that may facilitate examination of these issues within any particular organization, based upon an organizational addictive model, which is developed in this paper.

## Introduction

Promises of miracle productivity increases gained business process re-engineering (BPR) the reputation as the world’s most fashionable fad, with widespread adoption by ® rms in the early 1990s (Heygate, 1993; Champy, 1995). However, the successes of \`the new wonder drug of Western managers’ (Earl and Khan, 1994) have remained largely theoretical, with wide spread disappointment in industry (see, for example, the 1993 North American survey conducted by Moad, 1993). The extent of this disappointment is re¯ ected in estimates that approximately 70% of BPR efforts fail (Grint, 1995). Other reports show most reengineering projects consistently falling short of dramatic or even expected bene® ts (Bartram, 1992; Harvey, 1995; Willcocks, 1996).

Recent research papers have suggested that the fundamental cause of this mixed performance record is that the majority of research on managing change processes has focused on the technical (e.g. information technology (IT)) and functional issues (e.g. corporate vision), without giving due attention to understanding the parallel change processes within and involving individuals. Willcocks and Mason (1987) commented that \`the human and organisational, including political and cultural issues in change are frequently underplayed, even neglected, in practice. . . . All too often budgetary and time constraints, together with a concern for technical issues lead to a focus away from the softer, behavioural factors that are in fact often strong determinants of longterm success or failure’. Tinaikar et al. (1995) upheld these assertions. Their research showed that 95.9% of all the articles referring to BPR focus on technical issues. The impact of the human agent seems to have taken a back seat, with only a handful of articles discussing the social issues of BPR. Hence, despite a rapid transformation in business activity, management is still bound by technocracy which has \`led to a mechanisation of processes and a trivialisation of the human agency’ (Tinaikar et al., 1995, p. 110).

A more balanced approach to BPR must be taken, which takes account of the social and political perspectives in order to \`gain the commitment of key people (Smith and Willcocks, 1995). Tinaikar et al. (1995) applied a social constructionist view to BPR. Their analysis concluded that \`While most of BPR thinking seems to focus on the technical core of the process, it ignores that the process lies in a certain social context which varies across and within organisations. The humans interacting with the process make sense of it on the basis of this context and construct ± so to speak ± an outer shell of meanings round the inner technical core of the process. And it is this constructed process through which the organisational members appropriate symbolic meaning’ (Tinaikar et al., 1995, p. 113). The basic unit of consideration is that of human attitudes and preconceptions.

We can therefore conclude that it is at the individua level as well as the organizational level that BPR must proliferate for success. Individual roles and attitudes about the re-engineering process are critical to the outcome of change. Oram and Wellins (1995) claimed that it is the failure to understand the formation and modi® cation of attitudes during BPR that accounts for the failure of the majority of BPR projects. Recent empirical ® ndings (Willcocks, 1996) further emphasize the dominance of human attitudes in affecting the success of BPR projects.

This paper views BPR as the result of the social interactions between individuals and social groups (e.g. workers and management) and focuses on the attitudinal and behavioural issues that determine success or failure. Until now, little work has been conducted that aims to understand why it is so hard to change and manage people’s attitudes during re-engineering projects. By approaching this problem from a novel point of reference, this paper hopes to reap helpful and applicable insights.

The aim is to develop a model derived through a study of addictive family behaviours that may explain behaviours within organizations. Within a dysfunctional family unit, the members respond to certain circumstances with a given set of reactions, which are the basis of an \`addictive system’ , which is self-perpetuating. In the organizational context, as in the family environment, these dependencies are often process or situation based. Thus, in certain circumstances individuals or even entire departments can behave and react in ways that are similar to the addictive behavioural patterns of dysfunctional families. Thus, the development of a model based on the internal dynamics of a dysfunctional family system may be used to pinpoint some of the sociohuman aspects that emerge strongly as major barriers to the success of BPR projects.

## Research methodology

The research was conducted in the course of process change initiatives implemented in a leading UK-based information provider. An ethnographic study was undertaken to document the \`predictable patterns of human behaviour’ and to understand and describe the social and cultural scene from the \`emic’ (or insiders) perspective. The emic view describes the insider’s perspective of reality. This perspective compels the recognition and acceptance of multiple realities. The nature of this type of study insists upon the triangulation and cross-checking of acquired data in order to pinpoint these realities. Where data is being collated on an individual’s perceptions and reactions to others, and to change, the use of only one or two research tools can leave the researcher with a skewed misrepresentation of the data. To gain a holistic perspective a number of research tools were adopted. These include desk research, interviews, questionnaires and participant observation.

The ® rst step was to survey and extrapolate from diverse literature sources to create a framework for research within the organizational context. The juxtaposition of process and substance addictive theories within families to an organizational context needed extrapolations of the research studies and academic literature on both sides. The application to a speci® c company necessitated an understanding of its internal dynamics and functioning. This was achieved through the examination of organizational charts, departmenta hierarchies, project management procedures and decision-making processes.

Telephone interviews were conducted with counsellors with expertise in addictive behaviours and dysfunctional families to gain an insight into addictive dynamics. The resulting research framework was created in the form of an \`organizational addictive model’ (OAM). This is discussed later.

The ® eld research consisted of a selection of interview, questionnaire and observation techniques. The interviews focused on perceptions of corporate and divisional behavioural patterns and were conducted with stakeholders from different levels in the organization. Semi-structured but informal interviews of between 60 and 90 min in length were conducted with personnel managers, technical directors and business managers. Product development project managers were interviewed using ¯ exible, indepth sessions of similar duration. Finally, a sample of product developers were interviewed on a casual basis. The triangulation of data created a cross-referenced information base that would ensure the accuracy of the analysis. Regular participant observation throughout provided a valuable view of events as they occurred during the change process.

The analysis of the research data was centred on identifying patterns. These were derived from interviews, which were cross-checked with other evidence for the recurrence of the same pattern. To aid in this process, the comments made in the interviews were tabulated using the following headings: self (referring to own personality, staff (attitudes towards other staff ), work type (preferenced in the type of work and people one works with), work environment (perceptions of the workplace and corporate structure, changes (views pertaining to the recent changes), attitudes (attitudes towards the changes) and aspirations (personal aspi rations of those interviewed).

The amassed data were applied to the organizational addictive model, resulting in the recognition of dysfunctional corporate relationships.

## Model development 1

The development of the organizational addictive model begins with a juxtaposition of ideas from various sources. The purposes of the following analysis is to show how the concept of \`addiction’ may be justi® - ably applied when examining organizational change processes.

## The outdated map and organizational defences

Peck (1983) likened our individual perceptions to reality to \`a map with which to negotiate the terrain of life’. The maps may be described as a set of ways of perceiving and responding to the world. To go from A to B we follow a certain route on our map, and, thus, we undertake a set of actions or reactions that will get us there. However, the world is a \`dynamic reality’; thus, to be accurate, our maps have to be continually revised to account for this changing reality. Revision making is a painful process and, as with most painful procedures, is avoided. We ignore the new information that is essential for accuracy, attempting to manipulate the world so as to make it conform to our view of reality. Rather than change the map, an individual may try to destroy the new reality.

This concept is mirrored in the work of Argyris (1990) on organizational defences. He suggested that human beings develop \`Theories-in-use’ (TIUs) which are \`the master programmes that people hold in order to be in control . . . especially in the face of hostility or embarrassment’ (Argyris, 1990). These TIU are the melting-pot for \`designed errors’. Such errors are easily described by examining working professionals. A professional’s actions are skilful in that they are spontaneous and automatic. These actions are born out of their TIUs and can be counter-productive due to the very fact that they are usually spontaneous and rapid. Skilful actions are taken for granted, which causes unawareness which, in turn, is often a prerequisite to error. The errors due to skilled and unaware incompetence are designed in an individual’s outdated \`map’ . At this point we may consider how \`addictive behaviours’ are related to the above discussion.

## Addiction ± its application to the organizational context

In all the references to addiction and addictive processes in this paper there is a heavy reliance on the work of Schaef (1987) concentrated on addictive behaviours within society as a whole and speci® cally families. However, as will be illustrated, this has close parallels with the organizational and work environment. De® nitions for some critical terms are found below (these are by no means comprehensive or technical de® nitions).

(1) Addiction. An addiction is any process over which were are powerless. It takes control of us, causing us to do and think things that are inconsistent with out personal values leading us to become progressively more compulsive and obsessive. They can be divided into two major categories: substance addictions (heroin and alcohol) and process addictions (gambling, work and worry). Both function in essentially the same way.

(2) Addictive system. This is a system that calls forth addictive behaviours. The individual begins to operate out of an addictive process. An addictive system is a closed system in that it presents few choices to individuals in terms of the roles they may take and the directions they may pursue.

(3) Co-dependence. \`An addiction to another person or persons and their problems, or to a relationship and its problems; (Wegscheider Cruse and Lerner, 1984). Dependent persons rely on others to meet their emotional, psychological, intellectual and spiritual needs. Codependents not only have relationships with addicts, they exhibit many of the same characteristics as addicts.

According to Schaef (1987, p. 18), once we are an addict, \`we stop relying on our knowledge and our senses and start relying on our confused perceptions to tell us what we know and sense’. This is a self-reinforcing process, which allows us to remain addicted. If we are living in an addictive system, we begin to make assumptions that justify actively supporting our addictions to the extent that we distort reality to defend our views (see Peck, 1983).

The application of this to the errors designed through skilled incompetence is that professionals, like addicts, often stop relying on their knowledge and senses and start relying on their perceptions. Furthermore, just as the addict does not question his/her assumptions out of fear of recognizing his/her fallacy, these errors are often never challenged due to defensive reasoning (Argyris, 1990) and organizational inertia. Defensive reasoning occurs when inaccurate premises and their respective inferences are followed because their validity is not questioned. This leads to unjusti® able conclusions which persons feel that they can convincingly defend. New ideas that may challenge skilled incompetence are blocked by \`idea-killers’ that take the guise of comment such as \`it is not policy’ or \`we have always done it that way’ (Hornstein, 1986). Holding these premises leads to behaviours that bypass and cover up the causes. Schaef (1987) called them the \`characteristics of the addictive system’ and a few of them are dishonesty, confusion, forgetfulness, defensiveness and fear.

It is important to understand that these characteristics primarily apply to the \`addictive system’ (the addict and the co-dependents) not just to the \`addict’. Schaef (1987) commented in her work with alcoholics that rehabilitation counsellors often recognize certain behavioural patterns linked with the addiction in other family members. Therefore, in recent years the treatment of addictions has moved towards recognizing that it is not enough to treat the addict, but the emphasis of the treatment must be directed at the whole family system, which is an addictive system. The direct application to the work environment is that co-dependency is not a symptom uniquely found in addictive family systems, but, as Whit® eld (1993) stated, it \`affects not only individuals, but families, communities, business and other institutions, and states and countries’.

This section may be summarized by the following comment by Argyris (1990): \`Management and workers in many organizations create a world that is contrary to what they say they prefer and contrary to the managerial stewardship they espouse. It is as if they are compulsively tied to a set of processes that prevent them from changing what they believe they should change. If this is true, then both management and their staff could be in a very fundamental sense not in control’ . . . they simply may be part of an addictive system of behaviours.

## Model development 2 ± the addictive system model

Having shown that the behaviours apparent in addictive family units have strong similarities with organizational units, we may further investigate the comparison by developing a pair of parallel models, the ® rst model. The family addictive model (FAM) (Figure 1), focuses on the dysfunctional family unit, conveying the concept that not only the addict but the entire family unit forms an addictive system which displays addictive behaviours. Once the processes and dynamics governing this model have been discussed in depth, a mirror image of this model which is applicable to organizations, the organizational addictive model (OAM) (Figure 2), will be introduced. The relevance to the OAM of the processes and dynamics described will then be illustrated by using a case study approach.

## The dysfunctional FAM

From the outset it must be made clear that the FAM is by no means a comprehensive model for the dynamics evident in a dysfunctional, addictive family. Its purpose is simply to convey the main features that are apparent.

(3) Enabling and co-dependent behaviours ± triangles ± the dynamics of the addictive system. A basic understanding of the dynamics of the addictive system may be described through the concept of triangles. It will rely on the work of Whit® eld (1993) to explain this concept. A triangle is an unhealthy relationship between three people. Whenever the pain of a two-person relationship becomes unbearable, one or both of them may bring in and involve a third person to help relieve their pain (Figure 3). This forms a triangle as shown in Figure 3. Stage 1 shows a calm relationship in which neither person is suf-® ciently uncomfortable to triangle in a third person, place or thing. Stage 2 illustrates the occurrence of con¯ ict. The more uncomfortable person, A, triangles a third person, C. The result of this, depicted in stage 3, is that a substantial amount of the con¯ ict has been transferred mostly out of the original twosome and into the relationship between B and C. The concept of triangles is old: \`three is a crowd’ and they exist in all human relationships. Care must be taken not to assume that all three-way interactions are unhealthy triangles. A threesome may well exist with three healthy two-way relationships, allowing a ¯ exible, healthy interaction (Figure 4). The purpose of a triangle is to stabilize the two-person system when it is in danger of disintegrating. The third person or object allows the other two to avoid facing the threatening issues between them. Each triangle member may take on the enabling roles previously described. Using alternative terminology we may label the members the victim, the persecutor and the rescuer. Within an addictive family unit, the victim± persecutor dysfunction often takes the form of a hostage captor relationship. Hostages do not want to be with their captors, but they remain with them out of fear. Just as hostages may identify with their captors and take on some of their behaviours, so too does a co-dependent mirror some of the behaviours of the addict. This is a survival ploy, as the hostage does not actually want to be in this relationship. It is important to note that a triangle is not simply a closed system with ® xed dynamics, but the dynamics may be reciprocal, i.e. the roles assumed in the hostage± captor relationship may shift back and forth, but the dynamic remains the same. Due to the behavioural similarities displayed, there may appear to be a super® cial closeness amongst the members in the triangle, but in actual fact there is only \`fusion’. This means that the boundaries between the members of the triangle are unclear. This helps to maintain the triangle, as any one member ® nds it hard to distance themselves from the triangle as they are uncertain of their own boundaries. When there are more than three members in an addictive system, \`interlocking triangles’ can arise. These occur when the con¯ ict of one triangle over¯ ows into one or more other triangles. This is shown in Figure 5. It is interesting to note that with three people, only one triangle is possible. However, with four people there are four potential triangles and the scope for dysfunction is far greater.

(4) The addictive system. The addict, the family members and the enabling patterns constitute the addictive system. Any system has its contents, roles and processes. We tend to assume that if we change the contents, everything else will fall into place, i.e. if we give people a new set of facts, then their behaviour will change accordingly. However, processes are far more dif® cult to change and are far more powerful than contents. Thus, for example, the content of a message may change radically, but the behavioural process behind it may remain the same. The processes are the secret strengths of the system; they are what perpetuate it. A discussion of the principal processes apparent in an addictive system will follow (Schaef, 1987; Bradshaw, 1988).

(i) The process of promise (myth-making). People within this system look expectantly towards the future and not recognizing, owning and dealing with the present. The promise of the system is that things are going to get better as long as we conform to the system. An example of this is that battered wives often stay with their abusive husbands because they honestly believe that they will not do it again. Thus, the focus on the future keeps everyone in the system static, allowing the system to perpetuate.

(ii) The process of absorption/pseudopodic ego. The addictive system at some point may need a system that does not possess the controlling and deceitful aspects that are inherent to it. It needs the positive aspects of the \`healing’ system to perpetuate itself by giving false hope to its members. If this is the case, the addictive system can reach out and totally absorb the external system, to the extent that the other system becomes indistinguishable from the addictive system. It simply claims the foreign system as its own. This is similar to a co-dependent enabling an addict to perpetuate their addiction. The co-dependent may have positive intentions, but the addictive system uses them to perpetuate itself.

(iii) The process of the external referent. This is a process of self-de® nition. It requires people within the system to develop their concept of self through external referents. People learn to judge themselves by how other people judge them and they respond to situations according to what is expected of them.

(iv) The process of invalidation. The addictive system de® nes itself as reality, thus everything else i unreal. The process of invalidating that which the system does not know, understand and thereby cannot control means that large areas of perception and knowledge are lost. A primary function of any addiction is to make ourselves and our own processes invisible to ourselves and others.

(v) The process of trashing/fabricating \`personality con¯ icts’ . Trashing is a process that is used against individuals to call their credibility into question. It aims to render people powerless and destroys their in¯ uence within a group. Projection (a process whereby individuals accuse others of certain traits and feelings they themselves possess) is often a factor that is evident in trashing. For example, people who accuse others of being power hungry are often power hungry themselves. The fabrication of personality con¯ icts has a similar effect to that of trashing. By an individual claiming that someone has a personality con¯ ict with them, they suggest that any comments made by the other person are no longer objective, but are unfounded and totally subjective. Thus, the system will discredit the messenger in order to dismiss the message.

(vi) The process of dualism. The process of dualistic thinking is basic to the addictive system. People are conditioned to think that this or that is right. Hence, they oversimplify the complex environment in which they are. By breaking down a multifaceted problem into two dimensions, individuals can accentuate the illusion that they are in control. This process prevents the addictive system from generating and considering alternatives; therefore individuals are often forced to make choices between two equally unacceptable alternatives. The individual then remains \`stuck’ in the system and an illusion of stability perpetuates.

Bradshaw (1988, p. 82) commented that a dysfunctional family \`has either con¯ ict or con¯ uence, but never contact’ and it is by these processes that contact is avoided.

Having described the FAM in detail, it is essential to recognize the holographic or fractal-like nature of the addictive system. In very simple terms, a fractal is a pattern with the property that it may be fully replicated by focusing on a small section of the original pattern. Thus, by reducing addiction system 1 into the centre of the model in place of the \`addict’ , the mode replicates itself with the other family units assuming the enabling roles. The application of this approach, therefore, is relevant at any level of grouping.

## Reframing the behavioural analysis of re-engineering

## Model application and analysis

The application of the processes and dynamics described previously to an organizational context will now be explored via a case scenario approach. This case scenario will be based upon an actual case study that was conducted whilst examining the potential outcomes of change initiatives undertaken in a wellknown company. However, for the purpose of this paper, the study has been generalized into a familiar scenario that is often found in product development businesses. Thus, the reader may relate the situations described to experiences he or she has had.

Let us consider a company where the corporate structure consists of the board of directors, the product development division (PDD) and the business division (BD), which is shown in Figure 6. This is an oversimpli® ed example of the structure of many companies engaged in product development. However, it serves to convey clearly a birds-eye view of the corporate dynamics under consideration.

We will begin with the following scenario, which is present, in varying degrees, in a large number of ® rms. The following analysis will explore how, based on these initial conditions, a scenario may progress which illustrates how the processes of the addictive system may manifest themselves in an organizational environment.

(1) The board of directors is dominated by individuals with backgrounds in either sales, marketing or accounting. There is a weak presence of technically oriented directors.

(2) There is, therefore, little promotion of and commitment to product development at the board level.

(3) All customer interaction is with the BD ± with little or no contact with the PDD.

A lack of commitment to product development from senior management is the initial cause of dysfunction (Figure 7). Couple this with the predominantly nontechnical backgrounds of the majority of the board members and con¯ ict has arisen between the board and the PDD. Management attention is drawn to other areas, leading to minimal support, interest and recognition of development. This dysfunction has severed the communication channels between these two members of the triangle and there is no direct path between the PDD and the board. Thus, as in a family, when there is a breakdown in communication between two parties, a third member ± the BD ± is triangled in order to form an alternative line of communication.

The perpetuating dysfunction existing between the PDD and the board forces them to triangle in the BD into an unhealthy triangle (Figure 8). Communication between the two now involves the BD as an intermediary. Development is forced to promote its views via the BD, hoping that they will permeate up. Although central to the company, the PDD is now peripheral to the decision-making processes and their contribution is therefore restricted. The board views the BD as the family favourite and is an absentee parent to the PDD. This view is conveyed by a developer who comment that \`In order to be successful in this company you must have a strong leaning towards the business division or be from it. Ideally, you should try to get out of product development.’ It is interesting to note that in many ® rms the PDD is still seen as a cost centre, but the BD is viewed as the revenue generator. This distinction exempli® es the differences in the respective roles played.

The fact that the board concentrates the majority of its dealings with the BD has noticeable effects on the corporate structure and the interdivisional relationships. As in stage 3 in the formation of a triangle (Figure 3), the original con¯ ict is mostly transferred away from the initial dysfunction into another between the PDD and the BD, i.e. the uneven attention shown to the divisions has caused divisional barriers (Figure 9). Interdivisional communication may be further hampered if, as in this case, a vertical and functional rather than a process-oriented organizational structure exists. As in a family, communication often takes on the dynamics of \`parent and child’. Just as a parent does not tell a child more than he or she needs to know, the business professionals do not tell the developers any more than they need to know, holding back information that would be useful to the more business-oriented developer.

The consequence of being unable to communicate directly with both the board and with customers is that the PDD has no process by which to validate themselves internally. Thus, it is forced into taking the BD as its external referent. It must rely on the BD for a direction, purpose and success criterion. Recognition of success as a division is now dependent on the perceptions the BD has towards its work. The coercion of the PDD into accepting this dynamic may be described by the following comment made by a product development manager: \`In the past we never got anywhere by pointing out problems, so now we think that it is a waste of breath to say anything. We do not bother anymore and we still agree to things that we do not believe in.’ The result of the above dynamics is that, on the one hand, the PDD is made the scapegoat/victim and, on the other hand, it feels like it must compensate for the incompetence of the other two members of the triangle.

At this stage we may liken the PDD to a hostage as it does not want to be in its current position, but it remains due to three factors. The ® rst of these is fear. The PDD fears that the company will collapse if it decides not to compensate for the corporate inadequacies. The second factor is that of shame. By losing its ability to validate its existence internally, coupled with its \`separate’ image, the PDD feels somewhat shameful of its role as scapegoat. Shame leads to attempts to control one’s environment, which results in a sense of responsibility for the welfare of the company. This behaviour matches co-dependent behaviour in that it simply allows the relationship to perpetuate.

The scenario described above has a tendency to be self-reinforcing. The reader may question why things do not change within the PDD and it is here that we may examine how the processes of the addictive system are clearly manifest.

The status quo is not disturbed due to the process of promise. The \`carrying’ behaviour of the PDD may be equally driven by a hope for improvement in the future rather than being driven by a need for control. At a corporate level, the reasons for a lack of change are due to two distinct dynamics within the company, these being \`power politics’ and interdivisional attitudes. The triangle is more or less rigid due to the concentration of power within the BD and its backing from the board. This rigidity is accentuated by the resulting interdivisional attitudes that have resulted. The following view was aired by a developer whilst research was under way: \`it is due to the attitudes of each department of the other that stops things changing. Developers are often seen to be \`zogs’ wearing sandals and having beards, who like to \`play’ . Business professionals are not involved or interested in product development, assuming that the technicians job is easy.’ This comment implies the presence of the processes of invalidation, trashing/fabricating personality con¯ icts and dualism.

The result of the BD not being involved in the PDD and, therefore, not understanding product development, is that it invalidates the role that it plays by assuming that it is easy. The BD trashes the PDD by labelling the product development staff as \`zogs’ . The PDD trashes the BD by saying that they are not involved with development, hence justifying their belief that everything that the BD say and do is ¯ awed. The process of dualism causes people to view things in extremes ± black or white, this or that. This is clearly manifest in the them and us attitude that is so prevalent in the interdivisional relationship. To summarize the effect of the them and us attitude, reference is made to a comment made by Bradshaw (1988, p. 82) \`a dysfunctional family has either con¯ ict or con¯ uence, but never contact’.

## Implications

Having developed the case study by introducing a fourth player and subsequently investigating a number of possible scenarios, how possible dysfunctional relationships may be averted is brie¯ y discussed. The points made here are a few of the actual recommendations made to the company on which the case study is based.

The initial and central key to the restoration of the overall dysfunctional condition is that the board must be visibly committed to the PDD. The board members must be made to \`own’ development projects and to be easily accessible to product development managers. A recovery in this relationship will also facilitate the recovery of the rift between the BD and PDD.

The PDD must establish its own identity, i.e. it must validate itself internally and have an internal referent. The PDD must break its dependence on the BD for its \`concept of self’. This may be achieved by the following.

(1) A more active role in liaising with customers. This will allow developers to see the direct application of their work restoring the internal referent.

(2) A shift from being seen as a cost centre to being a pro® t centre. This will promote the process of self-validation. This can be further enhanced if the PDD conducts development work for other companies. This will turn the PD into an independent revenue generator.

(3) A direct involvement in corporate decisionmaking processes and, through the empowerment of product development managers, the necessary authority to implement decisions.

The new product development director must be seen to be committed to the PDD. This is essential at two levels. Firstly, to combat the activity of the process of absorption in disabling the positive aspects of change. Secondly, for any re-engineering project to succeed, employees must feel that the re-engineer is approachable and is concerned about them. This is achieved through various ways, for example an open-door policy and participating in divisional social events.

## Discussion

The relevance of addictive behavioural patterns to the understanding of organizational dynamics was demonstrated in the previous section. They deliver both an insightful diagnosis of existing problems and plausible predictions of how situations may develop and perpetuate. The reader, however, may feel uncomfortable with this approach due to the introduction of terminology and mindsets, which are as yet unfamiliar to management theory. Nevertheless, the strength of this methodology relies on its natural intuitiveness.

## Reframing the behavioural analysis of re-engineering

By virtue of living in a family and social environment that displays the roles, dynamics and processes of the addictive system, we have all grown up with an intuitive recognition of them either by unconscious participation or, if fortunate, through active understanding of them. These family interactions are sublimated patterns that have perpetuated themselves within other social interactions and environments. Therefore, it is no surprise that these patterns are evident in a commercial/organizational environment. An understanding of these processes would therefore provide a natural and powerful management tool.

## Conclusion

When the effects of working within a dysfunctional addictive system become too detrimental to ignore, people tend to \`reorganize’ in an attempt to rectify the malaise. Thus, managers are constantly trying to reorganize corporate structures, processes and resources in order to eradicate unwanted patterns. However, where this pattern arises from and demonstrates dysfunctional addictive behaviour, recovery is not achieved through reorganization unless the underlying cause and processes that sustain these patterns are speci® cally addressed. Reorganizing will simply accentuate confusion.

The solution must take the form of \`rehabilitation’ , which will enhance the likely success and ef® cacy of an ensuing re-engineering project. The ® ndings of addictive behaviour therapy could be of substantial bene® t to existing methodologies of change management.
