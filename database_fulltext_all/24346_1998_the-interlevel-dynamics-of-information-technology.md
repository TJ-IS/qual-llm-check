Journal of Information Technology (1998) 13, 139± 149

# The interlevel dynamics of information technology

DAVID COGHLAN

University of Dublin, School of Business Studies, Trinity College, Dublin 2, Republic of Ireland

Levels of analysis are common frameworks for describing organizational dynamics, both in the general organizational and the information technology (IT)± organizational literature. Levels of analysis ± individual, group, intergroup and organizational ± are typically presented as constructs under which particular organizational dynamics can be grouped and analysed. Rarely is there any emphasis placed on how events on one level can affect the dynamics of other levels. This article introduces Rashford and Coghlan’s framework of organizational levels which describes levels as systemically interrelated and interdependent and, through examples, argues that the IT± organizational literature needs to adopt a more dynamic construct of levels of analysis to account for how IT affects individuals, teams, interteam coordination and organizations concurrently.

## Introduction

Consideration of the role of information technology (IT) in organizations is very complex. There is a considerable literature in both respective areas and on the interface of one with another. When the literature interfacing IT with organizational dynamics is examined, it is noticeable how static the frameworks and approaches appear to be. It is as if IT and organizations were viewed as a closed system. On the other hand, organizations operate as vibrant sociotechnical systems where events are both cause and effect in a recursive manner. This article addresses some central issues in this regard. First, some observations from the contemporary organizational± IT literature are noted. Then some wellestablished organizational frameworks regarding organizations as systems are revisited. It is pointed out how levels of analysis are typically presented as static headings. The Rashford and Coghlan (1994) framework of organizational levels which views levels as systemically interrelated and interdependent is introduced. The impact and role of IT in organizations is juxtaposed with Rashford and Coghlan’s (1994) use of levels with a view to contributing to a more dynamic model of the role of IT in organizations.

## Organizations and IT

Orlikowski et al. (1996) pointed to the ubiquity of IT in today’s organizations and the transformation of work and concluded that there are many relationships which elude easy characterization and categorization. Markus and Robey (1988) noted the shortage of well-grounded theory on how to address the social and organizational aspects and issues relevant to IT. In their view, the existing literature on IT and organizational change does not support reliable generalizations. Crowston and Malone (1994) argued that any study of the effects of IT on organizations must be grounded in a theory of organizations, technology and the linkage between the two.

The systems approach to understanding organizations is well-established. Two systemic approaches to understanding are critical to viewing the interrelationship between the construct of organizational levels and IT. Organizations are open systems, i.e. a set of interrelated elements ± people and technology, which transform various inputs into desired outputs and which are in¯ uenced by their external environment (Katz and Kahn, 1978; Rousseau, 1979; Hanna, 1987). A second understanding of organizations as systems is the \`recursive’ systems model which represents organizations as patterns of feedback loops ± sequences of interaction which link and integrate the elements of a system (Senge, 1990; Balle, 1994; McCaughan and Palmer, 1994). The sociotechnical approach to organizations as systems contains both approaches by attributing equal importance to both the social and technical elements of work organization (Weisbord, 1987; Trist and Murray, 1993). Accordingly, an understanding of organizations as vibrant systems must account for the equally important elements of people and technology.

The systemic nature of organizations accounts not only for the formal organization ± goals, policies, structure, technology, etc. ± but also for the informal organization ± attitudes, relationships, feelings, politics, culture, etc. Schein (1992b) described organizationa culture as patterns of basic assumptions which have been passed on through generations of organizational members and which are unnoticed and taken for granted. While there is a growing awareness of the importance of understanding an organization’s culture, it cannot be assumed that organizations have only one culture. For example, Schein (1996) posited that issues in organizations can be traced to the differences in subcultures which exist in chief executives, engineers and operators. Similarly, he re¯ ected that managers and IT specialists are different subcultures with different assumptions and a different language (Schein, 1992b).

A common framework for reviewing organizational dynamics is through levels of analysis. Levels of analysis refer to the micro-level social units with organizations and to the macro-level organization as a unit (Pfeffer, 1982). Levels of analysis have also been applied to the impact of IT on organizations. In their review of the IT organizational research, Rousseau (1979) and Fry (1982) cited three levels: individual, subunit and organizational. Turner (1983) utilized individuals, pairs, small groups and large groups. Swanson (1987) suggested individual, organization and the market. Ang and Pavri (1994) grouped their review into three levels: societal, organizational and individual. While Venkatraman (1991) did not refer explicitly to levels of analysis, he did explicate an interorganizational phase which has emerged through interorganizational networks and electronic linkages between organizations.

Levels of analysis are typically presented as constructs under which particular organizational dynamics can be grouped and analysed. Rarely is there any emphasis placed on how events on one level affect the dynamics of other levels, that is what happens if the team affects the individual and vice versa and so on through the organization. In the literature on the impacts of IT on organizations there is an equal absence of discussion on the dynamic interrelationship and interdependence of one level with another. Accordingly, there is a need to have a more dynamic construct of levels which re¯ ects the complexity of behavioural systems in organizations.

The management of people in organizations requires that the dynamic relationships between an individual and the organization, the effective functioning of teams, the coordination of interfunctional departments and the competitive strategy of the organization be viewed as a behavioural system. Therefore, the management of organizations must be such that it deals with not only the individual’s relationship with the organization, but also with how teams function, how interdepartmental work and relations are coordinated and how the organization competes in its external environment. A signi® cant approach to understanding the behavioural dynamics of an organization in systemic sociotechnical terms is through the construct of levels of organizational behaviour. While the notion of levels tends to refer to position in an organization’s hierarchy (supervisor, manager, senior manager, etc.) it also describes increasing levels of complexity ± individual, team, interdepartmental group, organization, industry sector, etc. These are levels of complexity because an industry sector is made up of organizations, which in turn are made up of multiple departments and functions, which themselves comprise teams which are made up of individuals.

If levels are dynamically interrelated and interdependent then it is useful to see how IT operates in such a dynamic system.

## Rashford and Coghlan’s (1994) four levels of organizational behaviour

Rashford and Coghlan (1994) presented levels in terms of how people participate in organizations and linked them to provide a useful tool for managers, consultants and teachers of organizational behaviour (Table 1). Their framework describes four levels of participation or involvement in organizations. These levels can be viewed as degrees of participation or as degrees of complexity, depending on whether one approaches the question from the point of the view of the individua moving towards the organization to participate or the part of the organization viewing the commitment of individuals.

In the Rashford and Coghlan (1994) framework, the least complex approach, from the point of view of the individual, is the bonding relationship that the individual has with the organization. This involves a utilization of membership and participation in the organization in order to meet personal life goals (level I). The more complex approach to participation exists in establishing effective working relationships in a face to face team (level II). An even more complex involvement exists in terms of the interdepartmental group or divisional type of interface where teams must be coordinated in order to achieve a complex involvement (level III). Finally, the most complex is the relationship of the total organization with its external environment in which other organizations are individual competitors, competing for scarce resources to produce similar products or services.

The study of the relationship between IT and life in organizations bene® ts greatly from the construct of organizational levels (Table 2). Technology is perceived to be a key to economic survival as it provides a competitive necessity to business organizations (level IV) by providing better and faster information across complex functions (level III), enabling the rationalization of work and the better functioning of teams (level II) and by the transformation of work practices and a different use of employee skills (level I).

## The impact of IT on the individual level

The individual level describes the reciprocal relationship between an individual and an organization, in which both attempt to meet their own needs through an appropriate bonding contract. From the individual’s point of view this means a relationship with the organization whereby some core elements of personal life goals are met. Management’s perceptive at the same point, however, is that individuals somehow belong to the organization in an appropriate psychological contract. When the tasks at this level are reasonably and adequately met, individuals can allow the organization and its goals to be a source of personal goal motivation. Individuals will still retain their own individuality while \`belonging’ to the organization. In contrast, it is the function of management to motivate each individual in the organization in the hope of enhancing growth and effectiveness. Therefore, management’s ideal goal is to create a matching process in which people are able and encouraged to become involved and ® nd that the work situation develops them as human beings while the organization bene® ts from such an involvement.

Developments in IT have meant an essential change in the nature of work itself. There are two points of view, one being that IT means that there are less jobs and a degrading of skills and the other that IT provides the opportunity for new jobs and an expansion of employment (Osterman, 1991). In Osterman’s (1991) view it is dif® cult to disentangle the content and design of jobs from their social context, i.e. what an organization is doing when its management restructures and realigns its workforce because of more advanced IT. While the impact of technology affects the organization’s competitive position in its environment it must also be integrated into the organization’s HRM system of job classi® cation and accompanying training and development.

Zuboff (1988) mapped the change in work in terms of the replacement of physical, experienced-based work (deskilling) with new forms of mastery through intellectual work (reskilling).

As IT is applied to the production process, what effect will it have on the grounds of knowledge? What will it take \`to know’? What kind of knowledge will enable competent participation in the processes associated with making things? Given that computer-based automation continues to displace the human body (deskilling) yet the informating power of technology creates pressure for a profound reskilling (p. 56).

Such a change has altered a change in the process of knowing and the nature of knowledge. Hitherto, employees’ expertise developed through an actionlearning process of (1) sensing information, whereby one could pick up physical cues, (2) making sense of them and drawing inferences based on experience and (3) acting on those inferences. In the same vein, managers operate from the same model as they sense what is going on from being physically present and then act on their inferences. IT demands that knowledge be integrally linked to faith in the machine. A change in such fundamentals as the nature of knowledge and the process of knowing creates a crisis of identity for those in the workplace. Zuboff (1988) reported that the transition from physical to intellectual work in which the bases for learning and knowledge are radically altered created a crisis of identity for the workers and the need to develop a new sense of self.

IT may also involve a change in the role of middle managers (Pinsonneault and Kraemer, 1993). In this regard, Schein (1992a) noted that resistance to the introduction of executive workstations was founded on managers’ view of their role as one in which they worked primarily with people, engaged in face to face contact and relied on their gut and intuition to process information. Accordingly, they viewed IT as not being relevant to their role as executives.

If IT changes the nature of work, not only in terms of what is done and how, but also in terms of changing the fundamental process of learning and knowing, then its impact on the bonding process between the individual members of the organization and the organization needs to be considered. IT involves tighter integration across functions and more interdependence between activities, more rapid speed and real time response and more costly consequences of errors and breakdowns. As traditional organizational boundaries are broken down, there are consequences for the de® nition of organizational roles and for the formulation of career paths for the individual. The management of organizations, therefore, must understand the nature of the impact of IT on the bonding relationship and consciously work at resetting the psychological contract in the light of the changed nature of work. Clearly this is a complex task and beyond the scope of this article. However, it can be noted that management’s utilization of consultation, performance review, career interviews and other motivational approaches can enable the individual to adap to changing situations of job and role.

## The impact of IT on the face to face team level

The task issues for the face to face team are setting and achieving objectives and allocating the work to the members, while at the same time managing the relationa issues of how the members work together and the team process of managing meetings, solving problems, making decisions, managing con¯ ict, etc. (Rashford and Coghlan, 1994). Level II dynamics occur not only in formal organizational teams but also in networks, temporary teams, task forces, committees and informal groups.

\`Groupware’ is a term used to describe both an approach to business teams driven by the global, competitive, technologically-enabled environment and the creation and usages of the electronic tools for team communication and the interpersonal team processes of creating trust, building the team, solving problems and making decisions (Johansen et al., 1991; Ciborra, 1996). In Johansen et al.’ s (1991) view, the electronic tools in themselves are not suf® cient for effective team work. A team leader must also understand the dynamics of working groups, be sensitive to team process and be able to manage people.

A critical element in the management of people and teams is how management exercises its leadership and control functions (Tannenbaum and Schmidt, 1958; Schein, 1989). Zuboff (1988) indicated that because the informing function of IT makes information transparent and available and because the technology demands individual intellectual skills, the role of the supervisor is called into question. No longer are supervisors and managers separated from the workforce by virtue of a distinction between blue and white collars. No longer has the manager or supervisor access to selected information. Accordingly, in Zuboff’s (1988) view, IT has major implications for the de® nition of the role of the supervisor and for the role of power in organizations. Schein (1992a,b) reported that the instantaneous availability of information across an organization has, in many instances, led to over-control and a re-emergence of McGregor’s Theory X assumptions in a new form, as managers contact subordinates to question what they see happening on their screens at the same time as the subordinates see the same data. Such experiences have led to subordinates either renegotiating appropriate time delays with their superiors or ® nding ways to disguise the information and subvert the technology process.

Zuboff (1988) posed some key questions about the impact of technology on organizational life. Will technology be used for increased surveillance, remote management techniques, automated management and management trying to impose certainty and control? Will intelligence be stored at the expense of human capacity to critical judgement, creating a docile, dependent, cynical workforce? Will it mean new forms of mastery and the opportunity to imbue jobs with more comprehensive meaning?

This technological transformation engenders a new approach to organizational behaviour, one in which relationships are more intricate, collaborative and bound by mutual responsibilities of colleagues. As the new technology integrates information across time and space, managers and workers each overcome their narrow, functional perspectives and create new roles that are better suited to enhancing value-added activities in a data-rich environment (Zuboff, 1988, p. 6).

In summary, IT has major implications for the working of teams and the realignment of how a team process works in a technological context. It also has major implications for the exercise of supervisory and managerial functions, both in terms of what the roles mean in a technical context and how supervisors and managers behave, given the availability and accessibility of information.

## The impact of IT on the interdepartmental group level

This third level can be made up of any number of face to face working teams which must function together to accomplish an interdepartmental or divisional purpose, such as manufacturing, sales or marketing. This interdepartmental group level needs to have critical information which passes beyond the boundaries of individual teams in order to implement programmes and projects at a range beyond their direct contact. In large organizations where complexity, size and distance dissolve immediate personal relationships, it is imperative that this third level functions well. From management’s standpoint, the team’ s task within the overall group is to perform effectively in its own right, while at the same time have a clearly de® ned commitment to the interdepartmental group. When this third level is working effectively, people in the interdepartmental group are capable of obtaining information and converting it into decision processes, enabling the implementation of complex programmes or operations. The task at this level is to map the ¯ ow of information and partially completed work from one team to another. The management needs are that these teams form an integrated whole. The key tasks at this third level are the division of organizational functions and resources and the structure of the information system to support the differentiation. So how these functions are compatible, how the work ¯ ows from one function to another, the quality and timeliness of information exchange and the process and outcomes of collective bargaining are critical. The process of performing a complex function and making an appropriate distribution of scarce resources, such as personnel and money, are the key ventures of the interdepartmental group level. It is a highly political situation in which the inbuilt structural con¯ icts of multiple-interest parties need to be resolved. As a group, this diversi® ed mass of differing functions and interests must negotiate an outcome that adequately re¯ ects the balance of power and a distribution of resources among competing coalitions. The essential elements in level III dynamics centre on issues of power and on how power is exercised in the allocation of resources and the accessibility of information.

From management’s view the technical issues at this level require an ability to locate dysfunctions. Dysfunctions occur in the ¯ ow of transferring information and partially completed work or services from one team to another. The entire process must be viewed, understood and successfully handled in order to produce the product or service. Because of a huge number of individuals and teams engaged in particular activities in a large organization, this process is often dif® cult to see. The interdepartmental group’s process then must focus on becoming a functioning work unit to build on successes and learn from mistakes. Dif® culties also arise at this level due to the lack of re¯ ective and corrective skills. The discovery of negative information is dif® cult because it is often hidden in the interfaces between one team and another. In recent years, business process re-engineering has been utilized as a process solution to level III dysfunction.

IT’s impact on an organization is typically focused on the tasks at this level. Technology must meet the organization’s requirements for more accessible, usable information, thus reducing the cost of coordination. Madnick (1991) illustrated how IT provides opportunities for extensive communication networks and accessible distributed databases for intraorganizationa coordination, such as e-mail, EDI and electronic conferencing. At the same time, the consequences of the availability and usage of information through IT affects the structures of the organization (Gerstein, 1987; Schein, 1989; Venkatraman, 1991; Galbraith, 1994) and the communication patterns, both forma and informal (Zuboff, 1988; Carroll, 1990; Galbraith,

1994) and challenges the culture of the organization, particularly the relationship between subcultures (Schein, 1992b, 1994).

The formal structure of an organization typically de® nes the coordinating relationships across hierarchies, functional areas and power systems with regard to decision making, information ¯ ows and resource allocation. With regard to organizational structure, IT breaks down the traditional divisions of organizational boundaries by connection functions and units which would have been separated formerly, for instance bridging sales, accounts, credit and distribution (Gerstein, 1987). A structure of ¯ atter organizations, with the capability for more lateral communication, is perceived as essential in today’ s competitive environment (Galbraith, 1994). IT transforms the traditional centralization± decentralization debate from a debate about opposites to a consideration of mutual interdependence (Blom® eld and Coombs, 1992).

Electronic communication enables fundamental changes in organizational forms, which in turn generate the requisites for communication system redesign (Fulk and Desanctis, 1995). The traditional forms of organization which emphasize vertical control are challenged by the new forms of coupling which are enabled by electronic work¯ ow, concurrent engineering, stockless production and so on, which enables horizontal coordination.

Intergroup dynamics can be signi® cant in the operation of the informal organization. Schein (1992b) posited that the senior management of an organization and the IT community can be viewed as two subcultures, each making assumptions about the nature of information, the nature of people, the learning process, organizations and management.

An examination of those assumptions strongly suggests that they may be very different and those differences account to a large degree for the problems of implementing IT solutions. I am suggesting that the most important step in improving the utilisation of IT is to recognise at the outset that the IT community is making certain assumptions that must be nonevaluatively examined in relation to the assumptions of management and of the eventual users of the technology . . . To the extent that these assumptions differ and possibly con¯ ict with each other, intercultural processes must be invented and designed to open up better communication between these two subgroups (Schein, 1992b, pp. 293± 4).

In summary, while IT’s impact is generally perceived as in¯ uencing the tasks and issues at the interdepartmental group level through a more effective coordination of information availability and usability, there are considerable feedback loops connecting the impact of the technology with the organizational design and structure, the nature of communication and the interrelationships between the subcultures of the technology people and the organization’s management.

## The impact of IT on the organizational leve

The fourth level of organizational behaviour describes the organizational goals, policy and strategy level, which constitutes how an organization exists in a competitive environment by exchanging products or services in order to obtain much sought after resources. Consequently, an organization needs to be capable of re¯ ecting on its own strengths and weaknesses, as well as engaging in proactive relationships to determine and deal with the opportunities and threats from the external environment. The assessment of strengths, weak nesses, opportunities and threats result in a selection process which establishes programmes, services and products. These procedures aim at accomplishing the goals of the organization and adapting to external envi ronmental demands. IT aims at helping an organization be more competitive by being more ef® cient in (1) its operations, particularly in cost minimization and processing information and (2) providing a quality of service to customers and clients (Galbraith, 1994).

In the volatile environments of globalization, worldwide competition and deregulation, the business strategy of an organization and IT need to be strategically aligned. Venkatraman (1991) identi® ed this alignment in terms of the business strategy, the organization’s infrastructure and processes, its IT strategy and IS infrastructure and its process so that the business process and the business network are redesigned to exploit IT’s capabilities and rede® ne the scope of the business. IT creates new capabilities for conducting business with suppliers and customers ± JIT and EDI.

Schein (1994) re¯ ected on the relationship between an organization’s culture and its ability to adapt and innovate in its external environment. Typically technology is market driven, yet it is culture which determines how technology will be used and how it will in¯ uence the structure and processes of the organization. Culture is created around the organization’s core technologies which relate to what the organization is about in the ® rst place. Schein (1994) posed the question of what kind of cultural assumptions must be pre sent to facilitate the organization’s processes, which will increase the organization’s ability to learn, to adapt and to innovate and argued for conscious efforts to be devoted to specifying the characteristics of the culture which favour the kind of IT, organizational processes, both formal and informal and organizational structure, which increase the likelihood of innovation taking place.

## Towards a ® fth level ± the networked organization

Rashford and Coghlan (1994) articulated four levels of organizational behaviour ± the individual, the face to face team, the interdepartmental group and the organizational. These four levels are not intended to be comprehensive or exclusive; rather they re¯ ect working constructs which are useful in diagnosing organizational issues and act as the basis for intervention. In recent years, the concept of an industry sector has become more prevalent as like organizations form peer associations in order to have a political voice and exchange on mutual common concerns. Interorganizational relations are build around relations with supplier and customer organizations. Accordingly, it seems useful to identify and highlight the development of a ® fth level which has a particular application to the impact of IT on interorganizational relations.

The networked organization is a consequence of the advances in IT in creating organizational interdependence (Chisholm, 1998). The issues of quality, service, cost and time act as driving forces for organizations to enter into forms of strategic alliances and cooperation with their suppliers and customers through extensive communication networks, accessible databases and enhanced human interface workstations (Madnick, 1991; Rockhart and Short, 1991; Rotemberg and Saloner, 1991; Venkatraman, 1991).

In summary, each of the four organizational levels is impacted upon by developments in IT. The critical issue at each level is how the utilization of IT helps achieve the outcome of each level ± bonding for the individual, functioning teams for the team, coordination for the interdepartmental group, adaptation for the organization and organizational interdependence for the interorganizational.

## Interlevel dynamics and IT

As organizations can be viewed as recursive systems there is an essential interlevel dynamic in that each of the four levels has a dynamic relationship with each of the others. This relationship is grounded in systems dynamics, whereby the relationship each of the four levels has with the other three is systemic, with feedback loops forming a complex pattern of relationships (Senge, 1990; Balle, 1994; McCaughan and Palmer, 1994). Dysfunctions at any of the four levels can cause dysfunction at any of the other three levels. An individual’s inability to manage the technology can be expressed in dysfunctional behaviour in the team and affect a team’s ability to function effectively, which in turn affects the individual’s ability to cope and ultimately the bonding relationship with the organization. If a team is not functioning effectively, it can limit the interdepartmental group’s effectiveness which may depend on the quality and timeliness of information, resources and partially completed work from that team. If the interdepartmental group’s multiple activities are not coordinated, the organization’s ability to compete effectively may be affected. In systemic terms, each of the four levels affects each of the other three (Figure 1).

Interlevel dynamics are critical to the processes of organizational learning and change, as change not only has an impact on the organization, the interdepartmental group, teams and individuals, but also involves a complex movement through the organization as individuals and teams respond to the change agenda and apply it across the interdepartmental group (Coghlan, 1994, 1995, 1997; Rashford and Coghlan, 1994).

It is essential that the role of IT in organizations be viewed not only in terms of its impacts on each of the levels but also as an interlevel process. As Ang and Pavri (1994) pointed out \`There is often a blurring of distinctions between levels of analysis as studies on one level intrude on the boundaries of the other levels. This is unavoidable as IT impinges on the gamut of human issues’ (p. 22). Accordingly, there is need for a most systemic view of the role of IT in organizations, a systemic view which works from a levels perspective which conceives of levels not as static headings under which particular impacts can be grouped, but as dynamically interrelated and interdependent.

## Case examples of interlevel dynamics and IT

Orlikowski reported on two cases which illustrate the interlevel process. In one case the introduction and implementation of Notes involved a complex iteration of individual frames of reference with group frames, as individual workers, managers, administrators, consultants and technologists interacted and negotiated the value and use of the new technology (Orlikowski and Gash, 1994). In the second case, the introduction of Notes technology, both intentionally and unintentionally with planned and unplanned outcomes, changed the work of individuals (level I) and created more proactive collaboration in the team (level II) and interdependent coordination mechanisms (level III) (Orlikowski, 1996).

To take a case example from practice. An IT consultant was invited to an organization to advise its senior management on its selection of an updated IT system. He initially met the managing director and then in turn interviewed the managers of the manufacturing, marketing and ® nance functions, respectively, each of whom were members of the senior management team. He also met the IT director, who was not a member of the senior management team. From these interviews he formed the following picture. The senior management team was an intensely political arena; some of the members disliked other members intensely. They were unanimous in their distrust of the managing director. They did not share information readily with one another and, in fact, individual managers had little idea of what was going on in functions other than their own. None of them had much idea of what they required of a new IT system. Each commented that they left IT matters to the IT director. The IT director felt very frustrated. He experienced that while IT issues were being left to him, he could not get enough information or policy direction from the senior managers on which to make recommendations. It subsequently emerged that the capability of the previous IT system, which was now to be replaced, was largely unrealized. The expectation was that this would happen again with the new system and a signi® cant capital investment would not reap its potential bene® ts.

To anyone who re¯ ects on this case it is clear that the IT agenda for this organization is secondary to the organizational issues. In other words, any effort to resolve the technological issue of choosing a new IT system may well be doomed to failure unless some key organizational issues are resolved ® rst. These issues can be named through the levels’ framework. There are level IV issues ± the sense of a need to update the IT system to enhance competitiveness. There are level III issues ± the relationships between the business functions themselves, between the business functions and the IT department and the lack of informed knowledge of the role of IT in the business. There are level II issues, particularly the inability of the senior management team to work as a team and the absence of a working relationship between some of the senior managers and the managing director. There are level I issues ± the dissatisfaction of several of the senior managers and the demotivation to confront the issues and attempt to solve the problems. It is also clear from the case that each of these issues affected each of the others. The individual dissatisfaction fed into the lack of teamwork and the lack of teamwork affected the lack of functional coordination. The lack of functional coordination meant that corporate decisions about IT investment were hampered, which in turn reinforced the lack of teamwork and individual dissatisfaction. Each event was both a cause and effect of other events. In effect this was a recursive system, involving both organizational and organization± IT dynamics.

For the consultant, the critical issue was at level II. If the senior management team could engage in a team process to enable team formation, then the cycles of negative effects at the other levels could be reduced and a suf® cient base for an analysis of IT needs and requirements be built so that a new system could be assessed, selected and purchased.

This case illustrates the dynamic interrelationship between the four levels and between organizationa dynamics and IT. The interlevel organizational dynamics could be viewed on all four levels with the team level (level II) the critical one. The organizational± IT issue was at level III ± the lack of policy as to the role of IT in and between each of the functional areas. If the organizational level II issues could be advanced, then there would be a forum for the organizational± IT level III issues to be confronted and resolved.

## Interlevel dynamics, IT and organizational change

Information technology’s many impacts on the business ± its capability to alter competition radically, its impacts on organization boundaries and structures, and its potential to modify the way work is performed, the skills required of people, the role of supervision and the basic pattern of cultural assumptions that establishes \`what it feels like to work around here’ ± makes clear the need to carefully manage change (Gerstein, 1987, p. 30).

The process of large system change is an adaptive coping cycle as the information driving change is taken into the organization, processed and turned into actual changes in production, service or managerial activities and exported into the market-place (Schein, 1980, 1997). Such a sequence involves individuals and teams reacting to the change and the realignment of the change at the interdepartmental group level in order that an entire organization changes (Coghlan, 1994, 1995, 1997; Rashford and Coghlan, 1994).

## The interlevel dynamics of IT

The key to organizational change with regard to the introduction or development of IT is alignment, where the capabilities of the organization and the requirements of IT are aligned with the requirements of the organization and the design of IT (McKersie and Walton, 1991). Peppard (1997) presented a framework with three perspectives: a technological imperative in which IT acts as an inducer of change, an organizational imperative where it acts as an enabler of change and an emergent perspective where IT acts as a catalyst. McKersie and Walton (1991) described three routes whereby new technology acts as a catalyst for organizational change. The ® rst route is one where organizational change has an IT dimension and the conditions for technological change already exist. The second route describes the situation in which the organizational change is followed by IT implementation and IT is pulled into place. The third is where organizational change and IT occur simultaneously. In their view, what is critical is that a vision of what the changed organization, both the organizational and IT systems, will look like be articulated and the change process mapped accordingly.

The process of planned change involves the analysis of the present state in the light of the articulated desired future state to determine the work to be done, the creation of a change plan and the active development of commitment in the organization to the move to the desired future state (Beckhard and Harris, 1987). The implementation of a change plan and the development of commitment requires the top management to explain the purpose of the change, paint the picture of where the organization is going, articulate the plan of how to get there and create a part for each to play in achieving the desired results (Bridges, 1991). Key roles are played by middle management, the users of IT and unions. Helpful mechanisms, such as task forces, project teams, stakeholder meetings, training, etc. are utilized. Such processes involve core interlevel dynamics as individuals react to the change agenda, bring that reaction to teams and groups and as, teams and groups, interact with other teams and groups across the organization. Individual organizational members’ responses to the IT change agenda and their subsequent participation in team and group meetings, their negotiation of outcomes across the interdepartmental group and, ultimately, their participation in the technology as active users depends not only on a successful alignment of IT and the organization but on the successful management of the change process across the four levels.

The management of change in a sociotechnical context requires not only adjusting technology but facilitating learning and meeting resistance, which occurs in individuals, in teams, between teams and across functions (Coghlan, 1997). This involves moving beyond a view of organizational levels as static units of analysis to a dynamic construct of levels as systemically interrelated and interdependent.

Gerstein (1987) supported a levels approach in placing great emphasis on the need to manage change and stressed the following points in this regard.

(1) Strategic thinking is the key to the successful utilization of IT.

(2) The chief executive of® cer as chief strategic architect must understand the strategic nature and potential of IT and speci® cally manage its evolution.

(3) A number of uses of IT entail major reorganization at the level of the work group, the department and the entire organization.

(4) The application of IT that alters the ® rm’s core technology and therefore is closely linked to culture may be ® ercely resisted.

He concluded by noting that the management of the IT function has become increasingly dif® cult as a result of these considerations. Accordingly, in terms of the Rashford and Coghlan (1994) framework, sociotechnical change in complex systems requires attention to each of the four organizational levels and to the dynamic interrelationship between them.

## Concluding remarks

In the introduction to the MIT study of the impact of IT on the corporation of the 1990s, Scott-Morton (1991) summarized the ® ndings of the study.

(1) IT is enabling fundamental changes in the way work is done ± production work, coordination work and managerial work.

(2) IT is enabling the integration of business functions at all levels in and between organizations.

(3) IT is causing shifts in the competitive climate of many industries.

(4) IT presents new strategic opportunities for organizations to reassess their mission and operations.

(5) Successful applications of IT will require changes in the management of organizational structure ± enabling lower cost production and the disintegration of traditional forms or organization.

(6) The major challenge for managers in the 1990s will be to lead their organizations through the transformation necessary to prosper in globally competitive environments.

In this article, some of the applications and impacts of IT on organizational life, as outlined by Scott-Morton (1991), have been reviewed through the construct of the four organizational levels. One particular framework of four organizational levels, that of Rashford and Coghlan (1994), was presented and developed in order to provide a construct in which the interrelationship between individuals and an organization, the dynamics of the working teams of which they are members, the coordinating function of interteam relationships and an organization’s strategic endeavours can be viewed in a systemic framework. Such a focus on interlevel dynamics is essential in order to understand and manage organizations.

IT affects the individual and the individual’s work experience and relationship to the organization. It affects how teams work and the nature of the managerial role. It affects how information is available across functional boundaries and therefore transforms the structure of organizations, both formal and informal. The systemic nature of organizations demands that the impact of IT not only be considered at each of the four levels but also for the dynamic interrelationship between each of the levels. The organization± IT literature rarely considers levels in this dynamic manner, but tends to use levels of analysis in a static manner. Some examples from the literature and one from practitioner experience were used to illustrate the value of viewing organization± IT issues in terms of recursive systems of individuals, teams, interdepartmental groups, organizations and interorganizations. Such a framework is critical to further research into the impacts of IT on organizational dynamics.
