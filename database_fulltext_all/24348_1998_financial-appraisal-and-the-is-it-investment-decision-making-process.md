# Financial appraisal and the IS/IT investment decision making process

JOAN BALLANTINE AND STEPHANIE STRAY Warwick Business School, University of Warwick, Coventry, CV4 7AL, UK

This paper explores the techniques used by organizations to appraise Information Systems (IS)/Information Technology (IT) investments, and concentrates, in particular, on techniques of capital investment appraisal. We draw on relevant studies reported in both the accounting and ® nance, and the IS literature, which have addressed their usage. Where possible comparisons are drawn between both sets of literatures. The results of a survey that speci® cally examined IS/IT investment appraisal practices of a sample of UK companies is also presented. Among the issues discussed include the extent to which capital investment appraisal techniques are used to appraisal investments, the importance of the techniques used and the problems attendant on the decision making process.

## Introduction

There are two primary reasons why IS/IT investment appraisal is important. First, organizations are devoting high levels of resources on IS/IT investments. Expenditure in the UK and the US on IS/IT investments is such that it now represents a substantial element of capital expenditure. According to Willcocks (1996), IT spend in the UK in 1995 was 33.6 billion pounds, and expected to rise by 8.2%, 7% and 6.2% in subsequent years, representing an average of over 2% of turnover, or, in the public sector, an average of £3546 per employee. Even by the early 1990s the comparative spend in the USA was larger. For example, Maglitta and Sullivan-Trainor (1991) quote a ® gure of 2.7% of corporate turnover being spent on IS/IT investments, while government statistics suggested that, by 1994, computers and other IT amounted to half of all business spending on equipment ± not including the billions spent on software and programmers each year (Sager and Gleckman, 1994). However, actual spending levels are likely to be higher than those actually reported (Keen, 1991), given that the total organizational costs associated with IS/IT investments (for example, staff training, organizational restructuring costs, employee time and so forth) are unlikely to be included in reported costs.

Secondly, along with increasing IS/IT spending levels and the global economic and competitive climate which organizations face today, concerns have been voiced over IS effectiveness measurement, cost justi® - cation and cost containment. Between 1984 and 1997 these latter problems ranked among some of the top key IS issues in studies conducted in the US, the UK, the

Gulf and the Republic of China, (Dickson et al. and Nechis, 1984; Niederman et al., 1991; Badri, 1992; Clark, 1992; Price Waterhouse, 1992; Galliers, 1993; Wang and Turban, 1994; Pollard and Hayne, 1996; Pervan, 1997).

There is a considerable body of literature that has addressed the issue of IS/IT appraisal (see for example, Lucas and Moore, 1976; King and Schrems, 1978; Ginzberg, 1979; Hamilton and Chervancy, 1981; Chandler, 1982; Klein and Beck, 1987; Hawgood and Land, 1988; Hirschheim and Smithson, 1988; Parker et al., 1989; Willcocks, 1994), and there appears to be general agreement that appraisal of IS/IT investments is a task which most organizations feel they have a responsibility to perform (Carlson, 1974). However, despite the existence of this wealth of literature, even as at 1997 the IS community appears to be no nearer a solution to many of the problems associated with IS/IT appraisal.

Whilst we discuss some of these inherent problems later, it is worthwhile at this point to consider why organizations appraise IS/IT investments. Farbey et al. (1992) suggest that IS/IT appraisal serves various objectives. These are:

(1) to justify investments;

(2) to enable organizations to decide between competing projects, especially if capital rationing is an issue;

(3) to act as a control mechanism over expenditure, bene® ts and the development and implementation of projects;

(4) to act as a learning device enabling improved appraisal and systems development to take place in the future.

Other reasons of a similar nature have also been identi® ed (Ginzberg and Zmud, 1988; Angell and Smithson, 1991) and these include:

(1) to gain information for project planning;

(2) to ensure that systems continue to perform well;

(3) to enable decisions concerning expansion, improvement, or the postponement of projects to be taken.

All of the above reasons place appraisal in a positive and constructive light and view it as an important part of the decision-making process, in addition to contributing to the development and re® nement of that process. However, as we shall see later there are many practical problems associated with the appraisal process. Before moving on to discuss capital investment appraisal techniques in more detail, we will brie¯ y outline how we are using the term \`appraisal’ in the context of this work.

The terms \`appraisal’ and \`evaluation’ are frequently used within the IS literature in a synonymous manner. Although there appears to be no general agreement as to their exact meaning, we use the term \`appraisal’ here to imply an ex-ante consideration of IS/IT investments. Thus the activities carried out during the feasibility study stage of systems development would be included within our de® nition of appraisal. \`Evaluation’ , on the other hand, is more frequently used in the literature to imply a much wider consideration of investments at different times, for example, evaluation might be carried out as the project is being developed, implemented or indeed after implementation. However, we would like to stress that appraisal and evaluation are viewed as aspects of the same decision-making process, in addition to recognizing that both activities are intrinsically linked within that decision making process.

## Capital investment appraisal techniques (CIAT)

Capital investment appraisal techniques (CIAT) have been discussed widely in the accounting and ® nance literature (see for example Carsberg and Hope, 1976;

Sundem and Geijsbeck, 1978; Brealey and Myers, 1981; Scapens and Sales, 1981; Gurnani, 1984; Mills, 1988). Various empirical studies are reported which have attempted to establish a relationship between organizational characteristics (for example, the size of an organization) and the use of particular techniques of appraisal over time. In addition, other studies have developed comparisons of national and international practices; considered the wider organizational and social in¯ uences on the adoption and use of CIAT; and appraised the ef® cacy of speci® c CIAT (Klammer, 1972; Petty et al., 1975; Gitman and Forrester, 1978; Schall et al., 1978; Kim and Farragher, 1981; Moore and Reichert, 1983). While this literature acknowledges a heterogeneity in appraisal techniques, it fails to consider adequately the possible implications of the heterogeneity of the objects of appraisal. In particular, the accounting and ® nance literature largely fails to discuss the application of CIAT for the speci® c purpose of appraising IS/IT investments. In addition, the majority of empirical studies reported in this literature fail to report upon the problems encountered when using CIAT for appraising IS/IT investments, or indeed other capital investments. Furthermore, relatively little consideration is given to the extent to which the techniques are considered important in the context of the decision making process.

Despite a lack of discussion of the application of CIAT in the context of IS/IT appraisal, empirical studies reported in the accounting and ® nance literature do provide us with a general picture of the extent to which CIAT are used to appraise capital projects in general, in addition to indicating how patterns of usage have changed over time. Studies have consistently shown that payback has been the most widely used CIAT for appraising capital investments. The more \`sophisticated’ techniques (see Klammer, 1972 and Haka et al., 1985 for a discussion of \`naive’ and \`sophisticated’ techniques of investment appraisal) of Net Present Value (NPV) and Internal Rate of Return (IRR) have for some time been gaining in use and importance within organizations, particularly in the UK (Pike, 1982). However, given the period over which the studies were conducted, it is likely that many of the investments considered were not of an IS/IT nature.

The use of ARR appears to have declined relative to the other three techniques considered.

A similar analysis of the use of CIAT by UK companies is provided by Pike (1988), in which he reports on the adoption of sophisticated capital budgeting techniques (NPV and IRR) and their effect on decision-making. Pike (1988) presents a trend analysis of the change in use of CIAT by 100 large UK ® rms, over the period 1975± 86 (roughly corresponding to the same time period used for the studies shown in Table 1). The results largely con® rm those of Sangster (1993), indicating that the more \`sophisticated’ techniques of IRR and NPV, are being used more frequently than has historically been the case.

In the US similar trends in the use of CIAT have been observed. An analysis carried out by Gurnani (1984), indicates the extent to which CIAT are used by US corporations (see Table 2), and indicates a similar picture to that of the UK with the more \`sophisticated’ techniques ® nding more widespread use in the capital investment decision making process of organizations.

While Table 2 reports on an earlier time period than that of either Sangster or Pike, it does show an increase in the use of IRR and NPV techniques and a decrease in the use of both ARR and payback. What is also apparent is the less widespread use of ARR, NPV and payback in the US, compared to the UK.

Despite the wealth of empirical studies reported in the accounting and ® nance literature on the use of CIAT for investment decision making, as stated earlier there is little speci® c work in that literature that addresses the application of these techniques to the appraisal of IS/IT investments. Consequently th importance of the techniques used in the IS/IT investment decision-making process, and the speci® c problems encountered when using the techniques for this purpose, remain largely unreported. Before going on to discuss our research ® ndings which attempt to address some of these issues, we will consider some of the broad arguments, both for and against, the use of CIAT in the IS/IT investment decision making process.

## Arguments against using CIAT to appraise IS/IT investments

The main arguments against using CIAT to appraise IS/IT investments, not surprisingly, are not found in the accounting and ® nance literature but in the IS literature. In the main the arguments seem to stem from the belief that IS/IT is inherently different to other types of capital investment and, as a result, ® nancial techniques which have historically been used to appraise capital investments are inappropriate for appraising IS/IT investments. For example, a typical argument, in the case of IS/IT investments, is that organizations know there will be unanticipated bene-® ts from such investments, but cannot estimate them, or that the repercussions of IS systems spread beyond functional or department areas so that an organization can never fully attribute costs and bene® ts to a particular system or to a particular function/department. In addition, it is argued that traditional ® nancial techniques of appraisal (for example NPV and IRR) do not allow for the inclusion of important intangible costs and bene® ts of IS/IT investments.

More speci® cally, Hochstrasser (1992), for example argues that existing appraisal techniques are ® nancially oriented, with particular emphasis on the bottom line (i.e. pro® t). Such techniques, he argues, are regarded as inappropriate given the nature of IS investments. For example, \`projects aimed to ©improve customer supportº , or to ©offer better market informationº might, in the short-term, be impossible to quantify’ (p. 18). Others make similar arguments. For example, Willcocks (1992), suggests that \`it is now widely accepted that certain measures do not assist the process of establishing how IT adds value to an organisation’ (p. 107). Miller (1993) makes the point that conventional cost bene® t analysis is becoming less and less relevant for measuring IS/IT performance, while Schell (1986) argues that little guidance is given in determining intangible costs and bene® ts when using ® nancial techniques. Additionally, Bacon (1992) argues that the capital budgeting theory, on which CIAT are based, has a number of major limitations including the assumption that cash ¯ ows and discount rates are known with certainty (see also Strassmann, 1997).

## Arguments for using CIAT to appraise IS/IT investments

Despite the arguments presented in the IS literature that CIAT are inappropriate for appraising IS/IT investments, there is an extensive accounting and ® nance literature which argues that CIAT are appropriate techniques for appraising capital investments. Such arguments stem from the fundamental assumption that an organizations’ primary objective is that of maximizing shareholder’ wealth. Investment decisions then should be made in order to achieve this objective. Lumby (1981) argues that \`in order to help in making such (investment) decisions, and to ensure that they are consistent with each other, a common method of appraisal is required which can be applied equally to the whole spectrum of investment decisions and which should, in terms of the decision structure . . . help to decide whether any particular investment wil assist the company in maximising shareholder wealth (via dividend or share price maximisation’ (p. 11). As a result, the accounting and ® nance literature has largely focused on the development of appraisal tech niques which are consistent with the theory of shareholder maximization, which in turn has led to an emphasis on ® nancial techniques of appraisal. For example, the use of NPV is consistent with maximizing shareholder wealth. Other techniques, however, which although not entirely consistent with the primary objective of the organization, are widely used. Payback, for example, is widely used despite its’ concentration on liquidity and risk exposure.

The accounting and ® nance literature does however recognize that there are other considerations which need to be assessed when making investment decisions. Lumby (1981) argues that no \`method of investment appraisal can give a de® nitive decision. They cannot tell a company’s ® nancial decision maker to ©investº or ©not investº , but can only act as a decision guide. . . . All they (the techniques) will do is help to communicate information to the decision maker; but when the decision is ® nally made, it is based on a whole range of very diverse considerations which are beyond our present capabilities to encompass in ©overallº decision making formulae’ (p. 11). Despite recognizing the limitations of the techniques, in addition to acknowledging that such techniques can never replace managerial judgement, Lumby (1981) argues that their usage can help to make judgements more sound than would otherwise be the case.

In summary, while the accounting and ® nance literature does not directly address many of the practical issues of appraisal in relation to IS/IT investments, it does recognize that there are limitations in the use of CIAT for the purposes of investment decision making. It is not clear, however, from either the accounting and ® nance, or IS literature, whether the claims made by those opposed to the use of CIAT in the IS/IT area are not atypical of the problems and dif® culties encountered when appraising capital investments per se. As a result it remains a moot question as to whether or not IS/IT project appraisal is uniquely different to that of other forms of investment.

## Appraising feasibility: survey ® ndings

Based on the results of a survey carried out by Ballantine et al. (1994) during the later half of 1993, we ® rst consider the extent to which CIAT are used to appraise IS/IT investments. The survey examined the IS/IT appraisal practices of a sample of organizations drawn from the Times’ Top 1000 companies. Using a systematic sample to ensure the correct size distribution of companies contained within the sample, a total of 98 responses to the questionnaire were received (a response rate of 55%). Of these, 97 provided responses suitable for analysis and it is the responses of these organizations that form the basis of the following results.

The respondent organizations represented a wide range of industrial sectors, with the largest number of responses from the manufacturing, construction, electronics and engineering, and wholesale/retail sectors, which accounted for 54% of the total sample (see Figure 1). Turnover of the sampled organizations for 1992 ranged from $\mathcal { L } ^ { 9 }$ to £10 000 million, with a mean and median of £589 million and £170 million, respectively. The IS/IT budget allocation within the organizations ranged from $\mathcal { L } 0$ to £160 million, with a mean allocation of £7.4 million (median allocation of £1.3 million). Only two companies reported zero budget allocations for 1992, stating that they had no requirement for investment in IS/IT in that year. The cost of the most recent IS/IT project varied from £10 000 up to a maximum of $\mathcal { L } 5$ million, with a mean and median cost of £404 800 and £150 000, respectively.

The mean cost of the most recent IS/IT project undertaken by the respondent organizations, as a percentage of turnover, was 0.5% (median 0.1%), whilst the mean cost as a percentage of the 1992 total IS/IT budget allocation was 58% (median of 12.3%). The IS/IT budget is an average of 1% of 1992 turnover (median of 0.7%), which is comparable to the levels of expenditure reported in earlier studies. However, it is worth noting that just over 40% of the sampled companies had less than 0.5% of their turnover allocated to the IS/IT budget in 1992. This might be best explained as a result of greater control over expenditure as companies recovered from the recession in the UK during this period. Further analysis, indicated no statistically signi® cant difference (at the 5% level of signi® cance) between the mean size of the IS/IT budget and the industrial sector within which the company was primarily engaged.

The respondents who completed the research instrument were mainly employed within IS/IT related positions (ie IS Director, IS Manager, Data Processing Managers). Only 12% hold ® nance related positions, and these tended to come from the smaller organizations who responded. A total of 57% of respondents are employed at the corporate level, whilst 33% were employed at the business unit level of the organization. The respondents are employed within relatively high managerial levels with 57.1% and 28.6% employed within one and two reporting levels of the Chief Executive, respectively, and just over 9% reporting directly to the Chief Executive.

## Extent of appraisal

The survey identi® ed the appraisal practices in respect of both the most recent IS/IT project undertaken by respondent organizations, and IS/IT projects in general. The reason for asking about both of these was that in some cases we wished to relate responses concerning appraisal to speci® c aspects of the project (e.g. its size) and in other instances we wanted to ascertain what typically went on within organizations. However, it should be noted that asking individuals about the \`last time they did something (i.e. the most recent IS/IT project) may generate a response of what they typically do (i.e. IS/IT projects in general ± see Moser and Kalton, 1971; Belson, 1964) rather than what they actually did with the speci® c project in question.

Table 3 shows the extent to which the respondent organizations appraised the most recent project and IS/IT projects in general. What is clear, regardless of whether one considers the most recent project, or IS/IT projects in general, is that the vast majority of respondent companies undertake some form of appraisal. It is also the case that appraisal was statistically signi® cantly more widespread (at the 1% level of significance) for the most recent project than for all projects (87% versus 62%). We believe this is for two reasons. First, companies are now more likely to appraise projects than they have been historically because of the need for cost containment as a result of the recession in the UK (and elsewhere). Secondly when asked about the most recent project, respondents may have elected to answer about the most recent \`signi® cant’ project where signi® cance is measured in terms of size or importance. It is, unsurprisingly, more likely that \`signi® cant’ projects will be appraised.

It is also noticeable that the results indicate a lower incidence of feasibility appraisal than that indicated by Willcocks (1992; 1994), who reported that all organizations in his sample (50) appraised at the feasibility stage. In contrast Farbey et al. (1993, again in the UK) found only 56% of the (16) projects they investigated were subject to appraisal. The ® rst two reasons imply that feasibility appraisal is clearly not relevant in all investment cases. This is supported by Farbey et al. who identi® ed that the decision to go ahead with a number of IS/IT investments (44%) was taken at a high level on a \`got to do’ basis, or that \`the organization had a formal justi® cation procedure which was by-passed because the project could not ® t into it’ (p. 113). However, taking the remaining reasons together, a somewhat disturbing picture emerges in that organizational problems such as a lack of time, importance or project or enthusiasm, and support of key personnel appear to hinder the appraisal process within organizations.

Further analysis indicated that feasibility appraisal is not associated with the absolute cost of IS/IT projects. However, as might be expected, appraisal was found to be associated with larger budgets, larger companies in terms of turnover, and proportionately larger projects when measured in relation to the organization’ s total IS/IT budget.

Only a small percentage of respondent organizations use the more sophisticated discounted cash ¯ ow techniques of NPV and IRR to appraise feasibility. The reasons for the less widespread use of both NPV and IRR compared to that reported earlier from the accounting and ® nance literature are possibly due to a combination of factors. These might include the educational backgrounds and experience of the individuals carrying out the appraisal, the number of respondents who are unfamiliar with the techniques and the fact that the organizational policy may dictate that a particular technique be used. Alternatively, however, it might be that both NPV and IRR are simply perceived not to be appropriate for appraising investments in IS/IT. The high usage of payback is probably best explained because of its simplicity and its focus on liquidity.

The pro® tability index (PI) and return on management (ROM) were rarely used to appraise the most recent project. This is partly explained by the relatively high percentage of respondents who appear to be unfamiliar with these techniques. In addition, it is more likely to be explained by the relatively moderate use of NPV, for which the PI is a complimentary technique.

The majority of respondent organizations in the survey used more than one CIAT to make a decision regarding the feasibility of the most recent project; 20.9% used only one technique, 19.4% used two techniques, 28.4% used three techniques, 16.4% used four techniques, and 14.9% used more than four techniques for appraisal purposes. The maximum number of techniques used to appraise project feasibility was eight. This compares with Pike (1988) who reports the use of fewer techniques (in the accounting and ® nance literature) than that indicated here (however, as in most of the studies reported in this literature, Pike (1989) makes no distinction as to the type of investment considered in his study). One might conclude from this that those responsible for appraising IS/IT investments prefer to use a combination of techniques for decision-making, which might support some of the arguments presented in the IS literature earlier, as opposed to the more dominant use of a smaller number of techniques as reported in the accounting and ® nance literature.

Further analysis of the data indicated that there was no tendency for the usage of a particular technique to be associated with the cost of the most recent project or with the size of the IS/IT budget. However, larger IS/IT projects do have a tendency to involve the use of a greater number of techniques than is the case for smaller projects.

## Importance of techniques used

Respondents were also asked to indicate the importance of CIAT in terms of making a decision regarding the feasibility of IS/IT investments (see Table 5). CBA was perceived as by far the most important technique to appraise the feasibility of the most recent project, with Payback and ROI/ARR used as secondary measures. This con® rms that organizations are at least attaching relevant importance to the techniques that they actually use. In addition it seems to con® rm that for some organizations the ® nancial techniques used have an impact on the IS/IT investment decision making process. However, we cannot ignore the extremely high percentage of respondents who feel that the techniques used do not rank as ® rst, second or third in importance in terms of making the investment decision. This is obviously problematic and would seem to suggest that the techniques are being used in a ritualistic manner as opposed to adding any real value to the decision making process.

## Review of ® nancial techniques

When asked to indicate whether the techniques used to appraise IS/IT investments were subject to review, only 47% of organizations indicated that such a review takes place, 29% do not review the techniques used, and the remaining 24% do not know whether the techniques are reviewed or not. Of those who do review the techniques used, 23% review them annually, 37% review them every two to three years, 20% review them every four to ten years and a further 20% did not know how often they were reviewed.

There is a positive relationship between the cost of the most recent project and the number of problems arising with appraisal, with more problems being encountered with higher project costs. This relationship is perhaps indicative of the fact that higher project costs are generally associated with greater complexity, and increased exposure to risk.

## Incorporation of relevant factors

The survey also identi® ed the extent to which taxation, in¯ ation and opportunity costs were taken into account during the IS/IT appraisal process. Just under half of the companies surveyed take account of both in¯ ation and taxation (47% and 46% respectively), while only 29% take account of the opportunity cost of the IS/IT investment. In addition, we found that only 7% of respondent organizations adjust the discount rate used (when using NPV and IRR) to take account of the risk of the project. Overall, we ® nd the inclusion of these relevant factors disappointing, and indicative of the fact that organizations might not be applying the techniques used to appraise IS/IT investments in an entirely appropriate manner. If this is the case, then it is likely that organizations are not getting the maximum bene® t possible from using such techniques in the decision-making process. Alternatively, however, organizations might believe that tax, in¯ ation and opportunity costs are such an insigni® cant element of the total costs of IS/IT investments that they are not worth incorporating within the techniques used, or that their determination is too time consuming.

The incorporation of risk and uncertainty within the decision-making process is markedly more prevalent than that of the above factors. In total, 55% of respon dent organizations took account of risk/uncertainty when appraising their most recent IS/IT project. Table 9 indicates the nature of the techniques used to incorporate risk/uncertainty, the most popular techniques used being sensitivity analysis and brain storming, which is probably best explained by their simplicity and ease of application. Despite a relatively high percentage of organizations who take account of risk/uncertainty, we would have expected this to be higher given the uncertain and risky nature of IS/IT investments.

## Satisfaction with the appraisal techniques used

In order to ascertain the levels of satisfaction with the techniques used to appraise IS/IT projects generally, respondents were asked to indicate, using a ® ve-point Likert scale (0 ± Dissatis® ed, to 4 ± Satis® ed), their level of satisfaction. The results generally indicated high levels of satisfaction with the techniques used among the individual respondents. Given that all of the respondents are heavily involved in the appraisal process within those organizations sampled, it is unlikely that given the high levels of satisfaction found here, that appraisal practices, and their associated problems as outlined here, will substantially alter much in the future.

## Conclusion

In this paper we have discussed the issue of appraisal, and in particular we have identi® ed the extent to which CIAT are used in determining IS/IT project feasibility. The accounting and ® nance literature, as previously discussed, focuses almost entirely on the application of ® nancial techniques in the investment decision making process. Although the literature acknowledges a heterogeneity in appraisal techniques, it fails to consider adequately the possible implications of the heterogeneity of the objects of appraisal, and largely ignores the problems of using ® nancial techniques when appraising investments in IS/IT. On the other hand, the IS literature is fairly dismissive in it’ s rejection of ® nancial techniques of appraisal. However, the results reported here do show that ® nancial techniques are widely used by organizations to appraise IS/IT investments, and that this trend is likely to continue in the future, despite the arguments presented in the IS literature that they may be inappropriate for such a task, and the arguments presented here that the techniques are not necessarily perceived to be important.

In summary the results of the empirical survey (some of which are not reported here) have highlighted a number of both positive and negative aspects of appraisal, with respect to IS/IT investments (see Figure 2). The challenge for organizations in the future is to address the negative aspects of appraisal, in order to improve and minimize their impact, whilst at the same time, building on the positive aspects that have been identi-® ed. Given increasing expenditure levels and the changing nature of IS/IT, appraisal is clearly an important issue within organizations. However, fundamenta
