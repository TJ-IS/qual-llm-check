# Bridging the user± IS gap: a study of major information systems projects

ANDREA TAYLOR-CUMMINGS

Oxford Institute of Information Management, Templeton College, Oxford, UK

A \`culture gap’ between IS professionals and their business counterparts has been blamed for many of the problems and failures that have plagued systems development in the last four decades. A rich model of integration is developed based on normative, organizational and work group features. The model is utilized to analyse survey results and case studies of 12 major IS projects being implemented in the UK retail and electronics sectors. The research provides detailed pro® les of goal, transitional and unhelpful practice and states, and highlights key success variables in achieving user± IS staff integration on major IS development projects.

## Introduction: the research issue

A \`culture gap’ between IS professionals and their business counterparts has been blamed for many of the problems and failures that have plagued systems development in the last four decades. So deeply ingrained is the belief of its existence, that the concept persists unquestioned, despite the lack of precise de® - nition about what it actually is. \`De® nitions’ used are merely examples or symptoms of its existence, but the term is so widely accepted that knowledge has perhaps been acquired through osmosis. What is suggested, however, is that the costs of the existence of a culture gap between these two groups is escalating to phenomenal proportions. From his survey of IT Directors, Grindley (1995) found that 47% of IT Directors stated their main problem was the culture gap existing between IT and business professionals, and 56% believe that the culture gap is losing or seriously delaying IT opportunities for their company to gain competitive advantage. The dif® culty is that despite the elusive nature, the origins and sources of the destructive forces involved must be identi® ed before the issue can be adequately addressed. The persistence of the symptoms strongly suggest that previous methods have not been conclusively successful.

This research sought to address the issue of the culture gap on the premise that the destructive effects stemmed from negative attitudes and preconceptions developed and perfected over time, rather than resulting from fundamental differences between the two groups. The research borrowed perspectives from literature on Organizational Behaviour, in an effort to develop a richer understanding of the issue. By viewing organizations based on two metaphors ± organizations as cultural systems, and organizations as politica systems (Morgan, 1986) ± a de® nition of the problem was developed based on concepts of cultures and subcultures, diverse interests, con¯ ict and power. This allowed a more complex model of human nature to be incorporated, representing an attempt to address the criticisms of oversimpli® ed, rational models used in previous IS approaches. These concepts also provided a richer understanding of possible solutions, and a research model of integration based on the concept of shared values through socialization processes was developed and tested based primarily on cases studies, and augmented by a large scale questionnaire survey. The results suggest that socialization makes positive contributions to overcoming the user± IS culture gap.

## Previous contributions

Problematic relationships between IS specialists and their business counterparts have been a recurring concern since the application of computers to business problems in the 1950’ s (Somogyi and Galliers, 1987; Avison and Fitzgerald, 1996). Several attempts at improving the effectiveness of user± IS relationships have been made over the years. Among the ® rst of such attempts is the introduction of the Systems Development Life Cycle (SDLC) in which wellde® ned phases of development provide a basis for management and control (Davis and Olson, 1985). Later, in¯ uenced by the prevailing systems theory, attempts to address the \`technical competence but social incompetence’ (Mumford et al., 1978) of such structural methods led to the emergence of the concept of user participation, with notable contributions on the subject from Mumford et al., 1978; Land, 1982; Mumford, 1983, 1985.

Various system design methodologies (for example the Socio-Technical System (STS) approach, (Bostrom and Heinen, 1977), Soft Systems Methodology (Checkland, 1981), Joint Application Design (JAD, developed by IBM in 1977) and ETHICS (Mumford, 1987)) were developed to incorporate ideas of user participation based on a paradigm of learning. Other techniques emerging from this idea include the use of:

(1) prototypes (Earl, 1978; Naumann and Jenkins, 1982; Robey and Markus, 1984);

(2) multidisciplinary project teams (Lockett, 1987);

(3) Steering committees (Gibson and Nolan, 1974; Robey and Markus, 1984; Lockett, 1987);

(4) integrators (Lawrence and Lorsch, 1967) and facilitators (Mumford, 1987);

(5) third party intervention (DeBrabander and Edstrom, 1977; DeBrabander and Thiers, 1984);

(6) user project managers, champions and sponsors (Runge, 1985; Lockett, 1987; Earl et al., 1988).

Education is the suggested long term solution to improved relationships (Mumford et al., 1978) and has been given consistent emphasis over time. Based on marketing concepts, and the drive to \`get close to the customer’ (Peters and Waterman, 1982), new IS structures like decentralization, federal arrangements and the use of account managers have been adopted. Consistent with the emphasis on new structures to forge better relationships is the drive to elevate the status of the head of IS to board level, or at least to the level of top management team (Feeny et al., 1992).

Partnerships (Henderson, 1990) and hybrids (Palmer and Ottley, 1990; Skyrme, 1992) represent other proposed solutions to the problem of poor relationships between IS specialists and their business counterparts.

## The assumptions

In surveying the integrating mechanisms proposed, two key observations are made. The ® rst is that a premium has been placed on communication as a means of improving user± IS relationships. Despite the ambitious intentions of the concept of user participation, all the mechanisms seem to address negative attitudes and preconceptions indirectly through increased communication rather than directly as an issue in its own right. With few exceptions, notably Lucas (1975), most research embracing the relevance of user attitudes primarily investigated attitude towards the information system being developed and the changes imposed, rather than attitudes towards and perceptions of IS personnel (see for example, Schewe, 1976; Robey, 1979; Ginzberg, 1981). This suggests that the culture gap has been interpreted consistently as \`communication gap’ , with the implicit assumption that improved communication and understanding will alleviate the problem.

By comparison with the concept of integration, these solutions merely attempt to improve the translation of business requirements into system features by supposedly facilitating better communication. However, more communication is not always the answer to poor communication. Rather, more communication may increase the number of opportunities to demonstrate that a problem exists, thereby aggravating the situation with no attempt at resolving it. Further, by emphasizing the need to have \`middle men’, the suggestions have perpetuated the \`Them and Us’ attitude.

The second, perhaps more fundamental observation is the underlying commitment to an oversimpli® ed, rational model of human nature. The techniques described all represent rational attempts at improving the effectiveness of user± IS interaction, with little consideration for inevitable; emotive behaviour. As Argyris (1971) highlighted, systems development is an emotional as well as a rational process. This emphasis on rationality re¯ ects the underlying functionalist assumptions which characterize much of the developments in the ® eld of IS in particular and organizational and management theory in general. As Hirschheim and Smithson (1998) indicated, functionalism has not been a particularly successful paradigm for understanding organizational and societal life, as the object of study, people, does not lend itself to study through positivistic means. It is no surprise therefore, that the integration mechanisms suggested so far in the IS literature understate the problem.

## Towards a richer model

Unfortunately, paradigmatic changes are dif® cult to accomplish in practice and attempts to cross boundaries usually result in shifts towards the extremes of the same paradigm (Burrell and Morgan, 1979). Given the persistence of problematic user± IS relationships, despite the noble cadre of academics that have sought to address the problem, the promise of a complete shift across paradigms might be foolhardy. Even if such a shift were possible, the approaches may be so alien and far removed from current practice that implementation would be signi® cantly delayed, if the concepts were incorporated at all.

While such a theoretical breakthrough may be every doctoral student’s dream, a more modest goal has been set for this research. The primary aim is to generate richer understanding of the culture gap and how it can be addressed, by borrowing perspectives which use more complex models of human nature. As Pondy et al. (1983) suggest, attention to more complex patterns of human activity within the functionalist framework can facilitate the development of more enriched concepts and techniques, representing a move towards greater understanding of organizations. In so doing, signi® cant contribution to knowledge may derive from shifts which represent movement closer to the ideal.

## Theoretical foundations

Morgan (1986) proposes that because organizations are complex, paradoxical phenomena, it is necessary to use difference perspectives to study them. Although each perspective may be uniquely useful, none captures organizational life in its entirety. Traditional organization and management theory has been developed based on mechanical and organismic metaphors of organization which over-simplify the nature of organizations by focusing on the formal and rational aspects of organizational life (Morgan, 1989). In addition to these two metaphors, Morgan (1986) proposes ® ve other metaphors ± organizations as brains, cultural systems, political systems, psychic prisms, ¯ ux and transformation and instruments of domination which can be used to study organizations, each of which focus attention on different aspects of a complex social phenomenon. The relevance of two of these metaphors ± organizations as cultural systems, and organizations as political systems ± to the study of IS has been emerging as a consistent theme in the literature (for example, Robey and Markus, 1984; Willcocks and Mason, 1987; Willcocks, 1991; Galliers ., 1992).

## Organizations as cultural systems

Central to the cultural metaphor is the appreciation that organizations are made up of individuals possessing higher order characteristics, that is, they are able to use language, attribute meaning to events, share meaning with others, in short to act symbolically. By studying organizations as cultures, attention is drawn to a set of features which may otherwise have escaped the attention of the researcher (Morgan, 1989). Culture ± a term widely used, yet ill de® ned ± encompasses ideas of shared organizational ideology manifested and sustained by beliefs, language and ceremonial acts (Meyer, 1982), legends, stories and myths (Mitroff and Kilmann, 1976) and collective rituals (Ouchi, 1981). A culture gap can therefore be said to exist when the values and norms held by two groups differ substantially in issues of relevance to each group.

Gouldner (1958) recognized that experts ± de® ned in terms of specialized knowledge and skills ± are likely to have different norms and values from that adhered to by other members of the organization. Later debates about professionals in bureaucracies and professional cultures provide support for Gouldner’s ® ndings. This idea of different and competing value systems in organizations is consistent with Lawrence and Lorsch’s (1967) thesis on differentiation and integration. These authors suggest that the more differentiated groups become, the more emphasis should be placed on integrating mechanisms.

Together, these ideas provide a useful background for understanding the emergence of different subcultures and the resultant user± IS culture gap. Different training and socialization has produced a body of specialists with differing languages and views of the world. Until recently, managers felt they had no choice but to accept the professional judgement of system developers, and the IS department was allowed to regulate itself and operate independently from the rest of the business (Willcocks, 1991). IS specialists have been left to develop their own values, working habits, reward systems and language which is different from, and not understood by, the users with whom they interacted. Further, IS personnel place an emphasis on developing specialist skills and keeping up-to-date with state of the art technology rather than ensuring that organizational objectives are met (Grindley, 1991).

As Poupart and Hobbs (1989) suggest, analysis of the various types of organizational subcultures that exist leads to increased understanding of work environments, and the resultant identi® cation of traits which should be modi® ed to guarantee productivity success in the future culture. Schwartz and Davis (1981) further suggest that cultural analysis allows incompatibility between intended strategy and existing norms and behaviours to be identi® ed.

The cultural metaphor also embraces concepts which allow organizational phenomena to be understood in terms of the expressive and symbolic. The attitudes and preconceptions held by users and IS specialists about each other may be viewed as the product of a particular socio-historical context which has been perpetuated through various symbolic actions, rituals and folklore over the years. For example, physical isolation of the IS department serves to reinforce the mystical \`ivory tower image, and the perception of \`bof® ns’ and the \`long beard and Jesus sandals’ image that goes with it. It also perpetuates the myth that the issues involved in systems development are too technical for users to understand. The attitudes and perceptions currently held are not just based on memories, but are kept alive through the stories jokes and slogans (for example, \`them and us’) which have become ® rmly embedded in the patterns of speech within the organization, as well as the wider social context. Some of these perceptions have even outlasted the practices from which they originated (Smircich, 1983a).

Cultural analysis allows taken-for-granted assumptions to be questioned, raises issues of context and meaning and surfaces underlying values and beliefs (Smircich, 1983b). By understanding the symbolic processes which facilitate the continued existence of these attitudes and preconceptions, attempts can be made at updating \`realities’ to be more consistent with the changes that have occurred. The cultural metaphor therefore provides useful insights, not just in terms of understanding the user± IS culture gap, but also in terms of suggesting ways for addressing it effectively.

## Organizations as political systems

Viewing organizations as political systems encourages focus on concepts of interests, con¯ ict and power, concepts which are generally discouraged when assumptions of rationality prevail. The concept of diverse interests is consistent with the concept of subcultures, as it encompasses a complex set of predispositions embracing goals, values desires, expectations and other orientations that encourage action in one direction rather than another (Morgan, 1986). Lucas (1975) identi® ed the relevance of applying the power and con¯ ict models of organizations to the relationships between IS specialists and the rest of the organization. He found that information services have a high score on all variables in the power model, and the potential for high scores on all variables in the con¯ ict model. As Robey and Markus (1984) note, systems development can be both rational and political, hence it is essential for those engaged in the process to be aware of what is really going on.

Concepts of culture, potentially diverse and con¯ icting interest and unequal power distributions therefore provide a richer understanding of the culture gap than is currently offered by the prevailing \`communication gap’ interpretation. This richer dimension highlights the inadequacies of previous attempts at integrating users and IS, as well as identifying issues that must be taken into consideration in discussing possible solutions.

## Con¯ ict and cooperation

The approaches to con¯ ict resolution can be placed along a continuum based on two extremes: behavioural solutions, which cause manifestations of con¯ ict to cease without changes in attitude on the one hand, and attitudinal change solutions which change the attitudes and perceptions of the protagonists so they no longer see anything to ® ght about (Neilsen, 1972). Most of the recommendations concentrate on the behavioural solution end of the continuum. Contingency theorists, notably Woodward (1958), Burns and Stalker (1961), Lawrence and Lorsch (1967) and Thompson (1967) discussed integration and con¯ ict resolution in terms of formal structures and explicit procedures, what Pascale (1990) refers to as the \`hard’ issues of integration. More recent authors suggest the use of \`softer’ ideas like superordinate goals and organizational culture. Fundamental to these later integration mechanisms is the appreciation that con¯ ict is endemic and cannot be \`wished away’ . Rather, con¯ ict is fundamental, has a potentially constructive role in achieving organizational effectiveness, and must be explicitly dealt with.

## Organizational socialization

Organizational socialization is the process of effectively communicating important organization values to employees. It involves the relinquishing of pre-existing attitudes and norms and the acquisition of new self images and involvements. The speed and effectiveness of socialization determine an employee’s loyalty, commitment, productivity and turnover (Schein, 1968). Despite the criticism that socialization compromises the right to individuality (Smircich, 1983a,b) and destroys creativity and innovation (Schein, 1968), the fact is that socialization occurs whether deliberately planned or not (Pascale, 1985). Where socialization occurs haphazardly, there is no common agreement on social rules, and the absence of such leads to breakdown in communication, trust and consistency. Mixed signals surrounding such things as rewards, promotions, career paths criteria for being on the fast track or termination inevitably generate a lot of gossip, game-playing and counterproductive expenditure of energy (Pascale, 1985). Pascale (1985) identi® ed seven steps of socialization in strong culture ® rms that have remained successful over time:

(1) Careful selection of entry level candidates.

(2) Humility-inducing experience.

(3) Promotion is inescapably tied to a proven track record.

(4) Congruent evaluation and reward systems.

(5) Careful adherence to the ® rm’s transcendent values.

(6) Reinforcing folklore.

(7) Consistent role models and traits.

## Potential contributions of socialization to the user± IS culture gap

Socialization can therefore be part of a programme to develop an IS culture, which, according to Willcocks (1991), may be de® ned as:

\`the shared/sharing of: norms, values and understanding, visions, knowledge, skills/competencies, continuous learning related to IT needs, cooperative relationships with IS professionals and commitment ± supportive of existing and required IT usage at organizational, departmental group and individual levels.’

The potential bene® ts to be gained from socialization derive from its ability to:

(i) rede® ne objectives and commitments;

(ii) challenge prevailing preconceptions; and

(iii) improve work group processes.

The ® rst two points affect global attitudes and perspectives, while the third addresses speci® c work groups. These are discussed as follows.

## Rede® nition of objectives and commitments

One of the main accusations directed at the IS department is their lack of understanding of the business, and their apparent disregard for business priorities, objectives and constraints. The accusation is not entirely without truth, but this is perhaps more a fault of the organization than of IS personnel. From as far back as 1974, Gibson and Nolan recognized the need of a trade-off between professional advancement of specialists against the need for organizational performance.

\`To cater to specialist professionals, for example, a company might isolate them in a separate department, imposing few organizational checks and gearing quality control to individual judgment or peer review. Such an arrangement might motivate a systems analyst to become the world’s best systems analyst. Emphasis on organizational values in contrast, suggests that the company locate and control the specialists in such a way as to increase the changes that short-run goals will actually be achieved on schedule. This strategy risks obsolescence or turnover among specialists, but it successfully conveys the important message that some specialist skills can advance a management career . . . At the time of hiring, the specialist’s expectations for freedom and professional development should be explicitly discussed in the context of organizational structure and controls . . . as part of the ©psychological contractº .

Similarly, Rosenbaum (1990) noted that:

\`Many technical professionals don’t have a real understanding of the marketplace, the competitive situation, and the economic climate. They will perform better with this knowledge ± and have a fuller understanding of the value of their contributions.’

Previous socialization, both primary, in terms of upbringing, and secondary, in terms of professional training, has established a set of values, objectives and commitments that may not necessarily be congruent with those of the organization. Despite the recognition of these differences, little has been done to directly address this. Nota (1988) highlighted that the socialization process at organizations which have adopted a proactive stance to employee commitment and satisfaction is more encompassing and long lasting than traditional orientation processes. They begin with lengthy interview processes which involve multiple rounds before selection is made, and continue through extensive induction programmes. One of the main outcomes of elaborate selection processes is congruence of values on issues that are important to both parties. Organizations can use the selection process to rede® ne the objectives and commitments of IS recruits where possible, or allow those that will not \`® t’ to deselect themselves.

Induction programmes may involve visiting other parts of the organization and perhaps working in other functions in order to develop an appreciation of each other’ s work. Such processes would allow IS personnel the exposure and the opportunity to understand organizational objectives and to appreciate their role in ensuring they are met. Other practices found by Louis et al. (1983) to be useful in the socialization process were the use of mentors, business trips and socia events. The alignment between reward systems and organizational objectives is also key in rede® ning the commitment of IS personnel.

It could be argued that IS personnel are inherently unwilling to buy in to organizational values. This was certainly the case in the past, as they thrived on high demand for their skills, with a consequent high turnover rate and low commitment to organizations. Recent changes in society, however, have begun to alter the distribution of power. The development of off-the-shelf application packages, fourth generation languages, personal computers and local area networks have eroded the need for expert power and demys ti® ed much of the development activity. Consequently, IS professionals, who once were in great demand and more committed to their professions than organizations, may increasingly become more dependent on organizations. The increased sophistication in the layman’s understanding of the technology has challenged the authority of IS professionals and the entire systems development process. Further, the systems being developed now are more innovative, seeking to extract competitive advantage from new processes facilitated by technology. As a result, \`knowledge’ of the way forward no longer resides in any one group of individuals, and new arrangements for cooperation amongst groups are necessary for effective performance. The changed context creates a more favourable climate for this approach to succeed.

## Challenges to prevailing preconceptions

While it has been established that there may be fundamental differences between users and IS, there are preconceptions which do not necessarily apply today, but persist because they remain unchallenged. An example is the ever present user image of the IS person dressed in \`long beard and Jesus sandals’. This may have been true in the past, but the cosmopolitan nature of society today would suggest that such notions should be challenged. Despite the fact that certain personality traits may choose certain types of jobs, as Seiler (1963) suggests, it is very seldom, if ever, that groups composed of people with identical or even closely similar personalities are encountered.

Historically, because of space and environmental requirements, data processing departments and computer centres were kept in remote locations. Despite developments over the years which facilitate closer location ± decreased size of computers, emergence of IS development staff (as opposed to operational staff), PC networks ± some organizations persist with previous physical arrangements. Physical isolation has therefore allowed preconceptions to remain unchallenged. This is evidenced by the folklore developed by both users and IS being passed down from generation to generation without question, despite the fact that both worlds have progressed signi® cantly. These preconceptions perpetuate a user± IS divide which could be reduced by encouraging visibility and exposure between the two groups.

Given the chance, IS personnel may well develop non-IS acquaintances based on other mutual interests. Nota (1988) found that recruitment in high-commitment organizations proceeded in groups consisting of people from different departments. The result of this method is that interdepartmental links and networks are established from the very beginning. The orientation period is usually an emotionally intense time for new recruits, so those undergoing the process together tend to form bonds that remain very strong. The implication is that IS should be involved in induction processes with recruits from other functions, so interdepartmental links can be established. Due to the historical baggage associated with de® nitions of IS personnel, interaction on non-IS ground may encourage a different set of assumptions and expectations to be applied to the rela tionship. Socialization processes that encourage shared experience between the two groups by way of induction programmes, personal/career development training programmes and organization wide social events allow criticisms which are more imagined than real to be chal lenged. Farris (1981) suggest that individuals who have social contact with one another are more apt to interact informally regarding work-related matters. Interdepartmental links or networking can therefore encourage exchange of ideas, perspectives and approaches to tasks, while generally improving relationships.

The development of referent power amongst IS staff could also be a potential bene® t of establishing links with other departments. This is a useful strateg for gaining credibility amongst the users in general, but it is of particular bene® t to IS project leaders. Traditionally IS personnel have ® lled the role of project leader for systems development projects, even if users are included in the team. While IS leadership is not ideal in some instances, it is a frequent and at times necessary practice in systems development. The research conducted by French and Raven (1959) revealed that effective leaders depend heavily upon expert and referent power to in¯ uence personnel in their organizations. The use of legitimate and coercive power was a negative factor in obtaining effective support from organizational members, and the results with reward power were mixed. Since this study, signi® cant changes have occurred in business and society, rendering legitimate, coercive and reward power even less effective today (Olivia, 1992). Society has become more sophisticated and multicultural, with new organizational structures based on team work requiring new working habits and management practices. As Olivia (1992) points out:

\`Today it is clear that the team leader often has just expert or referent power options open to him or her. The other options are not viable. If team members feel these options are being applied to them, they will refuse to perform their duties, will ignore orders given to them and will very often ask management to remove the team leader from the team. Managers practised in the use of formal authority may ® nd themselves in a very unfamiliar situation, should they be assigned to lead a matrix-type team which contains people not part of their organization.’

If, then, only two types of power are relevant to systems development going forward, IS project leaders will be at a disadvantage if they do not have the opportunity to develop referent power with their business counterparts, opportunities which they have been denied by physical isolation and separate socialization.

## Improved work group processes

The fundamental justi® cation for socialization is that it creates a set of shared values and superordinate goals that allow otherwise disparate groups to work effectively together. Socialization therefore has the potential to direct the energies of users and IS staff away from defensive behaviour and towards effective cooperation in the task at hand. Zmud and McLaughlin (1989) suggests that organizational socialization is critical in secondary groups because of the inherent dif® - culties of establishing member motivation and commitment. Secondary groups refer to groups established to perform tasks on a temporary or permanent basis, but where such tasks are not directly linked to career progression. Whereas systems development is a primary task for IS, it is often perceived by users and their management as secondary. Organizational socialization provides an answer to the often felt necessity to establish group norms different from those of the member’s main work groups.

An appreciation of each other’s work, resulting from cross-functional exposure and interdepartmental links, would contribute to more effective relationships at team level. For example, appreciation of the work of IS personnel can help to demystify the system development process, thereby subsiding the associated fear of the unknown. Also, by understanding the user’s environment, IS personnel could develop new perspectives on user’s priorities and orientations. As Pascale (1985) noted, the socialization process creates a common language and social code of conduct, thereby effecting improved performance in project groups and con¯ ict resolution.

From the foregoing discussion, one can conclude that socialization has the potential to improve user± IS relationships and reduce the effects of the culture gap. However, while companies have developed elaborate recruitment, induction and other socialization procedures for most employees, they have not been as deliberate about the socialization experience of IS personnel. With increasing dependence on the technology as a source of competitive advantage, greater attention should be given to the socialization of the people who make the technology work.

The usefulness of socialization in addressing the user± IS gap may seem far-fetched because of the determinedly unsociable images of IS staff which prevail. However, Krasner et al. (1987) have already encountered situations in which the shared values created through such processes have removed the destructive in¯ uences of a culture gap:

\`Although the model of organizations held hostage by brilliant, unkempt deviant programmers is popular, it seemed an anachronism from our observations.

In fact, we observed some of the best software development taking place in organizations that had created cultures of egoless programming. In these cultures, individuals take pride in both their own work and the overall achievements of their groups. By carefully selecting new personnel, and then using peer pressure and management to enforce cultural norms, high degrees of performance were attained by some organizations. Furthermore, strict cultural norms meant that rigorous formal standards, such as documentation standards, need not be strictly maintained. In these cultures, violating a standard became an asocial event . . . these factors create a cultural framework from which a group communicates.’

## A richer model of integration

It is the contention of this research that in attempting to integrate the IS function into the organization, the attention has always been on Strategy, Structure and Systems, (\`harder’ issues) while the other issues of Staff, Style and Shared Values (\`softer’ issues) have consistently been ignored. This is in keeping with Pascale’s (1990) suggestion that in general organizations have not appreciated the role of \`softer’ mechanisms. Socialization, however, offers suggestions on how each of these levers (that is both sets of \`hard and \`soft’ levers) can be manipulated in the quest for improved user± IS integration.

## The research model

For the purposes of the research, integration was de® ned in terms of the perceived quality of relationships existing between users and IS specialists, consistent with the de® nition offered by Lawrence and Lorsch (1967). Figure 1 suggests that there are three variables through which the combined set of socialization processes are operationalized. Essentially this model has separated the \`hard’ or tangible processes which are easier to access from the \`soft’ or less tangible processes, while recognizing that the processes may operate at organizational level in a distinctly different way from how they operate at group level.

Normative Arrangements encompass all processes through which the softer issues of staff characteristics, management style and shared values are transferred and implemented, establishing identity with and a sense of belonging to the organization. It includes the selection criteria used in recruiting IS development staff, induction processes and career/personal development training.

Organizational Arrangements cover strategy, and its consistent translation into structure and systems, which together provide a mutually reinforcing environment. This includes issues like the physical location of IS development staff relative to their users, and performance evaluation criteria.

## The research design

Given that the primary aim of this research was to explore the relevance and potential contribution of socialization to user± IS integration, case studies pro vided the primary research methodology (Yin, 1981; Benbasat et al., 1987). The ® eldwork was divided into three phases. The ® rst phase, aimed at increasing learning, involved an investigation of three pilot case studies, one of which provided a longitudinal dimension to the current study based on previous research in this organization.

The second phase consisted of case studies of ten leading edge companies who were major operators in their industry, and provided the bulk of the research data. Leading edge companies were targeted in the hope that investigating their successful project developments would shed real light on effective methods for user± IS integration. The ten companies investigated consisted of ® ve of the largest high street retailers and ® ve major operators in the electronics ® eld. The company names and sizes (in turnover) are shown in Table 2. In two of the retail companies, Marks and Spencer and J Sainsbury, permission to investigate two projects which were relevant to the research was granted. As a result, a total of twelve projects (seven retail and ® ve electronics) were investigated and included in the study.

These case studies involved in-depth, semistructured interviews with at least six people in each organization, interviews lasting about 45 minutes. The IS Director was interviewed to gain an understanding of the background, culture and structure of the organization and the IS department. An outcome of this interview was the identi® cation of a project group to be researched. In each project group, interviews were held with the sponsoring Business Director, the project manager, the top user/IS specialist (whichever the project manager was not), a lower level user and a lower level IS specialist. Each project group member was asked about their recruitment, induction and socialization experience, and about their perception of the relationships existing between users and IS specialists in the organization in general, as well as among members of their project group. Where possible, personnel managers responsible for IS staff were also interviewed, to gain information on existing policies versus the practices described. On several occasions, other members of the organization who became familiar with the research volunteered to discuss issues and further explain the organizational context. These discussions were frequently held informally over lunch, and contributed to a richer understanding as well as validating the data received in the \`of® cial’ interviews. Documentation in the form of company reports, appraisal forms and any other relevant publication also provided triangulation.

## Research results: case studies

The measure of integration calculated from the structured questionnaires used in the interviews, though crude, provided a convenient overview of the positioning of the case study projects in terms of relative degrees of integration and effectiveness. Two key observations provided a context for making sense of a potentially overwhelming mass of data. The ® rst was that user± IS integration and effectiveness on all the project teams were \`good’ , although two projects were distinctly better than others.

Companies were allowed to identify the project(s) to be investigated, and without fail each offered the one(s) which re¯ ected the latest state of thinking about IS projects, management and organization. In each case, the decision on \`best practice’ was arrived at through a painful process of trial and error, re¯ ected in the comments about previous \`bad’ experiences and the improvements derived from current arrangements. This led to the second observation, which is that the projects seemed to have achieved integration and effectiveness through different means.

Against this background, the qualitative data was analysed ® rst by using the tapes to develop detailed case studies of each project (Eisenhardt, 1989) and then by using the model elements to facilitate crosscomparisons between projects. In conducting crosscomparisons, patterns of similarities and differences, exceptions and extremes were identi® ed and the ® ndings evaluated against explanations offered by the model. From this process three points of signi® cance emerged. First, the descriptive data suggested a need for re® nements to the model elements; secondly, a pattern of desired and undesired practices became evident; thirdly, certain factors were consistently identi® ed as key to the degree of integration and effectiveness achieved. These are discussed in the following subsections.

## Re® nements to the mode

With the exception of \`Con¯ ict resolution’ (W3), al the model elements were considered relevant to user± IS integration. \`Con¯ ict resolution’ did not feature as an element in its own right, but seemed more appropriate as part of the management style of the project manager. As such, it has been removed from the list of model elements. The model elemen \`Corporate culture’ (N1) had to be unpacked into two subelements which emerged as important to integration: \`Bureaucracy’ (N1-a) and \`IS commitment’ (N1-b).

\`Bureaucracy’ is used to describe corporate culture in terms of the amount of in¯ exibility in an organization.

This determined the ease with which organizational members from different functions and with different levels of seniority could function in the same team. In other words, the presence of bureaucracy at an organizational level had a direct impact on the effectiveness of relationships at team level. \`IS Commitment’ examines the nature of any overall goals/values collectively recognized by members of the IS function. One of the main reasons for seeing IS people as \`Different’ was the perception amongst the users that IS staff were removed from business concerns. Conversely, it seemed that the more committed to business objectives the IS function was seen to be, the better the relationship that existed between IS and the user community. This element was distinct from the presence or otherwise of a Mission Statement.

The model element \`Project Management’ (W5) also had to be unpacked into \`Methodology’ (W5-a) describing the use or otherwise of formal methodologies, and \`Project Structure’ (W5-b) describing the reporting line for users and IS staff involved in the project team. A ® nal modi® cation to the model resulted from the recognized in¯ uence of the status of the head of IS (O1-b). This emerged as a signi® cant in¯ uence on user± IS relationships in terms of the extent to which the head of IS was included in top management discussions and the credibility gained amongst business colleagues based on perceived commitment to business objectives.

## Desirable and undesirable practices

A sense of desirable (\`goal’ ) and undesirable (\`unhelpful’) practices for each of the model elements emerged, along with a sense of movement in the direction of desirable practices. For example, inclusion of IS development staff on induction processes which involved recruits from other departments, visits to stores/plants and which lasted for weeks rather than days (\`intensive’ induction) was considered very effective in enhancing user± IS integration. Conversely, the lack of IS involvement in induction processes, or the presence of induction processes which were for IS staff only was considered unhelpful. Similarly, locating the IS development function close to users (for example, on the same ¯ oor, or on different ¯ oors of the same building but strategically locating photocopiers and coffee machines to encourage casual contact between the two groups) improved user± IS integration. This is contrasted with arrangements in which physical distance causes interaction to be limited to a workonly basis. Discussions also revealed that certain (\`transitional’) practices were not ideal but represented a workable compromise between the ideal and what was feasible given the organizational context. Table 3 provides a summary of the ® nal set of model elements, the categories used to describe the range of experiences and the suggested usefulness of these states (referred to as \`goal’ , \`transitional’ and \`unhelpful’) in improving user± IS integration. The de® nition of each of these categories is given in Appendix B.

The pro® le of the projects in terms of the amount of goal, transitional and unhelpful practices provided consistent explanation of the relative degree of integration and project effectiveness, and supported the initial observation that integration and effectivenes were achieved through different routes. Concurrent with this suggestion is support for the proposition that poor implementation of one of the three variables may be compensated for by good implementation of the other two. Perhaps more importantly, the case study data suggests that the model elements do provide an explanation of the degree of user± IS integration and project effectiveness, and that there are no positive associations between \`goal’ states and improved perceptions of relationships and project success.

## Key factors

The concept of \`goal’ states in particular was sharpened by the identi® cation of certain factors that interviewees considered key in achieving the degree of integration that existed. These factors included the use of multidisciplinary teams (versus the use of intermediaries or separate user/IS teams), the credibility and management style of the project manager, the presence, formally or informally, of an account manager, the \`right’ mix of skills on the team (as opposed to in any given individual) co-location of team members, the use of social events and multiple delivery of functionality to the business in short (6± 9 month) timescales. These ® ndings are consistent with the theoretical framework developed, as well as suggestions in the IS, Organizational Behaviour and Project Management literature. However, further attempts at unravelling the characteristics which render these factors particularly useful to integration and effectiveness revealed one signi® cant commonality: each of the factors had the inherent ability to challenge preconceptions and create new attitudes amongst IS, the business community, or both. In other words, each of the key factors identi® ed has both an instrumental and an expressive role (Daft, 1983) in improving user± IS integration. The expressive role is evidenced by improved perceptions and attitudes resulting from increased visibility and the resultant credibility facilitated by the various arrangements. For example, multidisciplinary teams and close physical proximity provide the occasions for preconceptions to be challenged (visibility), while simultaneously encouraging effective communication, immediate access to relevant expertise and speedy resolution of issues.

## Research results: survey

A large scale survey was developed from the case study ® ndings with three speci® c objectives in mind:

(1) To gain empirical evidence about where organizations are in relation to the model elements;

(2) To test how closely organizations \`® t’ with the pro® les of \`goal’ , \`transition’ and \`unhelpful’ states, that is, empirical support for the model of transition;

(3) To discover if the trends emerging, in terms of the suggested goal states, as well as the direction of those trends, were supported by data based on a larger sample.

The questionnaire solicited response for three time periods: arrangements that previously existed, current arrangements and plans for the future. Perceived success/satisfaction with systems development provided a surrogate measure of the effectiveness of user± IS relationships.

## Current practice

Table 4 provides a summary of responses under \`Unhelpful’ , \`Transition’ and \`Goal’ states for each of the time periods: PAST, NOW and FUTURE. While it was immediately apparent that the survey data did not support any suggestion that these represented three holistic \`stages’ , the basic concept of movement through the states is supported in a number of ways. ## Support for goal states

The identi® cation of \`goal’ states is supported ® rst by reference to the future choices of respondents. For 14 of 19 model elements, the identi® ed \`goal’ state was the predominant FUTURE choice (Table 4); for another element it was an equal ® rst choice. Only in the \`Induction’, \`Team Selection’, \`Team Building’ and \`Methodology’ elements were the \`goal’ states predicted from the case study analysis not recognized by the survey data. \`Goal’ states were particularly strongly favoured for the model elements \`IS Commitment’ (77%), \`Selection Criteria’ (67%), \`Project Structure: User/IS contact’ (74%) and \`Superordinate Goals: Success Criteria’ (83%). In other words, respondents were most consistent about the future, and in line with prescribed \`goal’ states, in their plans to implement/ accomplish perceptions of IS staff as providing a core/strategic function to the business, recruitment criteria based on relevant skills, multidisciplinary system development teams and success criteria based on delivering business bene® ts.

To further test the power of \`goal’ states, respondents’ expressed current satisfaction with systems development was regressed against the number of matches between their current arrangement (NOW responses) and \`goal’ states. A positive and signi® cant $( \mathinner { p \mathopen { \left/ { \vphantom { \left( p \mathrm { 1 } \right)}  } \right.}  \kern - delimiterspace } = 0 . 0 0 0 1 )$ correlation was found between the number of \`goal’ states and expressed satisfaction. Similar regressions of satisfaction against the number of \`unhelpful’ and \`transition’ states showed negative, but not signi® cant, associations in both instances.

Although the survey was designed for a different pur pose, analysis of the survey data also highlighted predictors of satisfaction which were consistent with key factors identi® ed by case study interviewees. A stepwise regression of current satisfaction with systems development against NOW responses yielded ® ve variables as signi® cant determinants of satisfaction with systems development, which together accounted for over half of the total variation in satisfaction effected by the 19 variables. These ® ve variables also emerged as predictors of satisfaction based on other stepwise regressions, as discusses below.

However, one point of caution must be made in discussing the results. In all regressions reported, the adjusted $R ^ { 2 }$ was no more than 0.12. Despite the fact that the regression models themselves are signi® cant, the amount of variation explained is very low. There could be a number of explanations for this. First, there may well be other variables contributing to a measure as generalized as \`satisfaction with systems develop ment’. Secondly, the responses did not discriminate widely on a ® ve point scale. Thirdly, all respondents were IS staff who might not be expected to be the most objective assessors of organizational satisfaction with IS development. Nevertheless, the signi® cance of the regression models and the predictors of satisfac tion identi® ed, are consistent with case study ® ndings, hence are included in the discussion.

## Discussion

Both the case study and the survey results support the relevance of socialization to user± IS exist simultaneously. This mutually reinforcing nature of the integration arrangements is supported by Skyrme (1992).

This author found that companies successful in their use of IT used more bridging mechanisms to bring IS and the business together, and that the use of several such mechanisms in combination has a synergistic effect.

The research therefore provides practical suggestions on arrangements which improve user± IS integration. Against this background, the discussion following is aimed at teasing out issues that stand out from the research model and need to be addressed directly. Speci® cally, discussion focuses on:

(1) ® ndings which challenge current thinking and practice;

(2) an emergent order of implementation of the model elements;

(3) contributions of the research; and

(4) limitations and implications for future research.

## Challenges to current thinking and practice

The results of the survey indicate that the research could make signi® cant contribution to the practice of IS by providing suggestions about \`best practice’ where induction and team building processes are concerned. Additionally, the research ® ndings raises cointegration, and provide evidence which upholds the research propositions made. Normative, Organizational and Work Group arrangements all make positive contributions to user± IS integration, and poor implementation of one of these arrangements is compensated for by good implementation of the other two.

The concept of \`goal’ states was also endorsed by the survey results, and in most cases the survey respondents supported the arrangements de® ned as \`goal states based on the cases studies. In addition to the individual contributions made by each \`goal’ state, the results suggest that a collective power is accrued when a number of \`goal’ states raise questions which challenge current IS thinking.

Based on the theoretical foundation developed, and the supporting evidence provided by the case studies, induction processes shared between IS and non-IS recruits, involving exposure to the main operations of the business and encouraging group activity (for example, project assignments) are more effective in forging good user± IS relationships. However, some organizations are moving from shared basic orientation programmes to IS only programmes. One possible explanation for the predominance of basic orientation programmes for IS staff (rather than intensive) might be that the organizations surveyed only have basic orientation procedures for all employees, whether IS or not. This would suggest the lack of a strong corporate culture (Pascale, 1985).

The implications of the research model developed is that formal team building sessions in a newly formed team makes greater contribution to developing effective user± IS relationships than does the development of team skills through personal/career development training. Team skills possessed by particular individuals may allow mutual adjustment and promotion of team spirit to occur faster, but they do not make direct contributions to the process itself, whereas formal team building sessions do. Team social events are also more effective for the same reason. Awareness of the processes facilitated, and the importance of such processes to effective user± IS relationships can provide guidance on practices to implement in the future.

The ® rst challenge to current IS thinking concerns the emphasis on hybrids. The research suggests that having the right \`blend’ of people is more important than having business and IS skills embodied in a single individual. Skyrme (1992) also reported a dispute about the need for hybrids on cross-functional teams versus having a \`hybrid team’ whose overall composition has a wide representation and a full complement of skills. Additionally, the trend towards implementing hybrid policies is questioned, the implication being that cross-fertilization experience for IS staff should be based on individual choice. The issue of re-entry to the host function and loss of currency has already been recognized (for example, Skyrme, 1992), but still poses a constraint on implementing cross-fertilization processes in practice.

The wisdom of contracting out rather than including contractors (if necessary) on an in-house development team is also challenged by the research, based on the need for close proximity and informal communication. However, this challenge must be tempered by reference to project context. The case study projects were by de® nition, projects which involved signi® cant organizational change, and it is in this context that the task of integration becomes more critical, as well as more complex. It may be that contracting-out is a feasible arrangement for other project contexts.

## Implementing the model of integration

The consistency with which key factors emerge as signi® cant determinants of satisfaction suggest an order of implementation of the model elements. It could be argued that these factors are not necessarily more signi® cant than other model elements, but are easier to implement because of the level at which they are introduced. Each of these factors is either local to the IS department or to project teams and therefore represent integration arrangements which can be immediately and effectively implemented in organizations. Multidisciplinary teams for example, are local to the

IS and user departments involved, and have a direct impact on the attitudes and perceptions of those working on the team. This may in turn have a knockon effect, in¯ uencing the attitudes and preconceptions of the host departments to which the team members belong. In so doing, they lay the foundation for successful implementation of other integration arrangements.

However, while these new attitudes may ® lter to other members of the respective departments, the absence of additional methods at organizational level may create a distinction between those on the team and their respective departments, as was observed in some of the case studies. Thus, while these factor may be useful for explaining team relationships and project effectiveness, they do not provide suf® cient explanation for the general user± IS relationships that were found to exist in the case study organizations. This suggests that the key factors may be a starting point, but certainly do not represent the end of the journey. The fact that key predictors of satisfaction primarily occur at work group level, may therefore be a re¯ ection of a general lack of attention to factors at the corporate level, especially where normative arrangements are concerned. This suggestion is supported by the survey ® nding that most respondents are predominantly in the \`transitional’ states for current normative arrangements.

On this basis, it may be more useful to visually represent the model elements in terms of the level at which they operate predominantly. For example the credibility gained from successful delivery of business bene® ts may lay the foundation for including the IS director on the company board. Intuitively, a time-lag will exist between the implementation of the arrangements and experiencing the effect. The main point, however, is that efforts to achieve user± IS integration should be consistent and holistic in approach. It should be noted that there is no signi® cance to the ordering of the arrangements within each circle.

## Limitations and implication for future research

Selection of case study companies was deliberately biased towards large successful corporations implementing projects involving signi® cant organizational change. The bias towards larger organizations was translated into the survey sample by the choice of databases used for company addresses. The effect, therefore, is that the ® ndings are probably biased towards medium to large corporations. Firm size, in terms of the number of employees may effect several organizational processes crucial to the research (Attewell and Rule, 1991). Speci® cally, socialization may occur differently in small ® rms. It may be that the emphasis on challenging preconceptions and attitudes renders the concept just as relevant but perhaps easier to implement in small organizations. For example, visibility based on close proximity and increased opportunity for casual contact may be a natural process in small organizations.

Emphasis on projects involving signi® cant organizational change potentially limits the applicability of the ® ndings to such project contexts. Since each additional integration arrangement incurs extra costs (Lawrence and Lorsch, 1967; Thompson, 1967), it may be that the suggested \`goal’ states are unnecessary luxuries for other project contexts. However, given the current urgency amongst researchers and practitioners alike to discover ways in which IT can be used to gain significant and sustainable competitive advantage, the key systems being developed will by de® nition involve signi® cant organizational change. It is in this context that the task of integration is more critical as well a more complex, hence it is in this context that the integration arrangements suggested by the research are most relevant.

The emerging trend towards outsourcing raises potential concerns about the applicability of the research. The ® ndings suggest that in-house develop ment is the preferred arrangement for the project context investigated, and that where contractors are used, they should be brought in to the organization as part of the development team. This may be a biased ® nding, given that only in-house development teams were investigated. However, there is currently no signi® cant research suggesting how such projects would fair if outsourced. This is one obvious area for future research. Speci® cally, does outsourcing effectively address the problem of the culture gap or is it merely deferring poor relationships between user and IS departments to an interorganizational level? Is contracting-out a feasible option for projects involving signi® cant organizational change? The contrasting ® ndings about the usefulness of proprietary methodology may also be a useful area for further investigation.

Another possible area for future exploration emerging from this research is the question of whether or not socialization can alleviate \`bof® n’ characteristics. In other words, did IS specialists develop characteristics of poor communication and lack of interest in/commitment to the business as a result of the way they have been managed, or because of innate, inherent characteristics of the individuals concerned? Essentially this means introducing the nature versus nurture debate into the IS arena.

The potential impact of more \`user-friendly’ development tools, (for example CASE and Object Oriented design) and off-the-shelf packages has not been considered in this research. It could be argued that with such tools and packages, the need for very technically competent IS staff, and associated \`bof® n’ tendencie is removed, with a concomitant decrease in, if not alleviation of, the problem of the culture gap. Eventually, this could render the proposed integration arrangements redundant. However, similar claims have been made by a succession of development tools, such as fourth generation languages, and various software packages over the years, but the effects of poor user± IS interaction still abound. Also, given the project of signi® cant organizational change, it is not likely that a packaged solution will be suf® cient, and even if one is used, it may need tailoring to the speci® c organizational context. Furthermore, the survey data was not limited to projects involving signi® cant organizational change and the integration arrangements suggested still emerged as relevant to system development success.

One ® nal point about the applicability of the research related to timing. If the culture gap is the result of a generation gap, then the more positive attitudes of later generations brought up in this IT era will eventually erode the effects of the culture gap. However, given the signi® cance of current development efforts, the problem cannot be left to time. Also, preconceptions may continue to be handed down from generation to generation if measures are not taken to challenge the relevance of the stories and myths that perpetuate them.

## Contributions

The unique contributions of this research derive from the distinctive approach taken to tackle the issue of the user± IS culture gap. Based on the proposition that the destructive effects of the culture gap resulted from deep-seated attitudes and preconceptions that have remained unaddressed over the years, the research sought to identify integration mechanisms that were successful in effecting positive attitudinal changes, and improved user± IS relationships. Unlike previous approaches which have emphasized rationality, the issue of the culture gap was viewed using a more complex model of human beings and organizational life, embracing concepts of culture, interests, con¯ ict and power. This facilitated the development of a richer model of integration which has been supported and endorsed by the ® ndings of the study.

The speci® c contributions of the research are threefold. It provides:

A challenge ± to confront the culture gap. Rather than accepting the suggested inevitability of poor user± IS relationships based on individual differences, the attitudes and preconceptions should be confronted directly, with the expectation that the negative effects will be removed. Indeed, the very appropriateness of the term \`culture gap’ should be questioned. The research suggests that \`culture gap’ should be replaced by a term re¯ ecting what it really is ± an excuse for, rather than the cause of ineffective working relationships.

A context ± for implementing previously suggested integration mechanisms. By focusing attention on the symbolic, expressive function of integration arrangements as well as the instrumental function, the research provides a context for understanding why previous methods have and have not worked. For example, a historic neglect of the social context provides some explanation for the low level of user participation which other researchers have found. Attention to the expressive role of certain arrangements also cautions against the assumption that mere appointment of project champions, sponsors and account managers is suf® cient to accomplish the desired integration. By addressing the psychological climate and social context of development project teams, the research therefore provides a foundation for the effective implementation of other integration mechanisms previously proposed.

A course ± of action giving practical suggestions for the future. This research provides empirica evidence of the relationship between socialization practices and improved user± IS integration, and gives practical suggestions on overcoming the user± IS culture gap and achieving effective relationships. By identifying \`goal’ arrangements and synergistic effects, the research provides a course of action and a direction for the future. Using Schwartz and Davis’ s (1981) terminology, this research provides a means of adjusting the culture to reduce the amount of risk associated with developing systems involving signi® cant organizational change. Additionally, it offers practical suggestions on how to move to later, mature stages of proposed growth models by strengthening bridges between users and IS (Gibson and Nolan, 1974) and achieving integrated harmonious relationships (Galliers and Sutherland, 1991). For practitioners, the speci® c implications are: (i) confront the culture gap; (ii) implement key factors immediately; (iii) engender a commitment to business objectives amongst IS staff and (iv) treat IS staff as \`One of Us’ .

## Conclusions

This research provides empirical evidence of the relevance and usefulness of the concept of socialization to creating effective working relationships between IS pro fessionals and their business counterparts, and overcoming the user± IS culture gap. By focusing on the three mutually reinforcing domains of socialization (normative, organizational and work group arrangements), the research identi® es preferred practices which are positively correlated with improved user± IS integration, and project group effectiveness. As integration mechanisms are costly, alternate arrangements may be just as feasible in other contexts. However, given the context of this research, the practices identi® ed provide a mutually reinforcing environment which results in improved effectiveness of user± IS relationships on

## Bridging the user± IS gap

projects involving signi® cant organizational change. It may be that the key factors identi® ed ± multidisciplinary teams, credible project managers, short delivery timescales, success criteria based on delivering business bene® ts, close physical proximity, and team building processes ± are suf® cient mechanisms to ensure integration and effective work relationships, but attention to the entire social context provides a more coherent approach and lays the foundation for other mechanisms previously suggested in the literature to be more effective.

This research has suggested that if attention is given to the areas identi® ed in the list of model elements, and consistent efforts towards achieving suggested \`goal’ states made, the destructive effects of the user± IS culture gap can become a thing of the past.

## Appendix A: De® nition of the model elements

Operationalization of the variable \`NORMATIVE ARRANGEMENTS’ included investigation of:

N1: the presence of a corporate culture in terms of commonly held beliefs and values evidenced, for example, in the popularity of certain slogans, and the extent to which this has permeated the IS function;

N2: the selection criteria used to recruit IS development staff, in terms of the emphasis placed on technical, business and interpersonal skills;

N3: the opportunity for IS development staff to gain exposure to the business through induction processes and other types of socialization practices (for example, inclusion in company social events) and the extent to which organizational values are effectively transferred;

N4: the opportunity for IS development staff to attend personal/career development training courses, with an emphasis on developing managerial and interpersonal skills;

N5: the opportunity to gain crossfunctional experience and the feasibility/relevance for IS development staff.

## Operationalization of the variable \`ORGANIZATIONAL ARRANGEMENTS’ included investigation of:

O1: the structure of the IS department in general and the systems development group in particular in terms of how \`user facing’ versus internally focused the structure encouraged the department members to be;

O2: the physical location of the IS development function, and the amount of informal interaction with users encouraged by the degree of proximity;

O3: the skills (technical, business, interpersonal) implicitly rewarded by career paths and role models;

O4: the reward systems used for evaluating and motivating systems development personnel (for example, if/how user/IS cooperation is encouraged).

Operationalization of the variable \`MANAGEMENT OF THE WORK GROUP’ included investigation of:

W2: the presence and type of team building processes, whether through formal training sessions and/or social events;

W3: the process, rules and procedures used for con¯ ict resolution;

W4: the physical location of team members in terms of proximity, and the type of communication (formal or informal) predominantly used;

W5: the process of allocation of roles and responsibilities in terms of the use of speci® c project methodology;

W6: the criteria used to select project managers (for example, technical skills, user background, project management skills);

W7: the presence of superordinate goals which provided a common sense of purpose and shared values amongst team members.
