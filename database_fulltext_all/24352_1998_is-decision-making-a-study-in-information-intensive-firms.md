Journal of Information Technology (1998) 13, 55± 66

# IS decision-making: a study in information-intensive ® rms

MARK J. PROUDLOCK, BOB PHELPS AND PAUL R. GAMBLE

Sems Research Institute for Information Systems, Surrey European Management School, University of Surrey, Guildford, GU2 5XH, UK

This paper looks at IS decisions within small professional organizations. The study investigates three aspects of IS decision processes drawn from the literature. These focus upon organizational approaches to planning, risk management and product selection, and the factors that impact upon each process. The results of an exploratory survey undertaken in nine ® rms are presented and compared with the ® ndings of existing research. One expectation is supported; two prove unsupported.

## Introduction

The use of information systems (IS) to achieve competitive advantage is expected to be particularly important in information-intensive ® rms (Porter and Millar, 1985). A category of ® rms that clearly falls within this de® nition is professional practices. These ® rms, such as consultancies and lawyers, are staffed by a high proportion of professionally quali® ed staff and rely upon their knowledge and information to attract clients and perform their tasks. Such ® rms, with a few notable exceptions, tend to be small- or medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). Small size offers advantages to researchers since access may be easier and the number of participants in IS decisions is typically smaller. Further, being small means that many of these ® rms are at different stages of the growth cycle and thus enable comparisons between different stages of development. This study therefore concentrates on small professional ® rms in order to investigate their approach to IS decisions and to develop a framework of factors and their interactions which can guide future research.

While a vast and ever-growing body of literature exists covering all aspects of information technology in large, well established organizations, there is a lack of empirical research focusing upon IT and the small business (Doukidis et al., 1996). Even where such research has taken place, its purpose has, until recently, remained limited to identifying the levels of information technology and general computer use within small enterprises (e.g. Farhoomand and Hrycyk, 1985; Kagan et al., 1990; Naylor and Williams, 1994). If research is to understand the processes driving IT use it must investigate the decision-making processes occurring within these ® rms.

The cost of computer hardware and software has decreased dramatically while, at the same time, software has become increasingly user-friendly (Naylor and Williams, 1994). As a result, information systems have become more accessible for the small business. However \`only a few small ® rms seem able to convert this potential into practice’ (Fuller and Jenkins, 1994, p. 12). As Fuller asserts, small ® rms \`appear to suffer from numerous problems at all stages of the process of adopting IT systems’. It is evident that certain conditions, often found in the small business environment, can affect the IS decision-making process. Doukidis et al. (1996, p. 191) describes the main characteristics of such an environment as centralized and impulsive, involving a lack of delegation and a lack of conscious planning. The authors suggest that such ® rms can undertake only short term planning due to the high degree of uncertainty within the small business environment. Further, it is asserted that the small ® rm infrastructure fails to effectively de® ne or use its control and information systems, lacks standard procedures, rules and formalized systems, and suffers from poor job descriptions and job responsibilities.

Other conditions that are reported frequently in the small business literature include the lack of resources at hand. Such \`resource-poverty’ (Thong et al., 1994a) includes managerial time and availability of ® nance as well as less tangible assets such as knowledge and experience (d’Amboise and Muldowney, 1986; Kapp and Voora, 1992; Cragg and King, 1993). Such factors are believed to impinge upon a ® rm’s ability to plan ahead in terms of business and information systems strategy, as well as to make rational technology procurement decisions.

Given the conditions, what are the key IS decisions which need to be taken by small ® rms? One is IS planning; the literature abounds in articles promulgating the value of IS planning. Another is risk management; this is seen as an essential element in successful IS decisions (Phelps, 1996; Willcocks and Margetts, 1993). Technology procurement is the third; these decisions made by the small ® rm represent a signi® cant investment. These three aspects of IS decision-making represent critical aspects of IS management as presented in the literature. The current study therefore concentrates on formal information systems planning, information systems risk management and the product selection process.

Data were gathered through an exploratory study involving in-depth interviews with nine small professional practices consisting of ® ve architecture practices and four law ® rms. It is not claimed that the results are necessarily representative of IS decision-making in small ® rms belonging to either service sector. However, the results serve to provide an informative insight into current IS decision-making practices in the light of existing research.

## Three critical IS decisions

## (1) IS planning

Surveys undertaken in large organizations have consistently shown IS planning to be one of the top priorities of information systems and business managers (Niederman, et al., 1991; Galliers, et al., 1994). However, the literature suggests that small ® rms are failing to follow the example set by their larger counterparts and remain, in terms of modern management practice, in the dark ages (Doukidis et al., 1994). While it is acknowledged that the formal planning techniques of large organizations are not necessarily appropriate for small ® rms (Raymond, 1985; Kelmar and Noy, 1990), Malone (1985) reported over a decade ago that small organizations which had adopted more formal planning systems were encountering fewer IS problems. The value of planning is further emphasized by Cragg (1990), who found IS planning to be critical to IT success.

Yet on the basis of the literature, the majority of small ® rms seem to be failing to employ formal planning systems both in terms of business and information systems planning. While Montazemi (1988) found a lack of appropriate policies for the selection and use of information resources, Cragg and King (1993, p.53) report ® nding \`few formal planning and control activities associated with the computer, or for that matter, with the business as a whole.’ Furthermore, Doukidis et al. (1996) found that where planning does take place it is generally; (1) undertaken on an ad hoc, problem basis; (2) only an activity of the owner/manager; (3) informal, sporadic and closed; and (4) in¯ uenced heavily by advice from outside sources with less skills and experience than the owner/manager. He concludes; \`while IT is usually associated with a systematic approach to management and decision-making and its introduction requires careful planning . . . much SB (small business) management practice is based on short-term, informal, ad hoc lines’ (p. 192).

## (2) Information systems risk management

Conditions such as severe ® nancial constraints, a lack of in-house expertise, and a short-term management perspective imposed by an unstable environment, result in small ® rms shouldering a signi® cantly greater risk in IS implementation than their larger, resourcerich counterparts (Welsh and White, 1981; Thong et al., 1994b). The lack of understanding of computers and internal IS expertise is a problem in itself (Lees and Lees, 1987; Gable, 1991). The cost of failure in terms of implementing inappropriate technology may prove fatal for an organization \`lacking adequate ® nancial and productive cushioning’ (Doukidis et al., 1996, p. 192).

Information risk management strategies include buy rather than make approaches, following established trends and seeking advice. As Sherer (1993) asserts, the major advantage of purchasing off-the-shelf software, aside from providing economies of scale, is that it reduces risk signi® cantly during and following implementation. Chau (1995) and Malone (1985) report that a top priority of small business managers in selecting hardware and software is the popularity of the product under evaluation: managers were far more likely to purchase industry-standard and marketleading technology.

The engagement of external expertise also proves to be an important factor in terms of risk mitigation (Senn and Gibson; 1981). The employment of such external professional support can overcome knowledge gaps and guide ® rms in implementing appropriate IT. Doukidis et al. (1994), giving credence to the assertions of Schleich et al. (1990) and Wroe (1987), found that over half of the respondents involved in his study obtained external advice during the selection of hardware and software. Doukidis et al. (1994) also report on the use of neighbouring organizations, training companies and support organizations for advice. Other research suggests, however, that small ® rm managers are more likely to obtain advice from informal sources including immediate family, friends and acquaintances, who essentially have little or no knowledge of the business (Malone, 1985; Montsed, 1993). Such strategy essentially is ¯ awed and can increase the risk of implementing IT that fails to meet the ® rm’s needs. Furthermore, Naylor and Williams (1994) report that 40% of respondents in their study preferred to research and select their own IS rather than depend upon outside help.

## (3) Product selection decision-making

The nature of the supply environment is such that organizations are faced with a bewildering array of sources, confusing jargon, and technology which is evolving at a rapid and unnerving rate of change (Geisler, 1992). How, then, do small ® rms select IS products? While in some ® rms the motivation is based upon rational ground such as an evaluation of the costs and bene® ts, competitive pressures, or client expectations (Coopers and Lybrand, 1985), other ® rms purchase systems for ill-founded reasons such as their popularity, or their perception as a management prerequisite (Farhoomand and Hrycyk, 1985; Malone, 1985). Bene® ts are frequently anticipated which fail to materialize (Capaldo et al., 1995).

Huff and Munro (1985) identi® ed six phases of a rational IT selection process. These include; (1) awareness ± the technology is brought to the attention of the organization, but an information gap exists; (2) interest ± missing information is obtained; (3) evaluation ± the use of the technology is considered and a decision is made whether or not to try it; (4) trial ± the technology is tested in order to assess its appropriateness in terms of current and future needs; (5) implementation ± the trial is extended to full use of the technology within limited areas of the organization; (6) diffusion ± the innovation is extended to other areas within the organization.

Contrasted with rational selection is the opportunistic model. No formal planning takes place and the identi-® cation of technology is seemingly unsystematic. Thus, fewer resources are needed in terms of expenditure or specialist planning and IS skills. In his study of 79 small ® rms, Montazemi (1988) reported that only 9% of ® rms employed a full information requirements evaluation. On the basis of these ® ndings, he concludes that the opportunistic model most accurately describes the small ® rm IS selection process. Doukidis et al. (1994) and Chau (1995) support Montazemi’s ® ndings.

## Contingent variables

Internal resources of the ® rm have been identi® ed as contingent factors. Scholhammer and Kuriloff (1979) cite the lack of specialized in-house knowledge as affecting IS planning. Low levels of IS knowledge and experience within that structure represent an impediment to the selection process (Farhoomand and Hrycyk, 1985). Similarly, Cragg and King (1993) found that a lack of knowledge was the strongest inhibiting factor to the evolution of IS, with few improvements to existing IT infrastructures taking place in ® rms de® cient in knowledge and experience. Heikkila et al. (1991) found that ® rms with prior experience with computers were more likely to evaluate technology in advance, thus improving risk assessment.

Managerial time is a resource that is scarce in the small ® rm. Thus, in many cases it may quite simply be that owner/managers are so tied up with the dayto-day functioning of their organization that they do not have time to stand back and plan a strategy for the foreseeable future (Palvia et al., 1994). The level of ® nancial resources available within the ® rm also affects the selection process. Cragg and King (1993) report a ® rm that had been hit hard by the recessionary economy and was doing all it could simply to survive. As a result, the owner had no funds available for the purpose of upgrading the organization’s information system.

Size is also identi® ed as a decisive factor (Sabherwal and King, 1995), with decreasing levels of planning taking place the smaller organization (Cash et al., 1998). Size of the organization also appears to in¯ uence the product selection and risk assessment decisions. Doukidis et al. (1994) found, for example, that the smaller of the ® rms researched failed to realise the evaluative bene® ts of systematic techniques such as feasibility studies as part of the decision-making process. However, it should be noted also that because the levels of resources generally decrease with the size of the organization in question (Thong et al., 1994b), it is possible that small ® rm inability to undertake a systematic and formalized approach to product selection decision-making is more directly contingent upon the lack of in-house resources than upon size alone.

Although not directly addressed in the literature, it is proposed in this study that for professional ® rms the ® eld of the ® rm may also in¯ uence IS decisions. Cultural and educational differences are apparent between different professions and it appears credible that such differences may impact the decision processes used within the ® rm.

Finally, the nature of the small business environment is identi® ed as a barrier to a rational approach to planning and IS selection. As Fuller asserts, \`the strategic future of many small businesses is unpredictable and therefore largely unplannable. The owners know this and have a considerable aversion to planning’ (Fuller, 1996, p. 28). Thus, it is proposed that the degree to which ® rms perceive their environment as stable will in¯ uence their approach to planning, risk management and product selection.

## Methodology

While IT has become commonplace in most organizations, the bene® ts afforded to \`knowledge-intensive organizations (i.e. organizations reliant on a high degree of technical or professional skills) are claimed to be among the most signi® cant (Capaldo et $a l . ,$ 1995). Professional ® rms are prototypical examples of knowledge intensive organizations. This, together with the lack of small professional organization speci® c research in the ® eld of IT, motivated the choice of professional organizations for this study.

Two professions were chosen for exploratory investigation: architecture and law. No research (with the exception of that limited to identifying levels of IT and general computer use) has been undertaken in either profession. Criteria for choice of ® rm were a workforce of 50 or less (the 1989 Companies Act de® nition of small company), geographical location (Surrey, UK) and willingness to cooperate in the study. Furthermore, ® rms had to belong to the respective professional bodies, excluding ® rms such as architectural services. Willingness to participate in the study was obtained through approach phone calls. Such contact was targeted at either a partner or practice manager within the ® rm. It was proposed that individuals in these roles would have the necessary insight into the running of their ® rm to enable optimum data collection during the interview process.

Four law ® rms and ® ve architectural practices participated at the interview stage. The position of individuals interviewed within each ® rm was dependent to a degree on the size and structure of the ® rm. As Table 1 shows, in larger ® rms, someone other than a partner generally undertook the interview, while in smaller ® rms, a senior partner was the most frequent contact.

A semistructured interview technique was employed to ensure relevant questions were asked while enabling more in-depth investigation of issues speci® c to individual ® rms. As Table 1 shows, interviews lasted between 45 and 130 min depending on the time constraints on the interviewee, the levels of in-house IT and interest in IT, and the willingness and enthusiasm of the interviewee to impart information about their organization. Each interview was taped and transcribed prior to analysis.

The ® ndings obtained from the nine individual ® rms covers a varied spectrum in terms of organizational approaches to IS planning, IS risk management and IS product selection decision-making (see Table 2). As a result, initial attempts to view data from a hypothesis testing positivist perspective proved inappropriate. Instead, the analysis section follows a multiple case study approach, brie¯ y focusing upon the processes occurring within each ® rm. A ® nal analysis will consider emergent patterns in the context of existing literature.

## Firm $\mathbf { L } _ { 2 }$

Firm $\mathrm { L } _ { 2 }$ was a law ® rm employing 46 staff in total. It had a strong awareness of the potential of IT and sound ideas as to how computerized systems could increase internal ef® ciency and accountability. The ® rm viewed its use of IT predominantly from an accounting perspective (perhaps due to the ® nancial controller’s dual role as in-house IT expert), one example being the proposed implementation of software to measure the profitability of individual clients and fee-earners. While seemingly knowledgeable regarding the management application of IT, ® rm $\mathrm { L } _ { 2 }$ had only ® ve years of IT experience. Firm $\mathrm { L } _ { 2 }$ described itself as holding back during the recession but claimed that things were currently going very well. ## Firm $\mathbf { L } _ { 3 }$

Firm $\mathrm { L } _ { 3 }$ was a law ® rm employing 49 staff. IT was implemented 5± 6 years ago and a number of signi® cant developments have been made since. The ® rm was now totally networked with accounting, management accounting, time recording and payment handling functions all computerized. The senior partner interviewed believed IT to be effective within the ® rm and could see where further IT developments could bring about cost savings via task de-skilling.

Formal business and IT planning were fundamental to ® rm ${ \mathrm { L } _ { 3 } } ^ { \mathfrak { s } }$ approach. An IT committee had been organized consisting of three partners as well as other staff with an interest in IT. An external IT consultant was also employed to assist and guide the planning process. Solid plans were in place regarding future IT developments.

Firm $\mathrm { L } _ { 3 }$ followed a rational and considered approach to IT developments that, in turn, signi® cantly reduced its exposure to risk. The ® rm belonged to a group of 11 law practices that shared their experiences of IT. Firm ${ \mathrm { L } _ { 3 } } ^ { \mathfrak { s } }$ senior partner had thus encountered ® rms which had \`leapt in early’ with IT only to suffer significant problems. But we always cut a better deal by doing that in terms of money and have always made the right decisions in terms of what we’ ve bought.’

Cost-bene® t analyses and reference to the IT literature were part and parcel of ® rm $\mathrm { L } _ { 3 } \mathrm { \bar { s } }$ approach to IT product selection. The IT committee perceived the number of disparate lega software products available as a risk in itself and thus purchased market-leading off-the-shelf software where possible. Firm $\mathrm { L } _ { 4 }$ was a law ® rm employing 13 staff in total. It specialized predominantly in debt recovery. ## Firm $\mathbf { A } _ { 1 }$

Firm $\mathbf { A } _ { 1 }$ was a small architecture ® rm employing three staff including the senior partner. Despite its small size, ® nancial resources were not a problem for the ® rm. The ® rm operated in a niche market (acoustic design) and proved successful in doing so.

The senior partner claimed to lack IT knowledge. While computer-aided design (CAD) had been installed for over a year, the senior partner believed he was in touch with less than 15% of its full potential. The time constraints with which he was faced were cited as a signi® cant barrier to learning. No planning took place within the ® rm; work was accepted as it arose and in-house developments, including those IT related, occurred if and when they were perceived to be necessary.

The implementation and development of IT had followed an incremental and opportunistic route. This began six years ago with the purchase of a word processor and had now reach a stage where design was fully automated to the extent of exchanging ® les electronically via the Internet with other contributors to the design process. No consultant was approached at any stage during research, selection and implementation. The senior partner undertook all IT research informally, reading available articles about IT and how other ® rms within the industry were utilizing technology. He also referred to friends and acquaintances when seeking advice. Industry standard hardware and off-the-shelf software were purchased via mail order and any subsequent maintenance or software problems were tackled by an acquaintance with a background in IT. The senior partner admitted that the bene® ts resulting from IT were very different to those predicted.

## Firm $\mathbf { A } _ { 2 }$

Firm $\mathbf { A } _ { 2 }$ was an architecture ® rm employing between 17 and 22 staff depending upon workload. It had used IT in-house for eight years. The ® rm had 12 computers, none of which were networked in spite of significant duplication of software across workstations.

The senior partner described himself as learning predominantly from \`faf® ng about’. He had a comparatively developed technical IT knowledge and yet failed to see how integrating the system or spending money on training could increase ef® ciency and productivity.

Money and managerial time represented signi® cant obstacles for the ® rm. Such constraints were leveraged more so as a result of varying and unpredictable workloads. ## Firm $\mathbf { A } _ { 3 }$

Firm $\mathbf { A } _ { 3 }$ was an architecture ® rm employing 7 staff. Financial constraints were ever present effecting the ® rm’s ability to purchase new technology. The senior partner asserted that the ® rm had suf® cient work for 6 months, but the future was uncertain thereafter.

## Firm $\mathbf { A } _ { 4 }$

Firm $\mathbf { A } _ { 4 }$ was the smallest ® rm researched. Specializing in architecture, it employed only two full-time staff including the senior partner. The senior partner was well aware of the potentia bene® ts afforded by IT. Prior to founding his own ® rm he had worked in a large architecture practice that had implemented computers as early as 1983. As he asserted, \`I can see that information technology would be an enormous help. If I had the money I would go out and buy the technology because it would make me 50% more productive overnight.’

Currently, the senior partner uses a 286 with a basic spreadsheet for accounts. This was purchased second hand three years ago after an Amstrad PCW9512, borrowed from a friend, broke.

## Firm $\mathbf { A } _ { 5 }$

Firm $\mathbf { A } _ { 5 }$ was the largest of the architecture practices, employing 49 staff. While survival had been the primary goal for the last ® ve years, the ® rm was currently operating successfully and planning on expanding in the near future. Fluctuating workload was put forward as the greatest barrier to achieving objectives. In the past ® rm $\mathbf { A } _ { 5 }$ had dif® culty obtaining suf® cient work; at the time of interview it had suf® cient work.

The ® rm had an IT history going back as far as 1981. According to the associate interviewed, the ® rm was beyond the stage of simply automating existing processes. IT culture, it was claimed, had permeated all areas of the organization.

Firm $\mathbf { A } _ { 5 }$ employed a high degree of formal planning. Plans were formulated at board level and were disseminated openly throughout the organization. Several strategies were in place for the future. IT planning was part and parcel of this business planning with a de® - nite strategy in place for the following 12 months. This plan included a better networking of the current system and the implementation of internal e-mail. Firm ${ \bf A } _ { 5 }$ was proactive in its planning and implementation strategies. In general, technology was in place ahead of competitors and before being requested by clients.

The ® rm’s approach to product selection was cautious and built upon a historic use of a speci® c software product. This software was off-the-shelf and had a signi® cant share of the industry market. Technology strategy and selection was further facilitated and guided by the use of two external supplier in consultancy roles.

However, planning remains de® cient or nonexistent in a signi® cant proportion of ® rms.

## IS decision-making

Two interesting patterns emerged in terms of the ® rms employing planning. First, the ® ndings show a strong split between law and architecture ® rms with all law ® rms having formal or semiformal planning while only one architecture ® rm succeeded in employing planning at any level. This supports the contingent effect of profession and suggests that educational, attitudinal or organizational culture differences exist between the two professions.

Firm size can also be interpreted as playing a role with four out of ® ve larger ® rms employing a more formal approach to business and IT planning. The current study found that, in general, the smaller the organization, the greater the constraints faced in terms of ® nancial resources, managerial time and planning experience. Such constraints were preventing smaller ® rms from planning proactively, supporting the ® ndings of Sabherwal and King (1995) who found that decreasing levels of planning were undertaken the smaller the organization. The ® ndings of Fuller (1996) are also con-® rmed. The smaller architecture ® rms tended to perceive their environment as unstable and were thus averse to planning, instead operating their ® rms on a day-to-day basis and reacting to situations if and when the need arose.

There is virtually a complete correspondence between formal business planning and formal IS planning. This suggests an obvious though untested hypothesis that planning for the small ® rm combines elements of IS, ® nance, human resources and other functions, often done by the same person, which tend to be segmented in larger organizations.

While it is dif® cult to draw de® nitive conclusions from such a small and potentially idiosyncratic sample, the ® ndings suggest that as a ® rm increases in size (thus potentially freeing up more managerial time) and internal resources in terms of knowledge and ® nance, its ability and willingness to plan ahead increases.

## IS risk management

The current research supports the existing literature in ® nding a wide spectrum of risk management methodologies employed by small ® rms. However, patterns again emerge from the ® ndings.

Supporting the ® ndings of Doukidis et al. (1994), Schleich et al. (1990) and Wroe (1987), over half of the ® rms interviewed had employed the services of outside consultants, vendors and suppliers during the selection and implementation of IT. These included all four law ® rms, and architecture practices ${ \bf A } _ { 3 }$ and $\mathbf { A } _ { 5 } .$ Two of the law ® rms also sought advice from impartial support bodies. While one might expect the ® rms employing outside assistance to be those with little experience and self-perceived IT knowledge, this proved only to be true of ® rm $\mathbf { A } _ { 3 }$ . The other ® rms al perceived themselves to be knowledgeable regarding IT and each had signi® cant experience in its implementation and operation.

Other contingent factors may also affect the decision-making process in terms of employing external support. With the exception of $\mathbf { A } _ { 3 }$ and $\mathrm { L } _ { 4 }$ the ® rms employing support described themselves as resource rich. All bar $\mathbf { A } _ { 3 }$ perceived the environment as stable. While ${ \bf A } _ { 3 }$ represents something of an anomaly, it was evident that the sheer lack of in-house IT knowledge combined with severe constraints on managerial time had necessitated external support. Those ® rms choosing not to employ outside help preferred instead to undertake personal research into IT and rely on advice from friends, family and acquaintances. This was in keeping with the ® ndings of Malone (1985) and Naylor and Williams (1994).

Again, it is dif® cult to draw decisive conclusions from such a small sample. However, on the basis of the ® ndings it would seem that the larger and more resource rich the ® rm, the more willing it is to employ external expertise while smaller resource-poor ® rms are reluctant or unable to engage such support. The decision seems to be taken with little regard for actual levels of in-house IT knowledge.

The ® ndings of Chau (1995) and Malone (1985) were overwhelmingly supported with all ® rms (resources willing) choosing to purchase industry-standard and market-leading technology. While those ® rms engaging external support were generally advised to follow this route, those undertaking their own research ultimately reached the same conclusion. This was comparatively easy for the architects, as one particular CAD product had a signi® cantly greater market share than its closest competitor. The only CAD-user choosing not to use this software was ® rm $\mathbf { A } _ { 5 }$ which was building instead on its historical use of a speci® c software product.

Those ® rms that had employed external support also included maintenance and software support within their individual contracts. None of the other ® rms had any professional support whatsoever. All either chose to rely on acquaintances for support or were prepared to shoulder the risk of IT failure.

## IS product selection decision-making process

While the ® ndings of Montazemi (1988), Doukidis et $a l .$ (1994) and Chau (1995) suggest a rational and methodical approach to IT product selection and implementation to be atypical amongs small ® rms, the current study found evidence of such an approach amongs several of the ® rms researched.

Of interest is the emergent pattern of ® rms following such an approach; these include all four law ® rms and architecture ® rms $\mathbf { A } _ { 3 }$ and $\mathbf { A } _ { 5 } .$ . These ® rms, as one might expect, proved to be the same ® rms that had employed external sources of advice. It seems possible, indeed probable, that sources of external expertise had imparted a methodical approach that was, in turn, followed and replicated by the ® rms involved.

These ® rms, with the exception of $\mathbf { A } _ { 3 }$ also proved to be the ® rms with the greatest knowledge and experience of IT. This reinforces Heikkila et al.’ s (1991) ® nding that ® rms with prior experience with computers are more likely to evaluate technology prior to implementation. Size can also be interpreted as an in¯ uential contingent factor with ® ve of the six largest ® rms employing a rational and methodical approach to IT selection. This supports Doukidis et al.’ s (1994) ® nding that the smaller the ® rm the greater the likelihood of failure to undertake a systematic and formalized approach to IT selection. Such inability however (as stated by Thong et al., 1994b) is inherently related to the lack of in-house knowledge and resources typical of the small ® rm.

Firms employing a more \`off-the-cuff’ opportunistic approach to IT product selection included ® rms $\mathbf { A } _ { 1 } , \mathbf { A } _ { 2 }$ and $\mathbf { A } _ { 4 } .$ None of the ® rms had evaluated, at any point, their current IT platform in terms of how it could best be improved to meet current and future needs: in essence, each ® rm implemented IT via incremental opportunistic steps if and when the need arose. The information which senior partners used arose largely from personal knowledge, individual research and the informal advice of friends and acquaintances. It is dif® cult to conclude on the basis of such a small sample reasons as to why these ® rms followed the selection approach they did. Resource constraints, managerial attitudes and failure to learn from previous interaction with IT are put forward as possibilities.

## Conclusion

Given the small size of this study, no general claims can be made about the representativeness of its ® ndings. It does however point to several tentative conclusions and indicates some future research directions.

Size, internal resources (including managerial time, availability of ® nancial resources, and IT knowledge and experience) and the nature of the small business environment are all shown to affect the way in which small ® rms approach IT planning, risk management and product selection. In general, ® rms with higher levels of resources and whose senior partners perceived their environment as more stable displayed a more systematic and proactive approach to the three decision-making processes. This supports recent literature in this area and shows resource-constraints to be a small-® rm problem that continues to bring with it wider rami® cations. While little research has been undertaken into how \`profession’ affects decisionmaking within the small ® rm, the current study also found it to be in¯ uential as a contingent factor; further research may prove fruitful.

Also supporting of recent research was the ® nding that small ® rms employ a variety of risk mitigation strategies. While in several ® rms such risk mitigation was considered and proactive, other ® rms seemingly reduced risk unwittingly.

Two primary expectations drawn from recent literature are challenged by the ® ndings of this study. These are; (1) that most small ® rms fail to employ business and IT planning; and (2) that small ® rms approach IT purchases in a manner that lacks method and rigour. This proved not to be the case. Most ® rm researched employed IT planning and followed a systematic approach to IS product selection. These exploratory ® ndings indicate that the thinking underlying existing expectations in terms of planning and product selection may be too simplistic. In particular, a limitation is that existing research is based on a static view of IS decisions. It is probable that decision processes evolve over time and with experience. In that case, we should not expect to ® nd ® xed relationships between categories of businesses and IS decision processes. The relationships will be dynamic and change with time and experience.

It is here that a further process ± learning ± may warrant attention. One might expect a small ® rm to improve its approach to decision-making processes as incremental increases in IT knowledge and experience occur through its interaction with information technology. While this seems to be an accurate proposition on the basis of the current study’s ® ndings, it is also evident that a ® rm can only improve its approach to IT planning, risk management and product selection if it learns from its interactions with IT. A possible vein of future research might look at how approaches to planning, risk management and IT product selection change over time and the presence and interaction of organizational learning and external contingent factors in that process.

While the current study provides some answers, it raises many questions. These can best be answered through longitudinal research, focusing on how decision-making processes evolve over time within the small ® rm context. Such research can provide a greater insight into the processes undertaken within small ® rms and how individual contingent factors affect approaches to IT implementation and use. It is hoped that in doing so it can identify means of facilitating the implementation of IT in small ® rms.
