# Global Research Perspectives on Electronic Commerce: Introduction to the Theme Issue

Electronic commerce (e-commerce) can be broadly described as using information technology, such as the Internet and World Wide Web (Web), to enable commercial transactions, and it is revolutionizing the way companies conduct their business. Companies use business-to-business (B2B) e-commerce applications to manage their global supply chains and market and sell their products to corporate customers. In addition, business-to-consumer (B2C) applications enable interaction with individual consumers anywhere in the world. It is truly a global phenomenon.

Some of the organizational bene® ts of e-commerce include cost reduction, enhanced customer service, access to new global markets, and enhanced potential for partnering. But these come with costs and challenges including the need for hiring or retraining of information technology personnel, pro® t uncertainty in the new global markets, perceived problems with Internet security not seen with traditional retail sales, and dif® culty in gaining competitive advantage on the Internet. The rapid growth of e-commerce is outpacing our ability to understand and predict its future. This provides many research opportunities.

In the earliest days of e-commerce there were far more Internet users in the US than the rest of the world. Approximately seven years after the introduction of Web-based applications we ® nd that 1999 is the ® rst year where the number of US and non-US Internet users is approximately equal (an estimated 54 million people in each group). And it is predicted that by 2002 there will be 88 million non-US Internet users, compared with 68 million US users. These numbers illustrate potential for growth in global B2C e-commerce revenues, projected to be US\$35.3 billion in 2002, but B2B e-commerce is even larger, projected to be US\$268 billion in 2002. These numbers illustrate the dramatic growth in e-commerce when compared with 1997 worldwide e-commerce revenues, from both B2C and B2B, of only US\$7.4 billion.

While the US market is becoming increasingly saturated ± generally those that want to, and can get online, are already there ± the world at large has a bigger base to grow from and therefore represents the greatest long-term growth opportunity. True globalization will begin to occur over the next few years, due to the following factors:

Emergence of telecommunications deregulation (with associated lower costs) in previously controlled markets.

Increased PC and modem penetration particularly in Europe and the Asia/Paci® c Rim.

The attraction of e-commerce to businesses in Europe and the Asia/Paci® c Rim looking to draw revenues from a worldwide market.

The continued popularity of online services in Europe (eStats, 1999).

As organizational and consumer interest in ecommerce grows, so des interest on the part of researchers. There has been steady growth in the number of e-commerce research papers presented at the annual Americas Conference on Information Systems (AMCIS) sponsored by the Association for Information Systems (AIS). In 1996 there was no e-commerce track. The ® rst e-commerce track was in 1997 and there were 28 papers accepted for presentation. In 1998 this number jumped to 39. Due to dramatic growth in the amount and range of e-commerce research around the world, in 1999 there were four separate e-commerce tracks. These tracks included marketing and consumer behavior in electronic markets (14 papers), economic and organizational analysis of electronic commerce (11 papers), infrastructure and technology issues for electronic commerce (8 papers), and global IT management and global electronic commerce (19 papers). In addition to the growth in research conference presentations, there are several journals that focus on e-commerce research. These include the International Journal of Electronic Commerce, the Journal of Organizational Computing and Electronic Commerce, Electronic Markets, and a new addition, the Journal of Electronic Commerce Research, which is set to publish its ® rst issue in 2000. There have also been a number of special issues on e-commerce in other research journals.

The earliest e-commerce research focussed on security and online payment issues but we now know that the scope of business activities affected is much broader. Research studies from the past two to three years typically applied economic theory to explain the growth of e-commerce, but considering consumers to be economic agents focussing only on reducing their transaction costs was too simplistic. The best current research takes an interdisciplinary perspective on e-commerce issues combining information technology and systems with theories from ® elds such as marketing, psychology, operations management, strategic management and so forth.

This theme issue of the Journal of Information Technology includes eight papers presenting e-commerce research ® ndings from a range of global perspectives including work from North America (United States), Europe (United Kingdom, Norway, and Spain), and the Asia/Paci® c Rim (Australia, New Zealand and Taiwan). The articles included in this issue take different interdisciplinary perspectives, use a variety of methodologies, and address issues related to both B2B and B2C e-commerce. Each of the papers ® t into one or more categories in the following generic framework for e-commerce research proposed by Shaw, Gardner and Thomas (1997). The framework includes ® ve main categories:

l (EC1) enterprise management,

(EC2) global electronic commerce infrastructure,

l (EC3) interface with customers,

l (EC4) linking with distributors/retailers, and

l (EC5) linking with suppliers.

Table 1 lists the papers included in this issue along with the authors’ country (region) and research framework category. What is apparent in this set of papers is that it represents a global interdisciplinary perspective on a range of e-commerce research issues.

The ® rst paper (by Davis, Buchanan-Oliver and Brodie) uses the setting of an interactive homeshopping supermarket to examine the changing role of the retailer in electronic commerce environments (ECEs). It builds on a previous conceptual inquiry that proposed a conceptual model which posits that retailers in an ECE apply a trust-based approach to consumer marketing relationships. The paper provides additional literature and empirical evidence to support the proposition that the relationship between the retailer and their customers can be de® ned by the discon® rmation of two cognitive images (virtual and real images) of the online shopping experience.

The next paper (by Ranchhod and Gurau) investigates the effects of the Internet on the structure of the classical marketing channels. It is based on an empirical study of 500 Internet commercial sites. The paper identi® es the models of Internet-enabled distribution strategy implemented by Web-active companies, and then veri® es four hypotheses related with the factors in¯ uencing the choice and implementation of a particular strategy such as characteristics of the company, its clients, and the traded product.

The third paper (by Threlkel and Kavan) presents a B2B e-commerce study of Internet-based electronic data interchange (EDI). Recently published articles re¯ ect reluctance on the part of traditional EDI users to adopt the new Internet-based EDI. Reasons for the reluctance include the newness of the Internet technology, potential Internet legislation, lack of Internet standards plus the reliability and security of data transmission within the Internet environment. This paper examines management issues related to adoption of Internet-based EDI and describes existing and potential solutions available to both new and traditional EDI users. The paper concludes with some successful Internet EDI cases.

The fourth paper (by Strader, Lin and Shaw) present ® ndings of a simulation-based study of a B2B e-commerce issue, information sharing in convergent assembly (commonly associated with automobile and aerospace production) supply chains. The paper identi® es the mechanisms (business processes) required for effective supply chain management and the information system components and current technology needed to support these mechanisms. The impact of B2B e-commerce (enabling ef® cient information sharing) is evaluated by simulating supply chain performance, including inventory costs and order ful® llment cycle time, under various information-sharing strategies supported by the proposed infrastructure.

The ® fth paper (by Methlie and Nysveen) addresses the issue: How can banks retain loyal customers when moving to electronic banking? Previous work has identi® ed determinants of customer loyalty, but these studies were done in traditional markets. In this paper the authors focus on customer loyalty in online banking. The ® ndings indicate that determinants of loyalty are similar to those in the physical marketplace. However, customer satisfaction is found to have the most signi® cant impact followed by brand reputation. Implications of these ® nding on bank marketing strategies are discussed.

The sixth paper (by Sauer and Burton) describes a pilot Internet retailing project by one of Australia’s leading department stores. The case is used to describe lessons learned about Internet retailing and pilot projects. The department store’s decision whether to adopt electronic retailing is analyzed in terms of the opportunities and threats that face them and the capabilities required for success. The conclusion that is reached is that as yet there is no obvious place for established department stores on the Internet.

The seventh paper (by Subirana and Carvajal, to be published in the March 2000 issue) contends that electronic markets on the Internet do not have a disintermediating effect, but have the opposite effect, resulting in the increase of intermediaries. The authors introduce transaction streams which model how transactions are being conducted and help explain the types of new intermediaries that are appearing on the Internet. Mechanisms are described by which companies are exploring ways of extending transaction streams. To illustrate the model, the authors analyze transaction streams in the insurance industry and provide examples related to con® dence in electronic commerce.

The ® nal paper (by Pisanias and Willcocks) provides an examination of various broking markets that reveals signi® cant differences in the implications of e-commerce as a technological innovation. Drawing upon participant observation research conducted in 1998± 99 in the ship-broking market, the paper explores the limitations of the technological determinist perspective and puts forward a model to explore the changing nature and dynamics of market relationships generated by information ¯ ows via the Internet. Application of the model helps to locate reasons for slow adoption of the Internet in ship-broking markets.
