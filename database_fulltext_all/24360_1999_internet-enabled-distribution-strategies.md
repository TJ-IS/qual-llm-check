# Internet-enabled distribution strategies

ASHOK RANCHHOD and C· LIN GUR· U

Southampton Business School, Southampton SO14 0YN, UK

This paper investigates the effects of the Internet on the structure of the classical marketing channels. The advantages offered by the Internet have determined many companies to adopt different models of Internet-enabled distribution strategy. The choice and the implementation of these strategies will be in¯ uenced, as in the case of any other marketing channel, by the characteristics of the company, of its clients, and of the traded products. On the basis of an empirical study of 500 Internet commercial sites, the present paper identi® es the models of Internet-enabled distribution strategy implemented by the Web-active companies, and then veri® es four hypotheses related to the factors that in¯ uence the choice and implementation of a particular strategy.

## Introduction

Marketing channels have been de® ned as organized networks (systems) of agencies and institutions which, in combination, perform all the activities required to link producers with users to accomplish the marketing task (Berman, 1996). Each of these agencies or institutions perform a necessary function within the marketing channel, a function that can be usually transferred to another intermediary, but cannot be completely eliminated. These key functions are as follows (Kotler, 1997).

(1) Information: the collection and dissemination of marketing research information about potential and current customers, competitors and other actors and forces in the marketing environment.

(2) Promotion: the development and dissemination of persuasive communications designed to attract consumers to the offer.

(3) Negotiation: the attempt to reach a ® nal agreement on price and other terms so that transfer of ownership or possession can be effected.

(4) Ordering: marketing-channel member’s communication of intention to buy to the manufacturer.

(5) Financing: the acquisition and allocation of funds required to ® nance inventories at different levels of the marketing channel.

(6) Risk taking: the assumption of risks connected with carrying out the channel work.

(7) Physical possession: the successive storage and movement of physical products from raw materials to the ® nal customers.

(8) Payment: buyers’ payment of their bills to the sellers through banks and other ® nancial institutions.

(9) Title: the actual transfer of ownership from one organization or person to another.

Some of these functions (e.g. physical, title and promotion) take place in the forward ¯ ow of activity from the company to the customer; some functions (such as ordering and payment) occur in the backward ¯ ow of activity from customers to the company and other functions (information, negotiation, ® nancing and risk taking) occur in both directions (Kotler, 1997).

The development and the use of the Internet has transformed the structure of classical marketing channels (Morris et al., 1997; De Kare Silver, 1998). The characteristics of the World Wide Web (WWW) ± high speed of information transfer, high level of interaction, elimination of the limitations of physical space and time and high level of ¯ exibility and ubiquity (Berthon et al., 1997) ± offer commercial companies and manufacturers the possibility of assuming and controlling most of the necessary marketing functions comprised in a marketing channel.

This paper attempts to unravel the types of international distribution strategies used by companies that are active on the Internet. In particular, although the Internet is regarded as a much hyped medium that will destroy the need for the \`middleman’, it is important to observe empirically whether these intermediaries are truly on their way out. With the explosive growth of the Internet (Cronin, 1996a; Clemente, 1998) it is also important to understand what are the main factors that in¯ uence ® rms’ choice of a particular Internet-enabled distribution strategy.

## Transaction processes on the Internet

The use of the Internet has become increasingly popular in the last 5 years, particularly in industrialized countries in which the cost of accessing and building an Internet site is relatively low (Cronin, 1995, 1996a; Watson and Zinkhan, 1997; Clemente, 1998). The Internet has many potential uses, depending on the objectives and capabilities of the user (The Economist, 1998). It can be (1) a source of information, (2) a communication tool and (3) a distribution channel for products and services.

New developments in the ® eld of multimedia software have increased the range of information that can be transmitted, which can now be expressed in the form of printed text, image, sound or a combination of all these (Watson , 1998).

Taking into consideration the advantages of speed, interaction and ¯ exibility, it is easy to understand why the Internet is considered as a revolutionary tool for the development of commercial transactions. Many innovative companies have made attempts to transfer a part of their marketing activities on to the Internet, in order to bene® t from the advantages offered by this unique system of communication (Ghosh, 1998; Watson et al., 1998).

Many authors have analysed the impact of the Internet on the marketing strategies implemented by companies. Table 1 presents four opinions expressed in the specialized literature regarding the main strategic advantages derived from an effective Internet marketing strategy.

In the last 10 years, marketing techniques have placed a bigger emphasis on the interactive side of the commercial transaction, emphasizing the importance of communication techniques (relationship marketing, one-to-one marketing, etc.). Information became an important commodity in marketing, conveying competitive advantage to companies that transmit, store and process information effectively (Porter and Millar, 1985).

The ef® ciency of the Internet is higher for companies whose value-added contribution is concentrated primarily in the informatic side of their products (Benjamin and Wigand, 1995; Cameron, 1997; Watson and Zinkhan, 1997). There is a general trend in all areas of human activity to create more value through information, but this is particularly true for high-tech companies (informatics, biotechnology and electronics). Figure 1 outlines how some companies are balancing themselves on the information/transaction axis.

Nevertheless, the use of the Internet for commercial purposes logically implies the application and maintenance of certain standards related to (1) the reliability of the information exchanged, (2) the con® dentiality of the information exchanged, (3) the security of the transaction and (4) the compatibility of the Internet hard- and software systems.

The growth of Internet shopping has been slowed by two main problems (Soars and Woolven, 1995).

(1) The need to develop friendlier user interfaces. There is a wide range of computer hardware, modem speeds and software applications used to access the Internet, resulting in a nonstandard, complex environment. Despite the technological advances in the use of multimedia applications, detailed graphics pages are still slow for most users.

(2) The lack of an easy to use, secure payment mechanism. Consumers who reveal credit card details on the WWW risk information falling into unauthorized hands. A series of encrypting software programs has partially solved this problem, increasing the security of on-line currency transfers (Denning, 1997).

Firms can have multiple bene® ts using the WWW as a distribution channel (Hoffman et al., 1997). First, the WWW potentially offers certain classes of providers participation in a market in which distribution costs or costs of sales shrink to zero. This is most likely for ® rms in publishing, information services or digital product categories (Jones, 1994). For example, digital products can be delivered immediately; hence, such businesses may encounter massive disintermediation or even the eventual elimination of middlemen (Hoffman et al., 1997). Moreover, buyers and sellers can access and contact each other directly, potentially eliminating some of the marketing costs and constraints imposed by such interactions in the terrestrial world. This may also have the effect of shrinking the channel and making distribution much more ef® cient (mainly due to reduced overhead costs through such outcomes as uniformity, automation and large-scale integration of management processes). The time in which to complete business transactions may be reduced as well, translating into traditional ef® ciencies for the ® rm. However, such potential ef® ciencies must be tempered with market realities (Kline, 1995).

Second, business on the WWW transfers more of the selling function to the customer through on-line ordering and the use of ® ll-out forms (Michalski, 1995), thus helping to bring transactions to a faster conclusion. This permits a third bene® t in the form of capture-of-customer information. Obviously the use of these techniques requires reliable electronic hardware support systems such as hi-® systems, computers, video recorders, etc. (see Fig. 2).

When the product sold must be physically transmitted, the commercial use of the Internet has to be complemented by the implementation of a physical distribution operation. Even in this case, distribution can be coordinated and monitored on the Internet. The seller can provide a series of additional on-line services to customers, for example the possibility of tracing the progress of the product during its transportation and estimating its time of arrival (De Kare-Silver, 1998). Consumers perform a large part of the distribution function when purchasing from stores. With interactive shopping and distribution, this cost has to be borne by the retailer (Alba et al., 1997). However, the retailer will not have to bear the extra costs of transportation from the warehouse to the retail shop.

In the case of institutional clients (business-tobusiness transactions) it may be more important to supply a particular product at a precise moment (as in the case of materials and components for a just-intime assembly line) (Maddox and Blankenhorn, 1998). In other cases, the criteria of delivery speed will be paramount for the success of transaction (medicines, perishable goods, etc.). The Internet represents a common technological platform which permits better coordination, organization and management of the business operations between partners (Poon and Jevons, 1997).

In this context, the present study attempts to identify and analyse the strategies of Internet-enabled distribution used by companies for their international operations.

Because of its characteristics, from the outset the Internet can be considered a global virtual market (Crocker, 1997; Watson and Zinkhan, 1997). However, for the distribution of physical products, precise geographical locations can be important. For instance Amazon books guarantees a 24 h delivery system (www.amazon.com, 1998) which can only be realized easily in the UK if books or CDs can be accessed locally.

## The study and hypotheses

The marketing literature (Hill and O’Sullivan, 1996) outlines that a ® rm’s choice of a particular distribution channel will be in¯ uenced by the following.

(1) The consumer. Marketing organization, by de® nition, puts the consumer at the centre of every decision. The type and characteristics of customers (number, geographical distribution, frequency and value of purchase) will be extremely relevant in the choice of a particular distribution strategy.

(2) Product characteristics. Depending on the product features, a particular marketing channel will be more appropriate for its distribution. For example, in the case of perishable goods, speed and channel length are crucial for ef® cient distribution. The features of many industrial products (e.g. size, weight, expense and level of technology) will require a higher level of involvement of the seller in the physical distribution process or in the post-transaction stage.

(3) Organization. Organizational culture and characteristics in¯ uence distribution choice. Different businesses have different strengths and weaknesses which have to be carefully considered. A ® rm’s size, ® nancial strength, market position, and quality of marketing information are all relevant factors in deciding what to delegate to intermediaries and what might be best done oneself.

(4) Competition. Channel intermediaries, unless they are part of a vertical marketing system, will each have their own business agenda. It is therefore important to choose a distribution channel which will allow a high degree of control over the marketing channel. Finally, under the heading of competition, it should be considered that any channel choice has cost and revenue implications. Finding a cheaper way of getting the product to the consumer or ensuring early payment, can establish a long-term competitive advantage that is dif® cult to undermine.

(5) Environment. The external environment (politic, economic, sociocultural and technological) is constantly changing. Advances in communication and transportation technology have offered new ways of organizing and implementing distribution strategies. In this respect, the Internet can be considered as a major breakthrough.

(6) Strategy. As a component of the marketing mix, distribution needs to work in synergy with the other elements in a strategic combination. The shorter the chain of distribution, the more control the manufacturer has over the presentation of the ® nal product. Promotion and pricing of the product will be also heavily in¯ uenced by the capacity of the manufacturer to control the distribution channel.

The implications of these factors for the implementation of an Internet-enabled distribution strategy have not yet been thoroughly investigated. Some authors have argued that the Internet represents an opportunity for small and medium-sized companies to compete more ef® ciently with larger organizations (Knight and Cavusgil, 1996; Watson and Zinkhan, 1997). However, this does not necessarily imply the use of the same virtual strategies (Sarkar et al., 1998). On the contrary, considering the signi® cant differences between companies of different sizes, a standardized approach to an Internet-enabled distribution strategy is highly illogical (Gur° u and Ranchhod, 1999). The speci® city of products, types of organizations and targeted markets is also expected to in¯ uence the design and implementation of an Internet-enabled distribution strategy. In line with the discussions above four hypotheses were formulated and tested empirically. These hypotheses are outlined below.

In order to identify the strategies of Internetenabled international distribution, the WWW sites of 500 commercial companies were randomly selected, accessed and studied during November 1998. In order to be selected and studied, a company had to (1) have an active presence on the Internet-WWW site and (2) have an international commercial activity ± to sell products outside national borders.

As a result of this research, the common patterns of Internet-enabled international strategies were grouped into two main model categories: direct distribution and intermediate distribution. In a second stage, in order to identify the factors that determined whether companies adopted a particular model of Internet-enabled distribution, the following research hypotheses were formulated and tested.

$\operatorname { H } _ { 1 } { \mathrm { : } }$ The implementation of a particular Internetenabled distribution strategy depends on the size of the company.

This hypothesis was developed in order to test whether the size of a company made any signi® cant difference to the type of distribution favoured. There are many who argue (Downes and Mui, 1998) that smaller companies are likely to embrace the Internet more readily, given its relative cheapness and, hence, they adopt distribution strategies to match this new medium.

$\operatorname { H } _ { 2 } \colon$ The implementation of a particular Internetenabled distribution strategy depends on the characteristics of the clients, i.e. whether they are consumer or business clients.

This particular hypothesis is based on the earlier discussion on what in¯ uences distribution strategies (Hill and O’Sullivan, 1996).

$\operatorname { H } _ { 3 } \colon$ In electronic markets, due to the unbundling of channel functions, the length of the physical channel will be shorter than for comparable exchanges in traditional markets.

This particular hypothesis was taken from a seminal discussion by Sarkar et al. (1998), who investigated the effects of the Internet on the structure of marketing channels.

$\operatorname { H } _ { 4 } \colon$ The implementation of a particular Internetenabled distribution strategy depends on the characteristics of the product sold: digital or non-digital, value per unit, volume per unit and level of incorporated technology.

This particular hypothesis was tested to ascertain where and how the Internet could be an important marketing distribution medium. There is much debate on the use of the Internet for digital products, yet others argue that the medium is suitable for any type of product. It was therefore important to see whether the characteristics of the goods had any impact on the distribution strategies adopted.

## The results and discussion

Companies were classi® ed according to their number of employees, with small ® rms having a maximum of 100 direct employees, medium-sized ® rms having 101± 500 employees and large ® rms having more than 500 employees. The distribution of these categories in the sample studied is shown in Table 4.

The nationality of the companies con® rmed the predominance of US-based companies on the Internet.

Of the ® rms 399 were US-based (79.8%), followed a long way behind by UK-based companies with 33 (6.6%), Australia and Canada with 15 (3%), Taiwan with ten (2%), Japan, Italy and Singapore with ® ve (1%) and, ® nally, France and Germany with four (0.8%).

The clients were classi® ed according to their individual or institutional characteristics. As in many cases the companies were selling both to individual and institutional customers, a third category was created (see Table 5).

The products were initially classi® ed as digital or non-digital, since this is obviously one of the most important characteristics that permits the use of on-line distribution (Table 6).

The product’s value per unit was considered low for a price under \$100, medium for a price per unit between \$100 and \$300 and high for the products priced above \$300 per unit (Table 7).

The volume of the product unit was considered on a subjective basis. For example, it was considered that a book or a bottle of perfume has a small volume per product unit, a personal computer has a medium volume per product unit and a piece of furniture or a car was considered as having a large volume per product unit (Table 8).

Finally, considering the level of incorporated technology, the products were classi® ed as goods with low (books, shoes and chocolate) or a high level of incorporated technology (computers, cars and software) (Table 9).

Two main models of Internet-aided international distribution were identi® ed.

## Direct shipping

When direct shipping is offered, the customer can access the WWW site of the selling company to obtain all the necessary information about the desired product and order it on-line. Sometimes the company also provides the possibility of on-line payment but, as in the case of on-line distribution, this is normally offered as an option among different forms of accepted payments (telephone/fax/cheques, etc.).

In the second stage, the company ships the purchased (or the selected) product directly to the customer (see Fig. 4). If the client is another company or organization, the distribution will be considered a direct export, in which other intermediaries can be indirectly involved (exporting agencies, transportation and insurance companies).

For individual clients, companies normally use the services of international delivery companies (UPS, USPS, Federal Express, TNT, etc. ). Depending on the urgency of the request, different options of speed delivery can be chosen, obviously with a differentiated level of price.

## Intermediate distribution model

In the case of intermediate distribution, the products are sold through a network of subsidiaries or agents, located in different regional or national markets (see Fig. 5). Through this system of Internet-aided international distribution, foreign customers can access the site of the selling company in order to obtain information regarding the availability of different products and their characteristics, as well as the location of the closer dealer, agent or subsidiary.

The most sophisticated companies provide on-line connections to the WWW sites of their foreign subsidiaries or agents. In this case, the information, communication and negotiation related to the available products is shifted to the WWW sites of these units. Prices are presented in the national currency speci® c for each site and the conditions of delivery are adapted to the local characteristics and timetables. The use of Intranets connecting the information system of the parent company with its foreign units can signi-® cantly increase the ef® ciency of the distribution strategy.

Sometimes the potential customer can order the product on-line at the central site, which is then transmitted by the parent company to the foreign selling units. However, in most cases the customer is simply encouraged to contact the local agent, dealer or subsidiary, as in the case of the Ford Motor Company (www.Ford.com, 1998).

## Conclusions

The empirical research of 500 companies that use the Internet as an element of their integrated international distribution has shown that there are two main strategies that are applied.

(1) The direct distribution model which attracts international customers to the ® rm’s WWW site, provides them with all the necessary information about the offered products and then provides them with the possibility of buying the product on-line. Then, the acquired product is transmitted to the customer either through online distribution or through direct shipping.

(2) The intermediate distribution model which provides the customer with essential on-line information about the product, the company and its international distribution network. This helps the potential customers to identify the product they need, ® nd the geographical location of the closest selling unit and contact the appropriate person.

The application of the $\chi ^ { 2 }$ test to the empirical data con® rmed the formulated hypotheses. Traditional markets are typi® ed by linear ¯ ows (Stern et al., 1996), whereas electronic markets (Sarkar et $a l . ,$ 1998) are likely to be characterized by shorter, linear, physical distribution channels $( \mathrm { H } _ { 3 } ) ,$ though the transfer of product information may well be determined by the choice and use of cybermediaries. Despite the multiple advantages offered by the Internet in terms of accessibility, speed, interactivity and ¯ exibility, the implementation of a WWW site can help commercial companies integrate and control only some of the necessary functions of a marketing channel. The WWW site cannot, in most cases, completely replace the traditional intermediaries, but succeeds in increasing the ef® ciency of existing marketing channels through better coordination and control. The best solution is a synergistic integration of Internet and Intranet technologies with the logistic system of physical distribution. However, the potential con¯ ict between classical marketing channels and electronic commerce will probably represent an important problem for some companies (De Kare-Silver, 1998). This study shows that the choice of an Internet-enabled strategy for international distribution is in¯ uenced by size of the commercial company $( \mathrm { H } _ { 1 } ) _ { \cdot }$ , by the type of its customers $\left( \mathbf { H } _ { 2 } \right)$ and by the characteristics of the products sold $\mathrm { ( H _ { 4 } ) }$

The use of the Internet as an element of companies’ overall distribution strategy is still relatively undeveloped (De Kare-Silver, 1998). It can be expected that, in the near future, its importance will increase, particularly in the on-line distribution of digital products or as a channel of transnational communication between ® rms, customers, agents, dealers and subsidiaries (Cameron, 1997; Bickerton et al., 1998; Clemente, 1998).

The study has a number of limitations. It has taken a \`global’ view of distribution on the Internet; more segmentation of product/service categories and distribution preferences need to be explored. The study does not examine the dynamics of the transaction process and the distribution methods preferred by consumers/business clients (Paul, 1996; Morrison and Roberts, 1998) as they go through the search and evaluation process. The process of \`group buying’ and the development of Internet communities is also important (Armstrong and Hagel, 1996; Clark, 1997; Hof et al., 1997), as is the role of advertising (Ranchhod, 1998). The improvement in search engines and the use of eXtensible Markup Language (XML) (Arbortext, 1998, http://www.arbortext.com/xmlwp. html) for speedy linkages and intelligent sur® ng are also likely to change the nature of cybermediaries and of Internet-enabled distribution strategies, aspects that will need future attention. However, despite these limitations, a broad picture of the distribution methods used by companies in cyberspace is beginning to emerge and a longitudinal study of these will show how the patterns will change internationally over the next decade.
