# From traditional EDI to Internet-based EDI: managerial considerations

MIREILLE S. THRELKEL and C. BRUCE KAVAN

Department of Management, Marketing and Logistics, College of Business Administration, University of North Florida, 4321 St Johns Bluff Road South, Jacksonville, FL. 32225, USA

Traditional EDI/VAN technology has been used for almost three decades and has brought its users significant advantages resulting in increased productivity and ef® ciency. However, implementation constraints have restricted the technology to predominantly large corporations, thus limiting EDI’s reach to a broader base of small suppliers and customers. Internet-based EDI, with signi® cantly fewer implementation constraints, can play an important role in extending EDI bene® ts to a wider spectrum of businesses. Recently published articles re¯ ect a reluctance on the part of traditional EDI users to adopt this new communication modality. The reasons for this reluctance include the newness of the Internet technology, potential Internet legislation and lack of Internet standards plus the reliability and security of data transmission within the Internet environment. This paper examines these management issues and describes existing and potential solutions available to both new and traditional EDI users. Since few companies have embraced Internet-based EDI, this paper concludes with some successful Internet EDI-based cases. Along with these cases, the authors present a very broad description of the nature of the products and services currently available in the market today to support Internet-based EDI.

## Introduction

For some 30 years, electronic data interchange (EDI) has been a tool for allowing companies to automate and exchange data within their organization and with trading partners. Value added networks (VANs) have stimulated EDI growth by reducing the entry cost and complexity while providing a conduit for the electronic transaction itself. In many cases, VANs have improved transaction costs while simultaneously ensuring data reliability and security.

The rapid evolution of globalization mandates everfaster communication in a market without frontier. Today, the Internet has opened the door to new opportunities for companies around the globe to stimulate commerce (either EDI or non-EDI based). Indeed, the Internet allows for a speedy worldwide reach for a great range of companies. For existing EDI/VAN users, the Internet could expand their reach to suppliers and customers at costs lower than ever.

In spite of the alluring advantages of communicating through the Internet, companies using traditional EDI/VAN technology are reluctant to enter on to the \`Superhighway.’ Why? They have raised a number of concerns: the newness of the technology, existing investments in EDI/VANs, too much or too little legislation and lack of Internet standards, reliability and security.

Are these fears well-founded and justi® ed? Should companies using traditional EDI/VAN technology jump on to the World Wide Web (WWW) in its present form or should they wait and see? In an attempt to answer these questions, this paper will broadly de® ne EDI/VANs, the Internet and Internet-based EDI technologies and will examine the issues inhibiting EDI users from adopting cyberspace technology.

## Traditional EDI

The concept of EDI is reported to go back as far as the Berlin Airlift in 1948 when a paper-based standard document was developed to reconcile the data that came from a vast number of different ® les (Hirscheim, 1997). The electronic version of EDI appeared some 30 years ago in the transportation industry. Its original purpose was to automate the exchange of paper between organizations. When standards were introduced into the process in the early 1970s, EDI became a more simple and more reliable system. The bene® ts of communicating between separate organizations increased with the use of VAN technology. EDI/VAN use is now widespread within the airline, banking, credit card and motor industries though it is often limited to corporations who are large enough to invest in this costly system.

There are many de® nitions of EDI but it is generally de® ned as the computer-to-computer exchange of intercompany business documents and information through standard interfaces which requires hardware, software and communications technology that permit those computers to transfer the data electronically (such as purchase orders, invoices, medical claims and price lists). Though businesses may track similar data, they do not typically integrate or use the data in the same way, nor do they use identical application programs or hardware, making communication between them dif® cult and susceptible to error. EDI minimizes these problems by providing a common ground on which to work.

## The role of VANs

VANs are dedicated, private or third-party proprietary communications companies that provide for multiprotocol connectivity. A VAN is \`like a telephone switching station for data transmission’ (Kavan and Carr, 1992, p. S-87) to send large amounts of data to a trading partner. It provides other services such as electronic mailboxes and various translation tools that can be used to transform the data to or from a trading partner’s application-speci® c format into a standard format. VANs are bounded by the extent of their network although one VAN may interconnect with any of a number of other VANs.

One of the key advantages to using VANs is the ability to secure transactions. Using security measures such as ® rewalls, encryption programs and digital signatures, VANs protect sensitive and proprietry information. VANs also ensure reliability of transmission by providing a private, secure connection that guarantees data will get from point A to point B in a timely fashion.

## How widespread is EDI usage?

While there are in excess of 200 000 US businesses participating in EDI (Ferguson, 1998), these represent mostly Fortune 1000 organizations with few small to medium-size companies. EDI is often thought to be a solution only for large companies, their suppliers and customers because of the high implementation and maintenance costs particularly associated with the use of VANs (McGahee, 1999). Even among EDI users, the full potential of the technology has not yet been realized. According to a recent study based on a survey of 257 mid-sized US manufacturers with annual sales of \$10 million to \$500 million, few manufacturers often even use EDI (Garrison, 1998).

In another recent survey of suppliers reported by the Commonwealth of Australia, participants were asked to identify barriers to EDI from a list of nine, randomly presented potential barriers. Of the 227 respondents, some 40% identi® ed operating costs as the third greatest barrier to implementing EDI in their organization just behind \`lack of EDI-capable business partners’ (59%) and \`integration of EDI with internal applications’ (44%) (Commonwealth of Australia, 1998).

## The growth and future of EDI

The use of the technology has been and should be further boosted by the Federal Acquisition Streamlining Act of 1994, a federal law which requires the entire US Government to begin using electronic commerce (EC)/EDI. This means that a company wishing to sell goods and services valued under \$100 000 to any US Government agency but that is not capable of performing EDI-based transactions will lose existing and potential business. Though the US Government’s requirements for EDI use will no doubt expand the EDI user base, sizeable barriers still exist and will prevent the technology from becoming universal. The Internet, on the other hand, with its ubiquitous network, has opened new doors for those companies using EDI so that they may reap the bene® ts of this technology by expanding the scope of their trading partners and simplifying the process of communication while reducing costs further. Will EDI users continue to rely on VANs to communicate or will they convert to the Superhighway to connect with their trading partners? Will they adopt a compromise by combining traditional EDI and the Internet?

## The Internet

The Internet is not a new physical tool, but rather a tool whose utility became more apparent in 1993 when browsers appeared. On a commercial basis, its use is even more recent and has been applied primarily to product and service advertising and to interpersonal communication in the form of e-mail. Forrester Research estimates that on-line business trade of goods will grow from \$43 billion in 1998 to \$1.3 trillion in 2003. Additionally, the purchase of business services enabled by the Internet are estimated to reach \$220 billion during the same period (Putman, 1999).

Internet-based EDI is essentially moving the EDI transaction from the dedicated, secure and proprietary lines of VANs to the vast public network of the Internet. A case study on \`Financial EDI over the Internet’ gave an excellent description of the basic process of EDI over the Internet:

Like VAN-based EDI, data from application programs typically must go through a translation layer, which formats the outgoing data into the appropriate EDI format, such as X12 or EDIFACT. The formatted data must be encapsulated using MIME (Multipurpose Internet Mail Extensions), which have three prede® ned \`body parts’ formats for identifying distinct EDI data types: EDIFACT, EDI-X12, and EDI-Consent. Moreover, to ensure privacy, integrity, authentication, and non-repudiation, the data must also be encrypted using PEM. The encryption and encapsulation operations are typically performed together. The resulting data is wrapped into a RFC822 envelope, which is routed over the Internet using SMTP (Simple Mail Transport Protocol). On the receiving end, the reverse process applies. The data content is taken out of the envelope, and is then decrypted. The actual EDI content is extracted from the message body using MIME directives. This data is passed to the translator, which reformats it so that the recipient’s application program can read it.

(Segev et al., 1995, p. 7)

There are a number of differences between Internetbased EDI and traditional VAN-enabled EDI. These differences are presented in Table 1 (Mak and Johnson, 1998). From a study of available products and services for Internet-based EDI, Mak and Johnson (1998) developed ten scenarios for Internet transfers of information. These scenarios range along a continuum from the simple front-ending existing EDI applications with additional software to WWW-enabled viewing to integrated applications.

In the simpler scenarios, two types of connections to the Internet are examined. The ® rst is direct Internet connection, which requires front-end translation software to transmit and display documents or interface with existing in-house application systems. For the EDI user, the added software can replace existing software or can be an addition to existing software. The other connecting method is through third-party Internet VANS (IVANS). Trade can occur between an EDI user and a company without EDI, connecting directly through the Internet using front-end WWW-enabled software of their own choice. IVANS can set up a WWW page to perform translations and exchanges allowing subscribing partners to trade without additional front-end software. An exchange from an EDI user to a non-EDI trading partner can occur simply with an Internet connection and a standard WWW browser.

The more complex but more encompassing scenario involves a system architecture that integrates traditional EDI with Internet EDI [Figure 1]. In this scenario, there are large companies with an existing network of EDI-enabled trading partners (using various media of communication including the Internet) who would like to bring, in a cost-effective way, smaller traders using the Internet. These companies could implement the concept developed by Mak and Johnson, called Intelligent Gateway. It is \`a program, residing on the large trading partner’s computer system, which acts as a communications hub between various application systems and various types of business transaction recipients, and selects the appropriate translation and routing means automatically based on known characteristics of the receiver. (Mak and Johnson, 1998, p. 50)

Senn (1998) identi® ed three principal channels for Internet-based EDI. In addition to the aforementioned scenarios using ordinary browser capabilities, he described two other forms, which include the use of ftp (® le transfer protocol) capabilities or attaching an EDI-formatted ® le to an Internet-based e-mail. These channel transport mechanisms have existed longer than WWW-based capabilities and are subject to the same transport and security issues that apply to all Internetbased communications.

## Advantages of the Internet for EDI users

## Increased access to small suppliers

Due to its signi® cant investment in personnel, hardware, software and communication, smaller suppliers have mostly shied away from EDI. This has considerably reduced the EDI user supplier base. Increasing this base will bring downward pressure on prices, resulting in cost savings. The WWW provides smaller suppliers with an easy, inexpensive method of accessing data. EDI users can continue to connect with established suppliers through proprietary lines while deploying their supplier base using the Internet.

## Ubiquitous reach

The Internet is the largest computer network in the world, established in some 150 countries and reaching some 57 million users with the fastest growth seen in any industry. Its most remarkable and appealing characteristic is ubiquity reinforced by the fast growing mass of users, private and commercial alike. For EDI users, this signi® es an in® nite horizon with worldwide access during business and non-business hours to millions.

## Real time access

The higher cost of VAN services during peak business hours has pushed many companies to \`batch’ their data and transactions for transmission during non-business hours. That can translate into costly delays. On the WWW, access is available 24 hours at the same cost. Around the clock access can also reduce delivery times resulting in savings and increased customer satisfaction.

## Cost

A major issue frequently raised by EDI users is the cost of communication using VANs. According to Information Week, there are substantial savings for companies that move their traditional EDI transactions using VANs to Internet-based EDI. This article provided price comparisons (shown in Table 2) to demonstrate the favourable economics associated with migration of EDI from the traditional VAN to Internetbased connectivity (Adhikari, 1996, pp. 59± 60).

## Increased availability of software

EDI vendors as well as Internet service providers (ISPs) have scrambled to develop software ensuring interconnection, reliability and security of communication. The availability of a wide variety of such software is making the implementation of EDI signi® cantly easier. As Mak and Johnson (1998) demonstrated, there is much already available on the market as they studied some 30 suppliers of Internet-based EDI products and services. Muller (1998) also demonstrated the bene® ts bestowed upon small business through the expansion of the trading partner base using Internetbased EDIs. Even the area of ® nancial electronic commerce is beginning to adopt Internet expansion from the traditional EDI to reach smaller trading partners (Spielman and Dias, 1999). The application of Internet-based EDI is still too young to verify the impact of those bene® ts on a grand scale, but they are a good indication as to the potential improvements that companies might achieve.

## Are EDI users ready to \`make the jump’?

The advantages described above are signi® cant enough to have drawn the interest of EDI users who, as they should, are constantly searching for ways of reducing costs and increasing productivity. However, there seems to be a certain reluctance to adopt the new technology. Why? A number of issues have been raised and until they are resolved to EDI users’ satisfaction, there will be no massive rush on to the WWW. These issues range from not so critical, such as the weariness of a new technology, to real concerns, such as security in the communication of data. We examine them and investigate present and future solutions offered by the private and public sector.

## New technology and existing EDI investments

It has been pointed out in previous paragraphs that the commercial use of the Internet is only in its infancy. Investments into EDI technology may have run into hundreds of thousands of dollars, information systems (IS) personnel are EDI trained and pro® cient and EDI technology is tried and proven as well as secure and reliable. Why change what is not broken? All these factors indeed make the change to a new, unknown technology all the more dif® cult.

## Internet standards: are they suf® cient?

Feeling comfortable with the highly developed EDI standards, users are reluctant to abandon them for the \`fuzzy’ world of Internet standards. The key is building \`cyberconsumer trust’ insuring the privacy of on-line information (Hoffman et al., 1999). However, Internet standards organizations are actively addressing these issues. Two major bodies have emerged: the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) and the Open-buying Internet (OBI) consortium. The IETF, a task force of the Internet Society (ISOC), develops standards that it proposes to the Internet Engineering Steering Group for consideration with appeal to the Internet Architecture Boards. These are promulgated by the ISOC as international standards. They describe Internet standards as \`a speci® cation that is stable and well-understood, is technically competent, has multiple, independent, and interoperable implementations with substantial operational experience, enjoys signi® cant public support, and is recognizably useful in some or all parts of the Internet’ (Bradner, 1996, p. 3). Even normally competitive vendors are cooperating. Efforts to converge different approaches into an approved standard were made by Netscape and Microsoft. From their incompatible security standards DD and PCT, they jointly developed a convergent protocol, the Transport Security Layer (TSL), which has been submitted to the IETF for review. Standards are also being developed in the area of security, a highly sensitive issue with EDI users: IEEEX.509 certi® cation will support EDI, e-mail and remote authentication, but also other applications such as public keys on smart cards and encryption (Etzel, 1997).

Another standards body is the OBI, which emerged from an alliance in the private sector. In 1997, American Express and the Internet Purchasing Roundtable ± a group of Fortune 500 companies and their key suppliers ± released Version 1.0 of the OBI standards specifying the processes, formats, security and technical design for the buying and selling of \`indirect materials’ over the Internet (Garner, 1997; Dugan, 1999). The OBI speci® cations lay the foundation for interoperable EDI over the Internet: \`OBI builds on the decade-old ANSI X12 EDI 850 purchase order transaction, adapts it for IP and adds the Secure Sockets Layer (SSL) and X.509 digital certi® cation for security’ (Messmer, 1997, p. 88).

Vendors are also forming common grounds for standards. In 1997, Netscape and approximately 40 vendors launched the Crossware initiative which is \`an endorsement of a series of standards, including directory, security, and distribution, that will allow users to extend their Intranet applications to trading partners (Baum, 1997, pp. 67± 8).

As EDI standards develop, Internet standards acceptable to EDI users will also emerge from a combination of individual effort and alliances from Internet users, vendors, governmental bodies and national and international standards organizations. In addition, with the development of translation software, documents using EDI standards can be transmitted to recipients not using that technology.

## Too much or too little legislation?

Some EDI users fear a lack of legislation while others fear an over-regulated Internet industry. In 1997, the White House proposed a White Paper on the subject of electronic commerce, which should worry the former but alleviate the anxieties of the latter. An executive summary published in Telecommunications (1997) cited ® ve principles that are to guide the government support for the evolution of commerce:

(1) The private sector should lead. The Internet should develop as a market driven arena, not a regulated industry. It should encourage industry self-regulation and private sector leadership where possible.

(2) Governments should refrain from imposing new and unnecessary regulations, bureaucratic procedures or new taxes and tariffs on commercial activities that take place via the Internet.

(3) Where governmental involvement is needed, its aim should be to support and enforce a predictable, minimalist, consistent and simple legal environment for commerce. Where government intervention is necessary, its role should be to ensure competition, protect intellectual property and privacy, prevent fraud, foster transparency, and facilitate dispute resolution, not to regulate.

(4) Existing laws and regulations that may hinder electronic commerce should be reviewed and revised or eliminated to re¯ ect the needs of the new electronic age.

(5) EC on the Internet should be facilitated on a global basis. The legal framework supporting commercial transactions should be consistent and predictable regardless of the jurisdiction in which a particular buyer and seller reside (pp. 35± 6).

As principle (3) suggests, it is in the area of security that the United States government has so far been the most active as it is linked to national security and illicit operations of grand scale against the government. Encryption has been a sensitive issue as well as an issue of controversy. In the past the government has prevented manufacturers from developing and marketing encryption software with more than 40-bit encryption keys. Amid the clamour from the software industry, who claimed being at a disadvantage over foreign competitors who are allowed 128-bit-based encryption software, the federal government relented and extended the 40-bit limit in February 1997 to 56- bit encryption and in September 1997 further extended the limit to 128-bit encryption. At present, the issue of government control over the software industries and how they market their products globally is headed for the Supreme Court (Cimino, 1997).

On the international level, efforts are also being made. The European Community has adopted various directives such as the Distance Selling Directive and the Data Protection Directive, which are more protective than the laissez-faire approach of the US. This could potentially \`fragment the electronic market, replacing physical geographical borders with regulatory borders, created by differing legal requirements’ (Payne and Foley, 1997, p. 48). The European Commission has yet to publish its proposals and will not hold hearings on the matter until later in the current year.

In 1996, based on the work of a special, broadly represented working group, the OECD (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development) published a set of guidelines intended to promote the use of cryptography to \`foster con® dence in information infrastructures, to help ensure the safety of data and protection’ (Kamata and Peters, 1997, p. 13). The guidelines are not legally binding but rather act to point to an internationally coordinated approach to the issue of secure and ef® cient commerce throughout the world.

## Reliability of data transmission

\`With 3000 purchase orders, if we had only 95% come in on time, that would kill us, even if the Internet was free! And if we were down for a half a day, that would kill us too. It’ s critical for us to have a 100% connection 24 hours a day’ (Bartholomew, 1997, p. 45). Is the Internet reliable? The question is highly debated and much is currently written about this issue.

The problem has taken on new dimensions with the increased use of multimedia. Such increase in demand ± and it is expected to grow at an exponential rate ± has put great strain on the backbone of the Internet. \`As far as the underlying network goes, there’s no problem’ stated Leonard Kleinrock, one of the architects of the ARPANet, but he admitted, \`it will get overloaded when we get too much more multimedia’ (Schlosberg, 1996, p. 21).

The backbone of the Internet consists of segments that are owned by many long-distance telephone companies. At least 30 ISPs now offer a nationwide service over a backbone of high-speed circuits, ranging from T3 (45 Mbit/s) to OC12 (622 Mbits/s). Access to this backbone is provided by thousands of ISPs located at the fringes of this backbone. It is no surprise that, with the proliferation of ISPs, overload becomes an issue as the availability of bandwidth ± the rate of data transfer between two systems in kilobytes ± becomes problematic. \`Backbone providers have been unable to keep up with demand’ John Gerdermann, president of network MCI admitted. \`We were seeing 10% to 15% growth per month and fell behind in our planning’ (Radcliff, 1997, p. 99).

\`©The backbone infrastructure is adequateº , said Eric Bataller, technical liaison manager at CommerceNet. For business use in particular, the issue is not so much that there aren’t enough ©lanesº on the highway; much more crucial is how the traf® c is getting coordinated’ (Schlosberg, 1996, p. 21). Gareiss (1997), who developed a \`survival kit for doing business on the ©Net without getting burnedº’, suggested that business managers must become familiar with the architecture of the various backbones ± no backbones are alike ± before signing on with an ISP. He suggested that he consider and study various factors affecting the reliability of the backbone and that he ® nd answers to questions such as the following. Is the backbone switched or routed? Are there nodes in the cities that need service? What sort of bandwidth is available on heavily travelled routes? Are they redundant paths between nodes? What are their lists of NAP (network access points) connections? Does the carrier have nodes in every city where there is a corporate site? Speed is important, but quality and capacity are as important. In essence, the manager must ® nd how the ISPs are organized in order to obtain optimum reliability for the transport of their data. Gareiss concluded that \`True, picking the right ISP isn’t a long-term solution to Internet problems. But it’ s the best way for corporate networkers to tip the odds in their favor’ (p. 2).

## Reliability: existing and future solutions

What solutions are offered to keep up with the expanding problem of reliability? ISPs are considering three technologies to deliver the bandwidth: one is frame relay backbones developed by ISPs such as MCI, Sprint, Uunet, Netcom and PSInet. These backbones allow a company to lease a single line with a certain committed informed rate. Another technology packet is Sonet (Synchronous Optical Network). Sprint has recently installed gigabit switch routers from Cisco Systems Inc., to route IP packets directly over 622 Mbit/s Sonet links (Gareiss, 1998, p. 1).

Companies such as Home Corporation have deployed other approaches, which offer a high-speed over cable television infrastructure. It will use a proprietary Internet protocol (IP) classi® cation scheme to segregate traf® c by priority, charging companies \$30± \$40 a month for a shared 20 Mbit/s Internet connection (Bartholomew, 1997).

The ageing of routers is another problem. Many router tables housing IP addresses are full. When packets hit the overloaded router, the router cannot take the packet and simply discards it. A new version of IP addressing, IP Version 6, might solve the problems as it uses router table structures more ef® ciently and opens up longer addressing schemes, from the current 32 bits to 128 bits. Another solution to the problem of bandwidth is a proxy-based compression solution called the Venturi Bandwidth Multiplier: \`Venturi operates based on two proxies devoted to compression and decompression of data traf® c’ (O’Shea, p. 26). For instance,

Internet/intranet query goes out from a user’s browser to a Venturi client proxy on a PC or local area network workstation for compression. Then, it is transmitted over CDPD links to a Venturi compression server, likely located at the network access point (NAP) to the Internet, for decompression and ® nal routing. Responses are compressed at the server proxy and decompressed at the client proxy. Venturi can maintain an average throughput improvement of 30%, using a 28.8 kb/s modem. (O’Shea, 1997)

What does the future offer? The National Science Foundation (NSF) is using a 622 Mbit/s VBNS (very high-speed broadband network system) as the infrastructure for Internet II, \`a supersonic secondgeneration Net that links universities and research centers around the world’ (Gareiss, 1998, p. 1). Large providers are also implementing WDM (Wave Division Multiplexing) to increase the capacity on each ® bre route.

Another technology of the near future is wireless communication under the form of \`smart digital radios’ or \`broad-band digital radios’ as proposed by visionary Paul Baran

A smart radio could process phone calls, videos, teleconferences, geo-positioning codes, speed-trap lasers and emergency SOS’s . . . By drastically enhancing ef® ciency in the use of spectrum, broadband digital radios will lend new force to the [telecommunication] industry’s move to move up the frequency ladder toward bandwidth abundance. (Gilder, 1997, p. 106)

The reliability of data transfer will continue to remain a concern ± for the time being at least ± one which EDI users will have to investigate thoroughly before jumping on to the Internet circuit. Solutions to improve on the problem appear at a fast rate and there is no doubt that this trend will continue and will lead us into new technologies that even staunch traditional EDI users have not imagined!

## Security

Security is, without a doubt, the concern most frequently raised by network managers accustomed to the VANs’ high security and reliability. Unfortunately, breaches of security are frequent enough to bring doubt into the mind of those managers when dealing with the Internet. According to the US Department of Defense, there were 250 000 attempted \`intrusions’ into systems via the Internet in 1995, of which 65% were successful. A survey of 205 Fortune 1000 companies by Baltimore-based Warroom Research found that 82.4% have experienced a break-in and approximately 90% said they had insider threat problems (Gjertsen, 1998). The media, with its many horror stories, no doubt in¯ ames the issue, but events such as those cited above are enough to make an IS manager uncomfortable about the security of their highly sensitive company data. Lack of security could mean millions of dollars in losses in actual transactions or in market share lost to a competitor. Before EDI users consider the Internet as their preferred communications channel, measures to ensure the highest level of security for their company will have to be available. What does security relate to?

The National Computer Security Association listed ® ve key cornerstones for secure electronic commerce (Etzel, 1997).

(1) Privacy: transmission must be private between sender and receiver.

(2) Integrity: they must have integrity to ensure that the message has not been doctored along the way.

(3) Authenticity (and non-fabrication) assures both parties of each other’s identity.

(4) Non-fabrication.

(5) Non-repudiation: ensures that the sender cannot deny that he or she actually sent the transmission.

There are various techniques to ensure that each step is ful® lled: ® rewalls, authentication and encryption. The ® rst step in ensuring the security of data is the installation of ® rewalls. A ® rewall is an electronic gatekeeper that ® lters incoming traf® c by requesting, for example, names and addresses, account numbers or passwords. There should be several layers of protections: a ® rewall between the electronic commerce server and the outside world and another between the server and a company’s computer systems. If that company’s employees have access to the Internet, then they should go through a ® rewall as well. Further, if they have modems connected directly to their of® ce PCs, they should disconnect from the company’s network before dialling, lest their computers act as a conduit for hackers (Cornell and Frye, 1998). There are many commercial software products available on the market to install ® rewalls.

Digital authentication ensures the identity of individuals via digital certi® cates (IDs). The information provided in these certi® cates is veri® ed and sealed with the digital signature of a trusted certi® cate authority (CA) (Borg, 1997). CAs are third-party organizations, usually specialists in informational security or, more recently, industry-speci® c companies such as banks, who investigate and validate entities and then publish certi® cates bound to the public keys of those they authenticate. At present, there are some ten CAs in the US market. However, the growth of electronic commerce could signify a proliferation of CAs that could put in peril the very integrity of the profession: who is to know which CA is reliable? This might suggest that what will be needed is \`some form of accreditation, certi® cation or licensing of CAs themselves. Someone will have to vouch for the voucher’ (Cornell and Frye, 1998, p. 33).

In fact, such a body was proposed in the Electronic Financial Services Ef® ciency Act of 1997 which was introduced in November 1997. It is designed to provide a uniform, nationwide framework for greater electronic authentication. The bill proposes \`(1) to recognize digital and other forms of authentication as an alternative to existing paper-based methods; (2) to improve the ef® ciency and soundness of the nation’s capital markets and payment system; and (3) to harmonize the practices, customs, and uses applicable to electronic authentication on a uniform, nationwide basis. The bill would establish the National Association of Certi® cation Authorities (NACA) to register electronic service providers. It would also establish a standards review committee within the NACA, under Treasury Department supervision’ (Merrick, 1998, p. 40). Congress has yet to act on the proposal.

Encryption technology uses an algorithm to scramble and unscramble data for anyone who does not possess the cryptographic key necessary to decrypt the data. Encryption goes back to the Crusades when messengers transported secret documents in boxes with two keys. The ® rst key locked the box with the message inside but could not unlock it. The recipient used a second key to open the box. Keys afford security at the 40-, 56- and 128-bit level; the greater the number of bits, the more the security. For example, messages encrypted with 128-bit encryption are 309 485 009 821 345 068 724 781 056 times harder to break than the 40-bit encryption (Merrick, 1998).

Encryption technology on the Internet relies usually on two standards ± Secure Socket Layer (SSL) and Secure Electronic Transaction (SET). SSL employs public key encryption, a technique that uses a pair of asymmetric keys for encryption and decryption. Each pair of keys includes a public key and a private key. The public key is distributed widely while the private key is kept secret. Data scrambled with the public key can be decrypted only with the private key and vice versa (Merrick, 1998). SET has an advantage over SSL as it includes digital authentication. Mastercard and Visa, in a joint project, relied on SET to develop the SET Root CA which uses a unique, split key technique to secure electronic payment further. \`This is an important step forward towards the adoption of SET as a global payment standard . . .’ said Steve Herz, senior vice president for electronic commerce at Visa (Borg, 1997, p. 6). At present, SET is undergoing trials with some 38 banks across Europe (Bird, 1997). The Swiss have proposed CypherGate, a product of r3 Security Engineering A.G., a software so secure that \`several billion years would not be long enough to break the security code used!’ (Swiss Security For The Internet, 1997, p. 1).

## Vendors are scrambling . . .

Traditional EDI and VAN providers are reworking their strategies by jumping on to the Internet. As Bob Parker of AMR Research told it \`what’ s actually happening is that the people who traditionally used EDI are starting to migrate to using the public network to transport messages. It’ s a lot less expensive than a VAN . . . [and] the move to the Internet . . . [is] a ©VAN killerº , not an ©EDI killerº ’ (Hickey, 1999, p. 32). No wonder the VANs are scrambling.

For instance, some are launching a WWW-based service that enables large corporations to receive HTML-based purchase orders from smaller ® rms that traditionally have not used EDI software and VAN service. With this service, EDI standards can be translated into HTML form to allow customers who do not have EDI to exchange documents with EDI users. Documents sent over the VAN in standard EDI format are translated to HTML for posting on a secure WWW server. To send an EDI document, a user ® lls out an HTML form which is translated into standard EDI before it is sent (Wilde, 1997). Additionally, some vendors are supplying software to create and order from on-line catalogues including the management of a host of EDI-related tasks (Adams, 1997).

The giant IBM has shifted its entire business strategy to electronic commerce and is investing \`billions of dollars in resources and tens of thousands of employees in an effort to tie its customers more closely to their own customers and partners under the E-business rubric of IBM hardware, software, and services’ (Caldwell, 1998, p. 18). This has resulted in a wide range of electronic business solutions being developed and marketed by IBM, including AS/400e as a hardware package designed for Internet applications (Krivda, 1997). It includes extensive security options along with increased storage and memory space designed speci® cally to take advantage of conducting business over the Internet (Anonymous, 1997).

Yet another VAN product was introduced in early 1997, a suite of easy-to-use, PC-based software products which provides a communications fast track to electronic commerce. This product allows users to exchange EDI standard documents with trading partners, send and receive electronic mail and ® les, post reference information for key trading partners in a secure library and surf the WWW with Microsoft Internet Explorer. Customers can use their own Internet services provider (Adams, 1997).

Some companies outsource their EDI system to Internet EDI specialists. These providers extend EDI systems via the Internet. They host and set up password-protected WWW sites and catalogues for partners and sales associates. Small suppliers now need only a WWW browser, an Internet connection and a password to access the information. After the suppliers process their orders, they can hit a button to produce an invoice. Depending on volume, processing an invoice now costs no more than \$1.50 and perhaps as little as 30 cents versus the \$15 it costs to process the information manually. Cutting a purchase order might run at approximately \$9 instead of \$55. SNET has 112 suppliers and handles 1.5 million transactions per year via EDI, at a dollar value of \$1.2 billion (Sliwa, 1998). Still other VANs are partnering with WWW software companies in an attempt to provide a wide range of consumer opportunities. (Adams, 1997; M. Anderson, 1997; T. Anderson, 1997; Jones, 1997).

Products and services already abound on the market and EDI users must investigate the solutions appropriate for their application. With the growth of the Internet, the market promises to bring many more products of all levels of sophistication to provide EDI users with a vast choice of hardware, software products and services offering a wide range of prices and levels of quality.

## Examples of Internet-based EDI

There are a certain number of companies who have been aggressive enough to adopt new WWW-based technologies. Ventura-Lesbro Companies, a Long Beach, California-based corporation that includes transportation services, was asked by one of its shippers to begin sending delivery information via EDI using a WWW gateway service from St Paul Software Inc. \`Ventura-Lesbro goes to the St. Paul Web site and enters the required information about a shipment, which is then forwarded automatically to major customers. While the privately held company used to fax this information, using the Internet-based EDI service provides a time stamp and, more important, creates a ® le that is compatible with its suppliers’ information system’ (Ubois, 1996, p. 85). This procedure reduces the time that is important as the company is graded on delivery times. The user claims another advantage is cost: \`There isn’t much markup in the transportation business; 97 to 98 percent of the price of a shipment represents our cost, so anything we can do to get that down is important’ (Ubois, 1996, p. 85).

Mobil has recently pulled its lubricant supply business on to the Superhighway. Now the company transacts with their 300 lubricant distributors through the Internet using their existing EDI purchase orders based on the EDI/X.12 protocol (Abcede, 1997). A front-end software interfaces with a WWW browser to transmit data. The interaction between distributors and Mobil is simple: once the distributor’s status is authenticated with an ID and a password, desktop WWW browsers give them access for purchase orders and product and other information update. Transactions are processed and implemented according to the company’ s business rules, for example only orders that meet speci® c load and weight speci® cations are allowed to take place. An Oracle database, integrated with Mobil’s mainframe database, provides information about current inventory and company communications, as well as processed orders, invoices and payments.

Even US health care is jumping on the Internet EDI bandwagon. In an attempt to reduce medical costs, both the Health Care Financing Administration (HCFA) which is the United States federal agency that administers the Medicare, Medicaid and Child Health insurance programmes and the Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS) have issued draft policies concerning the maintenance of con® dentiality of transmitted medical data. The HCFA draft policy allows Medicare contractors to use the Internet for the transmittal of medical data in a reversal of prior policy (Moynihan, 1999). These procedures include guidelines for security and electronic signature standards. Such positions by the federal government of the most sensitive data should help alleviate concerns relating to privacy and security over the Internet.

The Minnesota-based Mayo Clinic is \`extremely satis® ed’ with their Internet-based EDI according to Gary Beatty, electronic commerce specialist for the internationally renowned medical facility. During a recent interview with the authors of this paper, he further noted that \`. . . and security is paramount to maintaining privacy and con® dentiality of our patient claim information’. He further reported that approximately 85% of the Mayo Clinic’s patient claims are electronically submitted to their trading partners who are responsible for payment. The Mayo Clinic is using the ANSI X12 837 (claim) and 835 (payment) record sets while experimenting with the claim status record set. Data are transferred over the Internet using standard ftp capabilities encrypted with Pretty Good Privacy (PGP) software. The Mayo Clinic uses the Internet, a private health care network (MEDNET) and virtual private networks to transport claims.

Beatty indicated that four critical security considerations were addressed in their decision to embrace Internet-based EDI: privacy, authentication, repudiation and data integrity. The privacy issue was addressed through encryption using a high-grade commercial encryption key capability provided by their PGP software. Authentication is accommodated through digital signatures applied to the ® le so that the receiver of the transaction knows who sent the transaction to them while hashing provides a high degree of data integrity. To address the reliability issue associated with Internet transmissions, the translation loop is closed with an acknowledgement transaction used for repudiation purposes. In this manner, the sender is assured the recipient has received the transaction and cannot claim it was \`lost on the Internet.’

In responding to a question concerning WWWbased EDI, Beatty responded that, within the health care industry, WWW-based EDI was dif® cult because there are so many payers. In Rochester alone, he reported over 5000 active payers and each of these WWW pages has a different look and feel, different IDs and passwords and that the Mayo Clinic got to the point where they said they do not want to do this any more (beyond initial piloting). He feels WWWbased EDI will be dif® cult from a security standpoint because of the many-to-many nature of the transaction resulting in encryption keys (unless you go to a public key or authority) and authentication issues. Traditional EDI is more point-to-point in nature or through a hub clearing house.

When asked about the adoption decision, Beatty responded that it was primarily a communications cost decision. They looked at the investment, time and effort and, although traditional EDI was available, leveraging the existing EDI processing structure was not a major issue. However, trading partners (mostly payers) were very hesitant ± it has been an education process coupled with the success of the process that has changed their minds. In fact, when asked about \`words of wisdom’ for potential Internet EDI adopters, Beatty commented that

I look at traditional-based EDI and how we have communicated in the past ± in that we have used the telephone line and we have communicated across basically public networks applying no security precautions to that data. And to think that the Internet is going to be any less secure than that is ridiculous. People have, in my mind, a false sense of security today on how we transmit data traditionally. People say \`oh, we have a private T1 line’ and a T1 line is a billing concept that the communication companies came up with. That data is still travelling across and sharing bandwidth on public lines. I believe it to be a false sense of insecurity on the Internet. If we can apply these security technologies to the transactions ¯ owing across the Internet, in our minds, the data will be far more secure than transmissions we have done traditionally.

## Implications for research

The area of technological adoption is one that receives considerable attention in the literature. Models from management information systems (as represented in Yetton et al., 1999) focus primarily either on the characteristics of the innovation or implementation process itself. However, both of these common adoption frameworks are con® ned to a one-dimensional adoption decision. EDI concerns multiple organizations ± it brings buyers and sellers together. As such, EDI adoption is a bilateral decision process. Adoption of the technology without corresponding adoption by other trading partner organizations renders the technology useless from an interorganizational perspective (Kavan, 1991).

The strategy literature provides more insight into the adoption of a partnered technology. Business systems may be generally thought of in terms of a chain of value added activities where pro® t becomes value created less expenses associated with the creation of this value. Since EDI transcends organization boundaries, the adoption of such interorganizational systems becomes a bilateral strategic positioning decision rather than the one-dimensional \`internal’ adoption decision. It would seem that the degree of acceptance by other organizational members (concentration) of the value chain would be a very important determinant in the adoption process.

In the organizational decision process to move EDI from the more traditional VAN approach to the Internet, the organization faces greater risks and additional costs associated with a migration and/or establishment of this capability. Correspondingly, such migration opens new opportunities with greater potential bene® ts. However, from a strategic perspective, the use of VANs has acted as a (cost) barrier to entry as evidenced by the dominance of larger organizations. By converting to the more open standards of the Internet, organizations are altering the strategic balance of the market-place.

Once organizations adopt technology, they must integrate it into their organizations. Different stages of Internet-based EDI maturity (diffusion) would suggest varying strategies for effectively managing and supporting such growth. Contingency models such as Brown and Bostrom (1994) for end-user computing might be adopted to provide the needed linkage between industry structure analysis, strategy and technology ® t.

Internet-based EDI has found a degree of acceptance because it addresses a very real need in today’s business world. Numerous opportunities exist for studies and theory development particularly in the areas of adoption, concentration effect, strategic considerations and organizational diffusion. The use of the Internet as a conduit for EDI transactions is being used to gain competitive advantage but few guidelines or frameworks exist to guide that endeavour. Since the basis of the information economy is a global network, one of the aims of this paper was to stimulate research interest in this area which is rapidly gaining greater acceptance from both practitioners and academics alike.

## Conclusion

EDI users reluctant to be moved into Internet-based technologies can take comfort that the issues of concern ± standards, reliability, security and legislation ± are being addressed and, in many cases, are already ® nding solutions. Technology moves at a terri® c pace and will answer all issues with the cooperation of public and private organizations. Perhaps, EDI users are confronted with more than a technological change; they must undergo a cultural change, a mind-set change to embrace a technology which promises to link each and every individual on the surface of this planet. Lou Gerstner, IBM chairman, summed it up well: \`E-business moves the agenda of the IT industry back into the CEO’s of® ce. The new mantra is growth ± globalization, cycle times, speed, and competitiveness. What we keep saying to our customers is that E-business is not a technological change. It’s a fundamental change in the way business will be done in their industries-aided, abetted, supported, and enabled by technology’ (Caldwell, 1998, p. 18).

Heineken, Mobil, Lesboro and the Mayo Clinic all recognized the need for change and have embraced the new culture reaping the promised bene® ts of the new technology: decreased costs and improved performance with a maximum level of reliability and security. As the technology is re® ned, the bene® ts will increase and the risks will be minimized, bringing even the most resistant organizations into the Internet EDI fold. Information is commerce. As the informationbased economy grows, it will be built upon a global network. That network already exists ± the Internet and the protocol for data communication already exists ± EDI. The marriage of these two technologies seems inevitable.
