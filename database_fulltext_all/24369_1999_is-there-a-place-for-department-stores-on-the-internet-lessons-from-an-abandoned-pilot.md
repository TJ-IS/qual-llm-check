# Is there a place for department stores on the Internet? Lessons from an abandoned pilot.

CHRIS SAUER

Templeton College, Oxford OX1 5NY, UK

SUZAN BURTON

Macquarie Graduate School of Management, Macquarie University, NSW 2109, Australia

The potential of Internet retailing is widely promoted yet some retailers have been slow to embrace the technology. Department stores typically have only an information presence on the World Wide Web. This paper describes a pilot project by one of Australia’ s leading department stores. The case is used to describe certain lessons about Internet retailing and the use of pilot projects. Department stores’ decisions whether to adopt electronic retailing are analysed in terms of the opportunities and threats that face them and the capabilities required for success. The conclusion is reached that, as yet, there is no obvious place for established department stores on the Internet.

## Introduction

Electronic marketing and selling is an extraordinary but as yet immature development in the history of retailing. It is heavily promoted by interested parties such as the media, consultants and technology suppliers. Economic forecasts and technology trends project rapidly growing access to the Internet and ready adoption of the technology by consumers. Statistics have shown growth in the use of the Internet of up to 400% in 2 years (Bannister, 1998). The unprecedented valuations during 1998 and 1999 of Internet-related stocks indicate investors’ con® dence, at least during this period, that the technology’ s potential will translate into commercial success. The promoters’ message to retailers has been clear ± the Internet offers an enormous opportunity for selling more and better and any company that fails to invest in Internet retailing risks losing market share to more progressive competitors. Exhortations such as \`Catch the wave or drown’ are said to be the \`advice of industry experts to retailers resisting the onslaught of the Internet’ (Anonymous, 1998). Inde® nite delay or caution is said to result in \`stagnation and missed opportunities’ (Deighton, 1996).

Behind the hype, however, there are some data points which suggest caution is warranted. While a few companies such as Amazon.com and CD Now have set the pace in terms of customer service at the virtual shop counter, commentators have noted that many electronic retailers, including Amazon and Barnes and Noble’s Internet business, have not reported pro® ts (Davidson, 1999). In one study, fewer than half the retailers surveyed reported that their WWW-based activities were pro® table at this stage (Ernst & Young, 1999). Another study suggested that approximately 43% of companies surveyed were considered to be \`grounded’ or to have made no increase in WWW sales (O’ Keefe et al., 1998). While it is unclear whether these ® rms are still incurring start-up costs or whether they do not have the right business model, such examples suggest that pro® tability is dif® cult to achieve. Such data should raise concerns among retailers about the commercial wisdom of going online and, indeed, nearly 24% of retailers surveyed in a recent study had no plans to do so (Ernst & Young, 1999).

There are therefore crucial questions facing retail ® rms. Is electronic retail the unquali® ed opportunity suggested by some of its more extreme advocates? If not, should certain types of retailer avoid trading on the Internet altogether? For those ® rms that do see an opportunity, will delay result in its being missed, as Deighton (1996) has suggested, or will it allow a later, better planned entry and avoid the problems encountered by earlier entrants?

Promoters of electronic retail have often emphasized the commercial opportunities without full consideration of whether they are equally available to established retailers and start-ups and without analysis of the differences that might affect this. Established stores face constraints in terms of their external positioning and internal processes. These give rise to questions such as whether their existing competitive advantage translates on the Internet and whether their distribution processes can be easily integrated with an Internet selling channel.

Department stores are an interesting class of retailer because they have enjoyed high status among consumers for so long ± for new shopping precincts, they are typically signed up ® rst and then used as a lure to attract other retailers. Yet, despite advantages such as brand names which immediately help consumers identify likely pricing, service standards and product quality and which provide more guarantee of security and commercial integrity than an unknown start-up, household names such as Neiman Marcus in the US, John Lewis in the UK or David Jones in Australia maintain essentially an informational presence on the WWW. If they are to take the next step to develop a trading or transactional presence they must determine what issues and challenges they face. The experience of the David Jones chain provides an important lesson concerning some of these challenges.

David Jones, an Australian chain of department stores, established a pilot WWW site for on-line selling in 1996, just one year after Amazon.com commenced operations. For some measures, the site was rapidly successful. It became the twenty-third most visited site in Australia and was the most used retail site in Australia by a large margin. On-line selling extended the store’s market with over one-quarter of sales coming from geographic areas and customers outside the stores’ traditional reach. However, Internet trading on the site was effectively discontinued in 1997 and the stores’ site is now predominantly information only, a strategy which was adopted at the outset by other retailers (Murphy, 1998). What caused such an apparent success to be abandoned? The objective of this paper is to draw lessons from the David Jones experience. It contributes to our understanding of the issues facing established retailers in considering whether to trade electronically with speci® c reference to department stores. It also contributes to our understanding of how established retailers should establish an Internet retailing presence by exploring the advantages and disadvantages of prototyping.

This paper covers the following.

(1) Key points from the literature relating to electronic retail are noted.

(2) The David Jones experience is reported.

(3) The lessons for the established retailer considering trading on the Internet are described.

(4) Lessons in relation to piloting such an Internet retail site are described.

(5) The opportunities and threats faced by department stores in relation to Internet retailing and their strength/capabilities compared with those necessary for success

(6) The question of whether there is a place for department stores on the Internet is answered.

## Electronic retailing

The advantages of retailing on-line have been discussed by a variety of industry and academic authors (see, for example, Quelch and Klein, 1996; Alba et al., 1997; Pavitt, 1997). The advantages for the consumer include novelty, convenience, access to rare or specialized products and increased ability to compare prices and, hence, obtain a lower price. The advantages for the retailer include a larger potential market, the possibility of local customization and lower ® xed and/ or staff costs. In particular, the Internet appears to provide an attractive channel for accessing the desirable but hitherto hard-to-reach market segment of \`time-poor’ consumers who are sophisticated in the use of electronic channels (Reynolds, 1997).

However, certain barriers limit the adoption of online shopping. These include access to the Internet, delivery costs, delays and concerns about security and dif® culties with the technology which can be cumbersome to use and is less accepted by some groups and personality types (Fram and Grady, 1997; Jones and Vijayasarathy, 1998). For retailers, the cost of setting up and integrating a WWW site with the existing business and the lack of an appropriate ful® lment and distribution structure are seen as barriers to online success (Ernst & Young, 1999). Ful® lment issues are a particular problem for retailers of physical goods who have no history of catalogue operations. However, even for retailers with extensive experience in catalogue selling, the cross-over to on-line selling may be constrained by technology, with slow communications limiting the use of the graphics required to display a catalogue on-line (Rowley, 1998).

Studies consistently report that on-line purchases are increasing, but this is generally from a low base. The Ernst & Young (1999) Internet shopping study reported that the percentage of households surveyed in the US who shopped on-line increased from 7% in 1997 to 10% in 1998. The number of households online increased over the same period from 20 to 26%. Another 1% of households said they were \`very interested’ in shopping on-line in the future and 7% said they were \`somewhat interested’. While these statistics suggest continued growth in on-line sales, the limited interest reported does not augur well for a large increase in on-line shopping in the near future. In contrast with this limited consumer interest, competition on the Internet has increased dramatically and will increase still more. The same study showed that the percentage of retailers selling or planning to sell on-line doubled in 1 year, from 34 to 76%, so retailers face the risk of an expanding pool of suppliers competing for a less rapidly increasing number of customers.

The empirical evidence and theoretical advice suggests that on-line success is neither certain not easy for retailers. Kalakota and Whinston (1996) suggested that traditional companies provide products looking for a market, while Internet retailing is a vast market looking for a well-de® ned mercantile process. The right mercantile process needs to seamlessly combine userfriendly WWW design, attractive product and ef® cient ful® lment. All parts of the mercantile process need to work together within an appropriate business model to attract and pro® tably service customers.

Unfortunately, there has been limited guidance available for new entrants. Such guidance as has been explicitly formulated has usually been achieved through pragmatic abstraction from the practices of perceived market leaders, often start-ups. For example, the Boston Consulting Group (1998) identi® ed what it termed the \`cornerstones’ of electronic retail (Table 1). While couched in terms of the principles of good practice, this framework is based on an analysis of the distinctive characteristics of electronic retailing channels as experienced by pioneers and the opportunities and challenges these new channels pose. Although it makes no explicit mention of the problems of integrating electronic channels with more traditional channels within the same business, we use the Boston Consulting Group framework in our subsequent discussion because, when applied in the context of an established department store, it helps us to surface the relevant issues and challenges.

## The David Jones case

## Research method

It was one of the ® rst department stores to commence selling on-line and its early success and ultimate withdrawal can provide lessons for other retailers.

Case study is an appropriate research method where research is exploratory (Benbasat et al., 1987; Yin, 1989). In view of the current immaturity of electronic retail (there are no models of sustained success and few examples of pro® tability), a case study is epistemically appropriate. A case study also has the advantage of accessing the type of experience from which industry itself learns (March et al., 1991).

As a consequence of this interpretive approach combined with our exploratory intent, we have not adopted any particular prior theoretical stance but have largely followed where interpretation has led us. Our decision to view the David Jones experience as a pilot or prototype emerged not from any prior commitment to strategic prototyping but from the company’s own re¯ ections on the experience and its obvious relevance in an area of commercial activity which can involve major strategic and, hence, irreversible bets and which is not well-understood by those engaged in it.

Other major players involved in the on-line selling experience had left the organization. The literature was searched for comparable case studies of department stores which had adopted then retreated from on-line selling. A contrasting retail strategy of an information-based WWW site has been reported (Murphy, 1998), but there appears to have been no other analysis of a department store which has retreated from a strategy of on-line selling. ## Case description

David Jones is Australia’ s eighth largest retail group ranked by market capitalization (Business Review Weekly, 1998) and is said to be the world’s oldest department store operating continuously under one name (David Jones, 1998). David Jones is generally considered to be the most upmarket retailer in the Australian market, an image reinforced by heavily advertised and expensive ¯ oral displays and a product range which includes high-priced, imported designer-ware. Its most pro® table customer segment is the professional female. The store has cultivated an image of excellent service, using an advertising slogan \`There’s no other store like David Jones’. Its reputation for service, quality products and a \`noquestions-asked’ returns policy has been associated with a perception of higher prices, a claim strongly rejected by the store, which advertises widely that it will match any competitor’s price.

In 1996, the market for on-line selling appeared attractive. Australian Internet usage increased from 12% in 1996 to 20% in 1997, one of the highest growth rates in the world at the time. While Australia has a limited user base because of its small population, it is an attractive market, with high educational levels, high disposable income and a traditional passion for consumer electronics. For example, at that time the country had the world’s highest penetration of video cassette recorders and mobile phones (Horwitt, 1997). The David Jones chairman’s address to the shareholders in 1996 recognized the potential for growth in home shopping and announced the launch of on-line shopping.

The publicy stated aim of the site was to \`provide customers, especially those geographically isolated from . . . stores, with another way of viewing and purchasing products’. It was believed that the site would be particularly attractive to customers living outside Australia who could order gifts for delivery to friends and relatives in Australia and New Zealand and, thus, avoid international delivery charges. The on-line initiative was also envisaged to be a valuable way of gaining a detailed pro® le of individual customers, with the potential for long-term customization. A store representative was quoted as saying that \`Although on-line commerce is in its infancy, we have to play in this emerging market-place’ (Horwitt, 1997).

David Jones had previously had an information only page on the Internet, but the new site was much more sophisticated. It was developed with expert help: marketing input was obtained from Saatchi and Saatchi, WWW design from The Harrow Group and the payment system was designed by Microsoft Consulting Services. The store had previously run a catalogue and mail order operation selling a limited part of its product range by mail, so had some experience in ful® lment. This was largely con® ned to the Australian market except for its experience in delivering food and wine gift baskets to Europe and North America.

The site allowed customers to go through an online catalogue or to search through different departments by keywords or price. It was designed to encourage repeated visits, offering a free greeting card service and incentives such as a gift with purchases or discounts for items bought over the WWW. The exact nature of the promotion was changed every 2 days to encourage repeat traf® c. Other features included the ability to enter personal characteristics for a gift recipient and the site would respond with gift suggestions. As well as advertising in its key local markets, the site was advertised in the print media in the UK and Asia (Horwitt, 1997).

The early history of the site seemed encouraging. It attracted a large number of hits and, according to store reports, was receiving approximately 6000± 8000 visits per day within 8 months of opening. For the ® rst Christmas during which the site was operating, more than 15 000 greeting cards were sent. However, less than 1% of hits were translated into actual sales (Plunkett, 1997), with a large percentage of hits from younger males, characterized within the store as \`techno-nerds’. Twenty-seven per cent of sales were from outside Australia, with the largest single customer reportedly living in Toronto, Canada. Another large segment of users was older professional males, a very different group from the store’s key target customers, professional women. The store’s business development manager was quoted as saying that, within 3 years, 5± 7% of business would be electronic, equivalent to building two or three new stores (Moriarty, 1997).

Operationally, however, the site was far less successful. Expansion into new geographic markets and customer segments tested the store’ s ful® lment ability. Orders arrived on the computer of one employee, who would walk to the relevant department, ® nd the item and arrange delivery and billing. Since the on-line product range was based around the store’s catalogue, which (in common with many catalogues) featured many products with a limited life, stock-outs were common. The on-line customer would then be contacted by phone to determine whether they were prepared to wait, with the result that many orders became manual and time-consuming. The cost of invoicing and delivering also made small orders uneconomical. After delivery costs, buying on-line was more expensive for the customer and volume was insuf® cient to cover the costs of maintaining the service (Carter, 1997).

During the period, the department store chain’s overall ® nancial performance disappointed the sharemarket, creating pressures for short-term measures to enhance pro® tability including cutbacks and, ultimately, resulting in the departure of the incumbent CEO. Faced with an on-line operation which was not pro® table, a poorly designed ful® lment system and company-wide ® nancial pressures, the site was downgraded to an information only page by the incoming CEO, with on-line selling restricted to Christmas food and wine baskets. There are no plans to resume more extensive on-line selling in the near future.

## Lessons from on-line trading

There are a number of lessons for retail stores in the David Jones example. To start, their experience con-® rmed the emerging truisms that Internet sites increase market reach and make the retailer an international supplier overnight. As we have seen, David Jones expanded its market, both geographically and into new demographic segments. Unfortunately, neither truism guarantees pro® tability. International orders proved more expensive to ful® l and without a unique product range did not appear to provide a sustainable market in the face of increasing on-line competition. Although the site was very successful at accessing two hard-to-reach local segments, younger technologically comfortable males and older professional men, neither group was a good match with the store’s key customer segment, professional women or its major product, medium- to highprice fashion. More importantly, neither on-line segment presented a particularly attractive longer term prospect: the young males tended not to buy and, while the older males accounted for a large percentage of sales, their purchases were often relatively low cost and low margin.

The store’s problems with ful® lment demonstrate the importance of an integrated logistics and supply chain. When the site was set up, demand was uncertain and the store decided to provide its normal catalogue products on-line and use a manual picking system. Essentially, ful® lment was seen as an extension of the store’s normal business processes, similar to the way in which telephone sales had always been handled. The increased demand of on-line sales imposed on a limited-life catalogue together with a manual ful® lment system resulted in stock-outs, frustrated customers and an expensive and timeconsuming ful® lment process. Using existing processes to supply and deliver on-line products is a tempting option for any store because it minimizes start-up costs and avoids the risk of setting up a supply for on-line orders which may not eventuate. However, as David Jones found, this is likely to result in problems; without an ef® cient supply process, stock-outs are likely to occur and an inef® cient ful® lment process will not be able to cope with growth.

A further problem for the department store, as David Jones found out, is that the customers who buy on-line may be very different from its usual customers. $_ { \mathbb { W } { \mathrm { a l - } } M { \mathrm { a r t } } }$ has also found this with its WWW site: its on-line shoppers typically do not buy the products which are the mainstays of Wal-Mart stores, i.e. clothing, cosmetics, motor accessories and linens (Kuchinskas, 1998). This is a lesson that has also been learned by department stores with large catalogue operations: catalogue buyers tend to be different people buying different products, compared to customers who visit the store. This results in two levels of uncertainty for the store which launches on-line selling; it is likely to ® nd itself selling to different customers and, to satisfy those customers, will need to move into new product ranges. David Jones’ one enduring success from on-line selling was in creating an on-line channel for its food and wine gift basket business. With a well-tested delivery process, the store could move its food and wine basket business on-line pro® tably in a way it could not manage with a much wider product range.

## Lessons from the pilot process

Information technologists often undertake prototype or pilot systems in order to manage the risk of investing in poorly understood technologies and business processes. Prototyping may be intended purely as a learning process with the pilot system being thrown away once learning has been achieved or it may be treated as a partial base from which a richer system will evolve as the learning is incrementally integrated into system development and enhancement (Hekmatpour and Ince, 1986; Gordon and Bieman, 1995). Prototyping may be horizontal, involving the development of a simulated interface that has no functionality behind it or vertical, involving a fully functional subset of the required characteristics of the system (Friedman, 1989). In view of the limited availability of experience and expertise in the technology, the lack of an established business model and the widely held view that pioneering companies such as Amazon have not yet become pro® table, it is plainly appropriate for department stores to pilot Internet retailing.

The David Jones pilot involved vertical prototyping of the retail sales process. There were several bene® ts from this approach. The prototype could be largely constructed and managed under the direction of a single champion, the business development manager. This permitted rapid decision making and progress because the pilot did not need to be tightly integrated into other business processes. It enabled David Jones to explore a number of key commercial and technical issues that would face them in a full implementation of Internet retailing including marketing, selling security and management information.

The downside of the David Jones pilot was that it did not address the critical commercial issue of order ful® lment. Internet retail start-ups can either trade from their own central warehouse like eToys (Sellers, 1999) or outsource all inventory and logistics like Dell Computers’ computer peripherals operations (Tully,

1993). However, like most department stores, David Jones’ inventory is geographically distributed across the retail ¯ oors of its stores and in small back-up store rooms. This arrangement is ef® cient for the store when the shopper picks their own goods but profoundly inef® cient when it comes to ful® lling Internet retail orders. Other than foreshadowing that any serious attempt to make Internet retail pro® table would require a totally new order ful® lment process, the pilot revealed nothing about how such a process might work.

In effect, the experience showed that a signi® cantly bigger investment would be required for a pilot from which the store could learn enough to proceed to design and implement a full Internet retail system and supporting business process. This might involve establishing a warehouse operation in a cheap location, in the form of a pseudo-store, from which to pick and distribute Internet orders. The pseudo-store would hold standard product lines that could be restocked, thereby avoiding having continually to delete products that would not be reordered from the WWW site. This would permit the company to experiment with integrated order ful® lment and could take advantage of the company’s established merchandizing competencies. A signi® cant disadvantage is that this system would involve duplication of inventory with the retail stores and consequently higher inventory costs. The wider the range of goods offered by the online site, the higher the inventory holding costs would be, a problem also faced by catalogue retailers. However, the immediacy of e-mail is likely to lead customers to expect a faster response to an electronic order leading to less tolerance of delays caused by stock-outs.

The case helps us to recognize the difference between a technological prototype motivated by the desire to learn about operational matters and a commercial pilot project motivated by the desire to explore a strategic alternative. The David Jones Internet experiment was largely driven by technological experimentation but it had potentially serious commercial implications. Because the pilot WWW site took orders from customers and actively promoted its success, David Jones achieved a prime mover position in on-line retailing. By downgrading the site, the store retreated from this leadership position at a time when the on-line market was continuing to grow. In doing so, it risked loss of customer goodwill, damage to its brand image and gave competitors the opportunity to seize the position of innovative retailer. The case shows the commercial importance of pilot implementations and the desirability of formulating a contingency plan for a managed withdrawal. The case also demonstrates the importance of specifying in advance clear objectives which de® ne the scope of the pilot and associated measurement criteria by which the success of the pilot can be evaluated.

## The challenge for department stores

The value of re¯ ecting on the David Jones case extends beyond the immediate lessons on the practicalities of becoming on-line retailers described in the previous two sections. It offers a stimulating context within which to make a more generic assessment of what electronic retail means for department stores and how they should respond. Therefore, in this section we consider the threat posed by electronic retail, the opportunities it might offer and the capabilities department stores typically have compared to those currently thought necessary for successful selling on the Internet. In short, we argue that the threat may be limited, that electronic retail offers little strategic opportunity because it is too easy to imitate or substitute for most of those department store strengths that can be readily translated to the electronic medium and that department stores are not well-endowed with the capabilities necessary to achieve outstanding success in selling through electronic channels. On this analysis, department stores would be advised to concentrate on their existing business rather than attempting to integrate electronic retail into their established operations.

## Is electronic retail a threat to department stores?

There are two main ways in which on-line competitors can threaten established retail stores. The ® rst is erosion of the market by competition. While there has always been price competition, in the virtual market-place department stores face new competitors. On the one hand, they are challenged by specialized retail stores such as Barnes and Noble who can offer a wider range of product in a specialized area. On the other hand, they are challenged by start-up companies without the cost disadvantage of bricksand-mortar shop fronts, who can deliver an equal or greater product range at lower cost. Caught between these two competitors, there is a risk that department stores will be subject to piecemeal cannibalization as they lose business in areas which are heavily traded on-line such as books, compact discs and those basic fashion items that are frequently restocked.

The extent of this threat is hard to estimate. Department stores have always faced competition from specialists. Indeed, part of their strength has been that they save customers from having to purchase from multiple specialists. The difference now is that electronic retail diminishes or removes the costs of time and distance. However, because electronic retail is most accessible to relatively af¯ uent customers for whom cost is often a lesser consideration, there would appear to be relatively little threat at present to downmarket stores. In contrast, it is reasonable to assume that more upmarket stores will experience some erosion of their business over time, but how much will depend on the extent to which their business favours in-person shopping and the extent to which their customers are time poor.

The second threat, then, is the possible decline of in-person shopping. This implies both reduced volumes of planned purchases and reduced volumes of impulse purchases, the latter being department stores’ source of pro® tability. An overall decline in in-person shopping would potentially eat away at the edges of the market with fewer people spending less time in shopping centres and precincts and, therefore, buying less from all shops including department stores.

On the other hand, in-person shopping may not be seriously threatened by the Internet. Though a clich‚ , it is salutary to observe that the merchants of doom were wrong in declaring the cinema dead when video arrived, just as the arrival of home delivery pizza did not spell the end of the dine-in pizza parlour. In 1996, non-store retailing (Internet, other on-line services, television home shopping and catalogues) was estimated to account for only 5± 10% of all retail sales, with Internet sales accounting for less than 1% of nonstore shopping (Alba et al., 1997) and being mostly con® ned to books, compact discs and certain lower priced products (Quelch and Klein, 1996). In contrast, there is always likely to be a role for the physical stores in areas such as high fashion, where it is important for the customer to handle the product. Thus, for David Jones, with its target market of professional women, electronic retail may prove to be very little threat. Perhaps more importantly, the Internet cannot reproduce all of the sensory and social elements of the in-person shopping experience. Retail shopping has been shown to be preferred to on-line or mail order shopping in at least one study (White and Manning, 1998). For many consumers and, arguably, for the most pro® table customers of the department stores, browsing online will never have the same attraction as in-store shopping. On-line shopping offers convenience and in-home purchases, but catalogue and television shopping have offered this for years yet the department stores have persisted. Moreover, it is possible that on-line shopping will start to suffer by compari-son with the enhanced in-person experiences currently being developed by advanced retailers who are seeking to reinvent shopping as entertainment to encourage increased traf® c in malls and shopping centres. Thus, there are various reasons why department stores may not be threatened by developments in electronic retail.

## Is electronic retail an opportunity for department stores?

To decide whether electronic retail constitutes an opportunity for department stores, executives must clarify the role of electronic sales in relation to the existing business. This involves a clear analysis of the store’s competitive advantage and consideration of whether this will extend to on-line sales. Department stores have thrived over the past century because they have decreased the search costs for customers ± their distinctive value proposition has been to offer the convenience of a particular price/quality trade-off for a wide range of goods displayed under one roof. Robert Wood, who ran Sears Roebuck from 1928 to 1954 during its expansion from catalogue merchant to retailer, expressed this when he said that Sears would be \`the purchasing agent for the American people’ (Stewart, 1998). However, the distinctiveness of this proposition is undermined by the ease with which an even larger range of goods can be offered on the Internet through a single access point without the overheads of physical stores.

On the Internet, the role of the department store as a provider of a range of products and services can be easily replicated without expensive investment in a physical network of stores and inventory. Single access points can be rapidly developed and take a variety of shapes including virtual shopping malls and portals such as AOL and Yahoo which provide new distribution channels for the customer to obtain a variety of products without physically visiting multiple stores. Such access points can provide a gateway to any number of specialist services. Some of these providers, because they do not have the ® xed costs of retail outlet, are able to compete on price as well as quality of service. The department store is then reduced to a known brand which may be useful in attracting customers ± the name of a department store like Macy’s will provide some brand value on-line ± but this value is likely to be short-lived as it is usurped by the new brand names of the Internet. Moreover, in electronic retail, where expertise in logistics and ful® lment are crucial capabilities, the key competencies of department stores (location choice, merchandise selection and personal service) represent a less compelling basis for competitive advantage.

A simple device for analysing competitive opportunity is Porter’s (1985) three generic strategies: cost leadership, differentiation and focus. We argue that it is unlikely that department stores will have the logistics experience to achieve cost leadership or the specialized skills to achieve on-line differentiation. And, while a retail store may be able to pursue a strategy of focus by building on its existing client base, the nature of WWW sites, with high set-up costs and low marginal costs per customer, is fundamentally inconsistent with a focus strategy. This leaves department stores with the risk of establishing a site which is neither the cheapest nor the most interesting and which consequently cannot generate economically feasible levels of WWW sales, a situation in which David Jones found itself and which has been described by Porter (1985) as \`stuck in the middle’ or the worst strategy of all.

Counsel of caution for department stores is reinforced by re¯ ection on the levels of success of existing electronic retailers. Currently, most of the major players are pursuing a price/volume strategy, rapidly developing increasingly complex WWW sites incorporating sophisticated marketing and sales techniques. Despite building high volume quickly, few if any of the major Internet retailers have yet declared a pro® t. It remains an act of faith on the part of the sharemarkets that some of these companies will be able to cut costs or increase revenue to the point where unit revenue exceeds unit costs. At present, it is estimated that the typical on-line retailer spends 65% of revenues on marketing and advertising costs compared to less than 5% by conventional retailers (Sellers, 1999). At the same time, the rapidly increasing number of retailers on-line suggests that ® erce price competition will continue. This is a game of high stakes with no fail-soft option. Established retailers have much to lose by joining this game.

It is also worthwhile for department stores to question whether, even if Amazon do eventually become pro® table, their success will translate into a general triumph of electronic retail. For example, it may be that there are only a limited number if markets in which electronic retail is commercially viable in the foreseeable future. One reason is that, ultimately, electronic retail may be perceived as an advanced form of catalogue shopping. While undoubtedly occupying a pro® table niche, catalogue shopping does not appear to have signi® cantly detracted from in-store purchases and has never been as successful in Europe or Australia as in North America. In small markets, logistics costs may simply always be too high. Where Amazon.com can sell some books in Australia because its wider product range and price advantage is suf® ciently great to compensate for the higher delivery charges and extra delivery time, an Australian-based company such as David Jones may be unable to conduct pro® tably the same commercial traf® c in reverse. We conclude that the opportunities for department stores are not as obvious as the hype might lead the unwary to believe.

## Do department stores have the capabilities to be successful electronic retailers?

We address this question from two perspectives. First, we consider the potential for leveraging the established strengths of department stores into an electronic form of business, using David Jones as a case in point. Second, we use the Boston Consulting Group’s cornerstones of electronic retail introduced earlier to look in the other direction and assess the likelihood that department stores have what it takes to be successful electronic retailers.

For on-line retailing to be more than a high-risk entrepreneurial venture, it must entail the store leveraging its relevant strengths such as its brand, merchandizing or logistics. David Jones’ experience reminds us that the logistics required by a department store are quite different from what is required by an electronic retailer and strengths such as the store network and salesforce are irrelevant in an on-line market. Merchandizing too is likely to require different skills. For example, David Jones sells a high proportion of up-market, non-repeat product lines, but most Internet retail strategies compete on price and volume. Unless David Jones could access a much larger market than it currently does, its existing merchandizing skills are likely to be irrelevant on-line.

This leaves the value of the brand which we discussed in more general terms in the previous section. For David Jones, this is associated with a highquality image at the top end of the market. It is probably this positioning which, in the pilot project, attracted the older male who does not enjoy shopping. The question for David Jones is whether there is a suf® ciently large and valuable market in the segments it can attract and, if so, whether the company can pro® tably exploit this segment, given that its brand currently only has high value in Australia and among Australians (approximately 19 million people) and that the Internet provides other ways of offering a similar range of products. Moreover, the success of the pioneering \`dot-com’ enterprises in building a global brand in a very short time suggests that the brand could be readily supplanted by an electronic competitor.

Our argument in the previous two paragraphs has been that the established strengths and advantages of department stores do not obviously translate to successful on-line selling. Our next step is to consider the extent to which department stores have strengths in what the Boston Consulting Group (1998) called the cornerstones of electronic retail. Table 2 summarizes this part of our analysis.

Department stores’ understanding of customer needs is valuable but limited in helping them establish successful Internet retail operations. While their ability to reduce search costs for customers by selling diverse products from the one location is easily imitated on the Internet and, hence, not a major advantage, their strength lies in the capabilities of store buyers who understand their target market segments. However, they rarely know their customers individually and in this respect are at a disadvantage against established Internet retailers who are able to identify customers when they ® rst enter a site and to tailor their experience according to characteristics stored on or inferred from their database record.

The information richness provided by experienced sales staff in relation to their products is of little value in Internet retailing where information needs only to be provided once for it to be continually available. Department stores are well practised in building sticky relationships with customers. These will be useful but it remains an open question how effectively this competence will transfer into a world where the CEO of Amazon.com can and does communicate at will with any or all of his customers. For example, department stores’ experience with personal shopping services could prove a competitive advantage, but it is not obviously easy to capitalize on this with multiple customers on the WWW. It is also unclear how well department stores’ ability to attract and keep customers in-store will translate into the world of virtual stores and, hence, permit them to leverage customer relationships and convert browsers to buyers. While David Jones does brand the in-store experience by appealing to all the senses through devices such as fresh ¯ ower arrangements and a piano player, the limitations of the Internet in this regard may actually devalue aspects of this particular capability.

In relation to the use of customer statistics to shorten market learning curves, the issue is not whether department stores can do it, but how long it might take them to learn to interpret them in relation to the new market segments reached by an Internet channel. Apart from the art of store location in relation to other stores, department stores have not typically marketed through linkages and associations with interest groups and similar. Since this is an area of Internet commerce where there is feverish innovation, a traditional department store would not be well-prepared to exploit connectivity in this way.

At the heart of these cornerstones is the issue of building a scaleable and defensible business model. We have already indicated that for department stores a focus or niche strategy may be the only option if they are to provide a distinctive service to their customers. Exactly what would be required to make such a strategy defensible is unclear. Unless the site is viewed as an essential service to customers which does not need to operate under normal pro® t requirements, stores will have to be able to achieve suf® cient scale to cover the costs of WWW site development and ful® lment. This implies having an ef® cient logistics system through which products are consistently available and can be delivered fast and at low cost. Unless they are experienced in catalogue operations, traditional retailers are likely to lack both the necessary system and the experience in managing it. In summary, it is dif® cult to identify capabilities that would give established department stores a head start in Internet retail and easy to see that they would have to undertake substantial learning. In the absence of a proven business model for Internet department stores, any venture will be a high-risk experiment.

In our analysis, electronic retailing for established department stores must be seen as a major strategic bet based on a limited set of relevant capabilities. The choice for department stores really amounts to either entering electronic retail as an almost entirely new and separate business or choosing to work on enhancing the in-person experience so as to further differentiate it from electronic retail. Thus, in the absence of a compelling argument for adding an electronic channel, it may be better for the department stores to get better at the game they know best, rather than try to beat electronic retailers at the game they are currently inventing.

## Conclusion

In this paper we have used a case study of the shortlived Internet retailing experience of an Australian chain of department stores as a vehicle for exploring a number of issues which confront an established retailer considering adopting a transactional presence on the Internet. The case has con® rmed some of the well-recognized advantages of a retail WWW site while also highlighting some of the obstacles to commercial success. In particular, we have emphasized the importance of having ful® lment logistics integrated with order taking and the dif® culties this poses for traditional department stores. The case has also shown us that pilot projects need to be carefully thought out so as to include ful® lment, but that this increases the cost of an experimental venture.

We have argued that department stores may not be threatened by electronic retail but equally we do not see electronic shopping channels as a clear and attractive opportunity for them. Since the business capabilities necessary for traditional store operations do not readily translate to the cornerstones of Internet retailing and since it remains to be seen whether a viable long-term business model emerges by which department stores might sell and deliver physical products pro® tably, a cautious approach of wait-and-see or a pilot initiative seems most appropriate.

This leads us to the questions we posed in the Introduction. First, will delay in establishing a retail WWW site result in missed opportunities or will it permit a later, better planned entry? The David Jones experience shows us that early entry did not confer any competitive advantage in that case. What it did provide was some data and some lessons which the store can use in any further examination of its Internet strategy. Second, should traditional retailers follow the same path as start-ups? In our analysis, traditional department stores lack a set of distinctive capabilities that would permit them to follow a different path from start-ups. Thus, if the start-ups do prove to have a successful business model, it follows that a department store would be advised to follow that path. This implies that a department store choosing to go on-line should set up a separate business in which it would develop the relevant capabilities rather than try to integrate with the existing business. However, our analysis demonstrates no compelling reasons for department stores to make the enormous investment of, in effect, setting up a new and very different business. Thus, in answer to our third question, whether certain types of retailer should avoid trading electronically, if there is any signi® cant place for department stores on the Internet, it is yet to be identi® ed. We think that for the time being they will do better to invest in reinventing themselves.
