# Teaching cases for information systems

## Introduction

The teaching value of cases is well recognized in business schools. They are widely used both in MBA programmes and in short executive development courses. Typically, they provide a moderately realistic context in which to explore the application of academic theories and models, though this, of course, does not exhaust their potential value. Their relevance is less well recognized in undergraduate IS departments and still less in computer science and other technical departments that sometimes house IS faculties.

Established case providers such as Harvard Business School, Ivey School of Business and Case Clearing House have typically set and sustained high standards of quality. The downside has been that the set of available cases is constrained, with access limited for some educational institutions by their, or their students’, ability to pay. The style of each providers’ cases is regimented, with some providers seemingly encouraging highly detailed, \`mega-cases’ that are perhaps not appropriate for all classes and purposes. While their life span varies, many lose their freshness quickly. This is particularly so in a fast moving area like IS/IT. The major case providers offer little room for innovation. Furthermore, because most of the best-known cases have US and Canadian settings, notwithstanding such famous cases as Singapore’s TRADENET, they can lack appeal for European, Asian and other students. Though in the last ® ve years we have seen a growing published supply of topical cases that are set outside the North American context.

In the past, teaching cases have often been written by doctoral students under the supervision of a senior faculty member. For the faculty member there has usually been little academic recognition for their effort. This is curious given that the case has often been the subject of rigorous and intensive research, and that the development of an effective set of teaching notes (or instructor’s manual) is both an intellectually challenging exercise and offers substantial academic value to other course leaders who use the case and notes.

In championing the introduction of, and acting as track chairs for, a refereed teaching case track at the Seventeenth International Conference in Information Systems at Helsinki Finland in December 1998, our purposes were to:

(1) increase the set of available IS cases

(2) ensure a continuing supply of up to date cases

(3) make available cases from a broad variety of cultural contexts

(4) encourage innovation in the preparation of teaching cases

(5) provide academic recognition for the production of teaching cases

(6) introduce teaching cases to a wider academic community than just business schools.

What struck us, as teachers, was the constant need for fresh cases addressing current issues ± as for example ERP implementation, and internet usage herein ± and cases offering new slants on perennial issues ± such as the Nortel and Riverbank cases in the present volume. As researchers we also felt that the teaching potential of all too many solid pieces of research were remaining unexploited. One reason for this has been that teaching cases have rarely been accorded the academic merit many of them deserve. As a result we provided detailed guidelines for the ICIS 1998 teaching case track, ensured that all entries were based on original research, and put all submissions through the same refereeing procedures as were applied to the research paper and case research streams that year. Some thirty cases were received and only eight were accepted, after revisions. Because of space constraints only short synopses of the cases could be published in the ICIS Proceedings, though the case presentations at the Conference were both detailed and well attended, and included discussion about teaching strategies and the issues emerging from each case. We decided that there was suf® cient demand, and suf® - cient academic merit in the cases, to proceed with a Special Issue in the present refereed journal. The eight cases underwent a further refereeing process, which resulted in their further development for teaching purposes. Several additional cases were put forward for refereeing but, of these, only that of Telstra’s National Marketing Centre survived the process. The result is somewhat innovative, we believe ± nine highly useful and topical refereed teaching cases being published for the ® rst time in a special issue of a peer reviewed, archival journal.

## What makes a good teaching case: some points

Critical evaluation both of the cases published here and of the cases submitted but not published helps us to articulate, brie¯ y, the strengths that make a good teaching case. Whatever the merits of alternatives such as ® ctional cases, or of cases assembled from journalistic press cuttings, or from an interview with the CEO, our own conviction is that academically well researched cases, suitably modi® ed, make the best teaching cases.

The Abstract is both an important marketing device for the case author and the source of crucial ©productº information for the potential instructor. In it, the topic area should be described (e.g. interorganizational electronic supply chain management in the Hong Kong freight forwarding business), courses for which the case is and is not suitable should be stated (e.g. suitable for electronic commerce courses for business students at graduate level but not for technical computer science students because it requires a basic understanding of supply chain management and corporate strategy), the type of student task should be speci® ed, and the support provided by the teaching notes should be outlined.

We have found that most cases include an interesting situation description or narrative. Situations that involve innovative technologies, applications or organizational and management interventions make for particularly valuable additions to the existing corpus of teaching cases. A timeline of events also forms an important backbone for organizing the case material.

Cases rarely describe a simple set of circumstances or events. Often a single case description could be used to teach about several important topics, say IS governance structures, BPR and implementation. It is important to restrict the focus so as to ensure the case is relevant to the student task. One of the toughest dilemmas facing the case writer is to what extent it adds educational value to seed the case with red herrings, leaving the student the task of separating pertinent information from the interesting irrelevancies that typically abound in real business problems. There is no approved resolution to this dilemma, but it becomes obvious that there is a strong crafting element in the construction of a usable teaching case.

Interest value alone, however, is not enough. Cases should be assembled around a task for the students. This task may be to solve a problem, recommend a decision, provide an analysis, or it may be to discuss the situation described. The better de® ned the task, the easier it is to structure and write the case so that the information students need is included without taking unnecessarily long to read. Cases are made more real and absorbing by the appropriate use of quotations from key players in the company described. Hard data such as company performance ® gures or accounts are best included in appendices, and tabulated where possible. That said, the clever case leaves itself open for several interpretations, rather than closing down on, and pointing the way too obviously towards one.

While most cases are interesting, it is the teaching notes that distinguish the excellent from the everyday. In writing the notes, case writers should bear in mind that as a result of their original research they will understand the case and will be able to respond ¯ exibly in class to unanticipated questions and ideas. By contrast, most instructors using the case may never have any direct contact with the organization in question. It is therefore crucial that the teaching notes be clear, provide potentially useful background to instructors, and provide an approved solution/set of responses to the student task. It is particularly helpfu if the notes give the instructor follow-up information, for example what a company actually decided to do and how it turned out. Other useful components of teaching notes include: the outline of a mini-lecture (5 or 10 minutes): a structured classroom process with suggested timings: resources to give to students ahead of reading the case - which could include a reading list or a list of relevant web addresses, particularly that of any companies/organizations involved. Prospective case writers should note that authors who have used their cases and suggested process in the classroom prior to ® nalizing their notes are invariably able to provide a lot more supportive guidance for instructors.

## The teaching cases

And so to the cases, there are nine cases in all in this Special Issue and we have taken the liberty of classifying them under four main headings, as below.

## Business strategy and information technologies

The ® rst two cases deal with companies whose main products and services are high technology based. Our ® rst, by Paul Richardson and Peter Weill, deals with Telstra, a major Australian telecommunications provider. In the early 1990s the Australian government began to deregulate telecommunications. By 1997 the industry was fully deregulated. The case focuses on the history and development of Telstra’ s National Telemarketing Centre, a major marketing channel and provider of customer and product support services. The case is particularly useful for looking at business and the customer service roles of call centres and related technologies within the competitive business context of a major telecommunications service.

The second case, by Keng Siau, deals with Xcert Software, Inc. Founded in 1996, it is in the business of developing Internet and Intranet security enhancement technology. As a start-up IT company it faces ® erce competition and experiences a range of problems. As well as developing an understanding of Internet security concepts, students are asked to analyze the case and make recommendations for ways forward.

## Major projects

There are three cases on this theme. The ® rst two deal with the rationale for implementation of an innovation that has gained a very high pro® le in the late 1990s, namely enterprise resource planning systems. Sabine Hirt and E. Burton Swanson look at the adoption of SAP in Siemens Corporation, while Jeane Ross has written a case in two parts on the history of SAP, reengineering and the IT function from 1994 onwards at Dow Corning. Both cases reveal the complex factors that make up major projects based around systems that go to the heart of business functioning. In the Siemens case there is a particular focus on the relationship between restructuring the business processes and the implementation approach, choice of packaged software and hardware, and the use and value of consultants. Dow Corning faced a legal crisis while addressing global competition. In this dif® cult business context the ® rst part of the case poses issues of how the IT function should be organized and managed. The second part then reports on SAP implementation and Dow Corning’s plans to reengineer global processes, with speci® c reference to the IT unit’s role in these. Both cases give students considerable insight into the complex processes of adopting ERP, and offer ample scope for students to speculate on likely outcomes and make informed recommendations.

The third case, by Charalambos Iacovou looks at a failed systems development project at a US-based hospital. Much can be learned from examining MIS failures and this case is certainly no exception. The author provides a detailed description of events, and suf® cient richness to invite considerable discussion on the relative importance of factors and how factors combined to produce the failure. The student is invited to consider how the project failure might have been avoided, and the lessons that emerge for the hospital so that it might avoid similar incidents in the future.

## The changing IT function

A perennial issue in IS has been the extent to which an IT function serves the needs of the larger organization. The Nortel and Riverbank Financial cases in the present volume bring a late 1990s focus to the debate about the ways in which, and the extent to which, IT functions need to change to address twenty ® rst century business environments. In \`Nortel ± Reinventing IS’, Olga Volkoff and Peter Newson plot the evolution of the company from a telecommunications products provider to one that provides more customized integrated offerings. The previously decentralized IT function takes on elements of centralization and is redesigned to focus on three key processes, but fully de® ning these and implementing the proposed changes represent signi® cant challenges for Nortel and its IT personnel. Students here can investigate whether these changes are the most appropriate, whether there are more viable alternatives, and how to respond successfully to the implementation challenges.

In Riverbank Financial, Kathryn Brohman and Duncan Copeland also deal with an IT group in transition. They describe the IT group as moving towards IT-business integration, increased IT employee satisfaction and decreased turnaround in systems development. However, the case poses the question whether as a result the IT group will ® nd itself unable to maintain leadership in technological expertise? If so, will this signi® cantly impact on business performance? The case provides a platform for investigation of these issues, and for consideration of ways forward.

## Electronic trading

It did not surprise us to ® nd a number of cases submitted to ICIS dealing with electronic trading. In the end two cases emerged from the refereeing process, one dealing with business-to-consumer, the other with business-to-business electronic commerce.

In \`Buy The Book’, Claudia Loebbecke, Philip Powell and Carl Gallagher deal with the launching of electronic commercial services at Co-op Bookshop, Australia’s largest academic bookseller. The case is highly useful for exploring the potential of the Internet for transforming businesses, investigating as it does not just selling books but also the possibilities of electronic publishing. Internet technologies are presented as business enablers and this opens up discussion on where the business should focus. The case also allows discussion on web site design and the directions in which web sites can evolve.

Jan Damsgaard’s case on Global Logistic System Asia Co. Ltd. looks at the electronic trading network established in the mid-1990s by four international airlines for facilitating their south east Asia air cargo distribution. The case gives a detailed account of the rationale for and development of the network, and opens discussion on the reasons for its seeming success, and how the network may be further extended. In particular the case allows students to locate the past and future development of the network in the context of its stakeholders and the competitive forces in the industry sector.

These, then, are the nine cases. The authors and we believe this has been a very worthwhile initiative and are pleased to be making available this Special Issue to our academic teaching colleagues. Feedback on the usefulness of the cases will be much appreciated and should be addressed to the relevant authors or to any Editor of JIT.
