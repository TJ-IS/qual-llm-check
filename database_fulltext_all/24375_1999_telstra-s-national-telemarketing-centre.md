# Telstra’s National Telemarketing Centre

PAUL RICHARDSON and PETER WEILL

Melbourne Business School, 200 Leicester Street, Carlton, Victoria 3053, Australia

Telstra ± until recently Australia’ s only telecommunications carrier ± is internationally recognized within the industry for providing a world-class telecommunications service to a highly dispersed and isolated population under challenging environmental conditions. In 1990, the Australian Government announced that it would end Telstra’s monopoly and move towards a deregulated telecommunications industry. The ® rst step was a competitive duopoly and, in 1991± 1992, a private carrier ± Optus Communications ± became Telstra’s ® rst competitor in the Australian market-place. On 1 July 1997 the industry was fully deregulated. Over this period, Telstra has undergone enormous changes ± none of which is more dramatic than the shift from an engineering-driven organization, to one with a marketing focus. The shift in emphasis from engineering to marketing is clearly re¯ ected by the establishment of Telstra’s National Telemarketing Centre (NTC). Though originally created in response to deregulation and the threat of its ® rst competitor, the NTC has grown to become a major national marketing channel for Telstra, as well as providing national customer and product support services. This case examines the function of the NTC within Telstra, the role of technology at the NTC and the way call centres affect organizational ¯ exibility and change the relationship between Telstra and its customers.

## Introduction

Robert Holland, national general manager for Telstra’s National Telemarketing Centre (NTC), had just arrived at his of® ce. He retrieved the morning newspaper from his briefcase and started looking through it ± he was keen to get the latest news on the booming Australian call centre business. However, before he could ® nd the information technology (IT) section, the telephone rang. He put it on speaker:

Good morning Robert, it’ s John here, I have a key account manager on the line from our corporate division. One of her major customers is a food producer who has just become the victim of an extortion attempt. You may have seen it on the news last night ± someone claims to have contaminated a batch of their baby food.

Anyway, they want to know if we can design and run a campaign to call retailers and advise them on the product recall process and generally reassure them that the replacement batch is sound. They also want us to accept incoming enquiries from concerned customers on a 1800 freecall number which they will provide to the news media and place in newspaper advertisements and so on. They are prepared to pay a very nice premium if we start immediately and they are ready to e-mail the list of retailers<sup>’</sup> names and numbers so we can get under way with the outbound calls.

Robert groaned and leaned back in his chair. With the names and numbers fed into the NTC’s various systems, they could have such a campaign designed in half an hour and under way as soon as they could get the additional staff in ± probably the next day. He hated to miss an opportunity like this. It was not just the missed revenue. When customers think about telephone calls they naturally turn to their telecommunications provider for help. This was one of those rare opportunities for Telstra to forge a close relationship with an important customer in a time of need; the cost of turning them away at a time like this was something that he could not place a value on.

Sorry John, at the moment we are running major inbound and outbound campaigns for the Commercial and Consumer [C&C] group. You can check with Mary if you like, but I very much doubt that we will have enough spare capacity to run or organize a campaign of that size. In any case, we have not yet formalized the processes for managing and charging external clients. I am afraid that you will have to refer the client to one of our private industry partners. Oh, by the way, you had better tell the account manager that it does not matter how much they are willing to pay ± if we interrupt the C&C campaign to take an external client we will not be around tomorrow!

This is exactly what John Hollingsworth, the manager of business development at the NTC, had already told the key account manager; she was persistent but he had turned away others before her for the same reasons. Following telecommunications deregulation in July 1997, the NTC was free to compete in the local telemarketing industry, but external work could not displace that of their internal clients and various restrictions (particularly on personnel numbers) prevented them from increasing capacity. (Following concerns expressed by private telemarketing companies, Telstra made an undertaking to Austel ± the government telecommunications regulator ± that it would not compete in the Australian telemarketing industry while the carrier duopoly arrangement existed.)

Furthermore, the NTC was a cost centre in the Commercial & Consumer Business Unit at Telstra ± not a pro® t centre. The NTC had to compete with other marketing channels for their budget allocation from the Commercial & Consumer (C&C) Business Unit. Commercial & Consumer also supply nearly 90% of the NTC’s work, the remainder coming almost entirely from other business units within Telstra on a non-pro® t transfer pricing arrangement.

Holland ¯ icked through his diary and then continued his conversation with Hollingsworth.

Thursday week I have to go cap-in-hand to the accountants and justify our budget for the next ® nancial year, which starts in about 3 months from now. So we need to ® nalize how many people we need, how much money we want and provide them with a business plan describing what we are going to do in return ± volumes, actions, strategies and so on. Getting requests like this from major corporate customers proves to me that we have something valuable to offer at a fair price ± I am sure we could be doing more to help important Telstra customers like them while improving service to our internal clients. As things stand, we are bound to end up negotiating on a budget that is one-third of what is required for the marketing our clients would like us to do. Come around to my of® ce now and we will go over the strategic options for the business case again.

## Telstra Corporation

For many years, the provision of telecommunications services in Australia was the sole domain of three government-owned instrumentalities ± the domestic carrier Telecom, the international carrier Overseas Telecommunications Corporation (OTC) and the national satellite carrier AUSSAT. In 1987, the Commonwealth Government of Australia undertook a fundamental review of the Australian telecommunications industry. This resulted in the announcement of wide-ranging reforms to the basic structural and regulatory framework of the telecommunications sector in November 1990. The reforms focused on the introduction of broad-based competition and a restructuring of the three monopoly carriers. In 1991± 1992, a competitive duopoly was established based on a single government-owned carrier (AOTC ± formed through the merger of Telecom and OTC) and a private sector carrier (Optus Communications ± a private company incorporating AUSSAT). In 1993, the name AOTC was changed to Telstra Corporation Limited. Both companies offered domestic and international services, with Optus initially obtaining a 10% market share. On 1 July 1997 the Australian telecommunications market was opened to full, unrestricted competition and shortly thereafter one-third of Telstra Corporation was \`privatised’ in a public listing in which the Australian public was strongly encouraged to participate.

Telstra provides a full range of telecommunication services and products to domestic and international customers. The corporation is headquartered in Melbourne, Sydney and Brisbane and owns approximately 10 000 sites throughout Australia (including business of® ces, line depots, major telephone exchanges and radio terminal stations (1995 ® gures). It also has business of® ces or joint ventures in 20 countries around the world. The Asia-Paci® c region is a particular area of interest and investment.

Telstra owns approximately 2% of telephone lines worldwide, is the third largest owner of submarine cables in the world and has one of the world’s largest digitized terrestrial networks with over 1 000 000 km of ® bre optic cable in active service. In Australia, Telstra handles more than 10 million local calls per day. The company’s customer base is the largest of any Australian enterprise, with more than 95.5% of Australian households connected to its telephone network.

In 1993± 1994, Telstra faced its ® rst full year of competition. Despite this, revenues increased by 5% on the previous year to A\$13.4 billion. Three years later, at the time of deregulation on 1 July 1997, Telstra’s revenue was A\$16 billion (a 5 year ® nancial summary for Telstra is included in Table 1). Telstra is one of the largest contributors to Australia’s national wealth with a value-added contribution of A\$10.8 billion in the ® nancial year 1996± 1997 representing 2.4% of Australia’s gross domestic product.

## Call centre background

Call centres are not an entirely new idea. It is possible for a business to establish a national (indeed international) presence for handling incoming customer enquiries or undertaking outgoing telemarketing campaigns by ringing customers from a prepared list of telephone numbers ± manually crossing each number off as it is called. As the business demands become more sophisticated, the complexity of the call centre increases with the introduction of real-time access to corporate IT systems, sophisticated computer ± telephone integration and purpose-designed of® ce plans with furniture catering for teams of specially trained call centre staff.

Call centres are a labour-intensive business with staf® ng costs usually accounting for 65% of a call centre’s budget (The Age, 1997). The average Australian call centre has 67 seats (The Australian, 1997d), with the largest approximately 800 seats. Research suggests that mid-sized call centres with 200± 250 seats (requiring approximately 2000± 3000 m<sup>2</sup> of of® ce space) are the most ef® cient (The Australian, 1997a). Call centre productivity is highly sensitive to staffrelated issues such as turnover, absenteeism and motivation and, therefore, effective human resource management is critical to the call centre. In their \`1997 Teleservice Benchmarking Study’ TARP Pty Ltd identi® ed a number of characteristics typical of best-inclass call centres; a summary of their report is included in Appendix A.

The call centre industry in Australia is currently undergoing a period of rapid expansion, as it is in the Asia-Paci® c region in general. One estimate (The Australian, 1997e) has suggested the Asia-Paci® c call centre business will be worth approximately \$US187 billion by 2002 and the Gartner Group (McCormick, 1997) estimated that the global call centre hardware and software market will exceed US\$10 billion by 2001. Of potential regional competitors, Australian operating costs are signi® cantly lower with an annual cost per seat of approximately \$US48 750. This is compared to \$US64 500 for Singapore and \$US65 250 in Hong Kong (The Australian, 1997e). In addition, Australia is perceived as having a mature call centre market with excellent technology infrastructure, skilled and multilingual human resources and cheap of® ce space. Melbourne rents, already the lowest in the Asia-Paci® c region (e.g. Melbourne \$US189 m<sup>-2</sup> compared to Singapore \$US1018 m<sup>-2</sup>; State Government of Victoria, 1997), have been declining over the last few years as a result of an excess in central business district of® ce space.

The Australian call centre industry is growing at 10± 25% per annum (The Australian, 1997b,d) with the number of Australian businesses using call centres growing at 20% per annum (The Australian, 1997f ). Of the 1200± 4000 call centres already in Australia, New South Wales has 60% (The Australian, 1997b) with Victorian, South Australian and Tasmanian Governments keen to increase their state’ s share. (Estimates on the number of call centres vary considerably ± presumably because of differing de® nitions as to what exactly constitutes a call centre.) Regional cities, such as Bendigo in Victoria, are also pro-active in attracting call centre businesses offering substantial incentives in addition to their claims that regional cities have lower staff churn rates, absenteeism and wages than metropolitan areas.

The industry is believed to employ more than 100 000 people in Australia with forecasts suggesting that there could be as many as 80 000 new jobs in the sector over the next 3 years (The Australian Financial Review, 1997). Survey results reported in a recent Victorian Government publication designed to promote Victoria’s call centre strengths have suggested that 66% of those working in the sector are female, 47% are full-time and 45% are 20± 29 years old (State Government of Victoria, 1997).

The growth in call centres re¯ ects an increasing recognition in business that they provide a valuable service to customers while lowering the cost of doing business and increasing organizational ¯ exibility. As many goods are becoming highly commoditized, service and support are increasingly becoming the only differentiators between competing products. For example, the National Australia Bank has two national call centres that handle approximately 50% of all customer enquiries ± 10 years ago 95% of these went through branches (The Australian, 1997c). The call centre enables the bank to provide services to customers from any telephone regardless of call centre staff location.

At present Australia has very little in the way of government regulation of telemarketing. The industry has a code of conduct that, for example, discourages outbound campaigns after 9:00 p.m. The level of regulation is much less than in the USA where, in some states, you are not allowed to outbound at all and most states require third-party veri® cation (where, after a telemarketing sale has been made to a person, another company must telephone that person and basically verify that the transaction details are correct).

## The National Telemarketing Centre (NTC)

Telstra established the NTC as part of its Commercial & Consumer Business Unit in May 1992 in direct response to deregulation and the arrival of its ® rst competitor ± Optus Telecommunications. The ® rst service offered by Optus was in the lucrative longdistance telephony market (often referred to as STD ± or Subscriber Trunk Dialling) and consumers were to be asked in a \`pre-selection’ process to choose by ballot whether they wanted Telstra or Optus to carry their long-distance calls. To defend its market share, Telstra set out to contact customers in Australia through direct mail, marketing communications and outbound and inbound telemarketing.

## Background

At the time the NTC was established in 1992, Telstra had approximately 150 call centres across the organization which employed over 5000 staff. Telstra’s existing call centres were almost entirely inbound, i.e. accepting calls from customers (in the context of the NTC, customers are the people who the call centre communicates with via telephone on behalf of their client who is the person or oganization who actually commissions the work) for customer service and support functions, such as directory assistance, billing, service activation, fault reporting, etc. The NTC was to be the ® rst call centre at Telstra set up for national marketing purposes, intended primarily for sales with a large component of calls to be outbound.

The establishment of the NTC also re¯ ected an important underlying change in Telstra as it shifted from being an \`engineering-oriented’ organization, with a strong engineering culture and management, to one with more emphasis on the customer. \`It is an interesting story in itself,’ Holland commented, \`the shift in perception at Telstra from one where the main asset was the telephone exchanges and cable in the ground to the marketing view that our main asset is the customer database.’ The centralized customer database is fed with customer contact information from most of Telstra’s 150 call centres (the directory services call centre being a notable exception).

The NTC was established in Burwood, in the eastern suburbs of the Victorian State capital of Melbourne. Melbourne was a convenient location because a large proportion of Telstra’s business, including its national head of® ce, is located in and around the city.

The NTC’ s initial task was to contact customers prior to pre-selection in their region and create a positive experience with Telstra. (Pre-selection occurred at different times for different geographical regions. Customers were required to ® ll in a \`ballot’ where they selected either Telstra or Optus as their long-distance carrier.) In addition to outbound calls to regions about to undergo pre-selection, the NTC also set up a \`free pre-selection hotline’ to answer any questions a customer may have had about choosing a long-distance provider. This also gave a representative the opportunity to optimize the customer’s account and offer other Telstra services and products. Mary Quinn, group manager for sales at the NTC, explained the nature of the campaign.

There was a major outbound campaign to customers who were going through pre-selection. The message was something to the effect of \`Hello. This is Telstra. How are you? We are just ringing you up to see if you are happy with your service . . .’ They were quite taken aback! We also had inbound telephone numbers and situations were created where we wanted customers to ring in and complain, since there was not much opportunity for them to do this previously. There was good customer reaction and we were very successful.

Indeed, many believe that the NTC campaign was a major reason why Optus achieved a lower market share from the pre-selection process than they had expected. On average, 13± 14% of Australian customers selected Optus as their preferred carrier, the remainder either choosing to remain with Telstra or did not return their ballot paper, in which case they remained with Telstra by default.

As the pre-selection process progressed region by region, the NTC quickly grew to have approximately 700± 1000 telemarketing representatives (TMRs) distributed over three sites around Melbourne. Quinn remembers this as an interesting time when all NTC staff were extraordinarily motivated by the excitement, importance and success of what they were doing. However, over the 3 years following its formation, the NTC started to develop a reputation for not working to normal Telstra processes. In particular, the high use of agency staff (none were permanent Telstra staff) had become a major issue with the unions. As a result of industrial action, the NTC was forced to make many of the agency staff permanent employees, the net result being a drop in size to approximately 500 TMRs, all of whom were now permanent Telstra staff.

## Telemarketing

Prior to deregulation of the telecommunications industry in Australia, Telstra rarely undertook marketing in any of its forms. When the NTC was established during deregulation, it became one of several marketing channels available. (Television advertising, direct sales and Telstra retail outlets are among the other marketing channels often used by Telstra.) The client’s marketing goals determined what channel was to be used and which products and what customer segments would be targeted.

\`In general terms,’ says Holland, \`the NTC’ s current goals are customer retention, win-backs, and product sales.’ Once per quarter a governance committee consisting of Holland, internal clients and other stakeholders meet to discuss what client work should be done by the NTC. The capacity of the NTC and industry partner(s) is taken into account and decisions are made as to which client’s work has the highest priority. Usually internal demand for telemarketing outstrips the NTC’ s capacity. The NTC’ s largest client is Commercial & Consumer, followed by Telstra retail shops, business and government and external clients as illustrated in Figure 3.

When a client has decided to use the NTC as their marketing channel, the business development group works through a standard campaign summary (or \`brief’ ) with the client in order to design a campaign that meets their speci® c goals. The brief describes the objectives of the campaign, the training requirements, IT and Information Systems (IS) requirements and schedules for training, operational reviews and so on. The business development group has a lot of experience in running telemarketing campaigns and are often able to add a great deal of value to the client’s campaign at this stage. A \`campaign coordinator’ is assigned to manage all aspects of the campaign for the client, from IT system con® guration to execution and campaign reporting. A campaign team meeting is then held where the brief is presented to all of the people involved.

Currently, the NTC’ s work is split approximately 50/50 annually between inbound and outbound campaigns. Inbound campaigns are usually conducted in conjunction with some other channel, such as television advertising or mail outs, because something is required to stimulate the customer to ring in (usually on a 1800 freecall number). On the other hand, outbound campaigns can be run independently and are usually much easier and faster to deploy. Outbound campaigns are most useful for things that are not very conducive to mail or television advertising, such as winning back customers, whereas inbound campaigns tend to have a much higher sales conversion rates per contact.

It is common for campaigns run by the NTC to involve a mix of inbound and outbound telemarketing. For example, the \`Lines #3’ campaign ran from August to September 1997. A magazine promoting Telstra products and services was mailed to 840 000 of Telstra’s higher spend customers from which the NTC received inbound calls ± but the main component of the campaign was a large number of outbound calls to the highest spend customers that did not respond. The campaign objective was to contact 400 000 customers who had received the magazine and sell network products such as Easycall, MessageBank and Faxstream. The sales conversion target was 20% and the NTC actually achieved a 47% conversion rate for those contacted ± equivalent to 129 248 network product sales.

Not all activities undertaken by the NTC are strictly telemarketing to external customers. For example, during the recent Telstra ¯ oat it came to management’s attention at the last moment that approximately 3000 Telstra employees had applied for shares but never received their share offer. Quinn explained what happened.

Concerned employees were ringing [the of® ce responsible] saying that they had not received their offer. There was a lot of concern about this because of the emphasis placed on employee participation in the ¯ oat, therefore the head of employee relations contacted the NTC at 4:15 p.m. on a Friday afternoon to organize an outbound campaign to contact these employees over the weekend. By 5:00 p.m. the NTC had organized 20 telemarketing representatives for a Saturday campaign and we managed to contact 95% of the list by Monday morning.

At 9:00 a.m. Monday morning Quinn got another call. This time it was a request to set up an inbound line to answer staff questions about the ¯ oat; 2000 calls were expected. Quinn described what happened.

I was on my way to work when they rang on my mobile phone. After they described how they wanted to set up an inbound campaign, I asked when the number was needed ± they said \`Now!’ . We did not have time to put the proper processes in place to get a new number so I looked for one that we were already using that was not getting many calls on it. I got the IT guys and voice people to pull the number out of the area it was going to and redirect it to another team. I got the telephone number to them at 10:00 a.m. and 5 minutes later we had our ® rst call ± we only just had time to set up the process.

A more typical example of the type of campaigns run by the NTC is the \`win-back’, where ex-Telstra customers are telephoned and encouraged to return to Telstra. The NTC has had huge success with win-back campaigns, a good example being when Telstra exited the \`Flybuys’ loyalty scheme on 30 June 1997. (Flybuys is a loyalty scheme originally launched by three of Australia’s largest marketers in 1994; Telstra joined Flybuys in 1995.) As Holland explained,

We recently ran a large-scale outbound campaign where we telephoned all Telstra customers that were in the Flybuys scheme and explained to them why we were exiting. The campaign was expected to be pretty boring ± a few hundred thousand calls going something like \`. . . I am just ringing to say that Telstra is no longer going to be part of Flybuys . . . [and explaining why]’ . So it was not exactly a \`feel-good’ call and the TMRs were a little apprehensive about that. But as it turned out, the campaign was enormously successful in terms of win-backs and even unexpected product sales.

Some other examples of telemarketing campaigns run by the NTC include the following.

(1) The \`Frontrunner’ campaign where high-spend Telstra analogue mobile customers were targeted with a special offer in order to encourage them to move to Telstra’s digital mobile service. This was a very dif® cult campaign because of the technical complexity of explaining to customers why they should be moving to digital.

(2) Pre-emptively telephoning areas where competitors are undertaking direct sales activities or offering services. For example, the NTC telephoned Telstra customers in areas where Optus were known to be laying cable at the time.

(3) The NTC do a large number of customer survey campaigns. In the past managers had no idea how well a new product would be received prior to its launch. Now they can test the potential market prior to launch using the NTC, e.g. by asking \`What do you think of this particular product idea?’

(4) \`Feel-good’ campaigns ± such as ringing customers who have just purchased a Telstra product and checking to make sure everything is okay and that they are happy with the service they have received.

(5) The NTC has had great success targeting particular ethnic populations such as Greeks, Italians, Cantonese and Vietnamese. These communities greatly appreciate being able to converse with someone in their own language.

## Information Technology at the NTC

## ATLAS

At around the same time as the industrial action, the NTC identi® ed the need for improved systems to support the TMRs. The existing \`pencil and paper’- based business process had TMRs manually dialling telephone numbers from paper lists and ® lling in forms during the call that others would later enter into Telstra’s core systems (e.g. to activate the service). Hollingsworth commented:

In the early days, most of the NTC telemarketing activity was selling Flexi-Plan, but the provisioning costs actually outweighed the sales! It was before my time, but apparently there were around 400 back-of-house staff on 24 hour shifts processing orders as opposed to 300± 400 actually making the calls! (Flexi-Plan discounts are a range of tailored savings plans which discount selected call types, for example local, long-distance or international calls.)

It was clear that IT was required to support and streamline this process. An IS plan developed by the NTC identi® ed and charted the business process shown in Figure 5.

Telstra’s existing core IS could not be used within this architecture for several reasons. In particular, ® ve different systems (and screens) would be required in front of each TMR in order to support the full range of marketing campaigns and the response time of these systems was not fast enough to support the 2± 3 min handling time of a typical telemarketing call.

What the NTC needed was a system that quickly extracted only relevant customer information from the multiple Telstra systems and seamlessly blended this into one screen of information for display to the TMR as they made the call. The TMR also needed to be able to mark the service for activation from the same screen.

The result was ATLAS-1, a telemarketing IT support system developed in-house. ATLAS ± together with the automation of outbound dialling and order entry ± was identi® ed as a critical success factor in improving the cost-effectiveness of the NTC. The business drivers for ATLAS and its architecture are described further in Appendix B.

Peter John Hartshorne, NTC systems manager at the time and a key ® gure in the development of ATLAS, described its impact.

ATLAS managed to compress data from multiple core systems down to one or two screens which can be tailored for each campaign so that TMRs have in front of them just the information they need to handle the call. Mostly as a result of this, time per contact decreased from 6 minutes to 4 minutes. Another big advantage is that TMRs need only 10 days training to use ATLAS ± whereas in regional call centres [where they also need access to all of the Telstra core systems] it takes up to 10 weeks to train someone.

## Predictive dialler

The introduction of ATLAS dramatically reduced the time per contact by providing customer information in a timely and succinct manner, but an issue still limiting the ef® ciency of the TMR (and an issue to have far reaching structural implications) was the time spent manually dialling telephone numbers for outbound campaigns. Because of the large number of people who are not at home during the week, the TMRs had to manually dial in the order of 30 numbers to get each contact. On a cost per contact basis this meant that weekday calls were much higher than evening or weekend calls when people were more likely to be home.

Therefore, most of the NTC’s outbound work was conducted in the evenings and on Saturdays. Even with higher labour costs ± rates 1.15 times the regular (weekday) rate for evenings and 1.5 times for Saturday ± the cost per contact was lower for evening and Saturday shifts.

The installation of a \`predictive dialler’ completely changed the situation. A predictive dialler is a computer-based system that can be loaded with a list of telephone numbers which it then proceeds to dial ± ® ltering out no-answers, fax machines, answering machines and busy signals. (Telephone numbers are tried in three different time slots ± morning, afternoon and evening. If there is no answer, the predictive dialler tries the number again in the next time slot and continues to rotate attempts through each slot for a total of nine attempts.) The predictive dialler paces itself to the rate at which TMRs are working through calls. The objective is to transfer ± without delay ± a person who has answered the telephone to a TMR that has just completed their previous call. (Statistically speaking, the larger the number of TMRs working from the dialler the more reliably this hand-over can be achieved. Therefore predictive diallers and the ef® ciency gains they can provide are only suitable for relatively large call centre operations.) The predictive dialler is integrated with ATLAS so that customer information is placed in front of the TMR as the call is transferred.

Installation of the predictive dialler boosted the productivity of the TMRs on outbound campaigns by 300%. More signi® cantly, by completely eliminating unsuccessful call attempts, it was now more economical to do outbound campaigns through the week and reduce the number of people working the penalty shifts. As a result, the entire outbound staff on Saturday penalty shifts could be moved to normal weekday shifts.

However, changing staff rosters is easier said than done and this is particularly true when the change would result in an effective pay reduction. A union requirement that at least 6 months notice be given prior to any changes in the rosters meant that some delicate negotiations and trading took place to enable the shift changes to be coordinated with the installation and commissioning of the predictive dialler.

## IVR

Inbound calls to the NTC are queued and distributed directly to telemarketing representatives by an automatic call distributor (ACD) which is integrated with ATLAS. Hence, the NTC does not use an IVR system to ® lter incoming telemarketing calls as they do for their customer service and product support activities. Holland believes that they could probably make more use of IVR technology in their telemarketing, particularly in large campaigns where a very large number of callers are expected. IVR technology, together with the introduction of Caller ID, would allow products to be activated directly by the customer within the IVR system, e.g. callers may ring the IVR in response to a mail-out advertisement for a product ± the customer only need say \`yes’ to a voice prompt and the IVR could activate the service automatically. (Caller ID is a network service where the caller’s telephone number is sent to the called party. At the time of writing, Caller

## Management relations

Telemarketing is very much a people business. Direct labour accounts for 64% of the NTC’s cost per contact, while IT costs account for 10%. Figure 6 shows a breakdown of the NTC’s cost per contact.

We are still struggling with the rosters, 6 months notice to change a roster is almost impossible to work with. The new enterprise agreement will allow more ¯ exibility in this area. Another restriction we are hoping to address is that, under the current arrangements, no more than 25% of our total workforce can be casual. Under the new workplace agreement there would be no restriction on the type of staff we can have.

Another key area of interest for NTC management is staff performance. We have far too many ® xed costs, especially in labour and property. There is a very clear shift as we try to move from ® xed costs to more variable costs. But at the same time, it’ s not doing us much good moving to variable cost labour if we still have high ® xed property costs and head-count restrictions preventing it from being fully utilized.

Unlike some other call centre organizations, the NTC has stringent occupational health and safety guidelines that translate into high property costs (for example, all NTC TMRs have ergonomic desks and chairs). Quinn remarked about her experience in benchmarking potential outsourcing partners:

We have state of the art properties, furniture, technologies, the best of everything really. You look at us and we have the technology to comfortably power through a few hundred thousand calls in a week if we want to, they simply cannot do it. So if you do not look at these other factors you are not comparing apples with apples.

## Outsourcing

As Telstra settles into a more competitive environment, it is taking a serious look at identifying its core business and those areas that could be outsourced. Telstra recently took the corporate decision to outsource its IT systems (including ATLAS) to a joint venture company in what is the largest outsourcing deal in Australia. Under the arrangement, Telstra will outsource all of its data operations and applications maintenance to IBM Global Services Australia (IGSA). Telstra will have a 26% stake in IGSA with the project management group Lend Lease holding 23% and IBM 51%. The value of the contract has not been disclosed, but The Australian Financial Review estimated it to be around A\$4 billion over 10 years. The call centre outsourcing industry is also booming in Australia as companies contract industry specialists to undertake telemarketing activities and provide corporate help-desk facilities.

In this environment of outsourcing, a major goal of the NTC has been to ensure that it is cost competitive with industry rates. John Hollingsworth, manager for business development, described the situation.

For a number of years I think we did not clearly know what our main value-add was to Telstra and there was a major threat of outsourcing. It is only in the last 6 months that we have taken that threat and turned it into an opportunity. We have now established the NTC as a highly competent campaign organizer and manager of external telemarketing ® rms.

The NTC has forged a close partnership with a number of private telemarketing ® rms to whom they outsource many marketing campaigns. In particular the NTC has identi® ed TeleTech as a strategic partner to whom they currently direct 90% of their outsourced work (refer to press release included in Appendix D). Hollingsworth explained the advantages of this arrangement.

Outsourcing has grown our channel enormously. Our restrictions on campaign size are now more funding related instead of infrastructure or head count related. Now if somebody wants to do a campaign the decision to go ahead is made purely on the overall promotions and advertising budget.

The \`Lines #3’ campaign is a recent example of where the NTC successfully coordinated a complex campaign across several outsourcing partners. \`Lines #3’ was also quite complex because it involved multiple products from multiple internal clients. Hollingsworth re¯ ected on some of the lessons learnt from this campaign.

The sales results were good, though we have some work to do on our reporting. We have problems because we were working over multiple sites using multiple outsourcing partners. This is the ® rst time we had done this so we had some system issues and report consolidation issues that surfaced.

The strategic partners currently operate their own IT support systems, with data batch translated between the Telstra and outsourcer’s systems in much the same way as ATLAS obtains the data from Telstra systems. The NTC is considering providing strategic partners with ATLAS and possibly providing access to other Telstra core systems so that they can provide customer support services in addition to telemarketing.

As John Hollingsworth explained, the most valuable lesson learnt in selecting an outsourcing partner was just how cost competitive the NTC were.

In selecting an outsourcing partner we did a pretty comprehensive review of a number of organizations to ensure that they could meet our standards and requirements for the sort of campaigns that we run. We found that our operating costs were about 14% more than the operating costs of a comparable private industry operation.

Now consider that our internal clients are paying our cost price ± we make no pro® t ± whereas outsourcing partners add about 12% pro® t on to their operating costs. Hence, when we compared their price to our clients against the NTC price to our clients, we were only about 2% worse.

This was very pleasing for our stakeholders and us. It was a major win because there is no doubt that the general perception was that we were 30± 40% behind in the price to our clients. Prior to this exercise there was no way to know for sure. Now that we have an apples for apples comparison we have a reasonable target to aim towards.

## The future

While waiting for Hollingsworth to arrive at his of® ce, Holland searched through the newspaper for the IT section. Sure enough ± surrounded by advertisements from new telecommunications companies offering cutprice, long-distance and overseas telephone calls ± there were several articles on call centres. Most were about various multinationals deciding to set up their Asia-Paci® c call centres in Melbourne and the virtues of Melbourne over Singapore, Hong Kong and other countries in the region. One article in particular caught his attention. In part it read.

. . . Australian call companies are well placed in such areas as systems integration, consulting, software, training, and carrier services.

However, while the technology is an enabler of service delivery, the call centre agents are the delivery mechanism and the greatest asset of the call centre. In this respect Australia’s skilled and multilingual work force is a tremendous competitive advantage. The problem facing Australia is that senior management don’t realise the power they are sitting on. As a result there is a lack of understanding of what call centres can contribute to the organisation and this is re¯ ected in the fact that call-centres are often viewed as a cost centre rather than a potential pro® t centre . . .

Holland put down the paper and thought about what he had just read. On the one hand, there was always the possibility that Telstra would outsource its telemarketing. While on the other hand, another major corporate customer had just approached the NTC looking for some urgent assistance with a telecommunications-related issue, yet he was unable to help them because of restrictions that are beyond his control. Indeed the demand from internal clients alone outstripped the limited resources he had available. Holland wondered; how could the NTC be positioned to best meet the corporation’s goals?

## Appendix A: Excerpts from the TARP Pty Ltd. \`1997 Teleservice Benchmarking Study’

## Study background

Four studies were conducted by TARP between October 1996 and May 1997.

(1) Customer care call centres.

(2) Telemarketing call centres.

(3) Credit and collections call centres.

(4) IVR systems.

Over 500 companies from more than 30 countries participated in the benchmarking study, including respondents from Australia, New Zealand, North America and the UK.

TARP identi® ed a group of call centres whose performance is superior to all others, designating them as best-in-class (BIC) and comparing their performance with all other participants who are identi® ed as non-best-in-class (non-BIC).

## Telemarketing call centres

## Key ® ndings

(1) BIC call centres demonstrate a more resultsfocused management style, e.g. an emphasis on hiring rather than initial training, specialized staff and results-focused performance measurement.

(2) BIC call centres optimize the use of technology, e.g. contact management software that captures both qualitative and quantitative information.

(3) BIC call centres measure and plan for higher ef® ciency, e.g. forecasting call volumes and staf® ng levels and have greater outbound activity than non-BIC centres.

## Inbound ® ndings

(1) Research showed that inbound BIC centres stay open longer.

(2) Inbound BIC centres have a stronger tendency to use the services of third-party bureaux.

(3) the majority of BIC centres had been operating for between 3 and 5 years.

(4) The majority of inbound BIC centres indicated that their staff were capable of handling calls for all products and services offered by the company.

(5) Inbound BIC centres prefer no to \`staff up’ to handle large call volumes, instead preferring to use the services of third parties to handle extra call volumes.

(6) One hundred per cent of BIC centres test for the voice capabilities of new staff, compared with signi® cantly smaller numbers in non-BIC centres.

(7) Inbound BIC centres have on average nine TMRs reporting to each team leader.

(8) Half of the inbound BIC centres reported their average representative tenure at between 2 and 5 years. This was almost double the equivalent ® gure for non-BIC centres.

## Outbound ® ndings

(1) Outbound BIC centres are twice as likely to undertake telemarketing to \`non-customers’.

(2) Outbound BIC centres start their calling later in the day than non-BIC centres during the week and on Sundays and close their calling later in the day during the week and on Saturdays.

(3) Outbound BIC centres generally prefer to manage their outbound telemarketing themselves rather than use third parties.

(4) All outbound BIC centres test potential staff for selling skills and a high proportion also test their voice.

(5) Outbound BIC centres have an average of ten representatives per team leader.

(6) The majority of outbound BIC centres reported that their average representative tenure was between 6 and 12 months, with the balance being between 1 and 2 years.

(7) Outbound BIC centres have a more rapid staff turnover than non-BIC centres, with a mind to keeping their representatives fresh and motivated to sell the products and services of the company.

(8) All outbound BIC centres cross- or up-sell during the call.

(9) Outbound BIC centres on average spend 33 more s on talk time than non-BIC centres, but only a total of 59 s on after call work time, compared with 215 s in non-BIC centres.

## Customer care call centres

## Key ® ndings

(1) BIC call centres are more likely than non-BIC centres to optimize the use of technology and use advanced customer interaction technology such as IVR systems and voice data integration.

(2) BIC call centres are more likely than non-BIC centres to log and analyse customer contacts. BIC centres also have more complex tracking systems to classify call types. This information is more likely to be used to improve processes and increase customer satisfaction.

(3) BIC call centres measure both representative and management performance with a wider assortment of measures than are used by non-BIC centres. The most frequently used measures are the number of calls handled, attendance and talk time.

(4) BIC call centres operate at a higher cost per contact than non-BIC centres, but this is regarded as the premium paid for delivering higher standards of customer service. Key expenditure areas for BIC call centres are salaries, telecommunications and bene® ts.

## Other ® ndings

(1) Call answering performance is better for BIC centres than for non-BIC centres, particularly in relation to average speed of answer. In addition, after call work time is signi® cantly less than for non-BIC centres, with New Zealand currently having the highest after call work time.

(2) The management of call volumes is achieved in BIC centres by reducing upstream processing errors and improving advertising communication with the customer. BIC companies are more inclined to analyse the various means available to reduce unnecessary calls.

(3) BIC call centres are more likely to forecast call volumes and call duration to within 5% of their actual call statistics. New Zealand centres were least likely to achieve this level of accuracy overall.

(4) The future will see BIC centres concentrating on optimizing technology as a major priority.

(5) Australasian call centres are signi® cantly less likely to measure customer satisfaction and employee satisfaction than other call centres.

(6) Satisfaction with contact experience is signi® - cantly higher amongst BIC call centres. In addition, they are more likely to exceed customer expectations of the contact experience.

(7) BIC centres have a higher percentage of fulltime employees than do non-BIC centres.

(8) Only 15% of BIC call centres outsourced any of their customer care calls and, of those who do, few outsource more than 25% of their call volume.

## IVR systems

Customer satisfaction is impacted most by the following.

(1) Customer education, e.g. awareness programmes.

(2) Number of ® rst menu options, e.g. defaults and user error increases with a higher number of menu options.

(3) Involvement of human factor experts.

(4) Systems testing.

## Comparison between Australia and North America

(1) North American companies receive on average up to 1 million calls into the IVR system each month, whereas Australian companies receive approximately 273 000.

(2) Australian respondents identi® ed the top four bene® ts resulting from IVR implementation as the ability to handle a higher call volume, a lower cost per call, broader hours of coverage and increased customer satisfaction.

(3) North American companies are inclined to use IVR for more complex applications than is the case in Australasia.

(4) North American companies have a higher proportion of calls successfully completed within the IVR system, compared with Australian users of IVR systems, who ® nd that a higher number of customers opt out of the system before completing the call.

(5) Australasian companies are less likely to test the application before going \`live’ .

(6) Both North American and Australasian companies tend to begin the call response with a greeting and are more likely to use a female rather than a male voice.

(7) Australasian companies have reported a higher increase in call volumes than in North America. However, North American companies reported greater reductions in Customer Service Representative (CSR) handling times.

(8) The average cost per minute of calls handled by North American respondents was lower than in Australasia, regardless of whether the call was handled by a CSR or by an IVR.

(9) North American IVR systems are more likely than those in Australasia to offer an opt-out option and dial-ahead/dial-through options.
