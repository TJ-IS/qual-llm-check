Journal of Information Technology (1999) 14, 277± 286

Nortel± reinventing IS

OLGA VOLKOFF and E.F. PETER NEWSON

Richard Ivey School of Business, The University of Western Ontario, London, Ontario, Canada, N6A 3K7

With changes in both technology and the environment, Nortel has had to evolve from a company that sells a collection of telecommunication products to a company that sells integrated packages of products which satisfy speci® c customer needs. In the past Information Systems (IS) was spread across the various product divisions, supporting a highly decentralized corporate structure. Now it has been charged with transforming itself to facilitate the company’ s need for greater integration, including a move to standardized systems. IS must rede® ne its role and restructure itself to ful® l its new mandate. After an extensive analysis and design exercise, the IS function has been revisualized as centring on three key processes: client management, solution delivery and business support. This represents a signi® cant change from a traditional IS shop that focuses on building applications and infrastructure. While there is broad support for the changes in principle, actually getting the new processes fully articulated and implemented presents a signi® cant challenge.

## Introduction

Nortel, a major player in the global telecommunication industry, was changing the way it did business and the Information Systems (IS) organization was faced with the challenge of transforming itself to accommodate the new direction. IS personnel had traditionally been spread across the various product divisions, supporting a highly decentralized corporate structure. As the need for both system standardization and a corporate-wide perspective grew, responsibility for IS was restructured, but client satisfaction fell. Nortel’s new business model demanded that IS simultaneously operate with a broad corporate perspective and respond to speci® c client needs. In 1995 a programme to \`reinvent IS’ was initiated. By 1996 a new structure for Nortel’s IS function had been proposed. The problem confronting Brad Taylor, the manager responsible for transforming IS, was ® rst to make sure the plan was viable and, second, to decide how to implement it.

While the 1990s were years of dramatic change for all corporations, the telecommunications sector was at the centre of the storm. Companies such as Nortel (Northern Telecom Limited) had to respond to the internal challenges created by the evolution and convergence of the telecommunication and computer technologies which were at the heart of their business. Nortel recognized that it could no longer operate as the supplier of a collection of technical products. Rather it had to reorient itself as the \`provider of solutions’ to customers who might require an integrated package of various products and who might need considerable assistance in designing that package.

Senior management recognized that the new dynamics would require a signi® cant remodelling of the company and its way of doing business.

In response, the company had refocused its strategy in a way that assumed more integration across business units. Clearly redesign of processes, systems and technology would be required in the corporate remodelling. For that reason, a major component of this integration was to be the responsibility of the IS group which was expected not only to realign itself to this revised structure, but actually to play a leadership role in the re-engineering of corporate processes. As the precise role for IS was not at all clear, the IS group certainly faced a daunting task as it prepared to play its part in an unfamiliar game. \`In the past we have played a traditional support role, more tactical than strategic, building applications and maintaining standards ± and our performance in those tasks was not always highly regarded,’ said Brad Taylor, director of IS. \`Not only do we have to signi® cantly improve our performance in our traditional role ± that is the minimum, but it is just the start. We must also rise to the challenge of providing leadership to all the other groups as they re-engineer the way they do business. The company’s new strategy means that our own structures and processes have to change at the same time.’

## The company and its changing environment

The Northern Electric and Manufacturing Company Limited was incorporated in 1895 to manufacture primitive telephones, wind-up gramophones and street call boxes. As an af® liate of AT&T for 43 years, it enjoyed access to technology and research which kept it at the forefront of technological innovation in its industry. It became a major supplier of electromechanical switches in the 1950s and 1960s and after the imposed severing of its link to AT&T in 1956, moved to become a leading innovator of new switching devices. While it had served only one customer, Bell Canada, for most of its history, during the 1960s the company began expanding into new markets beyond the borders of Canada. In the 1970s, under its new name Northern Telecom Limited, it led the way in digital technology, becoming the ® rst to produce a full line of digital communications equipment. In 1995, renamed Nortel, it turned its focus to the global market for public and private electronic networks.

Changing technology had altered the market. Nortel’s customers were no longer shopping for individual products ± a switching system or wireless transmission equipment. Instead the demand was for an integrated set of products that could address speci® c business needs. To be successful in such a market meant that the company had to present a single, uni® ed face that could offer customized, integrated solutions to the customers’ needs, drawing components of those solutions from many different areas of the business or even beyond it. Clearly this represented a signi® cant challenge to a company that traditionally had a decentralized, product-focused structure with all of its implicit fragmentation.

Being good at integrating different product technologies to meet customer requirements was not going to be enough. The problem was to deploy a set of common organizational processes, IS and technical infrastructure that could support the level of product integration required. Dif® cult enough for a small, domestic entity, the task of establishing common processes was compounded by the competitive demand for speed, the complexity of globalization and the lack of an integrated business structure. The challenge was to organize in such a way that separate business units could operate as one in providing integrated solutions and a single customer interface, without turning the company into one large ineffective monolith.

## Nortel in the 1990s

Between 1992 and 1996, Northern Telecom Limited rede® ned its corporate pro® le. The old model is re¯ ected in the 1993 annual report where the company described itself as \`the leading global supplier of fully digital telecommunications switching systems, serving more than 90 countries.’ At that time it employed approximately 60 000 people worldwide and operated 48 manufacturing plants. Among its major products were central of® ce switching systems, private branch exchanges and key systems, multimedia communications solutions, including asynchronous transfer mode (ATM)-based products, digital, high-capacity cellular mobile telecommunications switches and radios, wireless business communications systems and a complete range of optical ® bre transmission and cable products. Consolidated revenues in 1993 were US\$8.15 billion. Falling gross margins on most product lines resulted in a reduced gross pro® t of \$2.95 billion, down from \$3.51 billion in 1992 and the company reported a loss for the year. (See Table 1 for a summary of ® nancial information.) The need for a new strategy was clear.

The company, up to this time, had been segmented into a number of product organizations that supplied separate market organizations. Thus, the managers with product responsibilities had no direct line of sight to the customer. In mid-1993, in response to the changing market and falling margins, the company was reorganized into four network-focused organizations for the four major types of business: public carrier networks (PCN), wireless networks (WLN), broadband networks (BBN) and enterprise networks (EN). Each of the four main business units operated autonomously, was measured on its individual performance and anticipated little interaction with the other units. This structure supported (and was supported by) the Northern Telecom culture which was entrepreneurial and highly individualistic.

The change was part of the company’s effort, as it entered its second century and tried to recover its profitability, to move away from a \`product-box’ mentality. Nortel wanted to reposition itself as \`the preferred global resource for designing, building and integrating a world of dynamically evolving information, entertainment and communication networks’. The old vision ± \`to be the leading supplier of telecommunications equipment by the year 2000’ ± was replaced in 1994 with a new vision ± \`A world of networks’, the company’s vision for the future of telecom in a deregulated world of competition.

In the new \`network business’ structure, each of the business units had total responsibility for the design, manufacture and marketing of a complete suite of products into the customer base. The premise of this plan was segmentation of the customer base. The PCN group, for example, was responsible for 90% of the Nortel products purchased by Bell Canada. The expectation was that there would be little need for crossrelationships and that each business could be relatively autonomous. Thus, there could be focus and accountability. Across the four businesses, 35 general managers were established that would own and be accountable for a subset of the portfolio. While this created a clear focus on developing the various segments in the context of customer needs (rather than developing products on the basis of technical feasibility) and helped to build strength in the portfolio mix, it did little to promote integration across business units.

As the world of networks concept evolved, there was a heightened realization that even the network business focus was too fragmented to provide what customers wanted. To develop the total network opportunities required working with the entire product portfolio. A customer wishing to purchase a PBX (Private Branch Exchange) from the EN business, for example, might want a wireless component. With the four-silo set-up, this could imply two different orders, different delivery dates and separate invoices ± or worse. Large orders had to be segmented and entered into several different order entry and tracking systems. The solution in the short term was to throw human resources at the problem to shelter the customer from the fragmentation. The sales group had to spend time trying to make the Nortel system work rather than spending time with the customers. Unfortunately, while the necessity for building some common processes across Nortel was broadly understood, the silo structure gave little motivation to drive the signi® cant changes that would be required in processes and their supporting systems and technology. Taylor illustrated the problem with an example from IS.

IS developed core systems ± they represented about 20% of our investment. The trouble is, when we sent them out into the divisions, they were \`customized’. They might all bear the same name, but that is the only similarity left. That being said, we cannot just blow up the silos and make it one organization. The plain truth is that a corporation of this size needs to divisionalize. The problem is how to get the integration we require across network businesses without losing both the line of sight to the customer and clear accountability.

The typical response of a corporation to such a problem is to charge the IS organization with the task of driving the development of common systems because, as a group, they have visibility across the divisional structure. The underlying assumption is that each division will buy into the need for a common approach. The reality is that at an operational level you have similar processes de® ned in different ways and very different mechanisms for supporting those processes. When you compound this with the fact that each division has a different means of rewarding and motivating its employees, there is no way that an IS organization can ever carry out the task of integration. While there is a signi® cant role that IS can and needs to play in corporate transformation, the change must be led by the business. We cannot push solutions on to the clients ± they need to provide a pull.

That being said, much of the answer does lie in IS and the processes they support, so IS does have a signi® cant part to play. Our role, however, cannot be to own the change, but to motivate and provide leadership. With the challenges Nortel faces, we believe the answer to the integration issue lies in establishing common processes without breaking up business units. In the words of Paul Allaire, chairman and CEO [chief executive of® cer] of Xerox, \`What is different now is the enabling technology. . . . With that ability, you can enjoy the ef® ciency of a process orientation without losing responsiveness of divisional orientation.’ In other words you can have your cake and eat it too ± the challenge is getting there, particularly given the current structure, culture and reward system.

In response to these challenges, Nortel further evolved its business model during late 1995 and early 1996 (see Figure 1). While product and customer accountability was still intact at the network business level, the company had created a process owner role to lead process change and a number of other governance mechanisms to help the process owners succeed. The role of the process owner was to work with the operational teams within the network businesses to de® ne and drive the implementation of common processes and supporting systems. As indicated in Figure 1, the processes selected for attention included market order management, order delivery, global logistics, integrated product introduction, sales and marketing, and a number of corporate services. IS was expected to be a lead player as the company worked to implement this new business model.

## The changing role of IS

In the fullness of time, IS would have to create a system and technology infrastructure to support common processes. However, before that point could be reached, the IS group had a number of other tasks to perform. Not only was it responsible for helping its corporate clients de® ne and re-engineer key business processes, but it also had to de® ne and re-engineer its own structure and processes, which were poorly positioned to meet the new challenges. Through 1995 and 1996 IS played a signi® cant role in helping the company evolve to the new governance model, ensuring adequate business ownership of the processes. At the same time it addressed the need to improve its own capabilities, including rede® ning its relationship with its corporate clients. According to Brad Taylor, \`the critical ® rst step was not to start developing the set of information access tools and systems to help enable Nortel’ s strategy ± that would come in time ± but to get IS engaged with the business in such a way that the business leaders can make decisions and understand the risk at each step as we move towards the rich infrastructure of process, information, and systems that the corporation desires’.

In 1996 Nortel IS employed 1700 people in 40 locations (see Figure 2). Prior to 1990 and consistent with IS’s perceived role as a support function, a small corporate IS staff reported to the executive vice-president, along with corporate ® nance and human resources. In order to maintain its alignment to company structure, the rest of IS had a primarily divisional/locational focus and was highly decentralized, with small groups belonging to each division. This certainly helped to institutionalize the fragmentation noted earlier and resulted in incompatible systems, with similar information de® ned differently in different places. A fragmented systems environment was costly to support, could not be changed quickly and inhibited process integration. The decentralization was also the reason why core systems were so often modi® ed, further increasing support and infrastructure cost. A recent inventory identi® ed 1700 unique systems or instances of systems, including over 100 systems related to order management.

By 1990, the various IS groups had been brought under a single chief information of® cer (CIO), but while organized centrally at the executive level, they were still structured along line of business/location boundaries. The leaders of the IS teams dedicated to each business unit reported both to the CIO and the business unit head. Each team established its own development environment, with no corporate standards for areas such as systems architecture or languages used for developing systems. As a result, very little occurred to rationalize the systems environment. In addition, the team leaders found it dif® cult to be responsible for the con¯ icting objectives inherent in simultaneously trying to satisfy business clients, manage the development environment and support systems after delivery.

With activities still effectively decentralized, the most signi® cant change made with this 1990s reorganization was to centralize funding of IS. The divisions no longer paid for systems directly, but rather through a corporate allocation over which they had no control. While previously they had been motivated to reconcile cost and value, they were now anxious to ensure that they got their fair share of IS resources. The result was an inordinate level of demand, which rapidly exceeded supply. This made the situation worse in two ways. First, it negatively affected the relationship between IS and its clients, since IS could not satisfy all requests. Furthermore, when legitimate demand could not be met, the only recourse a business unit had was to create its own IS organization. This led to islands of IS with no central oversight whatsoever, which further compounded the problem of integration. With the growing realization of the need for common processes and enabling support technology and the recognition that IS had to be more than just a support function, the reporting structure was revised. Financial responsibility for IS moved to the client groups in January 1995, making them accountable for the cost/value. In addition, the CIO started to report to both the executive vice-president and to John Roth, COO (Chief Operating Of® cer), to ensure tighter business engagement. Even so, the structure did little to encourage efforts to leverage information technology (IT) across business units.

Given this history, the dif® culty in establishing appropriate IS± client relationships was not surprising. Brad Taylor described the dilemma.

Over our history, we have suffered from the same false perceptions faced by most IS organizations. While as individuals we are known to be dedicated and possess excellent technical skills, as a group we are thought of as a support function only and one which has great dif® culty delivering what is wanted on time and on budget. We are seen as being more interested in technology than in the business, focusing on our own or on a corporate agenda rather than on what our in-house clients regard as priorities. As a result, we have dif® culty forming relationships with clients to ensure they are active in the planning. In fact, to them we have been largely invisible, except when we ask for an increase in budget. The communication gap works both ways. From our perspective, clients making a request usually end up delegating the design work to the IS function. This is a recipe for disaster. Inevitably, what is delivered is not what was wanted and IS gets hammered by the client. In fact these systems should never be seen as belonging to IS ± clients need to be more engaged. That being said, in those rare examples where the client is fully engaged and partners with IS, the results are outstanding.

Given this uncomplimentary reputation and a structure that, though theoretically centralized, was effectively still aligned primarily with the business unit silos, it was perhaps illogical to have IS play a lead role in the evolution of common processes and supporting systems. However, it was recognized that IS had the technical knowledge of what would be required, a corporate-wide presence and a commitment to common processes and supporting systems. To lead this initiative, a new head of IS, Keith Powell, was appointed in April 1995 and given the newly created title of CIO and vice president of process engineering. In Brad Taylor’s opinion,

What Keith brought to the job was not a superior technical knowledge ± he brought the things that really matter. Speci® cally he has an outstanding reputation across Nortel, he has good relations with and is respected by the working level executives and he understands Nortel’ s business.

Powell’s primary task was to help Nortel move to common processes, but in a corporate context where this was unlikely to happen naturally. While, as pointed out by Taylor, putting a CIO in this position was common and explained their short job tenure, there was a major difference in this case.

Keith could see that the IS group would be unable to move the corporation to common processes by itself. He wisely undertook to ensure that John Roth, the COO, understood that the evolution of these new processes would have to be motivated by the business model and the change would have to be \`owned’ by the line managers. The role for IS is not to own common processes, but, with their perspective of the whole corporation and understanding of process and technology, to help the business leaders make the right decisions. IS can be positioned to put the right environmental attributes in place.

## In Powell’s words,

We cannot be the \`hard lever’ forcing our businesses to use common solutions, while at the same time trying to build client relationships and being dedicated to client success. The resolution to this is that the primary push for common solutions must come from core process sponsors in the business model and not from IS.

While the drive towards common processes was the overriding task, there was at the same time a pressing need for IS to improve itself in several dimensions, both to increase its credibility and to develop its process leadership capability. If IS had plans to play the role of process expert, it should be able to apply a process view to itself and, if IS planned to lead the re-engineering transformation, it had better have its own house in order. \`In a sense,’ said Taylor, \`IS is a microcosm of Nortel’ s silo structure and individualistic culture. Our own fragmented processes impede our ability to work together and ultimately impedes our ability to help Nortel achieve the integration they require.’ In other words, the ® rst order of business was to \`reinvent IS’. As articulated by Taylor, the new vision called for IS to be a professional services organization that was able to bring high realized value to its business partners, help them receive maximum returns on systems investments and ensure that delivery capability inside was best in class and competitive to outside benchmarks.

## Reinventing IS ± the plan

One of the challenges Taylor and Powell faced as they set out to transform IS was to overcome the perception that this was just another organizational restructuring in response to yet one more business fad and that, in the long run, all would return to business as usual. On the contrary, the fundamental misalignment between IS’s vision of a core systems infrastructure and its silo-based structure required signi® cant systemic changes. The ® rst step was to rede® ne the core processes of the IS function. Reengineering the core processes could not, however, be accomplished without paying attention to the people involved, their skills and experience and the existing organizational infrastructure.

To maximize support for the initiative, an extensive and broadly inclusive programme was structured which ensured the participation of all critical stakeholders. Eight design teams were formed, each led by a key director-level individual. Team members were assigned on a full-time basis. The eight team leaders formed a \`super-team’ to ensure coordination and integration across teams. In all, approximately 60 individuals participated, clients were involved, outside benchmarking was performed and industry best practices were reviewed.

The underlying design principle was a separation of the different roles ± satisfying business clients, managing the development environment and supporting delivered systems ± that had formerly all been the responsibility of each IS team leader. These leaders found it dif® cult to manage the contradictory demands they faced. For example, the operational demands of meeting system delivery targets often drew attention away from the consultative role of client management, such as seeking out opportunities to leverage IT in support of business needs. By the same token, client pressure to deliver products quickly often resulted in operational compromises being made. In particular, development was often started before requirements were fully speci® ed and products were delivered with minimal testing and documentation. Even the line marking the end of projects and the start of maintenance activities was blurred.

The result of the redesign programme was a new model for the operation of IS, centred on three key processes: client management, solution delivery and business support.

The client management process model focused on four key design elements.

(1) Dedication to client success.

(2) Intimate knowledge of the client’s business, including the client’s terms and language.

(3) Expert skills in building relationships.

(4) Expert knowledge of how process and technology solutions can be leveraged to achieve signi® cant business results.

The client management role was designed to parallel that of a client manager in a consulting ® rm. Critical measures for evaluating performance were client satisfaction and perceived client value. Client management was separated from the delivery aspects of the model to ensure objectivity and to drive the delivery part of the model to be best in class.

Approximately 120 client managers were needed to match up with the executive structure of each business unit.

The solution delivery process model focused on the construction and modi® cation of systems to meet business requirements. The key design elements included the following.

(1) Having repeatable processes.

(2) Exploiting opportunities to reuse whole or parts of applications.

(3) Creating and enforcing a rich and standard development environment of tools, techniques and technologies.

(4) Moving towards a more ¯ exible workforce where people can be assigned to projects on a need and career interest basis.

Uncoupling client management from development was intended to ensure clients and client managers provided adequate de® nition before a project was accepted. The plan was to follow a software factory model, moving away from building everything from scratch. Pooling development resources also created the ability to build critical resources and skills and to manage the systems architecture from a corporate perspective. The measures for evaluating the performance of this team were overall client satisfaction, delivery satisfaction, time to market and competitive benchmarking. Over time the solution delivery group would have to become the free market supplier of choice or the client management team would source solutions elsewhere.

The business support process model was concerned with looking after client needs with respect to delivered products. The key design elements included the following.

(1) Clear service level agreement with each client summarizing the environment being supported and the service expectation.

(2) One common and disciplined use of a call management process and escalation process.

(3) Root cause analysis and opportunity identi® cation.

One advantage of separating support and development was that project completion would be easier to identify. In the past a stream of ongoing enhancements regularly extended a project’s life, contributing to the perception of cost and time overruns. In addition, under the new structure development teams would be expected to provide complete documentation before turning a system over to business support. The critical performance measures for this service group included client satisfaction, mean time to failure, mean time to repair, call volume and cost.

These three main components of the model were also to be supported by enabling components such as ® nancial management, resource management, vendor management and infostructure/business planning (see Figures 3± 5).

## Reinventing IS ± the reality of implementation

As Brad Taylor looked over the plans and thought about the events of the past few months, he realized that, as dif® cult as the design phase had been, the hardest part was just beginning.

The design work certainly had its challenges: communication, integration across different design teams, ensuring clarity of principles of the new model, building buy-in for the changes throughout the organization. But these challenges start to pale as you consider implementation. At this point we have a pretty solid medium-level design. We have been working at this for about 6 months and now we are anxious to get moving.

In a traditional approach, the next step would be to form an implementation team structure to drive the changes in a systematic manner. However Brad and his colleagues felt a degree of uneasiness. The dif® - culty in following this route was in trying to obtain adequate ownership of the components of implementation. In fact, he found that, while people sitting on the old structure paid enthusiastic lip service, few actually took ownership of the necessary changes and he was afraid the foot dragging would doom the project.

We were still in our silo structure, each group continuing to service the client need. We did not have a good history of working well together. The new design was pretty easy for the senior leadership to agree to as there was not much to be threatened by and they did not have to change anything in their own turf. At the same time, the affected managers had created the environment we were moving out of and could easily become a barrier. We had an issue from the outset that, although many believed change was necessary, Nortel was not facing an immediate crisis, leaving others to believe the status quo could be maintained. Another concern was that, if the implementation teams and their leadership were not going to have to live with and be accountable for the results in the long run, that they might
