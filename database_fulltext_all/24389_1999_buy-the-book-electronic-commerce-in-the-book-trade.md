# Buy the book: electronic commerce in the book trade

CLAUDIA LOEBBECKE

KRAK Chair of Electronic Commerce, Copenhagen Business School, Howitzvej 60,

University Co-operative Bookshop Ltd, 80 Bay Street Broadway, NSW 2007, Australia

Exploitation of the World Wide Web (WWW) is a pipedream for many businesses, as they do not or cannot analyse their motives for having a web site. Many do not understand that there is more to a successful web site than having a corporate logo on a home page. They do not foresee the effort that goes into maintaining a web site, the increased competition from exposure to a \`global market’ via the Internet and the impact a web site will have on the existing business. This case study allows analysis of the opportunities and risks of launching electronic commerce (EC) services in the case of the Co-op Bookshop, Australia’ s largest academic bookseller. The case describes Co-op's difficult progression to a profitable WWW presence and investigates potential developments due to growing competition from \`global players’ and increasing amalgamation between bookselling over the WWW and electronic publishing. The case outlines the four possibilities by which a ® rm can pro® t from its Internet activities and transfers these general bene® ts to Internet service provision by bookstores. In particular, it directs attention to thinking of the core goals of the business, how a WWW presence helps to meet these goals and whether the introduction of a web site changes the direction of the business. This leads to a consideration of the nature of the web site. The case study allows exploration of the current customers and markets and why the ® rm focuses on these. Further, there are the issues of the resources required to set up and maintain a web site, how the site may be integrated into the existing business and its growth path. These issues are explored and modelled in the teaching notes and further background detail is given.

## Bon® re of the vanities: setting the scene

The bookshops on Sydney’s Oxford Street, many open until midnight, are an integral part of the entertainment package. Sales are directly in¯ uenced by \`what is happening on the street at night; if there is a good ® lm on, sales can be really good’. Book browsing has been a favoured form of entertainment for years and this is not about to change.

If browsing real books, in real bookstores, is such a pleasurable activity, why has the Amazon virtual bookstore on the World Wide Web (WWW) attracted such attention? And why is Barnes and Noble, whose Manhattan superstore is the world’s largest real bookshop, spending money hand over ® st to compete with Amazon in cyberspace?

Selling books over the WWW is one of the most competitive areas of electronic commerce (EC). It becomes even more intriguing given the amount of money being spent ± and lost ± in setting up on-line stores.

Exploitation of the WWW is a pipedream for many businesses, as they do not or cannot analyse their motives for having a web site. They do not foresee the effort that goes into maintaining a web site, the increased competition from exposure to a \`global market’ via the Internet and the impact a web site will have on the existing business. This case study analyses the opportunities and risks of launching EC services for the Co-op Bookshop. The case describes Co-op’s dif® cult progression to a pro® table WWW presence and investigates potential developments from growing competition from \`global players’ and increasing amalgamation between bookselling over the WWW and electronic publishing. It outlines Co-op’s experiences from their ® rst tentative steps in 1991, through to their ® rst web site in 1995 and the subsequent two redesigns to a successful WWW presence by 1998.

## Full disclosure: the Co-op case

## Company overview

The Co-op bookshop (www.coop-bookshop.com.au) is the largest academic book retailer in Australia and one of Australia’s top 1000 companies. It is wholly Australian owned and not part of any group. Co-op’s mission is \`to provide information services and resources, supported by quality customer services, so as to provide the greatest possible bene® t to members’.

Co-op opened its ® rst branch at the University of Sydney in 1958. By 1998, it had close to 1 million members served by over 45 branches located in four Australian states, on over 35 university and TAFE (college) campuses. It costs AUS\$15 to become a life member, which gives a 12% discount on all cash and WWW orders. Whilst best known as a supplier of academic books, approximately one-third of the Co-op’s business is general books. In addition, it has a library services division and subscriptions agency that provides services to large corporate and government libraries and organizations.

## The go-between: ® rst steps towards EC

Co-op has had an on-line presence since 1991 with the launch of a telnet service which provided their library account customers with electronic access to its database of ± at that time ± approximately 300 000 titles through a simple, text-based search and ordering facility. This service gave Co-op a distinct advantage and ® rmly positioned it as one of the leading book suppliers in Australia.

## Ordering via the Internet

In the early 1990s, the Internet was still in the realm of universities and governments. Many Co-op customers were academics, universities and federal organizations. Since the Internet grew in Co-op’ s established markets, it provided them with an advantage over the competition. Co-op saw the need for a fast ordering service; the (cannibalization) impact on the telnet service was minimal.

Co-op’s mail order and book research departments were already well established. \`Ordering via the Internet’ required minimal new equipment and no additional staf® ng. As technology improved, Co-op embraced it. New technology meant faster transmission of large amounts of data over the Internet and the WWW began to develop, offering a new medium for advertising and selling goods.

## Database access via the WWW

In late 1995, a decision was made to establish a web site as a user-friendly interface to Co-op’s database.

## Electronic commerce in the book trade

The initial plan was to develop the site so that it could be accessed by existing account and library customers to make ordering easier and to provide mail order customers with a new medium from which to order.

When planning the implementation of the web site, Co-op soon discovered limitations to the sort of site that could be offered. Few people in the organization understood the technology or what it involved. Over the previous 40 years, the company had been run in much the same way, using the same policies and procedures which served it well. The WWW was a challenge: it was the latest technology and Co-op was going to be the pioneer in Australia in electronic ordering.

No standards or comparators were available to base the web site on. The easiest and most logical solution was to develop a WWW-based front end to the existing database, in combination with Co-op’s telnet service, which looked attractive and was easy to use. The web site generated an e-mail order that was manually entered into Co-op’s system just like a hand-written letter or fax order. No extra staff were required as existing departments processed all the on-line orders. Maintenance of the site was to be controlled by the information systems (IS) department.

The site was launched in January 1996 and started slowly with fewer than 200 people accessing it in the ® rst month. However, the site was seen as successful; orders were coming through, people were using it and all at relatively low cost.

The move on to the WWW started a chain of events that changed the culture of the organization. Very quickly, PCs became mandatory on almost all desks. e-mail became a vital communication tool.

More and more customers were accessing Co-op via the web site. By late 1996, around 2500 people accessed it each month. The increase was attributed to growth in the number of home computers as well as businesses gaining access to the Internet.

## Hopeful monsters: redesign \`number 1’ ± towards the Co-op \`virtual bookshop

## Planning, development and implementation

Co-op’s potential market was expanding. Towards the end of the ® rst year of the web site, it became obvious that a major redesign was required. The numbers accessing the site meant that the resources used to support it were stretched. The web site was incompatible with advancements in technology such as proxy servers and Java. Internet security also obtained a lot of publicity in the media and a \`secure server’ was becoming a necessity for anyone selling goods or storing customer details on the WWW. In addition, competition increased and a growing number of online bookshops appeared on the WWW (for instance, Amazon).

After extensive discussions, a decision was made to redevelop the site from scratch. The goal was to be \`the Australian equivalent of Amazon’. The WWW presence was to become the Co-op \`virtual bookshop’, to boast a more personalized and faster service, secure transactions, a large searchable database, book reviews and on-line literary events. On the operational side, one full time staff member was planned to maintain the site. Orders generated should be automatically entered into the existing mail order system and customer details could be easily recorded and monitored.

The project was big. The investment was high, as were expectations. It was to boast not only ¯ ashy graphics. The plans included (1) a database of over 1.3 million book titles written in Java to allow fast searching and an advanced shopping basket mechanism, (2) a \`visitors’ lounge’ where people would ® nd information on membership and update their stored customer details, (3) a special event page named \`The Pulse’ where book launches and special events would be advertised and (4) a \`specialist services’ area where people would ® nd out about Co-op’s library and subscriptions services.

However, little research was undertaken by Co-op as to the expected level of success. US-based virtual \`superstores’ such as Amazon were generating millions in sales (while announcing a US\$7 million loss for 1997) pointing at a market for books on the WWW. The general consensus was to make the Co-op site as big and impressive as Amazon’s site, whilst providing a better and faster service.

After several months’ work, Co-op’s new \`virtual bookshop’ was launched on 18 May 1997, with considerable publicity. The launch coincided with the start of the second semester rush period. The site was heavily promoted in Co-op branches. As a result, thousands of students tried to access the site to order textbooks. Within 2 months, the number of hits to the site had tripled and orders had risen to approximately 600 per month. There were also positive reviews of the new site in several computer magazines.

From the beginning, Co-op tried to stand out and do things differently. The initial task was to make people aware of Co-op and to attract people to the site. The company organized an on-line chat with the author of a new book about computer hackers. It publicized this on newsgroups and other chat lines. As a result more than 150 people from all over the world viewed the live chat where people could \`type’ oneon-one with the author. The book became the biggest selling title over the Co-op web site.

## Cut rate kingdom

However, success came at a cost. There were many unexpected problems. The volume of orders required extra staff to process them. The in¯ ux of orders highlighted bugs in the existing mail order system. Most importantly, negativity towards the web site quickly developed among staff at all levels, mainly caused by a lack of understanding of the technology and a fear that the web site would \`steal’ sales from the branches. Staff saw it as something new that they did not understand and, more importantly, did not want to understand. Many felt that things had worked well for the previous 40 years and saw no need for the web site.

Looking back, a lot of the negativity could have been avoided through better communication between management and staff as to what exactly was happening with the web site. Many branches found it intimidating to suddenly receive orders from the web site without a good understanding of what the Internet was and how it worked. Although each branch already had an e-mail address, it was not heavily used. With the launch of the web site, the volume of e-mails sent to branches made checking several times a day necessary.

In addition, staff had to deal with a new type of customer ± the \`web customer’ ± who ordered from the WWW and expected the book on their doorstep the same afternoon. Co-op’s smaller branches did not have the facilities or staff to provide this kind of service.

These problems were unforeseen as the system was designed to be integrated with Co-op’s existing mail order system. It became obvious that it was a mistake to launch the web site without proper consultation, training and communication between staff and departments about the WWW. Said Carl Gallagher, Co-op Webmaster, \`it was not really a question of training, but more a need for information’.

By September 1997, the number of hits and orders began to decline. Initially, this was \`booked’ as being the end of the rush period. Concern was only raised when the decline continued for several months. A number of reasons were identi® ed. The most obvious was increased competition from other on-line bookshops. Barnes and Noble, a large US book chain, launched their web site and started an on-line \`price war’ with the already established Amazon. A number of Australian-based bookshops (Dymocks, Angus & Robinson and Collins) also went on-line. In Australia, at least ® ve bookstores are now on the WWW ± including small independents that could never compete with Amazon’s massive venture-capital backing and 300 staff, but which saw the Internet as a great new tool for reaching more customers. Obviously, what Australian Internet users want from local sites is Amazon’s 20± 30% discount without the high delivery costs (Sydney Morning Herald, 1997).

## Palimpsest: preparing the next round

To tackle growing competition, two more on-line chats were organized. Both were successful in promoting the site, but had minimal impact on sales. Orders were processed as fast as possible and new books were constantly added to the database. Functionally, the site was working well. Yet people were not returning to it and Co-op obtained little repeat business.

It was agreed that the static design of the home page was a major problem. People returned to the site, saw the home page had not changed since their last visit and so left, thinking that they had seen everything, even though changes were continually made. Internally, these problems gave those who were against the web site fuel for their arguments.

In October 1997 a decision was made to redesign the home page to make it more dynamic. Plans on how to make the change commenced. This time Coop learned from the mistakes of the past and listened to criticisms. The change aimed to be both cosmetic and procedural. Proper documentation and training was provided to staff. Reallocation of staff allowed orders to be processed more ef® ciently.

Co-op analysed their competitors’ sites looking for features that seemed successful. However, beyond incorporating similar ideas into their new site, the company aimed to offer something different that would set the Co-op site apart from being just another on-line bookshop.

## Roots: redesign \`number 2’

Co-op looked back to its roots and the foundations on which the web site was based, i.e. its existing business. Being Australia’s largest academic bookseller suggested features which would bene® t the majority of their customers, who are students. Co-op investigated how a person buys a book in a shop. Why do people come to Co-op branches rather than another retailer? Co-op then compared its insights to the existing web site offering. On this basis it started to design the new site.

A \`Student Centre’ was developed to provide a better service to students. It featured the following.

(1) An academic textbook database where students can search for textbooks by simply selecting a university and course.

(2) A student survival section with useful links to information covering topics such as coping with examination stress, essay writing and time management.

(3) An academic computer software section with a listing of best-selling student priced software.

(4) A page of student-related books such as university handbooks, student guides and books about the courses on offer around Australia and choosing a career.

The Student Centre was something not offered by any other on-line bookshop. Co-op had a major advantage in developing it due to their established links with universities.

When analysing customer purchasing habits in their branches they found that the average customer not looking for a speci® c book would enter a branch, browse the shelves and ® nd a title that drew their attention. The customer would then pick up the book, perhaps to read the back cover. If they liked what they read, they would purchase it.

There was no way the existing web site could replicate this \`impulse buying’. The customer basically had to know what they were looking for before they could buy. The solution was to develop a new section called \`Browse Our Shelves’ which listed several categories or subject areas. Clicking on a subject brought up a selection of titles each with abstracts, reviews and scanned cover images. This was an attempt to make ordering over the WWW similar to buying a book in a real-life store. These pages were to be updated regularly to re¯ ect new releases and best-selling titles; in doing so this would slowly build up the content of the site, making it more informative and interesting.

Co-op branches attracted customers by offering a 12% member discount off most books. While the deal was maintained on the existing site, making the Co-op offer the highest on-line discount in Australia, it was a problem to become a member on-line. Customers had to print out a form, ® ll it in and then post it to Coop. The membership application was then processed and a membership number assigned and sent back. The whole process took around 2 weeks before non-members were able to join and obtain a discount on their WWW orders. This procedure was redesigned; customers were automatically assigned a membership number and could then obtain a discount immediately. This allowed Co-op to obtain \`impulse buyers’ on-line.

The revamped site was launched in January 1998. With approximately 650 orders per month, it quickly became a success and achieved the goals that Co-op set for it. The company gained a lot of positive customer feedback about the design. The new home page, which was now changed every couple of days, succeeded in attracting customers for repeat business. In addition, remarkable interest in the \`feature sections’ such as the Student Centre and Co-op Chat was noticed. From January, sales generated from the WWW continued to increase month by month and total orders since the launch in May 1997 are 5385.

Monthly WWW sales are approximately \$40 000 but this only just covers the running costs.

Beyond its regular 12% discount, Co-op also offers 25% off selected new-release hard covers bought via its web site. In addition, it offers the \`cheapest postage in the universe’ deal ± \$3 per book, anywhere in Australia, regardless of weight.

## Future shock: further challenges from US bookstores

For small Australian book retailers and publishers, the challenge from Amazon and Co. does not just stop at the high cost of web site development.

The WWW has never had much respect for global boundaries and in publishing ± whether books or music ± territorial copyright laws are becoming unstuck. With Australia’s dependence on these international deals, that is a major issue.

Under the current territorial copyright system, books written overseas are made available in Australia either by a local publisher buying the local rights and printing the book \`down under’ or, more usually, by an Australian publisher taking the ® nished books and acting as an agent. Having bought the territorial right, the publisher gains exclusive legal right to sell that book in Australia. Every time Amazon sells a licensed book to an Australian, it is a breach of that copyright. However, territorial copyrights are violated all over the world all the time.

Another copyright issue that is being increasingly challenged by the WWW is that of publishing unpaid extracts of authors’ works. If on-line bookstores are to become compelling alternatives to the real thing, they need extracts from the books. That is what browsing is all about. However, systematic, unpaid publishing of even small sections of authors’ works concerns publishers, agents and authors. Newspapers have always paid for extracts from books ± will they continue to do so if on-line sites get them for free?

Nobody will put much effort into creating for the WWW, unless they are going to be properly remunerated. However, trying to restrict publishing on the WWW for fear of copyright is like \`trying to hold back the sea’ .

Recent evidence from one US publisher suggests that making books available on-line at no charge might increase print sales of those books. The Washington Post reported that National Academy Press, which put 1700 of its books on-line ± and available at no charge ± found that sales increased two to three times on some titles. The publisher concluded that people browse the books on-line, but if they want to read the whole thing they will buy the book.

And, ® nally, MIT’s Media Lab has promised a prototype of a leather-bound book with electronic pages in the near future. Instead of having to wait 6 months for a publisher to print a work, electronic book owners could be alerted the day the manuscript is ® nished and download it from the publisher ± or even the author. No printing, no distribution, no territorial licensing, no expensive inventory and no returns. Until then, Co-op and other Australian bookstores and publishers can expect continued Amazonian challenges from the Internet.

## Past and future issues for Co-Op

## Pre-launch issues

Before getting deep into the technical complexities of designing a web site, it is crucial to ® rst look at what one has to start with ± the existing business. The following questions have to be examined when aiming at a pro® table WWW presence:

(1) What is the core goal of the business? How will a WWW presence help to meet this? Will the introduction of a web site change the direction of the business?

(2) Why does one want a web site? Is the web site simply going to be an on-line company pro® le or is it going to provide a specialized WWW-based service?

(3) Who are current customers/existing markets? Why do they do business with the company? Which markets is one trying to attract through the WWW?

(4) What resources are required to set up and maintain a web site? How much is one willing to invest? How will the site be integrated into the existing business? How should the site grow?

Without thought about these, any web site investment could prove fruitless. How well did Co-op analyse these issues? Would their path to the current site have been smoother if they had undertaken a more substantial analysis ® rst?

## Bene® ting from the WWW

Next, consider the role of Co-op’s web site in enabling it to pursue any of the four possible routes by which a ® rm can pro® t from its Internet or EC activities, namely, (1) increasing the volume of books sold, (2) larger margins, (3) sale of advertising space on the web site and (4) EC as an additional product, particularly the potential of electronic publishing ± this involves consideration of timing for entering electronic publishing activities, electronic publishing value chain activities to be offered and digitalization of traditional products versus new product creation.
