# The effectiveness of expert support technology for decision making: individuals versus small groups

FUI HOON (FIONA) NAH

University of Nebraska-Lincoln, Lincoln, Nebraska 68588-0491, USA

JIYE MAO

University of Waterloo, Waterloo, Ontario N2L 3G1, Canada

IZAK BENBASAT

University of British Columbia, Vancouver, British Columbia V6T 1Z2, Canada

Expert support systems (ESSs) are increasingly used in organizations to support individuals and groups in decision making. Although ESSs have been shown to enhance the decision-making capabilities of individuals, their bene® ts in supporting group decision making are less clear. To the best of our knowledge, no empirical research has evaluated the effectiveness of the technology in the group setting or compared its usefulness for supporting individual versus group decision making. The results of this research show that ESSs bene® t decision making of both individuals and groups and novices are able to gain more from ESSs than experts. The ® ndings also suggest that groups outperform individuals in both with and without ESS support settings.

## Introduction

This research examines the ef® cacy of using an expert support system (ESS) to support individual and small group decision making. An ESS is an extension of the expert system (ES) concept (Luconi et al., 1986). Both ESS and ES refer to computer-based software tools that use arti® cial intelligence techniques to capture, represent and apply expert knowledge, so as to be able to mimic the behaviour of human experts in speci® c, narrowly de® ned problem domains.

These technologies have gained popularity for use in business organizations since the mid-1980s. Despite some setbacks, many companies remain enthusiastic proponents of the technology and continue to develop important applications based on the technology (Gill, 1995). Some of these applications include Carrier’s EXPERT (Heatley et al., 1995), Digital’ s XCON (Leonard-Barton, 1987; Sviokla, 1990) and American Express’ Authorizer’s Assistant (Feigenbaum et al., 1988; Rothi and Yen, 1990). They are used to assist in a wide range of tasks including diagnosis, prediction, planning and design (Sviokla, 1990; Heatley et al., 1995). Some companies have even indicated that their key businesses are strategically dependent on these systems and are likely to remain so in the future (Gill, 1995).

Traditionally, ESs were used to replace human decision making by transferring knowledge from human experts to the systems. However, this approach has not worked very well due to a few reasons, such as the dif® - culty in matching the expertise in these systems to that of the human experts and the dif® culty in keeping the systems up to date due to the evolving and dynamic nature of the environment. A partial solution to these problems was the extension of the ES concept to the ESS concept, which advocates the ES as a support rather than a replacement tool (Luconi et al., 1986). Thus, an ESS is developed to provide expert advice to complement the knowledge of decision makers and is intended to support rather than replace the decision makers.

A typical ESS provides two types of support to the decision makers: (1) analyses and recommendations given by the system and (2) explanations to explain domain concepts and to justify analyses and recommendations given. The ESS used in this research provided both types of support but not the solutions to the problem. The decision makers made the ® nal judgements after consulting the system.

Since their introduction, such technologies have been targeted at individuals. With the emphasis on teamwork, these systems are becoming popular as a group decision support tool (e.g. Swann, 1988; Sviokla, 1989). For instance, at Imperial Chemical Industries (ICI) an ESS (so-called decision assistant, knowledge-based system by Swann (1988)) that supports business planning is used to assist in group decision making. The system was not originally conceived as a group support tool but arose as one due to business needs. The system is increasingly applied in group sessions and is proving effective in that mode of use. Similarly, a group of ® nancial planners at The Financial Collaborative (TFC) have used an ES called PlanPower to help them perform ® nancial planning for their clients (Sviokla, 1989).

The cost of ESSs is usually high due to the time and resources involved in the development process. These high costs make it imperative to learn more about the effects of these systems in supporting both individual and multi-individual decision making. Although studies have been carried out to examine the use of ESSs in supporting individual decision making (see the literature review that follows), no experimental work has investigated the effect of expert support on group decision making (Benbasat et al., 1993). In this research, we are interested in studying the effect of expert support in both individual and small group decision-making settings. In addition, we compare the effectiveness of ESSs in both settings. In other words, we are interested in evaluating whether ESSs would provide greater bene® ts in supporting individual or small group decision making. To the best of our knowledge, this is the ® rst research in information systems to carry out a comparative evaluation of the bene® ts received by individuals and groups for the same type of information technology support (i.e. ESS support) for problem solving in the same task environment.

In this research, we are interested in answering the following questions.

(1) Do ESSs help individuals make better judgements?

(2) Do ESSs help groups make better judgements?

(3) Do groups or individuals bene® t more from ESSs?

Since expertise is a potential moderator of the relationships, the above questions were assessed for both experts and novices.

The following section reviews literature related to this research. The third section provides the research hypotheses while the fourth section describes the research methodology. The ® ndings are presented in the ® fth section and discussed in the ® nal section.

## Related literature

This section reviews empirical studies on ESS technology, as well as literature on individual versus group decision making, expert± novice differences and multiple-cue probability learning.

## Empirical studies on ESS technology

A number of empirical studies have evaluated various aspects of ESS technology. With the exception of

Sviokla (1986, 1989, 1990), these studies evaluated ESS technology in supporting individual decision making. Eining and Dorr (1991), Dhaliwal (1993), Mao (1995), Ye and Johnson (1995) and Gregor (1996) looked speci® cally at the explanation facilities. Dhaliwal (1993) found ESS explanation facilities improved performance, while Eining and Dorr (1991) and Gregor (1996) found no difference in performance. However, Eining and Dorr (1991) found the use of ESSs (both with and without explanation capabilities) bene® ted domain novices in terms of time and accuracy while Oz et al. (1993) found ESSs improved decision quality but had no effect on time. On the other hand, Gregor (1996) observed a positive relationship between frequency of use of explanations and problem-solving performance. Mao (1995) found decision accuracy improved with the provision of hypertext ESS explanations. Ye and Johnson (1995) found justi® cation (why) explanations to be the most effective type of explanation to bring about changes in user attitude towards the system.

Hsu (1993) showed that user cognitive style and interface design were two important factors in¯ uencing the transfer of knowledge from the ESS to users. One of Lamberti and Wallace’s (1990) ® ndings indicated that ESSs have a greater impact on improving the performance of low-skill users than high-skill users. They also examined interactions between knowledge presentation format, question type and task uncertainty in terms of speed and accuracy of decision-making performance. Peterson (1988) found ESSs improved the performance of users; this improvement in accuracy was greater for users who were inexperienced in the domain area than those who were experienced. On the other hand, Sviokla (1986, 1989, 1990) examined three ® eld sites with ESSs in active use and found ESSs increased the effectiveness and ef® ciency of the organizations at the expense of an increased rigidity in the task. As the ESS was being used, maintained and improved upon, problem-solving knowledge improved and problem structure increased.

Thus, there exists empirical evidence that ESSs improve the judgements of individual decision makers (Peterson, 1988; Eining and Dorr, 1991) and bene® t novices more than experts (Peterson, 1988; Lamberti and Wallace, 1990). (For a more detailed literature review, please refer to Gregor and Benbasat (forthcoming).) Unlike earlier work, this paper focuses on a comparison of the effect of ESSs on judgemental accuracy in the individual and small group decision-making settings.

## Individual versus group decision making

This section reviews the literature on individual versus group decision making. When individuals make decisions in a group, a number of advantages and disadvantages may be observed. These positive and negative factors were identi® ed and termed \`group assets and liabilities’ by Maier (1967) and \`group process gains and losses’ by Steiner (1972).

According to Steiner (1972),

actual group performance = potential group performance ± group process losses,

where potential group performance includes the process gains from working in a group.

## Process gains

When decisions are made in groups, more information and a broader range of knowledge are available for decision making (Steiner, 1972; Shaw, 1981), multiple perspectives and approaches are taken into account (Maier, 1967), synergetic effects can be obtained when members build on one another’s ideas (Osborn, 1957; VanGundy, 1984), errors are often checked and corrected (Shaw, 1981; Hill, 1982), stimulation from the group may encourage individual members to perform better (Shaw, 1981), members may learn and imitate more skilled members to improve performance (Hill, 1982) and acceptance of the decision increases due to participation in problem solving (Maier, 1967). As a result, the group as a whole may gain an increased understanding of the problem, work out solutions more thoroughly and make better judgements and decisions.

Similarly, process gains may occur with ESS support. For instance, the ESS may provide additional knowledge and perspectives to the decision makers. Members may build upon the ideas and suggestions given by the ESS or discover their mistakes or errors while comparing their analyses with the recommendations and explanations given by the ESS.

## Process losses

However, some negative aspects of group decision making are also observed. When individuals work in groups, more time is consumed in decision making (Husband, 1940), unreasonable social pressure may be used to push for conformity rather than for high-quality solutions (Maier, 1963), engagement in con¯ ict can delay decision making and create ill will among group members (Huber, 1980) and excessively conservative or risky decisions may be made (Myers and Lamm, 1976). The negative aspects of group decision making have been well documented by Janis (1982) and Steiner (1972).

Cognitive inertia can also be a problem in group decision making (Nunamaker et al., 1991) which may be overcome with ESS support. Cognitive inertia occurs when discussion moves along one train of thought without deviating because group members refrain from contributing comments that are not directly related to the current discussion. In some groups, individual domination is observed when some group member(s) exercise undue in¯ uence or monopolize the group’s time in an unproductive manner (Maier, 1967). On the other hand, free riding may occur when members rely on others to accomplish goals. This may occur due to cognitive loa® ng, the need to compete for air time or when members perceive their input to be unneeded (Nunamaker et al., 1991). Furthermore, some members may withhold ideas or comments during discussion for fear that they may be negatively evaluated.

An overemphasis on social± emotional aspects may also reduce task performance. Members may be reluctant to criticize the comments of others due to politeness or fear of reprisals (Shaw, 1981). The desire to be accepted and to be a good group member tends to silence disagreements, favour consensus and produce unreasonable social pressure for conformity. Inferior decisions may be made as a result.

In the case where ESS support is provided, free riding may occur due to decision makers’ reliance on the ESS. This may cause decision makers not to analyse the problem or case in suf® cient depth. On the other hand, the problem of cognitive intertia may be overcome by the ESS, leading to a more comprehensive analysis and complete discussion. Furthermore, the ESS may be used as a \`scapegoat’ by group members to raise disagreements in the group.

## Expert versus novice differences

Davis (1996) and Ettenson et al. (1987) identi® ed one major difference in problem solving between experts and novices. Experts exhibit a higher level of selective attention to relevant information and base their judgements on fewer cues than novices. Compared to novices, experts also have better cross-referencing and memory organization and a superior mechanism for relating problems to appropriate knowledge and courses of action (Bedard, 1991).

Miller (1956) and Chase and Simon (1973) experimentally observed an organization strategy known as \`chunking’ which is used by experts. The experts’ ability to perceive information about a problem in \`chunks’ of structural knowledge enhances later recall and ef® ciency of performance. Experts are capable of taking into account large amounts of information internally and integrating different pieces of information that are not simultaneously available perceptually. They have superior memory skills in recognizing patterns in their domains of expertise. Their internal representation of the available information is suf® - ciently precise to allow extensive reasoning and evaluation of consistency and suf® ciently ¯ exible to allow reinterpretation as new information becomes available.

Expert± novice differences can be summarized as follows. In comparison to novices, domain experts (1) perceive large meaningful patterns in their domain through chunking (Chase and Simon, 1973), (2) have superior short- and long-term memory for domainrelevant information (Chase and Ericsson, 1982), (3) are faster at performing the basic skills of the domain (Chi et al., 1988), (4) represent problems at a deeper (principled) and more elaborate level (Weiser and Shertz, 1983) and (5) make judgements based on fewer and more relevant cues (Davis, 1996).

## Multiple-cue probability learning

According to the \`multiple-cue probability learning’ literature (Balzer et al., 1989), task information (i.e. information about relations in the task environment) contributes to improved performance. Since the support provided by ESSs corresponds to information about the task environment (Dhaliwal, 1993; Nah and Benbasat, 1995; Dhaliwal and Benbasat, 1996), ESS support is expected to bring about an increase in decision quality. The task information provided by the ESS in¯ uences the cognitive system of the decision maker, thus leading to better judgements or decisions made.

## Research hypotheses

Four hypotheses were developed to address the research questions. Based on the multiple-cue probability learning literature discussed earlier, ESS support (which is a form of task support) is hypothesized to increase the quality of judgements ± such support is expected to bene® t both groups and individuals. Although prior work has provided support that ESSs improve the judgements of individual decision makers (Peterson, 1988; Eining and Dorr, 1991), there is no published evidence to support their ef® cacy in the group setting. As presented in hypotheses 1 and 2 we hypothesize ESS support to increase the quality of judgements in both the individual and group decisionmaking settings. We also expect both novices and experts to bene® t from ESS support in the individual and group settings.

## ESS effect on individuals

$\operatorname { H } _ { 1 } { \mathrm { : } }$ Individuals make better judgements with ESSs.

$\mathrm { H } _ { \mathrm { 1 a } } \mathrm { : }$ Novices working individually make better judgements with ESSs.

$\mathrm { H } _ { \mathrm { 1 6 } } \mathrm { : }$ : Experts working individually make better judgements with ESSs.

## ESS effect on groups

$\operatorname { H } _ { 2 } \colon$ Groups make better judgements with ESSs.

$\mathrm { H } _ { 2 \mathrm { a } } \colon$ Novices working in groups make better judgements with ESSs

$\mathrm { H } _ { 2 \mathrm { b } } \colon$ Experts working in groups make better judgements with ESSs.

As stated by McGrath (1984) in his review of the group decision-making literature, \`the judgements of interacting groups are more accurate than those of the average individual members’ (p. 73). Since groups offer the possibility of more accurate judgements than single individuals, particularly on tasks with considerable complexity or \`intentional depth’ (Hill, 1982; McGrath, 1984), we expect groups to make better judgements than individuals in the absence of ESS support. Such improvement arises from group process gains; group process losses are minimized if the group process is well managed.

## Group effect

$\operatorname { H } _ { 3 } \colon$ In the absence of ESS support, groups make better judgements than individuals.

$\mathrm { H } _ { 3 \mathrm { a } } \colon$ In the absence of ESS support, novices working in groups make better judgements than novices working individually.

$\mathrm { H } _ { 3 \mathrm { b } } \colon$ In the absence of ESS support, experts working in groups make better judge ments than experts working individually.

Since groups are expected to make better judgements than individuals in the absence of ESS support, we expect ESS support to offer greater bene® ts to individuals than groups.

## ESS effect on individuals versus groups

$\operatorname { H } _ { 4 } \colon$ ESSs produce greater improvement in the judgements of individuals than groups.

$\mathrm { H } _ { 4 \mathrm { a } } \colon$ ESSs produce greater improvement in the judgements of novices working individually than the judgements of novices working in groups.

$\mathrm { H } _ { 4 \mathrm { b } } \mathrm { : }$ ESSs produce greater improvement in the judgements of experts working individually than the judgements of experts working in groups.

By virtue of the knowledge and experience of experts, we expect both group and ESS effects to be lower for experts than novices. Since individual experts are capable of making good judgements on their own, they may not gain as much from working in groups as the novices. By the same token, ESS support is expected to bene® t novices more than experts, as reported in the work by Peterson (1988) and Lamberti and Wallace (1990). Despite the smaller effects in the case of experts, we expect these effects to be signi® - cant for both novices and experts.

## Research methodology

Two laboratory experiments were conducted ± the ® rst to study the use of ESSs by individuals and the second to examine the use of ESSs by groups. The same experimental task was used in both experiments. The task was a ® nancial analysis case involving the evaluation of a loan application by a hypothetical ® rm. Subjects were provided with the ® nancial statements of the ® rm for the last 5 years, ® nancial ratios derived from the statements and information on the purpose and size of the loan requested. Subjects were asked to assume the role of corporate loan of® cers and to assess various aspects of the ® rm’s ® nancial health. Based on the assessment, they made a recommendation regarding whether or not the loan should be approved and, if so, the amount.

A total of eight questions were given to the subjects. The subjects incorporated the recommendations and opinions given by the ESS to derive ® nal judgements on these eight questions. Questions 1± 6 required the subjects to rate, on a scale of 1± 10, the liquidity, longterm solvency, asset utilization, value of stock as collateral and quality of ® nancial and operating management of the company, respectively. Question 7 asked for an estimate of the predicted net income of the company for the coming year and question 8 concerned the amount of loan to be granted. Questions 1± 6 were used to evaluate the quality of judgements.

## Experimental ESS

A simulated ESS, named FINALYZER, was used for the experiment. It was developed based on the expertise of a panel of ® nancial analysis experts. This section provides a brief overview of how FINALYZER was developed and validated. Additional details are found in Dhaliwal (1993).

Financial analysis generally involves three steps. First, various categories of ® nancial ratios are computed. Second, each category of ratios is evaluated to yield judgements relating to each of the various aspects of ® nancial analysis, such as liquidity, capital structure and pro® tability. Third, these judgements are used to form the basis for making speci® c decisions or predictions, such as granting loans or forecasting share prices.

Due to lack of access to a commercial or proprietary ESS to support ® nancial analysis, a simulated

ESS was built. A group of six ® nancial analysts, whose job experience ranged from 12 to 23 years, were recruited for knowledge acquisition. While working on a ® nancial analysis case called Canacom (not the real name of the company), concurrent verbal protocols were collected from the senior ® nancial analysts to determine the type of analysis they performed and the kind of reasoning and explanations they used. The results became the basis for the simulated ESS labelled FINALYZER. The conclusions derived by the ® nancial analysts were extracted, grouped into subanalyses and incorporated into FINALYZER as recommendations under the respective subanalyses. To validate the system, two accounting professors, three doctoral students in accounting/® nance and two junior ® nancial analysts used the ESS to help them analyse the Canacom case. None was able to detect that FINA-LYZER was not a fully functional generic ESS for ® nancial analysis. When FINALYZER was used in prior research, it was considered highly realistic and useful by the participants (Dhaliwal, 1993). Subjects rated the level of expertise displayed by the system very high, as it compared with the best human experts in the industry.

The same ® nancial analysts whose knowledge was used to develop the ESS were also asked to provide a \`solution’ to the Canacom case. The solution was in the form of answers to the six judgement questions to be asked of the experimental subjects (i.e. liquidity, long-term solvency, asset utilization, value of stock as collateral and quality of ® nancial and operating management of the company). The ® nal set of answers agreed upon by the ® nancial analysts, after a two-round Delphi process, was taken as a benchmark of the \`correct’ judgements for the case (Dhaliwal, 1993) and were used for evaluating the quality of judgements made by the experimental subjects. The quality of the judgements was measured as the absolute difference between the subjects’ judgements and the panel’ s consensus judgements on the six questions.

In FINALYZER, each conclusion or recommendation has three types of reasoning-trace explanations to reveal why it is relevant and important (why explanation), how it has been reached (how explanation) and its strategic relationship with other conclusions in relation to the goal (strategic explanation). In addition to reasoning-trace explanations, deep explanations were also provided by FINALYZER to present the underlying generic domain knowledge for each of the concepts and procedures used. Similarly, three types of deep explanations for each concept or procedure were provided in terms of its de® nition (how explanation), why it is useful and important (why explanation) and relationships with others involved in the same task (strategic explanation).

FINALYZER displays three types of screens in the following sequence: (1) an information screen containing an index of relevant domain concepts and procedures, where the user can request relevant deep explanations from the index, (2) a data screen of relevant ® nancial ratios calculated from the ® nancial statements of the hypothetical ® rm to be evaluated and (3) recommendation screens, where the user can view the system conclusions and request reasoning-trace explanations on each conclusion (see Figure 1).

Furthermore, FINALYZER allows contextualized access to deep explanations via hypertext-style links. The contextualized access makes deep explanations available not only through the information screen prior to the analysis, but also from the system conclusions and reasoning-trace explanations. When examining deep explanations, users can access explanations on related domain concepts by following the hypertextstyle links. Therefore, deep explanations are not only linked to other related deep explanations but also integrated into other parts of system output (i.e. reasoningtrace explanations and conclusions).

## Experimental procedures

The ® rst experiment was run on an individual basis and the second experiment was run one group at a time. (For more information and details about the ® rst experiment, please refer to Mao (1995), and for more information and details about the second experiment, please refer to Nah (1997).) Figure 2 illustrates the procedure in both experiments.

Experiment 2 involved groups only. Subjects were ® rst given the relevant ® nancial information for the company to be evaluated. They then worked on the case individually and without any form of ESS support and produced a set of individual judgements concerning the case. Following that, subjects in the ESS-supported groups were provided with appropriate training to familiarize them with the ESS features. Another system for evaluating consumer credit applications that has similar features to FINALYZER was used for training. The goal of the training session was to familiarize the subjects with the features provided by FINALYZER. The subjects in the control group received no training. Next, the subjects worked in groups of three under the randomly assigned experimental condition (with and without ESS support) until a group consensus was reached on the same set of judgements they had made earlier. Finally, they were asked to make the same set of judgements again individually, taking into account what they had learned from their group discussions and from FINALYZER, if provided. Thus, the subjects made a total of three sets of judgements (pre-discussion individual, group and post-discussion individual judgements) during the experiment.

## Subjects

There were two groups of subjects: novices and experienced professionals. Novice subjects included undergraduate and MBA students in a business school who were either specializing in accounting or had taken accounting courses extensively. Experienced professionals whose work involved ® nancial analysis were recruited as expert decision makers. They typically possessed some related professional quali® cations beyond an undergraduate degree, such as certi® ed general accountant (CGA) and certi® ed ® nancial analyst (CFA) designations.

A total of 15 student subjects and 13 professionals participated in experiment 1. A total of 27 student subjects (nine groups of three) and 18 professionals (six groups of three) participated in the ESS-support treatment of experiment 2. A total of 24 student subjects (eight groups of three) participated in the control (i.e. no ESS-support condition) of experiment 2. However, due to the limited availability of professionals for experiment 2 (which required three times as many subjects as experiment 1), all of the 18 professionals available for experiment 2 were assigned to the ESS-support treatment, which meant that none of the professionals participated in the control. This was necessary to ensure that we had the recommended minimum of ® ve data points (i.e. ® ve decision-making groups) (Siegel and Castellan, 1988) in the ESSsupport treatment for professionals. We were therefore unable to assess hypotheses 2b and 3b due to this limitation.

## Experimental results

The results were analysed using non-parametric statistics (Siegel and Castellan, 1988), more speci® - cally the Mann± Whitney and Wilcoxon signed ranks tests. Non-parametric statistics were used because they are more general and do not require strict assumptions, as in the case of the parametric model, to be met.

## Judgements of novices

The ESS effect on individuals, as predicted in hypothesis $^ { 1 \mathrm { a } , }$ was tested using the data collected from experiment 1. The two sets of individual judgements made by the novice subjects before and after using the ESS (i.e. (a) and (b) in Figure 2) were analysed using the Wilcoxon signed ranks test. Recall that each subject was asked to make six separate judgements concerning the ® nancial state of Canacom. The mean of the absolute deviations of the subjects’ judgements from those of the ® nancial experts (labelled as panel’s consensus) whose expertise was used to build FINALYZER are presented in Table 1. (These means are provided for information purposes only since non-parametric statistics use mean ranks for the analysis.) A lower score means that the subjects’ judgements are closer to those of the panel’s consensus. Thus, a lower mean absolute deviation score is considered a \`better’ judgement by virtue of its closeness to the consensus judgement of the expert ® nancial analyst panel.

There is a signi® cant ESS effect $( \phi = 0 . 0 4 )$ , supporting hypothesis 1a, indicating that the quality of novices’ individual judgements improved when supported by an ESS.

Hypothesis 2a refers to the expected ESS effect on groups; it is expected that groups with ESS support should do better than groups without ESS support.

Because both group and individual judgements were made after the group discussion and, thus, were in¯ uenced by the discussion, both sets of judgements were used to compare groups with and without ESS support (i.e. comparing (d) versus (g) as well as (e) versus (h) in Figure 2). (Refer to Table 2 for the mean absolute deviation scores.) In both cases, the results of the Mann± Whitney test are signi® cant $( \hbar < 0 . 0 5 )$ supporting hypothesis 2a. Therefore, hypotheses 1a and 2a together imply that novices bene® ted from ESS use in both individual and group settings, i.e. there are clear bene® ts of ESS use for novice decision makers, either individually or in a group.

For the group effect, hypothesis 3a predicted that in the absence of ESS support, groups make better judgements than individuals. The group effect was assessed in two ways: (1) by comparing the accuracy of the average individual judgements made by the group members before and after group discussion (i.e. comparing (c) and (e) in Figure 2) and (2) by comparing the accuracy of the average individual judgements made by the group members before group discussion with the accuracy of their group judgements (i.e. comparing (c) and (d) in Figure 2). The Wilcoxon signed ranks test was used for the analysis and the means are presented in Table 3. In both cases, there is a signi® cant group effect $(  p = 0 . 0 5 )$ . This result indicates that novice decision makers did better in groups than individually.

Although the results of hypotheses 1a and 2a provided support for a signi® cant ESS effect, the question of whether individuals or groups bene® ted more from ESS support remained. Hypothesis 4a predicted that individuals would bene® t more from ESS support than groups, i.e. individuals are expected to exhibit greater improvement than groups in their quality of judgements after using the ESS. However, the results of the Mann± Whitney test indicate that the amount of improvement in judgemental accuracy was not different when novices used the ESS alone or in groups; this was true both when average individual judgements in a group were used for analysis (i.e. comparing (b)± (a) versus (h)± (f) in Figure 2) $(  p = 0 . 1 7 )$ or when group judgements were used for analysis (i.e. comparing (b)± (a) versus (g)± (f) in Figure 2) $(  p = 0 . 1 1 )$ . Table 4 shows the mean improvement in absolute deviation scores. Although the mean improvements in the individual and group settings presented in Table 4 are quite different, they are not statistically signi® cant, because the group improvement was uniform (all groups did better with ESS support) but for the individuals the pre± post improvement was not uniformly positive and had greater highs and lows.

## Judgements of experts

Whereas there is a signi® cant ESS effect for novices working on their own (hypothesis 1a), domain experts working on their own gained no signi® cant improvement $( \phi = 0 . 1 8 )$ from using the ESS (i.e. comparing (a) and (b) in Figure 2). ## Discussion and conclusions

Several interesting conclusions emerged from our data analysis. First, one of the key contributions of this research is evaluating the effectiveness of an ESS in the group setting. While prior studies have found novices to bene® t from the use of an ESS, this research shows that an ESS not only bene® ts novices who are making decisions on their own, but also bene® ts novices who are working in groups. In addition, novices bene® ted equally from the ESS in both the individual and group settings. This result therefore extends existing literature on ESS use.

This research also veri® es that, in the absence of ESS support, novices working in groups make better judgements than novices working individually. The above two ® ndings about novices (i.e. groups make better judgements than individuals when ESS support is not available and individuals and groups bene® ted equally from the ESS) suggest that novices using an ESS in a group setting will outperform novices using an ESS to solve the problem on their own. A nonparametric analysis that was carried out to test this conjecture produced positive results $( \hbar < 0 . 0 1 )$ , indicating that groups provided with ESS support will perform better than individuals provided with ESS support. This is an interesting ® nding as it indicates that the performance of individuals and groups cannot be brought to the same level using ESS support. In other words, regardless of whether ESS support is available or not, groups outperform individuals.

Our study also shows expertise to be a key factor mediating the effectiveness of an ESS. A comparison of the results on experts versus novices revealed that ESS effect was not as strong for expert subjects. This may be due to the fact that experienced decision makers are more assured of their expertise and methods and analysis and, thus, less willing than novices to deviate from their initial judgements based on the recommendations of the ESS. Furthermore, we should consider the fact that the quality of judgements was measured in comparison to another group of experts (the panel of ® nancial analysts whose expertise was used in building the ESS) and that there is the possibility of some difference in opinion between any two groups of ® nancial analysis experts. The results therefore provide stronger justi® cation for the development of ESSs for novice decision makers as compared to experts and the expectation that novices will exhibit a larger change in decision making behaviour when solving problems with ESS support.

Since this is one of the pioneering research papers which have compared the effectiveness of ESSs for supporting individual versus group decision making, the results of this study will be of interest to both researchers and practitioners. It is our hope that this research will spur the interest of other researchers to explore issues involving individual versus group use of information and decision support systems.
