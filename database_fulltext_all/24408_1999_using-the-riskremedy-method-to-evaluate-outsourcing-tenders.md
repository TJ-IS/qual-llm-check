Journal of Information Technology (1999) 14, 203± 211

# Using the risk± remedy method to evaluate outsourcing tenders

EDWARD LEWIS

University College, Australian Defence Force Academy, Canberra, ACT, 2600, Australia

The risk± remedy method is beginning to replace cost± bene® t or weighted scoring methods in the evaluation of information technology outsourcing tenders by the Australian Public Service. This paper describes the use of the method. It also describes some of the lessons that have been learnt from the use of the method in six tender evaluations: use the correct requirements, use requirements correctly, consider the cost of bidding, consider the cost of delay, check the bids carefully and use a design rather than a selection approach.

## Introduction

The Australian public sector needs better tender evaluation methods to handle the demand for it to outsource the supply and support of its information technology (IT). The lack of acceptance of existing methods by senior decision makers and the increasing interest in risk management has led to the development of a new evaluation method ± risk± remedy.

All levels of the Australian public sector (federal, state and local government) are undertaking a considerable number of evaluations of tenders for outsourcing, particularly for IT. Every Federal Government agency is required to consider the use of IT outsourcing by June 1999 (Fahey, 1997). In several states, particularly Victoria, local government agencies are required to outsource at least 50% of their services, including their IT infrastructure (mainframe, mid-range, data communications, desktop products and support services). Similar requirements exist in the other states.

In 1993± 1994 the value of these government tenders was approximately \$10 billion, with achievable contracting out reaching a value of \$31 billion over the next 2 years (Industry Commission, 1996). IT is thought to be approximately 5± 10% of this value (with an attainable value of \$1± 3 billion). The Federal Government is hoping to save over £900 million dollars through outsourcing IT by 2004.

There is strong pressure to outsource at the federal level, although the evaluation is regarded as being a \`market test’ to see whether outsourcing is justi® ed. The basis for most of the evaluation is still cost savings. Some agencies are looking to outsourcing as a means for gaining access to additional skills but that is not a commonly declared reason. These reasons are similar to those that in¯ uence most organizations in their consideration of outsourcing.

However, there are other factors in¯ uencing the demand for outsourcing that are particular to the Federal Government. The most important factor is the wish to encourage industrial development, with the government keen to encourage the use of Australian Small to Medium Enterprises, particularly in regional areas. Another undeclared factor is to reduce the number of people on the public payroll. Accordingly, the method used for choosing whether to outsource or not and to whom has to take account of these factors.

There are differences between public and private sectors when considering outsourcing that should be re¯ ected in the use of the evaluation method. The Federal Government does not allow in-house bids in IT outsourcing, with most comparisons being made between existing baseline costs and the bid costs. There is a tendency to outsource a complete set of services, such as all IT equipment and software, sometimes including applications development, rather than consider a more selective selection in keeping with the nature of the organization and its use of IT (Lacity and Willcocks, 1998). In addition, public sector agencies have to pay attention to \`community service obligations’ (such as providing uniform services to clients regardless of location). Thus, the evaluation method must acknowledge these differences.

There is a need for an evaluation approach that is capable of keeping up with the rapid increase in the number of outsourcing contracts, whilst ensuring that the particular concerns of outsourcing public sector IT infrastructure are taken fully into account.

## Available approaches for tender evaluation

There are three basic approaches to evaluating IT investments or acquiring information systems and services in the Australian Public Service (APS).These approaches are cost± bene® t, weighted scoring (multiattribute utility) and, now, risk management.

Initially the APS ± for the federal level of government ± was required to use cost± bene® t analysis in evaluating IT investments or acquisition (Australian Public Service Board, 1981). This approach calculated the net present value of the life-cycle costs for the proposed system, with an adjustment to the discount rate to re¯ ect the risk associated with the project (Department of Finance, 1992, 1993).

At the early stage in the use of this cost± bene® t or cost-effectiveness analysis (the terms being often, but erroneously, used interchangeably), the savings used to offset the costs of the systems were in positions forfeited or in jobs that would have to be provided to achieve the same level of effectiveness as promised by the system. The fact that the latter job-related saving was an evaluation device was sometimes missed, as there were industrial disputes over the jobs being \`lost’ through computerization.

Of course, there was considerable debate about the dif® culties associated with this approach. The debate occurred in the general literature (Morley-English, 1984; Mishan, 1988; Dorfman, 1996) as well as in of® - cial publications of the APS (Department of Finance, 1993). Examples of the dif® culties included the choice of discount rate, the treatment of intangible bene® ts and the use of adjustments to the discount rate to re¯ ect the effect of risk upon the evaluation. Another and possibly more fatal blow to the use of this approach was the limited understanding of the use of complex costing models. The evaluators found it dif® cult to build the costing models, particularly as many of the data were not available. In addition, the senior managers on the evaluation committees found it dif® cult to understand the implications of cost± bene® t ratios.

Although not sanctioned in any of® cial publications at the federal level, weighted scoring methods have been widely used since the early 1990s. There are some State Government publications describing how such approaches can be used (Department of Premier and Cabinet, Victoria Government, 1997) and the Federal Department of Finance and Administration ± the arbiter in such matters ± has marketed the all purpose evaluation tool, which uses a simple weighed scoring technique (Department of Finance, 1996).

These weighted scoring methods were based, mostly unknowingly, upon Edwards’ (1977) simple multiple attribute rating technique (SMART). This approach usually involved determining the sum of assessments of performance against a series of weighted criteria. In some cases, cost was a criterion, while in others cost was determined separately and then incorporated into a \`value-for-money’ ratio of score/cost or similar. Risk was handled by adjustments to the cost model by altering the discount rate used in the nett present value calculation or by adding risk-related criteria to the series.

Some attempts have been made to use Saaty’ s (1986; as one of many references) analytic hierarchy process (AHP) but this variant of weighted scoring has not achieved the popularity in Australia as it has seemed to achieve in the USA (e.g. Zahedi, 1985; Borthick and Scheiner, 1988). Perhaps the disputes about the validity of the AHP (e.g. Schoner and Wedley, 1989), such as rank reversal and scale shrinkage, have become too well known.

The variant of the weighted scoring method called nett gain (Lewis 1990a,b) has become more widely used in Australia. This method has been the basis, under licence as Select!Gain, for over 100 evaluations involving system selection or competitive tendering and contracting (CTC) for federal, state and local government agencies. This method is still being used extensively throughout the public sector.

I have found problems with this scoring approach as well. The main dif® culty is the acceptance of the approach by senior managers. The members of senior committees are often worried that it is a \`computer making the decision’. They feel that they are not being given an opportunity to exercise their judgement or involve political factors that could not be captured in the weighting scheme. The importance of this political in¯ uence cannot be overstated (Serafeimidis and Smithson, 1995; Strassman, 1995; Hart, 1997; Warne, 1997). There is often an apparent lack of transparency in the approach, so some agencies try to duplicate the results of the nett gain method using their own spreadsheets in order to \`understand the process’.

Any weighted scoring technique is very sensitive to the weights assigned to the criteria yet there is little done to ensure that these weights are assigned in a systematic, defensible way. I have seen a number of tenders where the project directors assigned weights based upon their own \`sound professional judgement’, without any use of the detailed stakeholder analysis that should have been carried out to determine the weights.

Thus, in response to requests from the tender evaluation team for a Federal Government department in 1996, I developed a different approach, based upon the concepts of risk management and avoiding the use of weighted scores.

## Interest in risk management

The use of risk management is not new in systems development (Boehm, 1981; Department of Public

Works and Services, NSW Government, 1996; Powell and Klein, 1996) or in systems evaluation (McFarlan 1981; Muller, 1989; Willcocks and Margetts, 1994; Santhanam and Kyparisis, 1995; Serafeimidis and Smithson, 1995; Strassmann, 1997). The use of risk management is similar in both applications.

There is growing interest in using risk management approaches in government procurement in the USA (US Navy, 1997), UK (Central Computers and Communications Agency, 1997) and Australia. The interest in taking account of risk in procurement in general in the APS is best exempli® ed by the recent booklet published by the agency then responsible for setting procurement practice (Purchasing Australia, 1996).

The process described in this manual generally follows the steps given in the ANZ Standard (AS/NZ 4360: 1995: Risk Management). These steps are: (1) determine the extent to which risk management is required, (2) establish the context, (3) identify the risks, (4) analyse the risks, (5) evaluate and prioritize the risks and (6) treat the risks. These steps are similar to those described in most discussions of risk management (e.g. Willcocks and Margetts, 1994; Charette, 1996;).

Although the move towards a risk management approach in tender evaluation is not new, there were some dif® culties that had to be overcome before it was acceptable to the agencies seeking to outsource their IT infrastructure. For example, the approach is very engineering orientated and the emphasis upon the analysis of the risk did not seem to be suitable for tender evaluations. Tenders could be analysed but how could the differences in their identi® ed risk be used to balance the differences in their value or in their price? The standards and booklets available cover what should be done to analyse risk but do not show how the analysis could be carried out in tender conditions or how the risks could be remedied in a fair and equitable way, as demanded by tendering probity.

In addition, most descriptions of the process give a detailed treatment of the analysis of the risk and an almost cursory mention of developing \`mitigation plans’ or other remedies for the risks (see e.g. Department of Public Works and Services, NSW Government, 1996; Powell and Klein, 1996). There is not enough attention paid to the costs of the risks and of the possible remedies as the basis for comparing options.

Accordingly, I wanted to make use of the riskoriented approach but give more consideration to the remedy of the risk and use the differences in riskrelated costs to evaluate the tenders.

## Description of the risk± remedy method

## Overview of the method

The risk± remedy method has been developed to meet the needs of evaluating outsourcing tenders in the APS. The method was designed to be ¯ exible for the different scope and scale of tenders and understandable to senior decision makers, yet compliant with the axioms or assumptions of sound selection theory. In essence, the risk± remedy method determines which tender has the least cost for achieving the \`ideal’ .

One of the major aspects of the risk± remedy method is the concept of \`risk’ that underlies the approach taken. Here risk is regarded as being more than the \`likelihood of unfortunate circumstances’, with treatment of risk having a negative outcome for stakeholders (such as in Willcocks and Margetts, 1994; Charette, 1996). I prefer broader and more positive de® nitions of risk, such as \`the chance of something happening that will have an impact upon objectives’ (Standards Australia, 1995) or the \`uncertainty of attaining a standard or threshold (US Navy, 1997). Risk is not something going wrong, of disaster or doom. The impact does not have to be fatal to the objectives, it could be just a deviation from the ideal, where the ideal could be to take up a future opportunity as much as to be perfect in the present. Risk is a plausible or possible shortfall in achieving the level of performance required now or in the future.

As pointed out by Charette (1996), if there is a certain shortfall in performance then we have a \`problem’. It is the presence of the likelihood of a shortfall that brings risk into the picture. Another major aspect is that, whereas most assessment processes look for what is right with each bid, in terms of how well they meet the criteria, the risk± remedy method looks for what is not right. It looks for what is not there ± for a lack of compliance and for the chance that the solution will not give us what is needed.

There is no thought in this method of taking the \`best of a bad lot’ in a passive selection process. Through risk-removal negotiations and other remedial actions, every attempt is made to move the options towards the ideal.

## Steps for carrying out the risk± remedy method

In brief, using the risk± remedy method to evaluate outsourcing tenders requires the following steps.

Figure 1 Diagrammatic representation of the risk± remedy process

(1) Before bids are received, technical specialists from the agencies establish the \`watch points’ that serve as indicators of risk for the tenders. Watch points are precise statements of a level of performance, preferably anchored around concrete evidence, derived from the criteria or \`values’ that represent the business needs of the organization. The watch points come from a systematic consideration of the risk drivers (similar to the framework suggested by Willcocks and Margetts (1994)), such as current and future external conditions, internal circumstances and the desires of stakeholders for inputs, processes, outputs and controls. The technical specialists pay particular attention to critical watch points called \`red cards’, where a chance of any shortfall in the level of performance leads to a \`professional foul’ warranting immediate remediation. Other watch points can be \`yellow cards’, where a large deviation or a series of deviations can call for action in determining remedies or attempts to remove the risk.

(2) After the bids are received in accordance with the conditions of tendering, technical specialists review the descriptions of the proposed solution and ® nancial specialists separately review the price schedules for the proposal.

The technical specialists consider how well each bid meets the watch points, looking out for risks (shortfalls in performance). They made their assessments using a simple word scale of one dimension only ± the plausibility that the bid will meet the stated watch point. The scale is \`will, should, might, might not, should not and will not’. The scale is designed to be equally spread over a ten-point scale (will = 1.0, should = 0.8, . . . will not = 0.0) and have the same meaning to most assessors, regardless of their nationality (it avoids the problem of \`quite’ , for example, which bewilders US versus UK judges). It is a qualitative scale that is meaningful and without a mid-point to lure those who wish to take the middle path too often. The assessors place the appropriate wordscale in front of the watch point, in effect describing how well they believe the bid performs, knowing that anything scaled below \`should’ for a red card will trigger remedial action.

(4) The technical specialists attempt to remove any uncertainty in their assessments through a series of clari® cation questions to the bidders. There is constant recycling between team and bidders until all uncertainty about the requirements (from us to them) and nature of the bid (from them to us) is reduced as much as possible. Due diligence and assumptionstesting sessions are used to ensure that the evaluations are based upon complete, accurate and consistent information.

(5) At the same time, the ® nancial specialists determine the price to be paid for each bid over the life of the contract and transition costs such as competitive neutrality or redundancy payments, suing the \`in-house’ option as a baseline and different scenarios of workload in a sensitivity analysis.

(6) Then the technical and ® nancial specialists work together to determine the nett present value of the expected costs of identi® ed risks. These costs are determined according to estimates of the impact upon the agency, such as loss of productive time by end users. These costs are adjusted by the plausibility estimate given in step 3 to give the expected cost.

7) The negotiation team, including the technical specialists and appropriately skilled negotiators, discuss the risks identi® ed with the bidders, to see whether the bidders can remedy the risks.

The bidder can (a) decide not to do anything, realizing that the cost will be added to the quoted price for the services, (b) accept the risk, by stating the shortfall will be met at its expense or (c) proffer an alternative to the bid, for a change in the price less than the cost of the shortfall. They can negotiate different levels of performance, to joint satisfaction, that are achievable by the bidders at no additional cost. Alternatively, they can determine other actions to be undertaken by the agency at its expense (for example, educational campaigns or additional contract management staff) that will remedy the risk for a cost less than that of the shortfall.

(8) The evaluation team calculates risk-adjusted costs by summing the lesser of the expected cost of each risk or the cost of the remedies identi® ed for the risk. This ® gure can be reduced by \`value-added services’ or adjustments to the cost that re¯ ect the value to the agency of offers that \`exceed requirements’. The timing of the risks is taken into account as well. As the risk adjustments can be treated though nett present value to re¯ ect when they occur, thus it is possible to adjust for the effects of time when dealing with these risks.

(9) Once these clari® cation and negotiation sessions are over, the evaluator completes the comparison table shown in Figure 1, with the quoted prices on line 1, the transitions costs on line 2 and the risk-adjusted costs on line 3.

(10) The evaluator calculates the evaluation total (prices plus risk costs) and subtracts the cheapest of these totals from the results for all bids (lines 4 and 5), so common errors are cancelled as much as possible.

(11) All of the team members work in a group to describe any risks that cannot be costed (for practical or political reasons) in a risk picture giving the impact upon the business (bottom lines in the comparison report). Various group processes complement the assessments made by individual experts. These teams can use group decision support systems, such as the Grouputer which is described in Lewis and Newton (1995) or simple group processes to \`pool’ their assessments. The group work is designed to expose the biases and to ensure that all issues are aired, noted and remedied. It is hard to maintain a bias towards a solution if your assessment has to be explained to peers working in the same area.

(12) The evaluation committee determines the most acceptable bid, considering whether the relative costs offset the differences in the risk picture. The ® nal decision belongs to the senior managers on the evaluation committee. They are given the comparison report and the voluminous supporting detail so they can balance price and risk. This balance between risks is made according to their perceptions of important political factors beyond the scope of the evaluation teams. The members of the committee make the decision and they become committed to its success.

(13) Later the contract manager uses the risk picture for further negotiation with the selected tenderer to ensure the accepted bid is managed with as low a risk as possible.

## Experience with the method

## Use of the method

The risk± remedy method has been used with six evaluations carried out by ® ve Federal Government agencies seeking to outsource their IT.

Table 1 shows the characteristics of ® ve of these evaluations in general terms, to protect any sensitivities associated with the projects.

Others have used a reduced variant of the method in evaluations for at least two other IT outsourcing projects.

These evaluation projects were similar in their intent: the selection of a supplier of IT products and services at a required level of quality for the cheapest price. All of the agencies were interested in a long-term contract, usually 5 years with options for extension. Most of the projects involved the supply of all of the IT infrastructure, although two of them went beyond to the supply of applications development.

In no case did the agency carry out any preliminary determination of whether more selective sourcing should be used, primarily because they were obeying a government dictate to \`market test’ the fullest extent of service. The basis of the selection was cost in most cases, with industry development being of predominant concern in some projects.

In no case did the agency have any in-house expertise in evaluation. Throughout the APS there is little such experience; usually one tender is the most that the agency members undertake. It is external consultants such as myself that provide the corporate knowledge for considerable parts of the APS.

On a more worrying note, in several cases the contract manager did not take part in the evaluation or negotiation of the bids. This lack of exposure to the intent of the bid and the relationship with the bidder means that the contract manager is vulnerable to misunderstanding the intent of the contract and misusing the relationship between supplier and agency. Although most contract managers that have been appointed to that position have done well, this arrangement has been a matter of luck.

## Lessons learnt from the use of the risk± remedy method

## Performance of the method

The risk± remedy method speeds the evaluation process. Some of the projects for which the method has been used have been completed in less than 9 months from the decision to consider the use of outsourcing to the signing of the contract. This period is noticeably less than that observed using conventional evaluation methods (for example, the evaluation time required for agency D was less than half the time taken for another agency working on a simpler but more costly tender using the Select!Gain scoring technique). The time reduction was achieved by being able to concentrate upon ® nding the shortfalls for a few measures, as generally only approximately 20 risks are identi® ed from over 200 measures. The use of group decision processes to form a consensus of judgements also seems to speed the evaluation.

The method leads to quicker contract negotiation. The risk± remedy process makes the implications of non-compliance often painfully obvious to the bidders. I have found that they will not agree to a clause until the implications for the adjustment to their price are shown to them. They can challenge the size of the adjustment, but when they see that it was calculated systematically and their opposition is based only upon abstract argument they almost always agree to absorb or remedy the risk rather than hold ® rm on negotiating points.

The method leads to more acceptable results. The evaluation committees for the projects were happier with the process, without any of the doubts expressed when weighted scoring processes were used.

## Remedies to the risks in the evaluation of outsourcing

Many risks can arise when evaluating IT outsourcing. These risks can be regarded as risks of management, process and content. Without being self-referential, the following description of risks is limited to those that can be remedied through the use of the risk± remedy method. There are other risks but their treatment will have to wait for a further paper.

## Use the correct requirements

An evaluation is only as good as the description of the requirements or values given in the request for tender (RFT). Unfortunately, there have been some examples of very poor descriptions of requirements. Once I even found one organization removing another organization’s name from an RFT and replacing it with their own, in order to \`save time’ in the issue of the

RFT even though the requirements were not the same. The evaluation process could be speeded up, at the likely risk of obtaining a good solution to someone else’s need.

The causes of the dif® culties in preparing a complete list of properly expressed requirements are almost always lack of time and lack of skill. Requirements can become dated if the preparation of the RFT and the evaluation takes too long, such as the 18 months that have been taken for some projects.

The risk± remedy method requires the evaluators to build values or requirements ± the \`ideal’ ± for the life of the project and to consider the plausibility that the bids will achieve the ideal throughout this life. The evaluators bring the requirements up to date before they are published in an RFT. The values can take account of scenarios of possible changes in business needs or in technology so that there are few surprises in the response of the bidders to the RFT, although I have not found many agencies willing to spend the time to develop scenarios fully.

The evaluators work with the negotiators to ensure that performance against the requirements is the basis for contract negotiation and for service levels in contract management.

I have seen evaluations that mishandled bids that \`exceed requirements’. In these cases, evaluators expanded the scales to make room for the extra \`value’ , thus ending up weighting the requirements incorrectly. The risk± remedy method looks at shortfalls against the ideal, which can be easily adjusted to take account of the extra performance offered by bidders and so it does not have to adjust scales as the evaluation proceeds.

## Use the requirements correctly

The use of \`mandatory’ requirements is one of the greatest dangers of evaluation. There was one agency ± not using the risk± remedy method ± that speci® ed over 300 mandatory requirements, including the nature of the processor to be provided. No bid could comply, so the evaluators had to roll back the mandatories until they had some compliant bids. This adjustment violates probity, as other ® rms might have bid if they had known that the mandatories were not really mandatory after all.

The risk± remedy method does not use mandatory values as such. The costs of falling short in such essential areas should be considerable and can be used to ® lter out options without having to alter the de® nition of the value.

Many RFTs in the Australian public sector still use technical speci® cations rather than functional requirements to describe what they want from the bidder. Some RFTs contain lists of tasks for the potential contractor rather than a description of the business need to be met in the way that the bidder regards as being best. Other RFTs describe a particular PC that is to be provided rather than the capability and capacity to support the end users. Accordingly, the agency preparing this type of RFT is accepting responsibility for the performance of the contractor, often unwittingly and is limiting the freedom of the bidder to use its expertise to provide the best solution.

The risk± remedy method tries to concentrate upon the business outcomes of the bids. The determination of the costs of the risks is related to the business impact of the proffered solutions. There are times when the agency has still insisted on using technical speci® cations within the risk± remedy process but the method can overcome the limitations to an extent by looking through the expression of the RFT to the ultimate quality of the bids.

## Consider the cost of bidding

A large outsourcing RFT can cost over \$1 million in direct costs to prepare and evaluate and the same for each bidder in preparing the tender. These costs intimidate potential bidders and so the potential best bid could be stillborn. There have been examples of tenders being received from only one bidder, as the competition had assessed their likelihood of winning being below the threshold that justi® ed the cost of bidding. This project did not proceed but the relevant minister has promised that the market test will be repeated under different arrangements later and \`this time outsourcing will occur’.

The risk± remedy method eases the evaluation process. If attention is concentrated upon only the risks that make a difference, the effort taken to prepare responses and to evaluate them can be reduced. The approach taken in negotiation of putting the cards on the table removes some of the disputes and delays in negotiation.

## Consider the cost of delays

A tender that is worth \$1 million a year, with an added value of at least 10% (the minimum to justify tendering), has an opportunity cost of \$9200 for each delay of 1 month. Thus, unnecessary delays from riskaverse steering committees can cost millions. For example, some tender evaluations of systems worth hundreds of millions of dollars have taken over 18 months, costing at least \$15 million in opportunity costs. For at least one project, further costs were incurred because the system, once delivered, was out of date and had to be upgraded in the ® eld. Of course, this cost has to be balanced with the cost of a \`bad’ decision, brought about by hasty or incomplete considerations.

As described earlier, the risk-remedy method speeds both the evaluation and the negotiation process. If it reduces the time to commencement of the contracted service by 8 weeks, which is possible, then the typical saving for the agencies using the risk± remedy method is over \$900 000 for savings from outsourcing claimed to be 25% on projects worth \$120 million over 5 years.

## Check the bids carefully

There are examples of bidders providing wrong information in their bids. They could be wrong because they have misread the RFT, miscalculated the required workloads or even misused the spreadsheet used to calculate their pricing. I have seen tenders that used yearly costs instead of monthly or typed 6000 instead of 2000 for the number of PCs to be supported. Almost every spreadsheet provided by a bidder has followed Panko’s (1997) ® ndings of 20± 40% of spreadsheets having logical or data errors. On the other hand, there have been evaluations that needed 100 subsidiary spreadsheets to explore all of the costs provided by the bidders. Accordingly, there is a need to ensure that information provided by the bidders is correct and that it is treated correctly.

A related risk is the failure of bidders to respond consistently to the RFT. They offer different solutions, with different capabilities, at different prices. What can be worse, there have been a number of RFTs that have asked for major sections of the bid to be negotiated after contract signature. It is impossible to evaluate these bids consistently.

The risk± remedy method encourages the checking of bidder information. The costs of the risks, including those identi® ed as a result of misunderstandings (by both parties), are explored fully with the bidders. They are given an opportunity to see the consequences of their bid continuing as expressed and to take action to remedy the mistakes.

## Use a design not a selection approach

The intent of any evaluation process should be to obtain the best solution for the organization rather than take the \`best of a bad lot’. The traditional evaluation of outsourcers tends to result in the selection of the bid with the highest \`score’ or \`value for money’, with no gaps in the solution that will perhaps be negotiated or managed later. The negotiation process is meant to close these gaps but, when the bidder knows that there is little room in which the agency can move, then the gaps can remain. There are a number of cases where the agency has declared the bidder to be \`preferred’ before the negotiations have begun. For some reason, these agencies seemed to be surprised when it became dif® cult to exert any leverage over the bidder.

The risk± remedy method adds the costs of the gap on to the price of the bid. If the bidder does not want to remove the shortfall then it may lose the business. This threat of being \`hanged’ does concentrate the mind wonderfully. The use of the method has led to the agency gaining contracts that are very close to their ideal.

## Conclusion

There is an increased demand in the public sector in Australia for the consideration of outsourcing of IT infrastructure. This increase in outsourcing is not being matched with an increase in the ef® ciency of evaluating outsourcing tenders. The risk± remedy method, with its emphasis upon rapidly determining which bid is the most ef® cient way of providing the ideal solution, is capable of meeting this demand.

There are many risks in evaluating bids for outsourcing IT. They include (1) shortfalls in establishing the right requirements leading to incorrect selection, (2) mishandling mandatory requirements leading to improper tendering, (3) depending upon technical speci® cations leading to contract dif® culties, (4) overexpensive bidding leading to restricted competition, (5) incorrect information leading to delays or incorrect decisions, (6) over-long evaluation leading to considerable opportunity costs and (7) selection on the basis of scores leading to less than ideal solutions. The risk± remedy method can remedy all of these risks.
