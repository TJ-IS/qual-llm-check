# An empirical study of the use of EDI in supermarket chains using a new conceptual framework

COLM FEARON AND GEORGE PHILIP

Management and Information Systems Division, Queen’s School of Management, The Queen’ s University of Belfast, Belfast BT7 1NN, UK

Electronic Data Interchange (EDI) is now widely established as an interorganizational system in almost all large and medium size industrial, service and retail sectors and the purpose of this paper is to discuss the experiences of six supermarket chains concerning the use of this technology. Whilst the advantages of conducting electronic transactions are generally recognized, consensus on methodologies for quantifying the bene® t success associated with interorganizational systems still remain elusive. It is argued that a comparison of pre-implementation expected bene® ts with post-implementation realized bene® ts can offer a reliable way of assessing the bene® t success from EDI and other information systems. In this approach, the bene® t success is expressed as a function of three bene® t states namely, ef® ciency, neutrality and de® ciency depending on whether the gap between realized and expected bene® ts is positive, neutral or negative respectively. In orde to identify the bene® t state of each participating organization, initially a series of semistructured interviews was conducted with senior managers followed by the administration of a self assessment rating instrument in the form of a questionnaire. This paper will discuss the ® ndings along with the testing of a new conceptual framework for examining the relationship between bene® t success, implementation approach and implementation success.

## Introduction

During the late 1990s, the supermarket retail industry throughout Ireland has been experiencing a number of structural changes associated with supply chain and customer interface management. These changes have been predominantly market driven by the acquisition of major household names, such as, Quinnsworth, Stewarts Group, Wellworths by larger multiples such as Tesco, Supervalu and Centra. There has also been expansion by other UK based multiples, namely, Sainsbury’s, Marks and Spencer and Safeway.

EDI has enabled both buyers (retailers, and distributors with upstream suppliers) and sellers (suppliers, and distributors with downstream retailers) to strengthen collaborative arrangements that ensure business survival and possible competitive advantage within different industry sectors (Johnston and Carrico, 1988; Keen, 1991; Cunningham and Tynan, 1993; Moss Kanter, 1994). Modern trading practices have re¯ ected upon the need for buyers to view their sellers as an integral part of their extended enterprise which spans over the whole value chain of corporate activities (Konsynski, 1993; van Heck and Ribbers, 1997).

An important role for interorganizational systems technologies (IOS), such as EDI, is to enable a consolidation of strategic IS bene® ts which strengthen long term partnerships between key buyers and sellers (see Cunningham and Tynan, 1993; Mackay, 1993; Riggins and Mukhopadhyay, 1994; Webster, 1995). For example, certain authors argue that if strategic bene® ts from EDI enabled inter® rm linkage are high, then supply chains can operate much more effectively over the long term, thus, conferring a sense of competitive advantage over supply chains that are not as well integrated (Clemons and Row, 1991; Bakos and Brynjolfsson, 1993; Clemons et al., 1993).

However, the extent to which these collaborative relationships are truly evident and the extent of strategic bene® ts enabled by EDI have been brought into question (Bergeron and Raymond, 1992; Cox and Ghoneim, 1996). Of particular signi® cance, is the different objectives of buyers and sellers in the supply chain which may inhibit a high level of EDI enabled inter® rm integration (Clemons and Row, 1993). Certainly recent evidence in Ireland and the UK would suggest that the ability to derive inter® rm strategic type bene® ts from EDI is low (Fynnes and Ennis, 1993; Reekers and Smithson, 1994; Cox and Ghoneim, 1996; Philip and Pedersen, 1997).

Severe doubt has also been cast over original claims of the sustainability of competitive advantage from IOS technologies such as EDI (Clemons and Row 1991; Senn, 1992; Galliers, 1993a; Booth and Philip, 1996; Philip and Booth 1998). Principal arguments stem from the fact that IOS technologies are easily duplicated, so any sense of competitiveness diminishes quickly as use of the IOS eventually becomes an industry norm (Clemons and Row, 1991; Senn, 1992; Booth and Philip, 1996; Philip and Booth, 1998). High realized bene® t outcomes achieved are not the result of introducing IOS technology per se, rather the consequence of integrating IOS with internal organizational change strategies and external supply chain initiatives (Senn, 1992; Venkatraman, 1994; Kettinger et al., 1997; Fearon and Philip, 1998).

It is against the above background that this paper aims to evaluate the performance of EDI and related interorganizational systems (IOS) initiatives from the perspective of retailers, distributors and suppliers. More speci® cally, there are two strands to this paper: ® rst, it sets out to investigate the nature of the bene® t success that buyers and sellers are receiving from EDI. This is achieved by synergizing two measures of bene® t success, the \`realized bene® t outcome’ and the \`bene® t state outcome’ (Fearon and Philip, 1998). Secondly, the alignment between EDI \`bene® t success’, \`implementation success’ (technical and business issues encountered during implementation) and \`IS development approaches’ (incremental/radical views of overall EDI planning) is examined using a conceptual framework ± the IS bene-® t success matrix (see forward, Figure 6). The holistic framework illustrates that a high realized bene® t outcome may enable a sustainable level of competitive advantage, only when companies are extremely innovative and are able to assimilate IOS/IS within their organizational strategies and supply chain networks.

## The degree of bene® t success from EDI

The impetus for the present study came from a number of sources. Generally, it is acknowledged that many IT projects are currently failing to deliver promised business bene® ts, despite a heavy IS investment (Willcocks, 1992; Booth and Philip, 1996; Grover et al., 1998). Most notably, there has also been a sense of hype concerning the potential of new technology in delivering business bene® ts (Holmes and Poulymenakou, 1996; Ward et al., 1996). There is also evidence to suggest that unrealistic expectations (Senn, 1992; Szajna and Scamell, 1993; Ward et al., 1996) and underestimated implementation capabilities have contributed to low overall levels of realized bene® t from EDI and other IS projects (Fearon and Philip, 1998). Another major concern, is the inability to tangibly quantify bene® t expectations before adopting an IS project, with no formal mechanism currently existing which identi® es best practice for achieving expected bene® ts (Massetti and Zmud, 1996; Philip and Pedersen, 1997).

Most existing EDI and IS evaluative criteria are, unfortunately, technical in nature and assume that successful technical implementation alone will be suf® - cient to ensure the realization of a large number of IS enabled business bene® ts (Galliers et al., 1995; Ward et al., 1996). In summary, there appears to be an absence of a tactical understanding of how the theoretical bene® ts from EDI in particular, can be fully operationalized in practice (Massetti and Zmud, 1996).

This tactical understanding is conceptualized by comparing and aligning the EDI performance criteria most often discussed in the information systems planning literature, (i.e. bene® t success, implementation success, approaches to EDI/IS planning and IOS enabled competitive advantage), through the application of diffusion of innovation theory.

## The need for IS planning and alignment

The need for strategic \`® t’ or alignment between IT objectives and business planning has been well documented (Lederer and Mendelow, 1987, 1993; Galliers, 1993b; Reich and Benbasat, 1996). However, actually being able to translate this into practice is proving dif-® cult (Burn, 1996). For example, Macmillan (1997) raises the point that relating IS to classical views of strategy formulation has inherent weaknesses. The classical view of IS planning advocates formulating a clear overall vision, translating this into clear objectives and performance measures which are the benchmark for new action plans and IS priorities. Typical problems for IS planners when using these models include: (a) getting bogged down in too much detail; (b) planner overoptimism; (c) plans not relating to practice; (d) inability to integrate various stakeholder viewpoints within the planning process; (e) not being able to align business and IS plans; (f) poor bene® t measurement capability; (g) dif® culty coping with the rate of change, whereby, plans have a such short lifecycle before becoming redundant anyway (Lederer and Sethi, 1992; Galliers, 1993a; Galliers et al., 1995).

Macmillan (1997) argues that whilst classical strategic planning models may clarify thinking, they are nevertheless, mostly theoretically elegant entities. The general criticism from Macmillan and other authors, is that it is dangerous to build IS strategy upon imitating these normative business planning models (Booth and Philip, 1996), when in fact these have come under severe criticism in recent years (Minzberg, 1987, 1994a, 1994b; Hamel and Prahalad, 1994).

However, there is still a strong need for planning IS development programmes in today’s complex business environment (Lederer and Sethi, 1988, 1992; Galliers et al., 1995). Macmillan (1997) argues, that building an IS strategy must not be based upon aligning aloof management and IS objectives through abstract theoretica models. Rather, the IS planning mechanisms used,

Therefore, based upon the above ideas, an alignment process was devised for evaluating the performance of EDI in the retail sector. In doing so, this paper recognizes the existence of four main EDI or IS performance variables, whose signi® cance are much debated within EDI/IOS literature:

(a) bene® t success (a synergy of \`the realized bene® t outcome’ and the \`bene® t state outcome’).

(b) implementation success (measuring technical/ diffusion attributes).

(c) IS development approach (comparing incremental/enterprise wide scale initiatives).

(d) a sense of EDI enabled competitiveness.

In line with previous research, we recognize that accurately evaluating IS performance in bene® t or competitive terms is an extremely complicated issue (Sethi and King, 1994; Willcocks, 1994; Smithson and Hirschheim, 1998) and at best it may only be possible to grasp a vague sense of overall IS performance.

However, we suggest that one way to evaluate this generic sense of IT performance is to attempt to isolate and measure relevant qualitative (subjective/perceptual) and quantitative (objective/factual) variables which typically affect IS development. Thereafter, these constituent variables may be cross-compared, illustrating their degree of relative alignment, as well as providing a holistic and conceptual view of EDI performance within the sample of organizations studied. In this way, even the more intangible strategic IS bene® ts and their signi® cance can be accounted for during IT evaluation (Farbey et al., 1995; Ward et al., 1996).

## Developing a more holistic view of bene® t success from IS

Sethi and King (1994) term IT research that evaluates competitive ef® ciency, business value and management productivity, achieved in conjunction with measurement of competitive advantage as \`outcome’ approaches. Fearon and Philip (1998) advocate that in order to understand the true nature of bene® t outcomes from IOS research, a synergistic measure of bene® t success must be developed.

This synergy is developed by integrating IS bene® t outcomes along two dimensions: (a) the realized bene® t outcome ± or, the actual number of realized strategic and operational bene® ts a company has achieved, regardless of initial expectations, as well as; (b) the bene® t state outcome ± or, extent of bene® t planning gap experienced within each company relative to the original expectations (see Figures 1 and 2).

It is the realized bene® t outcome which is probably the more common dimension that EDI and IS researchers and practitioners have concentrated upon in the past (see Banerjee and Golhar, 1994; Reekers and Smithson, 1994; Mukhopadhyay et al., 1995; Philip and Pedersen, 1997). The realized bene® t outcome measure has also been used by researchers to develop and understand EDI development patterns associated with diffusion of innovation theory (see O’Callaghan et al., 1992; Pfeiffer, 1992; Premkumar et al., 1994; Iacovou et al., 1995). For example, high realized levels of operational and strategic bene® ts, including competitive rewards are normally associated with a high level of internal and external diffusion of EDI (Williams, 1993; Premkumar et al., 1994; Swatman et al., 1994; Drury and Farhoomand, 1996).

In recent years, however, there has also been an increasing interest shown in a second dimension of bene® t success, concerning comparisons of realized bene® ts with original expectations. For example, Reekers and Smithson (1994) found marked differences between perceptions of motivating and realized EDI bene® ts in the UK and Germany, the two most mature EDI user countries in Europe. Cox and Ghoneim (1996) also noted the contradiction between pre-planning expectations and post-implementation outcomes concerning barriers to business process changes and EDI systems integration. When investigating the performance of customer based interorganizational systems, Grover and Teng (1994) labelled the \`extent of implementation’ as the difference between current bene® t realization and original objectives.

A possible reason for this interest in retrospection may be a general dissatisfaction with the ability of many companies to achieve the level of business bene® ts from IS which were initially expected (Ward et al., 1996). Therefore, researchers using the \`realized bene® t outcome’ alone as the primary indicator of bene® t success from IS applications cannot comment upon their success, as a measure of original expectations. Whilst the \`realized bene® t outcome’ provides a useful snapshot of IS performance, in terms of measuring a perceived level of bene® t \`output’ , it is the \`bene® t state outcome’ which effectively benchmarks the success of IS planning and decision-making processess that lead to that ® nal bene-

® t output.

Although, the above authors (Grover and Teng, 1994; Reekers and Smithson, 1994; Cox and Ghoneim, 1996) have attempted to quantify surrogate degrees of IS performance success, relative to intial expectations, no emergent frameworks have been developed to understand their interplay within the context of EDI. This is despite ever increasing calls for more holistic IOS analytical approaches, that bring together the various IS opportunity, planning and evaluation frameworks (Earl, 1989; Galliers, 1993b).

## De® ning a second bene® t success dimension ± The bene® t state outcome

The bene® t state outcome of any company can be de® ned as the extent of the planning gap, between preimplementation bene® t expectations and post-implementation perceptions of realized bene® ts (see Figures 2 and 3). If the bene® t planning gap (BPG) is negative then the company is diagnosed as experiencing a state of \`de® ciency’. If there is no bene® t planning gap and a company realizes the bene® ts it expected then the bene® t state diagnosis is termed \`neutrality’ . If the perceived realized bene® t was greater than original bene® t expectation, then the bene® t state diagnosis is considered to be \`ef® ciency’ (Fearon and Philip, 1998).

On their own, these bene® t state outcomes do not exhibit any theoretical or practical value. However, as the alignment process begins, the instrumental validity of the bene® t success perceptual measure increases. Furthermore, a clearer conceptual view of their interdependence emerges when aligned with: implementation success and the EDI/IS planning approach. The combined effect, increases the understanding of the circumstances leading to a sense of IOS enabled competitiveness (Fearon and Philip, 1998).

An immediate step is the synergy of the two dimensions of bene® t success:

## Rationale for comparing perceptual with objective performance measures

Both the above construct (see Figure 3), and the interpretation of each bene® t state (see Figure 2) are arguably very subjective measures of EDI performance, due in part, to their over reliance upon selfassessed perceptual ratings, but also related dif® culties associated with quanti® cation of intangible IS bene® ts (Jones and Beatty, 1998; Smithson and Hirschheim, 1998).

With these points in mind, some form of validation is required if our preliminary diagnoses are to be trusted. Therefore our synergized concept of bene® t success is quanti® ed by providing a comparison with the more traditional and objective \`symptoms’ of EDI performance found within the literature, principally, the level of \`implementation success’ (see Hwang, 1991; Pfeiffer, 1992; Williams, 1993; McGowan, 1994; Swatman et al., 1994; Galliers et al., 1995).

We have therefore interpreted EDI implementation success (see forward, Table 3) as the extent of:

external diffusion (approximate percentage of trading partners that are currently using EDI on a regular basis).

internal diffusion (breadth and depth of EDI transactions used by the organization on a daily basis; also, the extent to which business/ e-commerce initiatives use EDI).

technical and organizational problems which inhibit external and internal diffusion.

The presence of organizational and technical problems has been shown to inhibit EDI performance (Scala and McGrath, 1993; Swatman et al., 1994; Galliers et al., 1995), thus effectively raising barriers to successful EDI implementations and wider business processing re-engineering initiatives (hence inhibiting internal diffusion) associated with EDI (Cox and Ghoneim, 1996).

## Diffusion model of EDI development

We have adopted Williams (1993) basic diffusion model of EDI development. This has the advantage of integrating the more traditional stages associated with reaching internal IS diffusion i.e. acceptance, routinization and infusion (see Cooper and Zmud, 1990; McGowan, 1994; Rogers, 1995) with the concept of expansion and enhancement (Williams, 1993), which speci® cally caters for the external diffusion element associated with EDI and interorganizational trading partner linkages (see Table 1).

The association between a high realized bene® t outcome and high implementation success has been widely demonstrated in IT and EDI innovation diffusion literature (see Cooper and Zmud, 1990; McGowan, 1994; Swanson, 1994; Rogers 1995). Therefore, it would appear sensible to compare the characteristics of our synergized measure of bene® t success (i.e. both the realized bene® t outcome and bene® t state diagnoses) with implementation success (the level of external and internal diffusion achieved). In this way, any fresh understanding of bene® t success can be analysed in the light of a credible body of established EDI research. Figure 4, illustrates key emerging alignment relationships which are developing within an embryonic IOS bene® t success matrix structure. (See forward, Figure 6, for the ® nal version of the bene® t success matrix.)

It is important to note that \`Expansion’ refers to the managerial decision to add new trading partners to the existing EDI architecture (greater external diffusion). This action can be viewed as both a \`below the line of integration’ or \`above the line of integration’ decision, depending on whether the organization wants to integrate existing EDI applications with wider supply chain initiatives (e.g. Ef® cient Consumer Response, ECR) and interface better with internal applications (thus, supporting greater internal diffusion). The economic decision to add new trading partners (expansion) to an existing EDI architecture which is already over burdened represents a major stimulus for upgrading the entire EDI infrastructure and aiming for better internal integration in the process.

## Methodology

As part of a much wider research programme on the organizational role of information systems (Philip et al., 1995; Fearon and Philip, 1998; Philip and Booth, 1998), this study presents the ® ndings from six large EDI buyers and sellers in the supermarket retail sector in Ireland. It made use of both personal interviews and recorded telephone interviews in conjunction with a self assessed rating instrument, administered through a questionnaire (see Appendix). The interpretative approach used not only allows researchers the ability to investigate the bare facts, but also informant behaviours and their subjective meanings within a social, or in our case, a business context (Lee, 1989; Gable, 1994). It allows the opportunity to ask meaningful questions, in order to elicit a rich content of informant responses, thereby, increasing the general level of understanding of

## Contribution of EDI to the bene® t success of the retail sector

The extent of EDI’s overall contribution to bene® t success was measured during two stages.

In stage one, a preliminary diagnosis or interpretation of the perceptual measures was made, based upon analysing the self-assessed rating data (see Appendix) and interview discourse. This preliminary diagnosis determined the appropriate bene® t states for each company relative to their own individual trading contexts.

During stage two, the preliminary diagnosis was cross compared with the more objective \`symptoms’ of implementation success and IS planning, thus determining a \`con® rmatory diagnosis’. This involved analysing the more objective measures of EDI performance (see Table 3), namely: (a) number of years using EDI; (b) the types of business transactions performed; (b) approximated current EDI expenditures; (c) the frequency with which they were transacted on a regular basis; (d) percentage of trading partners using EDI. The interpretation of these objective measures established an implementation success contribution, in terms of a stage on the EDI diffusion model mentioned earlier (see Table 1).

The stage of EDI diffusion interpreted for each company was then further aligned with the approaches to EDI/IS planning. This indicated whether EDI was perceived as an incremental development, or part of a more enterprise-wide change programme, within each organization and its supply chain networks. The con® rmatory diagnosis (see Table 3), represented a sum of al of these constituent parts, thus attempting to increase construct validity and robustness of results. This was achieved by integrating the perceptual and objective measures of EDI performance, in order to determine a better understanding of the contribution of EDI/IS development for each company’s trading circumstances (Farbey et al., 1995; Smithson and Hirschheim, 1998).

Perhaps the most signi® cant element of all of this, is the conceptualization of preliminary and con® rmatory diagnoses on the bene® t success matrix (see Figure 6). The discussion of the bene® t success matrix uses both sample data from the companies and theoretical relationships explained earlier, in order to illustrate the usefulness of this holistic evaluation approach.

The medical terms used within the evaluations; \`symptoms’, \`preliminary’, and \`con® rmatory diagnosis etc., signify the importance to the researchers of building instrumental and internal validity of results, through a process of checking and cross comparison. It is also hoped that the measures and techniques used in this study can be incorporated by practitioners, as Macmillan (1997) argued, in order to be able to assimilate relatively simple planning mechanisms which are of real use to IS developers.

## EDI and a sense of competitive advantage

Seeking a planned sense of competitive advantage over other industry rivals by adopting EDI, simply did not occur, as EDI trading represented the wider industry norm. The reasons for adopting EDI varied between the companies in the sample, depending upon their position as buyer or seller within the retail supply chain. Originally starting as suppliers, Company 6 noted; \`Like most companies we originally adopted EDI because we were pushed by our customers’. For some companies, a sense of IOS enabled competitiveness was interpreted when the realized bene® t outcome began to steadily increase over time. Even then, competitive advantage over rivals, by virtue of having an EDI technology capability was not an expected, nor a sustainable outcome. Rather, the true sense of competitiveness related to strategic bene® ts derived from greater effectiveness of co-ordinating transactions of goods, services and information between key trading partners. Unfortunately, for most of the companies there is still a way to go before enhancement is achieved, coupled with that sustained sense of competitiveness, derived from true co-operation and collaboration (Cunningham and Tynan, 1993; Moss Kanter, 1994; Webster, 1995).

## Conceptualizing the degree of alignment: the bene® t success matrix (see ® gure 6)

The following conceptualizes both the preliminary and con® rmatory diagnoses (Tables 2 and 3) via the EDI bene® t success matrix. The matrix is intended to bring together various strands of IS research in an attempt to chart possible routes to EDI maturity from a multidimensional perspective. The matrix illustrates EDI development in the form of possible migration routes on the matrix from left to right relative to the performance variables discussed.

It is hoped that this multidimensional perspective can offer some clarity, in terms of determining a relative ® t between the various IT performance criteria (see Figures 1, 3 and 4. The matrix is not deterministic; it does not assume that a high realized bene® t outcome constitutes ultimate bene® t success for every company (Fearon and Philip, 1998). Rather, it should illustrate why it is so dif® cult to achieve a high realized bene® t outcome associated with inter® rm integration and sustainable IOS enabled competitiveness (Bakos and Brynjolfsson, 1993; Clemons and Row, 1993; Clemons et al., 1993).

It should also demonstrate the real need for considering a second dimension when evaluating perceptions of IS bene® t success (post implementation reviews which compare realized and expected bene® ts). The complexity of IOS technology is such that unidimensional measures alone (i.e. the realized bene® t outcome), cannot suf® ciently explain why anticipated levels of strategic bene® ts have not occurred in countries where EDI usage is supposedly mature (Reekers and Smithson, 1994; Cox and Ghoneim, 1996).

## Characteristics of the EDI/IOS bene® t success matrix

The matrix is divided into left and right sides: the higher the positions on the left and right sides then the better the company is at achieving high realized bene® ts from EDI. All companies are at some point on the matrix depending upon their own particular trading circumstances. The following will discuss migration patterns across the matrix based upon the experiences of the companies in the sample. An interpretation of the sense of competitive advantage can also be achieved by examining the quadrant positions and the expectations of the participating companies.

## Left side of the matrix: a starting point for EDI diffusion

For all companies in the sample, EDI development commenced on the left side of the matrix (A and B quadrants), re¯ ected by the introduction of one or more major business transactions, such as invoicing or price catalogues. If we examine the preliminary diagnosis and the rating instrument (see Appendix) we can see that for Companies 1 and 3 operational bene® ts from these EDI transactions were the main motivator for introducing EDI, (indicated by a \`VH’ or, Very

High expectation). This was re¯ ected in the tremendous operational bene® t outcome realized from a reduction of paper document processing and clerica error. There was also a corresponding strategic bene® t outcome associated with decreased administration cost for buyers from dealing with interorganizational transactions.

For example, Company 1:

. . . started with price catalogues for suppliers a good few years ago, keeping our price ® les synchronized with suppliers’ price ® les . . . from there we moved to invoicing and are developing that further.

Company 6 adopted EDI in response to their buyers, wishes, but have since proactively pushed EDI themselves:

The original bene® t (for the buyers) was reduced errors from invoicing . . . we then introduced EDI to reduce our errors on invoicing. We started later on purchase orders and are now moving into what we call sales order purchasing.

For buyers, EDI is often introduced as a means of reducing paper processing and improving data management associated with huge volumes of invoicing. Whilst this had an effect upon decreasing administrative cost and enabling faster trading cycles, strategic bene® ts associated with closer inter® rm linkage tended to develop more over time

Initially EDI got rid of the paper, we had to store invoice for several years, that’ s now gone. That was the main bene® t objective we had in mind and we got that straight away.

Also, as a ® nance department driven initiative, Company 1 noted:

We were very happy with the original bene® ts from EDI, the company started with price catalogues and from there moved to invoicing . . . the bene® ts anticipated were internal in that price ® les could be kept synchronous with the advantage of less paper. This is a notable advantage when dealing with up to 40 000 deliveries per week.

Whilst not claiming any advantage over competitors in the ® rst instance from EDI, Company 1 further noted; \`. . . we want to be leaders rather than followers, indeed, we would like to think we are the leaders’. To this end the company is increasing functionality and progressing on the right side of the matrix.

The left side of the matrix, and quadrant A in particular, represents lower levels of operating risk for IS within the business, compared with the more \`strategic’ right side (Farbey et al., 1995; Fitzgerald, 1998). ## Bene® t quadrant B: a false sense of advantage

The ef® ciency gained here represents an opportunistic ® rst-mover advantage from an attractive and unique IOS enabled innovation (Senn, 1992; Clemons and Row, 1993). A particular trait of companies in quadrant B is the ability to derive strategic and operational bene® ts, if EDI has not been used before in a region or industry. This was the case with Company 5 which was requested as a pioneer supplier for a major retailer:

Bene® ts rushed in the beginning, immediately improving our cash ¯ ow, especially with our major customer Tesco (who had piloted EDI with us). . . . We received immediate cash¯ ow bene® ts within the payment cycle as they promised to pay us within fourteen days. . . . In the case of another customer, they continue with bad practice and continued to pay us late so we do not get much bene® t there. But we do ® nd with a lot of the other customers that the sending of the EDI has improved the payment cycle.

However, the organizational problems came when other customers came on board, extra resources were needed and (Company 5):

Management began to expect the same bene® ts with new customers, with the same resources . . . EDI has been driven by the IT department and not the user departments, with the result that it has had to be managed and controlled as just another part of the IT function, not an ideal situation.

For the future, the senior IT manager in Company 5 believed that more integrated planning and resources would be needed in order to get bene® ts from trading with buyers in Europe.

The trouble with this initial bene® t state of ef® - ciency, is that the bene® ts of invoicing and purchase orders appear to senior management to be realized at a relatively low ® nancial cost and involve a low disturbance rate of traditional departmental activities. However, in order to maintain that sense of bene® t return a commitment to extra resources is always needed as the number of trading partners increase. As Company 5 noted; \`. . . more resources will be necessary in the future in order to increase the number of EDI customers that we plan to’. The IT manager expressed concern because of the need for senior management to make widespread integration and logistical changes before using EDI to trade with additional customers in Europe.

Therefore, as in the case of Company 5, the advantage of initial bene® t success represented by a bene® t state of ef® ciency, with a relatively high degree of external diffusion (less than ten trading partners now ± growing to more than twenty over the following three years); with a relatively low degree of internal diffusion (weak integration), is a situation to be weary of, if the stage of \`expansion’ has passed. The initial ef® - ciency gained, may represent a sense of competitive advantage for the business. However, in order for that advantage to be sustained, efforts must be made to remain IOS technology leaders which requires an accompanied increase in levels of internal diffusion.

The IT manager warned of this danger when EDI was ® rst being adopted for Company 3:

When proposing the initial development of EDI to the board, we told them to expect no bene-® ts in the beginning . . . there was no hype with EDI, that’ s because I knew enough about it. . . . There is a lot of work involved in controlling EDI over the longer term. It’ s never simple to add new customers, never mind new global standards. Each customer interprets standards in a different way and each wants different ® elds. . . . It is also important to think about interfacing directly with our applications right from the start. Aim for integration ® rst, otherwise it’ s double work, double entry . . . we do it the long hard way, but it’ s the best way.

From the above, we can see that matching future expectations with internal resource capability is important. A bene® t ef® ciency without the necessary resource commitment, is only a short term success, one which will require extra investment when new trading partners (expansion stage) are to be included, or additional functionality catered for.

## Right side of the matrix: realized bene® t effect increases

Expectations from IS are zealously high and so are the realized bene® ts if neutrality or ef® ciency are achieved (quadrant C). Typically, successful players in quadrant C are persistent innovators who are continually seeking IS enabled opportunities from restructuring inef® cient internal processes and external network operations in order to improve the ways business is transacted (Venkatraman, 1994; Kettinger et al., 1997).

The high operational and strategic bene® ts realized are similar to target quadrant B, but there is a greater sense of competitive advantage derived from integration of EDI information with other business initiatives such as ECR and data warehousing (Companies 2, 4, 6 are developing and re® ning ECR capability). To achieve a position in this quadrant, requires a lot of organizational commitment, high strategic IS investment, a distinctive and strong IT support capability, as well as the ability to be a successful innovator (Booth and Philip, 1996; Burn, 1996; Fearon and Philip, 1998).

For example, the IT manager in Company 4 noted:

We have 200 stores scanning on EDI, the plan is to have all retailers scanning, holding information on sales, down to individual sales, down to product level on a daily basis. You can build all sorts of sales information and if you have costs information you have access to reports, stock control and suggested ordering.

## The EDI analyst in Company 2 re¯ ected:

We are looking at ECR at the moment and the use of price catalogues and purchase orders . . . ECR and EDI go hand in hand. Strategically, [integrated] EDI is up there ± a big move away from rekeying. It gives us status for a data warehouse. . . . EDI and ECR are strategically driven by top management. You can’t do EDI if top management are not behind it and it takes a while to reap the bene® ts.

All the companies view the high realized bene® t outcome on the right side of the matrix as the position they want to be in. As Company 6 stated:

In the last two years we are realizing the bene® ts that are really possible and we are striving towards these. . . . Like most companies we adopted EDI because we were pushed into it by our customers. At the start, savings on paper were replaced by the installation of the EDI itself . . . [However] we are now looking at it within the company and enhancing the types of data being sent, then we can really see the bene® ts ¯ owing. If we have EPOS data coming back to us and delivery schedule data, then we can gear our systems towards using that data to drive the business forward.

If high expectations are not realized, then quadrant D is the consequence, representing strategic de® ciency, whose repercussions can be very serious for the business as a whole. For example, Company 5 could move into de® ciency if adequate resources are not provided during the next stage in development which will require the addition of many new trading partners. Indeed, de® ciency could happen to any of the companies in the sample.

A major consideration, emphasized by Company 6 is matching a suitable EDI architecture with a growing need for integrated functionality:

. . . a fragmented architecture does not work with EDI, from purchase orders through to ledger, if you have different and distinct systems without connecting interfaces then trying to integrate them with existing EDI simply does not work.

Therefore, in order to avoid serious de® ciency (i.e. quadrant D), with an implementation when business expectations are high, the underlying EDI architecture must be integrated with in house applications and be able to interface with many new trading partners’ applications. Companies 2, 4 and 6 believed there was great dif® culty in achieving this, especially, when message standards are proliferating and considered ineffective because many different trading partners want their own version of EDI messages to suit their particular systems.

## Crossing from left to right on the matrix

From a bene® t planning perspective, the ideal EDI development route should progress along the line of bene® t neutrality. Crossing to the right side involves a greater degree of planning risk for the initiator’s business, as greater IOS ¯ exibility (Golden and Powell, 1997) is required to suit: (a) the many internal information needs derived from closer integration of the sales, accounting, purchasing, production and distribution functions; (b) as well as satisfying those externally shared information needs belonging to key buyers and sellers. ## Implications for practitioners

The following practices were indicated by the respondents as the principal reasons why they believed their EDI development to be successful in delivering the desired bene® t outcome.

## Gain management commitment

All companies recognized the need for management to be behind IOS and electronic trading. As the IOS migration moves to the right side, that commitment requires greater ® nancial resources and a willingness to change inef® cient practices throughout the organisation and its supply chain networks.

## Learn to balance the short and longer term issues

Companies 3, 5 and 6 believed that every participating ® rm, buyer or seller, must balance short term problems with a longer term vision of where EDI is going for each company as a whole. For ® rst example, Company 6 noted:

The ® rst hurdle is getting over changes requested by the customer [buyer] which overrides any particular sentiment about design considerations. . . . It is simply a matter of juggling immediate priorities and resources with a vision of where EDI will bring the company.

## Integrate as soon as possible

Company 3 believed that a time will come when buyers and sellers realize that piecemeal and independent approaches to developing EDI networks do not work if signi® cant bene® ts are to be achieved. Company 3 also recommended that integration must be planned for well in advance of ® rst using EDI, otherwise functions such as invoicing are in effect, rolled out twice.

## Need for planning

Comprehensive planning was noted as a major factor in determining whether EDI was to be successful. The senior IT manager in Company 6 reported that when dealing with increasingly complex electronic trading situations; \`. . . a macro view must be taken and then decomposed, in order to determine the relevant IS requirements’. If there is no planning then; \`. . . temporary [add on] ® xtures become permanent features’, implying that it is easier to add extra functionality on a piecemeal basis, than it is to change the entire way business activities are performed.

## Trading partner co-operation

Buyers were of the opinion that their respective suppliers were not adopting EDI fast enough and sellers were hoping that more retailers would eventually join their networks. This factor was very signi® - cant for distributors who adopted a policy of forcing their own suppliers to adopt EDI in order to derive cost savings (Company 4). Buyers also held training seminars and prepared publications for respective trading partners, if required (Company 1).

## Get your VAN onside

Again, all companies believed that relationships with the VANs were extremely important for technical backup and catering for trading partner problems. Satisfaction with the VANs were generally very high. Company 3 typi® ed the relationship:

. . . because we are a big organization, we have a good relationship with our value added network. If we didn’t have that we would have a problem in that [our VAN] chases our suppliers along, setting them up, even if they hadn’t used EDI before.

Companies 5 and 6 termed relations with their VANs as an almost \`strategic relationship’, because of their high dependence on the VANs for critical business operations.

## Conclusions

This study is part of a wider research programme concerning the need for identifying more holistic approaches when evaluating the bene® ts of EDI and related initiatives. The paper critiques normative models of IS planning and offers a second dimension in the analysis of bene® t success. Comparing expected and realized bene® ts offers insight into how satisfactory EDI bene® t performance has been for the participating ® rms. Whilst not presented as a truly accurate measure of IS performance, the interpreted bene® t states offer a reliable degree of internal consistency (see Appendix) that are validated in the light of informant responses.

The alignment of preliminary diagnoses with other IS performance criteria leads to a con® rmatory diagnoses and their conceptualization in the form of the bene® t success matrix. The matrix offers new insight into how EDI develops and what aspects of technology management are important relative to particular quadrant characteristics. As the sample size is small, there is no attempt to provide statistical recognition in support of the frameworks or generalizations made, although this will be the aim of future work.

However, it was to be expected that the companies in the sample would be successful in their use of EDI as they represented the key players in their industry. What is of interest are the IS planning and implementation lessons to be learned from the study. Most of the companies are migrating to the right side of the matrix. There was a general consensus that the companies who will gain most advantage in terms of IOS bene® t success, will be those who are able to incorporate data from EDI and e-commerce systems in order to support much wider initiatives. These included: setting up data warehouse capabilities for information retrieval and data sharing; Ef® cient Consumer Response (ECR) strategies to cut retailer costs and increase consumer value; the promotion of further IS integration and innovative consumer, purchasing and logistics strategies.

The extent of the planning gap (BPG) is diagnosed by comparing realized values as a percentage of expected values.  
Three interpretations include: (a) \`Neutrality’ percentage values range between ± 19% to +19%. (b) \`De® ciency’ percentage values are less than or equal to ± 20%. (c) \`Ef® ciency’ percentage values are greater than or equal to +20%.  
It was felt that a score of +20% was an appropriate boundary limit with which to interpret bene® t states, allowing for perceptual margins of error. These percentage values represent a consistent view of EDI bene® t success, as perceived by senior managers who were deemed most knowledgeable about EDI development. The percentage values are not suggested to be wholly accurate. However, they were also interpreted within the context of interviews for validation purposes.
