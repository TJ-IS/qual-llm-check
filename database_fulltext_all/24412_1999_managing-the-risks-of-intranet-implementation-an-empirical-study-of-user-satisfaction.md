# Managing the risks of intranet implementation: an empirical study of user satisfaction

R. PHELPS AND M. MOK

Surrey European Management School, University of Surrey, Guildford GU2 5XH, UK

Much interest has been generated recently by the concept of intranets: the use of internet technology to build the communication and data storage infrastructure of the ® rm. While the advantages of such a concept have been widely publicized, the risks of moving to an intranet based infrastructure are less clear. \`Fear of the unknown’ may deter some ® rms from taking advantage of intranets. This empirical study is based in Singapore, a country chosen for its high internet penetration and usage levels. It investigates the factors underlying successful intranet developments. Intranet success as measured by user satisfaction is related to IS management risk factors. It is found that successful intranet implementation can be largely explained by standard determinants of IS success. Some implications for management of intranet developments are discussed.

## Introduction

Recently, intranets have become an area of considerable interest as ® rms move to adopt global Internet standards in their electronic communication systems, both internally and between collaborative groups of ® rms. Over 66% of US Fortune 100 companies are reported to be developing intranets (Booty, 1996). Wilder and Stahl (1996) report a survey of 110 US companies which showed nearly 60% of companies with over 1000 employees (and 40% of those with less than 1000 employees) had implemented intranets. Yet proprietary vendors of workgroup and communications packages, such as Microsoft and Lotus, offer alternatives to intranet systems which are more familiar to many ® rms. Moving from a proprietary or in-house system to an intranet therefore presents a risk which must be evaluated and managed. As with any new technology, the risk factors may not be well understood by ® rms, and so concern about unknown risks may prevent rapid uptake of the technology. The purpose of this paper is to present the results of an investigation into the determinants of successful intranet implementation. Giving ® rms information about the factors which determine the success of intranet implementations allows them to weigh up the risks from an informed viewpoint and take a rational decision regarding intranet moves.

Intranets have widespread uses throughout all levels of the ® rm. As a new technology their success may be affected by the general level of awareness and experience of IT networking in the user population. To counteract the possibility that acceptance of intranets may be temporarily affected by a lack of relevant technology use experience, we investigate intranets in a population (Singapore) where extensive information networking has already been implemented as a result of a government drive to become an \`intelligent island’.

We examine the factors which have been proposed in the literature to account for successful development and implementation of new IT systems and apply them to intranets. A set of six factors is found to account for 64% of the variance in satisfaction between intranet users. The implication drawn is that intranet technology does not present signi® cant new types of IT risk. Intranet development and implementation risks can be largely accounted for and managed by following guidelines relating to these six factors. Some suggestions for successful intranet installations are given following from these factors.

## Intranets

According to Forrester Research, intranets are de® ned as \`internal TCP/IP networks that carry internetdeveloped applications like the web’ (Morant, 1996). A \`classical intranet’ uses all the elements of the internet, is internally focused and has no public internet backbone. On the other hand, an \`extended intranet’ involves interfacility connectivity, extending access to key customers, suppliers and business partners (Passmore, 1996; Shankar, 1996; Vowles, 1996). The major components of an intranet are indicated in Figure 1. A recent survey of 50 major organizations found that 16% have already an intranet in place whilst 50% either plan to or are considering building one (Cortese, 1996). More than 66% of Fortune 1000 companies have developed or are developing corporate intranets (Booty, 1996). Holtz (1996) found that many organizations do not need to cost justify their intranet projects to the same degree as is necessary for other IT projects such as mainframe or client/server implementations. The bene® ts of intranets most commonly cited by authors and implementers are platform independence, fast and up-to-date information access and broad coverage (a wide audience) (Cross, 1996; Frank, 1996; Lunt, 1996; Trowbridge, 1996). When comparing intranet to other client/server communication systems like Lotus Notes, intranet stands out in terms of ease (as well as cost) of maintenance, open system standards and minimum hardware and software investment (Sprout, 1995; Eros, 1996; Finkelstein, 1996; Gibbs, 1996; Hercz, 1996; Nash, 1996; Wheatley, 1996).

## IS risk

The picture of intranet use painted above indicates that intranets are currently the subject of high levels of commercial interest but still in an early stage of technological acceptance and implementation. As with all new technologies, perceived risks may limit the speed of penetration and the breadth of intranet acceptance. This paper investigates some of the potential risks associated with intranet implementation.

IS risk embraces a number of different aspects. A selection of different perspectives can be found in the special issue of the Journal of Information Technology (Grif® ths, 1996). While IS project failures may spring from poor project management practice, or from the inherent dif® culty of the project itself, a stream of research (e.g. Beath and Ives, 1989, Kearney, 1990, Scott Morton, 1991, Willcocks and Margetts, 1993) indicates that many reasons for failure stem from the organizational environments of IT systems and the interaction between development processes and the larger organizational system rather than from the development process itself. We distinguish technical risk and business risk. Technical risk (can the system be developed?) is rooted in complexity uncertainty surrounding the technology while organizational risk (does the system produce a bene® t?) is rooted in the way in which the system achieves business needs. In the case of intranets this distinction is particularly important. Intranets borrow existing widespread technology from internet implementations. They pose no new technical challenges of their own as they follow existing de facto standards. Both the technology and the skills to implement it are common. The main risk facing intranet implementations is therefore the organizational risk. Barki et al. (1993) summarized risk variables found in the literature with a list of 35 factors. Many of these are technical risk factors (e.g. lack of development enterprise, number of users, task complexity); organizational risk factors are fewer and can essentially be summed up as lack of top management support, lack of user IT experience and support, extent of changes to business processes and quality of supplier support. Moynihan (1996) also found clarity regarding the intended use of the system as an important organizational factor.

Technical risks are overcome if the system is successfully developed and implemented. Organizational risks are overcome if the system is effective in use (after implementation). For the reasons discussed above we concentrate on the organizational risks of intranets and use system effectiveness as the criterion for evaluating the success with which these risks have been managed.

Views differ on how to measure the effectiveness of a deployed system. However the approaches suggested in the literature are independent of the type of information system. Intranets can reasonably be assessed using the same established criteria. System usage has been frequently used as a measure of system success (Lucas, 1978; Ein-Dor and Segev, 1988; Davis, 1989; Igbaria et al., 1990). Another stream of work has measured effectiveness by user satisfaction. User satisfaction is the affective attitude towards an object or an information system. Ives et al. (1983) and Ives and Olson (1984) have employed this variable as a measure of overall satisfaction with information systems.

Currently, there is a confusion on whether there exists a causal relationship between usage and satisfaction. Although system utilization may represent user acceptance of the application system, this is not true in all cases. For example, a user whose job requires monthly generation of complex reports may express satisfaction with the system (in terms of ease of use and usefulness) while the measure of system usage would be low (only 12 times a year). In this study we take the view that user satisfaction is the more fundamental measure of satisfaction. Existing instruments to measure user satisfaction relate to a multidimensional construct of satisfaction. Typically they consider dimensions of staff support, produce capability and user involvement and knowledge. They therefore try to assess a broad measure of satisfaction covering these distinct aspects. In this study we want to assess the effect of a set of proposed variables on user satisfaction. A multidimensional measure of satisfaction runs the risk that the satisfaction dimensions may overlap with the variables proposed as causes of satisfaction. This may give rise to spurious positive relations between hypothesized causes and satisfaction. To avoid this methodological dif® culty we use a simple unidimensional scale to measure overall feelings of satisfaction.

An alternative approach to measuring user satisfaction is the SERVQUAL approach (Parasuraman et al., 1985). However this approach remains controversial since respondents are asked to indicate hypothetical \`excellent’ service levels, in addition to actual levels. The theoretical arguments regarding validity and reliability of this approach are summarized in the critical review by van Dyke et al. (1977) and the rejoinders this attracted in the same issue of MIS Quarterly. In terms of intranet satisfaction, the SERVQUAL approach has two drawbacks in addition to the theoretical controversy noted above. First it measures service quality whereas we are interested in satisfaction with an implemented system. Second is presupposes user awareness of an appropriate \`excellent’ level of service; since intranets constitute a new breed of system it is unlikely that such a reference level exists among users as a stable concept. Hence this study instead used direct measures of user satisfaction and of its hypothesized determinants adapted from the IS literature.

## Factors affecting user satisfaction

A literature search for causes of user satisfaction failed to uncover any proposed causes which are speci® c to intranet implementation. In the absence of speci® c factors, we consider factors which have been proposed to affect satisfaction with information system in general. We consider these factors under two headings: commitment factors (is the system actively wanted by management and users?) and ergonomic factors (does the system help users in their tasks?).

## Commitment factors

In the information system development cycle, management involvement especially in the selection, goal setting and project initiation phase, has been found to be important in earlier studies (Ein-Dor and Segev, 1978; Lucas, 1981; Saarinen and Saaksjavi, 1992). Factor studies conducted by Kwon and Zmud (1987) have identi® ed management support as one of the factors commonly related to success. Process studies that focus on the stages of organizational change associated with the introduction of a new system, have found commitment to change and implementation effort as well as extensive project de® nition and planning to be the two most important determinants of the success of the change process (Lawrence and Low, 1993). Other studies have also shown managerial support to be more important than technical support in determining user satisfaction (Cheney and Dickson, 1982). These arguments appear to be equally valid for intranets as for other systems. We therefore hypothesize:

H1: Management support for the intranet has a positive relation to user satisfaction

The importance of user participation in systems development has been widely recognized in the literature (Ives and Olson, 1984; Mann and Watson, 1984; McKeen et al., 1994). User involvement has been predicted to increase user acceptance although several mechanisms: developing realistic expectations about system capabilities (Gibson, 1977); providing an arena for bargaining and con¯ ict resolution about design issues (Keen, 1981); fostering system ownership by users (Robey and Farrow, 1982); decreasing user resistance to change (Lucas, 1974); and by committing users to the system (Lucas, 1974; Markus, 1983). Furthermore, user involvement has been predicted to improve system quality by: providing a more accurate and complete assessment of user information requirements (Norton and McFarlan, 1975; Robey and Farrow, 1982); providing expertise about the organization the system is to support, expertise usually unavailable within the information system group (Lucas, 1974); avoiding development of unacceptable or unimportant features (Robey and Farrow, 1982); and by improving user understanding of the system (Lucas, 1974; Robey and Farrow, 1982).

H2: Perception of user involvement in intranet implementation has a positive relation to user satisfaction

Perceived usefulness can be de® ned as \`the degree to which a person believe that using a particular system will enhance his or her job performance’ (Davis, 1989). That is, people tend to use or not to use an application to the extent they believe it will help them perform their job better. Similarly, Larcker and Lessig (1980) in their evaluation of information reports have de® ned perceived usefulness as \`the quality that causes a particular information set to acquire relevance to a decision maker’ and the extent to which the information elements are \`a necessary input for task accomplishment’. The more an individual must rely on new technology in order to perform the required work tasks, the more salient are the beliefs regarding the technology’s usefulness (Martinko et al., 1996). According to expectancy theories, the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA), an individual’s ultimate decision to use a system depends on the individual’s per-

## Ergonomic factors

The Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) as developed by Fishbein and Ajzen (1980) and the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) proposed by Davis (1986) both show perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use as two important factors that would ultimately in¯ uence the actual usage of a system. Ease of use is hypothesized to have a signi® cant effect on attitude towards using the system. So, the easier a system is to interact with, the greater should be the user’s sense of ef® cacy and personal control regarding his or her ability to carry out the sequences of behaviour needed to operate the system. In this case, there is a similarity between perceived ease of use and Bandura’s (1982) self-ef® cacy theory, de® ned as \`judgment of how well one can execute courses of action required to deal with prospective situations’. In addition, the ease of use is also a contributing factor to increased performance. Effort saved due to improved ease of use may be redeployed, enabling a person to accomplish more work for the same effort. As such, ease of use is considered to affect perception of system usefulness. The model also postulates that ease of use in turn is affected by external variables. For example, many system features such as menus, icons, mice, graphics, colour, music and touch screens are speci® cally intended to enhance usability (Bewley, et al. 1983). The impact of system features on ease of use has been documented in various research studies (Benbasat, et al., 1986; Dickson et al., 1986; Mundorf et al., 1993). Training, documentation and user support consultants are other external factors that may also in¯ uence ease of use.

H4: Ease of use of an intranet has a positive relation to user satisfaction

A study conducted by Camillus and Lederer (1985) on the degree of effectiveness of end-user computing (EUC), as measured by user satisfaction, shows that satisfaction increases when users have a greater degree of availability and accessibility of the computerized information system. This is further supported by subsequent study conducted by Igbaria and Nachmann (1985). In their study, it was noted that because of the lack of free access to computer resources and lack of support, the end users become dissatis® ed with the system. Rivard and Huff (1988) also emphasized the importance of having free access to computer services.

H5: Availability and accessibility of the intranet has a positive relation to user satisfaction

A corporate intranet requires that information published on the intranet is accurate and up-to-date (Cronin, 1994). Palvia et al. (1992) note that this was one of the important criteria for FedEx when they developed the PRISM system. Fuerst and Cheney (1982) in their study on satisfaction with Decision Support Systems, concluded that accuracy of output and its relevance are of major importance. According to researchers such as Ives et al. (1983), Reimann and Warren (1985) and Miller and Doyle (1987) there is a positive relationship between timeliness of information and user satisfaction with the system. Morant (1996) notes the importance of ensuring accurate and up-to-date information. Reliability and accuracy as determinants of user satisfaction are suggested in studies by Lucas (1978) and Bailey and Pearson (1983).

H6: Information accuracy and reliability in the intranet has a positive relation to user satisfaction

These hypotheses suggest factors which impact user satisfaction. Perhaps of greater interest from a management viewpoint, is the question of the amount of user satisfaction they account for. If they can be shown to account for the greater part of user satisfaction then it can be inferred that the success of intranet implementation is largely reliant on the attention paid to these factors. Therefore in addition to testing the hypotheses an analysis of the explanatory power of these six factors will also be carried out.

## Instrument development

A 50 item survey questionnaire was developed in nine sections. The ® rst six sections sought data about the user’s perception of the factors hypothesized to contribute to user satisfaction (determinants of user satisfaction). These were followed by a section measuring actual user satisfaction level. A further section comprised questions about intranet usage. The last section measured demographic characteristics. The questions were adapted (by slight rewordings) to the intranet context from instruments used in previous IS studies relating to user satisfaction.

A pretest was carried out using ® ve respondents who had prior experience with intranets. The pretest showed that an average of 30 minutes would be required to complete the survey questionnaire. As a result of the pretest minor modi® cations were made to the questionnaire. The ® nal questionnaire was structured as follows:

## Determinants of user satisfaction

Seven questions asked about users’ perception of management support for the corporate intranet. Management support is measured by the extent of encouragement given to respondents to use the intranet system (two questions), the extent of training provided to the respondent (one question), the availability of technical support (one question), the management interest in users’ feedback (one question) and the degree of motivation to teams that contributed to the intranet success (one question). Finally, a question on overall rating on satisfaction and importance of management support was included as a check on scale consistency.

Eight questions asked about user involvement. These questions were adapted from various researchers (Swanson, 1974; Ives and Olson, 1981; Schultz et al. 1985; Doll and Torkzadeh, 1990; Hawk and Aldag, 1990). The indicator for user involvement in this case focuses on the amount of perceived control the user has and the extent of their participation in the implementation. A question on overall rating on satisfaction and importance of user involvement was included as a check on scale consistency.

Six questions measured accessibility and availability. These centred on the convenience of using the intranet (three questions) as well as the extent of intranet system availability and performance (two questions). An overall rating on the importance of accessibility and availability was included as a check on scale consistency.

Six questions measured reliability and accuracy of information. The measurement for this section is based on features such as up-to-dateness, accuracy, comprehensiveness, reliability and trust. An overall rating on the importance of reliability and accuracy was included as a check on scale consistency.

Seven questions measured perceived usefulness and were adapted from Davis (1989) with minor rewording to suit the intranet context. Of the 14 items developed by Davis, six were selected for brevity as there appeared to be overlap between some items. An overall rating on the importance of perceived usefulness was included as a check on scale consistency.

Nine items were used to measure perceived ease of use. These items were also adapted from Davis (1989). These items include ease of use, user friendliness of system, dependency on others, data format and ease of information retrieval. An overall rating on importance of perceived ease of use was included as a check on scale consistency.

## User satisfaction

Five questions presented under this section included the extent of the respondent’s intention to use intranet for their work, their attitude towards encouraging other staff to use the intranet as well as their opinion on whether the introduction of the intranet is a positive move for their organization and whether the respondents ® nd themselves worst off or better off without an intranet. An overall rating on satisfaction with the intranet was also included as a check on scale consistency.

Each item in the scales measuring each factor was measured using a ® ve point Likert scale where 1 represented strong disagreement and 5 represented strong agreement with the item statement. The average score over items in a scale indicates the degree to which the factor was present.

A section containing questions to measure the length of time respondents had used the system and the frequency of use was included. This helps to give a picture of usage of intranets at the time of the survey. The level of prior experience with the system and with similar information systems could affect user satisfaction (Igbaria, 1990) and so might need to be controlled. Adapting Igbaria (1990) for use in the intranet context, 7-point Likert scales were used to measure average weekly intranet usage and frequency of use.

Demographic variables may also have an impact on MIS success and user satisfaction (Rivard and Huff, 1988; Igbaria, 1990; Mundorf et al., 1993). Speci® cally, they suggested that males, older individuals and less educated individuals exhibit less positive attitudes towards MIS (Zmud, 1979; Igbaria and Parasuraman, 1989). O’Reilly (1982) also found negative correlation between level of education and frequency of use. Correspondingly, a ® nal section of questions on individuals’ pro® les including gender, age and education was incorporated into the instrument.

The reliability of each of the scales for measuring each of the factors proposed as determinants of satisfaction, and the reliability of the scale for satisfaction itself, was assessed by calculating Cronbach’s alpha (1951). Alphas for the items measuring the determinants of satisfaction were: management support (0.79), user involvement (0.75), accessibility and availability

## Methodology

For this study we chose Singapore, a society where government has essentially provided a large scale experiment by actively promoting the business and home use of electronic communications. Singapore provides an example of a high level of awareness of IT in general and Internet technology in particular. It therefore provides an indication of the level and determinants of success which can be expected in an IT network aware user base.

Its Tradenet system has won several awards overseas and has been taken as a model by other countries. Internet education has been extended to all secondary schools. Some child care centres and kindergartens provide internet awareness to children between 5 to 6 years old. By late 1996, Singapore with some 150 000 internet accounts, had the highest Net penetration in the region, as observed by the Political and Economic Risk Consultancy of Hongkong ± an indication that Singaporeans realize the importance of what has been touted as the medium of the future (Straits Times, 1996). New schemes have been introduced to boost Singapore as an internet hub. The government has committed S\$82 million to the Singapore One project, a major component of the government’s IT 2000 plan to turn Singapore into an intelligent island. Singapore One, launched in 1996, delivers multimedia services to the home, workplace and school via high speed internet access (Straits Times, 1997a). These indications suggest that the level of awareness of internet technology among users in the survey will be high.

Given the recent nature of intranet installation and the fact that intranet penetration is still relatively sparse, convenience sampling was chosen as an ef® cient data collection method. The major objection to convenience sampling, the probability of a nonrepresentative sample, was felt to be of small importance since the population of intranet users is rapidly changing. Initial users of any technology are liable to possess different characteristics from the population as a whole and the results of this survey must be interpreted in that light.

A questionnaire was distributed through ten survey administrators whose organizations have already put in place an intranet system. These survey administrators were contacted via phone to request their participation as survey administrators. They were briefed on the purpose of the study followed by an explanation of the instruments used. They were also advised on how to administer the survey. Each administrator was asked to obtain eight completed questionnaire responses.

The questionnaire was e-mailed to survey administrators in early June 1997. This was followed by a reminder through phone after one week from the e-mail date. By the end of June, a total of 54 replies were collected from the survey administrators either personally, through fax, by post or via e-mail. Of the 54, 16 cases did not answer fully all the questions in the questionnaire. A number of these (nine cases) left blank or stated \`not applicable’ or \`unable to comment’ to the question on how they perceived the intranet usage of their staff in the organization (question 4 under Intranet Usage). One respondent totally left out the demographic section. As for the rest of these 16 cases, only one or two questions were left blank. Since very few questions were left unanswered for these 16 cases, they were retained for the analysis with a \`neutral’ rating added for unanswered items.

## Results

## Demographic pro® les

The survey return covers a balanced proportion of male and female with slightly more male (28 cases) than female (26 cases). This is comparable to the proportion of male and female Singapore’s working population. The ages of the respondents lie mainly between age 25 and 34 with very few from age 40 and above (Table 1). In addition, the sample is highly quali® ed with 70% of the respondents having a degree or equivalent and only 2% not having any higher education quali® cation. The heavy weighting in favour of younger, well-educated respondents indicates that this pro® le is more likely to quickly take up intranet use. It may be explained by the fact that the younger age group would have been exposed to the technology during their school days or are willing to learn new technologies. Higher educational levels are likely to have exposed them to more new technology concepts. The older age group may experience higher anxiety in learning new technology and may also have the seniority to delegate intranet communications to their subordinates.

The responses indicate an absence of gender differences regarding intranet acceptance. This is consistent with the ® ndings of Gelen and Straub (1997) in a similar area, that there is no gender difference in the use of e-mail (although there may be differences in perceptions of it). One inference that may be drawn is that ® rms may need to consider additional training and support for older and less educated groups of workers if they are to achieve full bene® ts from intranet implementations.

## Intranet usage

The vast majority of respondents had been using an intranet for less than twelve months, showing the recent penetration of this technology (Table 2). Only 4% had over two years experience. The results thus re¯ ect a user base in the early stages of adaptation to intranets, as is to be expected from the new nature of the technology. In considering the effects of a lack of experience on user satisfaction levels, it is necessary to take into account the high level of internet awareness found in Singapore. This is likely to mean that even inexperienced users of intranets would be familiar with the basic technology involved and the type of user interface presented.

Frequency of use of intranets was less than might be expected (Table 3). Daily use (four or more times per week) was indicated by about 40% of respondents. Median usage was two or three times per week. However each session may involve multiple searches, e-mails or postings. Twenty-three per cent used the intranet for over ® ve hours per week, with very few (8%) using it for more than ten hours per week. Users can be divided into two groups: a low use group using intranets for below one hour per day on average (77%) and a high use group using it for over one hour per day on average (23%). This indicates that for the high use group, signi® cant multiple tasks were being carried out at each session, and intranets were not simply the vehicle for the occasional e-mail, as they may be for the majority low use group. The high proportion in the low use group together with 37% reporting use of once per week or less indicates that a substantial number of employees are following minimal usage behaviours towards the intranet. This suggests that intranet use policies and training are important complements to technology implementation; without them intranets may be used to well below their capabilities.

To analyse the effect of these factors in accounting for satisfaction with intranet implementations, correlation coef® cients were calculated between the scale measure of each factor and the scale measure of user satisfaction.

From Table 5, it appears that all the factors are signi® cantly $( \phi = < 0 . 0 1 )$ and positively correlated with user satisfaction. These signi® cant correlations are consistent with hypotheses H1 to H6. The relationship is especially strong with perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. This is consistent with Davis’ (1989) Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) which implies that perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness are positively correlated with intention to use a system.

From these correlations we conclude that all of the hypotheses, H1± H6, are supported in this study. However, these correlations do not show the explanatory power of the factors, i.e. how much of observed user satisfaction is accounted for by these six factors. To investigate this question, multiple regression analysis was carried out (Table 6). In management models, given the inherent variability due to different people and situational differences, it is highly unlikely that anything close to 100% of the variation will be explained by any model. Sixty-four per cent of the variation in measured user satisfaction is explained by the six factors together $( r ^ { 2 } = 0 . 6 4 )$ . This is a relatively high proportion (approximately two-thirds) of the variance and implies that attention to these six factors provide a good model to explain intranet implementation satisfaction or dissatisfaction. An implication is that attention to these six factors can address the greater part of the risk in intranet implementation. The remaining variance is due to other factors not in the model or to random variation.

However it can be seen that management support (MGT) has a negative coef® cient and that the contributions of the variables are only signi® cant for perceived usefulness and ease of use. Further investi gation reveals that these peculiarities are a result of multicollinearity between the variables, as shown in Table 4, where several of the factors are signi® cantly correlated amongst themselves. In order to understand how each of the factors is related to user satisfaction, regressions of each factor against user satisfaction were run separately.

It can be seen from Table 7 that all the factors are signi® cantly $( \phi < 0 . 0 1 )$ and positively related to user satisfaction. Perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use do provide the strongest explanations of user satisfaction (r<sup>2</sup> = 0.450 and 0.411), but the other four factors also explain signi® cant proportions of variation in satisfaction $( r ^ { 2 } = 0 . 1 2 8 – 0 . 1 9 3 )$ . This supports the hypotheses and indicates the signi® cant role which each of these factors plays in determining the user satisfaction with intranets.

## Discussion

Almost two-thirds of the variation in user satisfaction was accounted for by the six factors through the relation found with perceived usefulness and perceived use of use. A plausible interpretation of these results is that user satisfaction is a direct result of perceived usefulness and perceived each of use, and that management support, user involvement, reliability and accuracy, and accessibility and availability are all preconditions for users to perceive an intranet as both useful and easy to use. This implies that attention to all of these factors is necessary to achieve user satisfaction.

This ® nding supports the studies previously cited that suggested the importance of each of the six determining factors, but it also suggests a prioritization: that user satisfaction depends primarily on perceived usefulness and ease of use, with the other factors contributing via the construction of these perceptions. This result corresponds to other ® ndings in the literature, e.g. Iacovou et al. (1995) who found organizational readiness (including aspects of both ease of use via resources for training and usefulness via integration with business processes) and the perception of expected bene® ts to be the prime internal factors promoting high impact in EDI systems and to the ® ndings (e.g. Adams et al., 1992; Hendrickson et al., 1993) that perceived usefulness and ease of use are important explanatory factors for the diffusion of innovative information technology.

These results lead to the questions of how to improve perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness. The factors correlated with ease of use are accessibility and availability and reliability and accuracy; improvements in either of these may lead to improve ease of use. While perceived usefulness is also correlated with ease of use, the causal relation is more naturally interpreted as being from ease of use to usefulness.

Accessibility and availability achieved the highest rating of all the factors. This suggests that intranet technology is both suf® ciently reliable and user-friendly to avoid serious user problems. Nonetheless this result is based on a sample with a bias towards youth and good educational level; consideration should be given to training needs of older or less well-educated users. Management support may have a valuable role in encouraging such potential users to overcome their technological fears. A potential future threat to accessibility is posed by the need for security. As more company sensitive information is held on the intranet the need for security measures rises. The high rating for accessibility suggests that this is not a problem at present. But increasing security can interfere with availability. If the intranet is to be fully utilized security procedures need to be low effort on the part of users; new forms of security such as signature veri® cation or smartcards may be needed to replace the user overhead incurred by traditional methods such as changing, multiple passwords. Another trend that impacts accessibility and needs to be taken into account when planning intranets is the increasing mobility and hot-desking of staff. If the intranet is to form the backbone of the ® rm’s information storage and communications then access cannot be limited to ® xed of® ces sites. This should be recognized by allowing for mobile intranet access.

Reliability and accuracy were rated lower than accessibility or ease of use. This suggests that policies and procedures for data capture and validation offer opportunities for improvement. Since intranets are designed to give easy access to data they can be expected to encourage information searches. Reliability and accuracy may re¯ ect the quantity and timeliness of (the ability to put information onto the intranet in real time) data available on the intranet. This raises the question of whether information is centrally entered and controlled or whether it is wholly or partially the responsibility of user departments. Policies to encourage users to quickly update their data pages such as giving recognition to users for their intranet publishing and communication efforts may become an increasingly important management issue.

The ratings for reliability and accuracy may also contain a re¯ ection of the data indexing policies followed. The inability to locate information (e.g. by keyword search) even though it is stored in the system, due to inconsistent or undocumented indexation, may be perceived as a lack of reliability. A ® rm-wide policy for indexing data may be a requirement for reliability.

The reported levels of perceived usefulness were lower than for ease of use and for the other factors. This is consistent with an interpretation that perceived usefulness is dependent upon all of the other factors, either directly or through their effects on ease of use. The factors directly correlated with perceived usefulness are management support, user involvement and reliability and accuracy. Ratings for management support and user involvement were both higher than for perceived

## Managing the risks of intranet implementation

usefulness, indicating that while these factors may support usefulness of the system, they are not suf® cient to overcome user doubts about the impact of intranets on work processes. Thus while communication is an important aspect of intranet design, the integration of intranet design with business process design remains a fundamental requirement. An important implication for managers is the need to ensure the bene® ts of intranets are fully understood. Where intranets are seen merely as an additional communications tool or as an optional means of working, their usefulness is unlikely to be perceived as high. Perceptions of usefulness arise from the necessary use of intranets to perform business tasks. This can be ensured both by providing information and communication channels via the intranet that correspond to existing business needs and by redesigning business processes around intranet capabilities.

Reliability and accuracy of information is fundamental for an information system to be able to perform a useful business role. The relationship found between reliability and usefulness bears this out and suggests that policies to improve information reliability are likely to have a positive effect on perceived usefulness as well as on ease of use.

The remaining 36% of variation in user satisfaction unexplained by the factors in this study suggests that other variables may also play an important role in determining satisfaction. In particular, IS service levels as explored by SERVQUAL and similar service expectation based approaches but not included in this study may affect user satisfaction. If expectations of a system are higher than realized outcomes, the gap between expected and perceived service may modify the reported satisfaction levels. Both perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use could be affected by expectation gaps. Studies in this vein (e.g. Loh and Ong, 1998) have identi® ed the need for good IS service levels and the need for appropriate communications so that accurate expectations of the system are formed. While this study focused on satisfaction with the system, overall satisfaction ratings may have been coloured by the levels of IS service experienced and expectations formed about intranets.

The in¯ uence of Singaporean, or more generally East Asian, characteristics should also be considered in interpreting these results. East Asian countries have been found to have relatively high power distance compared to US and northern Europe countries and found to be essentially collectivist, contrasting with the more individualistic culture of the US, UK, Canada and Australia (Hofstede, 1980: Hofstede, 1991). Further East Asian countries have been found to have high longterm orientations (Newman and Nollen, 1996). It is possible that deference to authority (high power distance) together with a collectivist tendency to support group decisions may have resulted in an upward bias to the satisfaction ratings. On the other hand, the high level of internet exposure in Singapore may mean that the level of expectations is higher and so larger gaps with realized outcomes may have depressed satisfaction ratings. Yet again long-term orientation may suggest a lower level of immediate expectations in the longerterm context. Such opposing considerations illustrate the dif® culty of detecting the effects of cultural tendencies, which may in any case operate in subtle ways. The generalizability of these results can only be empir ically ascertained by replication elsewhere.

## Conclusion

Despite the worries about unforeseen risks attendant on the introduction of new technologies, this study indicates that the risks surrounding intranet implementations at least as measured by user satisfaction, are largely explained by the same factors proposed for other types of information systems. In accordance with other IT studies, perceived usefulness and ease of use emerge from this study as the prime factors underlying user satisfaction. While management support and user involvement have been reported at satisfactory levels and help to in¯ uence perceptions of usefulness, it appears that intranet usefulness is not perceived as highly as it could be in existing implementations. Usefulness must eventually depend upon the degree to which the intranet improves users’ performance of their business tasks. This implies that intranets should not be driven by technology considerations but should be designed and implemented in conjunction with a review of business processes. The relatively low ratings reported for reliability and accuracy also point to a management concern: the need for ® rm-wide data storage and indexing procedures. The role of user expectations, not treated in this study, is suggested as a possible explanation for the remaining variation in user satisfaction not accounted for by usefulness and ease of use. The expectations of intranet users remains an important topic for further research.

The transferability of the conclusions of this study to other regions depends upon the comparability of IS skill levels and in particular internet awareness, and on demographic and cultural characteristics. In regions where internet awareness is less or where education levels are lower or user ages higher, additional risks may be run in expecting staff to adjust their working patterns in line with the opportunities offered by intranets. Additional training may be called for to address such differences. Cultural differences may operate in subtle ways and it is dif® cult to predict differences in intranet satisfaction from them. More empirical research is called for to investigate cultural effects, especially when intranet implementation goes hand in hand with business process changes. Nonetheless, the overall pattern of these results is encouraging for intranet use. The bene® ts of intranets can be obtained without new or excessive risks and managed in the same manner as for the introduction of other new information system technologies. These ® ndings indicate that ® rms can undertake the step to intranet infrastructures and their bene® ts without needing to adopt a safety-® rst, \`wait and see’ strategy.
