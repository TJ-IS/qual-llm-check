This paper uni® es two apparently competing theories of information systems (IS) implementation by developing a model in which the contributions of innovation characteristics and implementation process theories are contingent upon the implementation context. To do this, we identify the different assumptions regarding the implementation context implicit in the two theories and, based on these differences, develop a contingent model within which to integrate the two theories. A partial test of the model within one particular context is reported using data collected from the end-users of an IS innovation introduced in a State health system in Australia. As hypothesized, we ® nd that, within the context, characterized by high individual level impact and low group level impact, the contribution of innovation characteristics to implementation success is higher than that of implementation process.

## Introduction

Information systems (IS) and information technology innovations are increasingly being used to drive organizational change programmes intended to deliver signi® cant performance improvements (Hammer, 1990). However, the results of such programmes have been mixed. Some IS innovations have been successful (Gallivan et al., 1994; Yetton et al., 1994; Robey and Sahai, 1996), but implementation failures are common and end users frequently reject innovations (Sauer, 1993; Markus and Benjamin, 1997). A careful inspection of the literature reveals two competing theories ± innovation characteristics theory and implementation process theory ± that are rarely the subject of comparative analysis within a study. Furthermore, tests of both theories have generated a number of null ® ndings (see, for example, Ginzberg, 1981; Fuerst and Cheny, 1982; Lee, 1986; Leonard-Barton and Deschamps, 1988; Cooper and Zmud, 1990; Markus and Keil, 1994). These observations are consistent with noncontingent theories being applied to a phenomenon which is context dependent; in which case, the need is to identify the critical contextual factors which explain the apparently inconsistent results. This is the goal of this paper, in which we integrate the two dominant theories within a contingent framework.

The two theories are distinguished by the different assumptions regarding the implementation context (hereafter simply referred to as context) implicit in them. Whereas innovation characteristics theory assumes that the context is characterized by independent adoption of technologies used to perform individual tasks (Fichman, 1992), implementation process theory assumes that the context is characterized by coordinated adoption of technologies by groups performing interdependent tasks (Ginzberg, 1980; Klein and Sorra, 1996). Fichman (1992) identi® ed the assumptions implicit in the innovations characteristics theory and found that support for the theory is contingent upon a match between the context and the assumptions of the theory. Building upon these insights, we propose a uni-® ed theory of IS implementation. We propose that the validities of both theories are contingent upon the context ± where impact on individual task performance is high, innovation characteristics have a signi® cant effect and where impact on group task performance is high, implementation processes have a signi® cant effect.

The proposed uni® ed theory has important implications for managers. The two theories focus on different impacts of the IS innovation and, consequently, prescribe different managerial actions for ensuring successful implementation. Innovation characteristics theory assumes that a user adopts a new technology for the performance gains it offers (Davis, 1989). The implication is that managers should focus attention on designing performance gains into the new technology. On the other hand, implementation process theory assumes that managerial action strongly in¯ uences enduser adoption of the new technology (Leonard-Barton, 1987; Leonard-Barton and Deschamps, 1988). Consequently, it emphasizes managing the implementation process. Thus, whereas innovation characteristics theory requires managers to ensure that the IS innovation has the right characteristics, implementation process theory focuses managerial effort on the process of implementation. From a managerial perspective, the proposed uni® ed theory helps managers identify the relative criticality of the two courses of action within different contexts. Not only does this enable managers to develop more effective implementation strategies by identifying the critical success factors within speci® c contexts, it also results in a more ef® cient allocation of managerial resources. In particular, by identifying the magnitude of impact on group task performance, it identi® es the level of managerial effort required to implement a particular IS innovation.

We begin by identifying the different assumptions implicit in the two theories and, based on these differences, unify the two theories within a contingent framework. Next, we select one context in the uni® ed theory and develop a hypothesis predicting the relative power of the two theories to explain successful implementation. Testing the proposed theory within one context serves as a preliminary validation that justi® es further investigations. The hypothesis is tested using data collected from the end-users of a computerized human resource information system introduced across multiple sites of a State health system in Australia. The sample characteristics and measurement instruments are described in the methodology section. As predicted, we ® nd that, within the context studied, innovation characteristics are more important than implementation process for the success of this IS innovation, thus providing support for the uni-® ed theory proposed here. Finally, the implications for theory and managerial action are discussed.

## Theory and hypotheses

The literature on IS implementation has investigated the effects of a wide range of variables drawn primarily from two theoretical domains,\* innovation characteristics and the implementation process (Ginzberg, 1980). Typically, the two dominant theories have been researched independently and are seen as competing explanations for implementation success (Attewell, 1992). Here, we propose that the two theories are not competing, but contingent explanations that hold within particular contexts de® ned by the different assumptions implicit in the two theories. Below, we ® rst identify these assumptions and consider how they impact differently on end-user performance. We then develop a contingent framework of contexts within which to integrate the two theories.

## Innovation characteristics and implementation process theories

## Innovation characteristics theory

The literature which investigates the effect of innovation characteristics typically assumes that end-user adoption of IS innovations is based on an evaluation of innovation characteristics. Early diffusion of innovation research identi® ed compatibility, relative advantage, complexity, triability and observability as characteristics of an innovation that in¯ uence its adoption (Rogers, 1962). Subsequent research provided empirical support for compatibility, relative advantage and complexity (Tornatzky and Klein, 1982).

Recently, Davis (1989) extended Rogers’ diffusion framework using a broad theoretical framework to propose \`perceived usefulness’ as a key innovation characteristic in¯ uencing use. This interpretation of the innovation characteristics framework not only avoids the confounding of compatibility and relative advantage (Moore and Benbasat, 1991), but also clari® es that end-users’ perception of innovation characteristics is integrally linked to the context of their task and role (Ramiller, 1994). Goodhue and Thompson (1995) also propose a task related conceptualization of innovation characteristics wherein task-technology ® t in¯ uences utilization and performance. Following from this, we propose that end-user adoption is in¯ uenced by an evaluation of two sets of innovation characteristics ± task relevance, the extent to which the end-user perceives the innovation is relevant to his/her task and role, and task usefulness, the extent to which the end-user believes it enhances task performance.

The different variants of innovation characteristics theory share a common assumption regarding the context in which the end-user adopts the innovation: independent adoption of individual use technologies,

similar to that underlying classical diffusion theory (Fichman, 1992). Within this context, the innovation is used independently and end-users accrue bene® ts through individual use. Further, organizational gains are the pooled sum of individual gains (Thompson, 1967). Consequently, innovation characteristics theory identi® es and explains the impacts of the innovation on the end-user’s task at the individual, rather than the group or organization, level of analysis. Support for the contingent effect of innovation characteristics theory is provided by Fichman (1992) who examined 18 studies of IS implementation and concluded that the theory found strong support only when the context met the assumptions of classical diffusion theory.

## Implementation process theory

In contrast, implementation process theory is concerned with the in¯ uence of managerial action on the enduser’s adoption decision. Here, implementation process refers to the organizational effort to diffuse an IS innovation within a user community. The effort covers the organizational and managerial resources expended on activities designed to promote novel behaviours among end-users and to diminish the forces opposing successful implementation (Kwon and Zmud, 1987).

There is considerable theoretical and empirical support for the role of managerial in¯ uence in the successful implementation of IS innovations. For example, Leonard-Barton (1987) ® nds that managerial supervision of end-users is needed because they resist change and trial an innovation only if prompted by their managers. There is also evidence that end-users await signals of support from top management before deciding to spend effort on learning an innovation (Zmud, 1984). Favourable management attitudes towards an innovation, signalled by appropriate incentives and sanctions, have also been found to be associated with implementation success (Howard and Mendelow, 1991). CEO and top management support have also been found to be correlated with successful implementation (Meyer and Goes, 1988; Guimaraes et al., 1992). The absence of such signals of support is likely to decrease the chances of successful implementation. In addition, other implementation processes including organizational efforts in providing training, infrastructure, consulting and technical support services (Howard and Mendelow, 1991; Ramiller, 1994; Klein and Sorra, 1996), are related to successful implementation.

Implementation process theory was developed, in part, in response to the perceived limitations of the innovation characteristics theory which was developed in a nonorganizational setting (Bayer and Melone, 1989). For instance, Tornatzky and Fleischer (1990) argue that two assumptions central to Rogers’ diffusion of innovation theory (1962), namely the invariance of the innovation across the population of potential adopters and the homogeneity of potential adopters, do not hold in the case of innovation implementation. Similarly, Attewell (1992) argues that the processes of information ¯ ow, communication and uncertainty reduction, which are central to diffusion theory, have only a limited role to play in organizational adoption. Instead, managerial actions and management support play a key role (Leonard-Barton and Deschamps, 1988). Further, Attewell (1992) argues that complex IS innovations are \`technologically demanding, fragile and lumpy’, unlike the reliable, commodity-like innovations considered in diffusion theory. Implementing such innovations is an uncertain process of knowledge discovery, skill formation and the mutual adaptation of the technology and the organization (Mankin et al., 1985; Attewell, 1992). Managerial commitment plays a critical role in the uncertain process of adoption by facilitating solutions to the numerous contingencies that arise during the various stages of implementation (Sauer, 1993; Sabherwal and Elam, 1995).

Implementation process theory, therefore, focuses on the effect of various managerial and organizational in¯ uences. It assumes the context to be characterized by coordinated adoption of technologies by groups of heterogeneous end-users performing interdependent tasks. Within this context implementation requires coordinated adoption by a critical group of organizational members (Ginzberg, 1980). Organizational gains are the outcome of sequential or reciprocal combinations of individual behaviour (Thompson, 1967). The critical issue is the replacement of existing task interdependencies by a new set of task interdependencies, and the coordination of task changes both within and across task groups. Implementing innovations within this context requires managerial and organizational interventions to ensure coordinated adoption by heterogeneous groups (Ginzberg, 1980; Klein and Sorra, 1996). Consequently, implementation process theory identi® es and explains the impacts of the innovation on the end-user’s task at the group level of analysis.

## A uni® ed theory

From the above it follows that the two theories identify and explain impacts of the innovation on endusers’ task at different levels of analysis. Innovation characteristics theory identi® es and explains the impact on performance at the individual level of analysis, whereas implementation process theory identi® es and explains the impact on performance at the group level of analysis. We propose that the levels of impact identi® ed by the two theories, namely, impact on individual task performance and impact on group task performance, represent two distinct dimensions that characterize the context (Figure 1).

We integrate the two theories within the above framework of contexts by proposing that the relative contribution of the two theories is contingent upon the context. Elaborating on the framework of Figure 1, Quadrant I is characterized by low individual level impacts as well as low group level impacts, such as in the implementation of a RAM Doubler package ± both innovation characteristics and implementation processes have small effects. Quadrant II is characterized by high individual level impacts but low group level impacts, such as in the implementation of a Decision Support System for Portfolio Managers ± innovation characteristics have a large effect but implementation processes have a small effect. Quadrant III is characterized by low individual level impacts but high group level impacts, such as in the implementation of a Financial MIS package ± innovation characteristics have a small effect but implementation processes have a large effect. Finally, Quadrant IV is characterized by high individual level impacts as well as high group level impacts, such as in the implementation of a Materials Requirement Planning package ± both innovation characteristics and implementation processes have large effects.

H1: In the context of high individual level impact and low group level impact innovations, the effect on successful implementation of innovation characteristics is stronger than that of implementation process.

To test Hypothesis 1, we control for the effects of other factors that have been found to be related to implementation success. Previous research suggests that the analysis should control for the effects of individua characteristics and informal support (Leonard-Barton and Deschamps, 1988). Individual characteristics include variables such as personal innovativeness, computer skill, and performance, rather than general personality types or demographic variables (Leonard-Barton and Deschamps, 1988). Unlike the implementation process and innovation characteristics variables, which are the immediate outcome of managerial action, these sets of variables are generally not subject to shortterm manipulation. At a minimum, they act as control variables when testing the above hypothesis. More importantly, as the secondary objective of this paper, their effects are analysed to add to the cumulative research ® ndings in this area.

## Methodology

## Research site

This research studies the implementation of a computerized Human Resources Information System, called Datapower, in an Area Health Service of the publicly funded New South Wales State health system in Australia. The Area Health Service is composed of a large teaching hospital and a number of small hospitals and clinics. Though the Human Resources (HR) func-

tion is centralized in the Area Administration, a large part of the work associated with the HR function is decentralized. This is because the requirements of the HR function vary across departments, such as Finance and Nursing, and across locations, such as the teaching hospital and the community clinics. In addition, the HR function is carried out at different hierarchical levels across those departments and locations. Consequently, there is little consistency in the execution or the reporting relationships for the decentralized component of the HR function supported by Datapower, the effective implementation of which is the subject of this research.

Datapower provides routine personnel related information, such as records, leave and entitlements. It also provides department level information on personnel related costs. This information is useful for departmental managers as the responsibility for managing staff budgets had been centralized to the departmental level. Prior to Datapower, the extent of computerization in the Area Health Service was very low. For most users, this was their ® rst exposure to an on-line computer-based information system.

Users obtain information regarding their unit by logging onto the central mainframe computer directly from their personal computers. They then use the various functions in the package to obtain the information and reports they require. Use of Datapower is voluntary and individual users use it with varying levels of complexity and sophistication. Users have the option of obtaining the information manually from the HR department, as they did prior to the introduction of Datapower. It follows that Datapower is an individual use technology which can be independently adopted. Its implementation context therefore falls into Quadrant II of Figure 1, which is characterized by high individual level impacts but low group level impacts.

## Sample

To study the implementation of Datapower, a questionnaire was mailed to all the 133 potential users of Datapower in the Area Health Service. A total of 96 responses was returned out of which 29 responses were incomplete and were excluded from the analysis. Responses were excluded if any of the variables in the model could not be calculated due to missing data. The missing responses were spread almost evenly across the variables. To evaluate the validity threat due to exclusion of responses, a supplementary analysis including all the 96 responses was performed by substituting the missing data with mean values. The results of this analysis are substantially the same as those reported here. Hence, the analysis is based on 67 responses which were suitable for analysis. The distribution of respondents is similar to their distribution in the population of 133

Datapower users. Thirty-two responses were from the main hospital and 35 from the smaller hospitals and clinics. Of these, 25 respondents were from Administration, 24 from Nursing, 8 from Allied Health, 7 from Support Services and 3 from other departments.

## Operationalization and measurement of variables

The research design modi® es and extends the model of secondary adoption tested by Leonard-Barton and Deschamps (1988). This research strategy has the merit of contributing towards a cumulative tradition in IS research and generating ® ndings which are comparable across studies. We extend Leonard-Barton and Deschamps’ model, which includes implementation process, individual characteristics and informal support variables, to include innovation characteristics variables in the model to be tested (Figure 2). Variations on this general model have been widely used in previous research (see, for example, Lucas, 1978; Maish, 1979; Fuerst and Cheny, 1982; Sanders and Courtney, 1985; Davis, 1989; Cooper and Zmud, 1990; Howard and Mendelow, 1991; Thompson et al., 1991; Guimaraes et al., 1992).

The measures used in this study are adapted from Leonard-Barton and Deschamps (1988), but differ in two respects from their study. First, we improve upon their measures for use, training, performance and network support to obtain measures with higher internal reliability. Second, two variables used in their study, subjective importance of task and task related skill, were excluded from this study. Subjective importance of task could not be adapted to the particular innovation studied here and, therefore, was excluded. Consequently, task related skill was also excluded as it is derived from the subjective importance of task measure. This is not expected to have a signi® cant impact on the results of this study as Leonard-Barton and Deschamps report nonsigni® cant effects for both variables.

## Dependent variable

## Implementation success

Frequency of use is the most commonly employed measure of implementation success (Leonard-Barton and Deschamps, 1988; Davis, 1989; Davis et al., 1989; Howard and Mendelow, 1991; Hartwick and Barki, 1994). In the case of independent use innovations, such as Datapower, usage is an appropriate measure of implementation success as organizational gains accrue from individual use. This is consistent with Saga and Zmud’s (1994) elaboration of the acceptance, routinization, infusion model of implementation success (Cooper and Zmud, 1990). Saga and Zmud propose that acceptance is represented by frequency of use, which is then causally related to routinization and infusion. In the early stages of implementation, when the innovation has not yet become routinized, usage is the only available measure of implementation success. Barki and Huff (1985) also propose that use is an appropriate measure of implementation success when use is voluntary, as in the case of Datapower. ## Data analysis

The primary purpose of this paper is to test the uni® ed theory of IS implementation proposed here within one quadrant, providing initial partial validation for the theory. We do this by testing H1: In the context of high individual level impact and low group level impact innovations, the effect on implementation success of innovation characteristics is stronger than that of implementation process.

A two stage procedure is used. In the ® rst stage, we estimate the regression coef® cients of all the variables in the overall secondary adoption model (see Figure 2). In the second stage, we compare the weighted average of the regression coef® cients of the innovation characteristics variables against that of the implementation process variables. The larger the departure of the resultant contrast value from zero, the greater is the relative effect of innovation characteristics as compared to implementation process. A t-test is employed to formally test the signi® cance of the depar ture of the resultant contrast value from zero (Cohen and Cohen, 1983, p. 479± 80).

The secondary purpose of this paper is to extend our understanding of the contributions to implementation success of innovation characteristics, implementation process, individual characteristics and informal support within a single model. The contribution of a block of variables, such as innovation characteristics, is tested by estimating its semi-partial R-square in a multiple regression analysis. The semi-partial R-square of a block of variables is the additional variance in implementation success explained by that block of variables, controlling for the effect of the other three blocks in the model (Cohen and Cohen, 1983, p. 145). Separate hierarchical regressions are conducted to estimate the contributions of innovation characteristics, implementation process, individual characteristics and informal support to implementation success.

## Discussion

The results of this study show that, within a context characterized by high individual level impact and low group level impact, innovation characteristics make a strong contribution to implementation success while the contribution of implementation process is weak. These ® ndings provide initial support for the uni® ed theory of implementation success proposed here. In addition, an inspection of the results in Table 6 shows that the ® ndings can be partitioned into a signi® cant independent in¯ uence of innovation characteristics and a nonsigni® cant independent in¯ uence of implementation process. This pattern maps directly onto the arguments advanced in support of the development of H1. Given that the former is consistent with the extant literature, it is the latter, the nonsigni® cant in¯ uence of implementation process, which effectively provides evidence for the uni® ed theory. Therefore, we examine in detail the potential validity threats to the latter ® nding. We then examine the results of earlier studies which provide additional support for the uni® ed theory. We also discuss the implications of the nonsigni® cant ® ndings for individual characteristics and informal support. Finally, we discuss the implications of the ® ndings for theory and practice.

## A uni® ed theory

The results presented in Table 6 show that innovation characteristics have a strong signi® cant independent effect on implementation success. Further, the results of the regression analysis (Table 4) show that both task relevance and task usefulness are signi® cant and, therefore, make unique independent contributions to implementation success. This is consistent with the fundamental premise of the secondary adoption model that innovation implementation is a process of internal diffusion arising out of numerous adoption decisions taken by end-users in the context of their tasks and roles (Kwon and Zmud, 1987; Leonard-Barton and Deschamps, 1988; Ramiller, 1994), and the uni® ed contingent theory developed here. According to this theory, end-user adoption of individual use innovations (Quadrant II) is driven primarily by the extent to which the IS innovation is relevant and useful for end-users’ tasks and roles.

In contrast to the ® ndings for innovation characteristics, the nonsigni® cant effect of implementation process is inconsistent with the previous literature which hypothesizes a non-contingent effect for implementation process (Schultz, 1984; Leonard-Barton and Deschamps, 1988; Meyer and Goes, 1988; Howard and Mendelow, 1991; Bhattacharjee, 1996). However, the null ® nding for implementation process is consistent with the uni® ed theory developed here. Indeed, it is the null ® nding for implementation process, as much as the signi® cant ® nding for innovation characteristics, which supports the uni® ed theory.

Since the operationalization of implementation process covers the key components recommended in the literature, the lack of support for the implementation process hypothesis cannot be considered spurious. As the null ® nding is both critical to the uni® ed theory and contrary to extant theory, we examined two possible validity threats. (Another possible validity threat, arising from studying a single organization, was also examined. The sample statistics presented in Table 3 indicate no evidence of range restriction on the measurement of management support, management urging or organizational support variables across the user department. This is not surprising as the data were collected from respondents across multiple sites and from multiple departments. The absence of range restriction limits the validity threat arising from studying a single organization). First, we show that the null ® nding is unlikely to be a function of the power of the tests. Second, we examine methodological differences between this research and previous research reporting signi® cant ® ndings for implementation process.

First, it is possible that, given the number of variables investigated and the size of the sample, the null ® nding for implementation process is a function of the low power of the test. A post hoc power analysis was conducted following the procedure suggested by Cohen and Cohen (1983, p. 154± 64). This analysis indicates that for a sample size of 67, the tests conducted have a power of 0.97 for a \`large’ size effect and 0.73 for a \`medium’ size effect. Results reported in previous studies (see, for example, Zmud, 1984; Dewar and Dutton, 1986; Leonard-Barton and Deschamps, 1988; Davis, 1989; Igbaria et al., 1990; Guimaraes et al., 1992) suggest that the effect sizes being investigated are medium to large. The results reported in these studies suggest reasonable conservative estimates of 0.10 for the incremental R-square contributed by each block and 0.60 for the R-square for the full model used in this study (Figure 2). These estimates indicate a medium to large effect size of 0.25, which corresponds to a power of 0.93 for this study. This exceeds the acceptable power range of 0.70± 0.90 recommended by Cohen and Cohen (1983, p. 162) for this type of research, suggesting that the tests do not suffer from low power.

Second, we examine the possibility that methodological differences could account for the difference in ® ndings with previous literature which ® nds support for implementation process. In fact, Table 6 does report weak evidence for an implementation process effect. While the strong test for such an effect, controlling for all other variables, is nonsigni® cant, the zeroorder R-square for an implementation process main effect is signi® cant. However, the semi-partial R-square for the entire block controlling for the other blocks is nonsigni® cant. This suggests that the signi® cant zeroorder R-square of implementation process may be due to the covariance of implementation process variables with other variables. This indeed is the case. Physical Access correlates signi® cantly with Innovativeness and Skill; and Management Urging and Physical Access have weak correlations with both Task Relevance and

Task Usefulness. The R-square of the Implementation Process block drops from 0.18 $( \phi < 0 . 0 1 )$ to 0.06 $( \phi > 0 . 0 5 )$ when Task Relevance, Task Usefulness and Innovativeness and Skill are introduced in the model.

This elaboration of the results in Table 6 suggests a technical argument which could explain some of the signi® cant results reported previously for implementation process. The results in Table 5 show that conclusions based on the zero-order R-square, which ignores the effect of other variables, can be different from those based on the semi-partial R-square, which controls for the effect of other variables. In which case, controlling for other variables there is no statistical support for implementation process, although there is weak support when the effect of other variables is ignored. A weak hypothesis testing strategy of this latter nature may have contributed to the signi® cant results reported for implementation process.

## Previous research

The null ® ndings for implementation process prompted us to re-examine the previous empirical literature to better locate the ® ndings of this study with reference to earlier ® ndings. An initial examination of the literature found that null ® ndings for an implementation process main effect were also reported by Ginzberg (1981), Lee (1986), Leonard-Barton and Deschamps (1988) and Goodhue and Thompson (1995). (Though Leonard-Barton and Deschamps (1988) report the direct effect of managerial in¯ uence to be nonsigni® cant, they do ® nd some evidence for the moderating effect of indi vidual characteristics on the relationship between managerial behaviour and implementation success. As part of a broader research programme, we tested the moderating hypothesis as an interaction effect between managerial behaviour and individual differences. The interaction effect was found to be nonsigni® cant. Methodological differences could explain the difference in ® ndings. Leonard-Barton and Deschamps (1988) used the sub-sample methodology to test the moderating hypotheses whereas we used moderated regression, as suggested by Franz and Robey (1986) and Stone-Romero and Anderson (1994). Furthermore, Leonard-Barton and Deschamps’ did not test the differences in sub-group betas to detect the presence of a moderating effect. An examination of their reported results shows that only 3 of the 24 incremental R-squares for management support and management urging are signi® - cant $(  p \leq 0 . 0 5 )$ , implying that all the differences in incremental R-squares between the sub-groups were nonsigni® cant (see Table 3, p. 1260). In which case, the ® ndings of both studies are consistent and provide no support for the moderating hypothesis and, there fore, for a second order effect of implementation process on implementation success. These results are available from the authors. The ® ndings of our study are not unique. Nonsigni® cant ® ndings for implementation process have been reported in the literature, but their implications have not been recognized.

The uni® ed theory proposed here explains these nul ® ndings, which provides further support for our argument. We speculate that null or weak ® ndings are obtained in studies where the group impacts of the innovation are low and where, according to the theory, the effect of implementation process variables is likely to be weak. ## Informal support and individual characteristics

To complete the discussion of the ® ndings, we widen the analysis to include the effects of informal support and individual characteristics. Table 6 reports a signi® cant zero-order R-square for informal support, but a nonsigni® cant semipartial R-square. This suggests that the variance that informal support shares with implementation success covaries with other variables in the model. An examination of the correlation matrix (Table 3) shows that this indeed is the case. Network Support correlates signi® cantly with both Task Relevance (r = 0.29) and Task Usefulness (r = 0.38), and Grapevine correlates weakly with Task Usefulness $( r = 0 . 2 3 )$ . A test of the Informal Support effect controlling for Innovation Characteristics ® nds Informal Support to be nonsigni® - cant. This suggests that the effect of Informal Support is mediated by Task Relevance and Task Usefulness.

The nonsigni® cant ® nding for individual differences is consistent with the ® ndings of other studies which have found nonsigni® cant effects for individual differences. For instance, in a meta-analysis covering 33 studies, Alavi and Joachimsthaler (1992) estimated the effects of cognitive style and personality to account for only 1% to 2% of the variance in DSS implementation success. The exception to the null ® ndings for individual differences is the weak evidence for the effect of performance reported in Table 3. This is consistent with the ® ndings of Leonard-Barton and Deschamps (1988) and Lucas (1975) who also found a weak negative effect for performance. The interpretation of these ® ndings is that some ex ante high performers are less dependent on the IS innovation, use it less frequently and ® nd it to be less useful than ex ante poor performers. This conclusion is consistent with the premise of the secondary adoption model that end-user adoption is largely motivated by expectations of performance gain (Davis, 1989).

While adding to our knowledge of the end-user adoption process, these ® ndings raise an interesting question for future research. This is that the effects of informal support and individual characteristics may also be contingent. First, the effect of informal support is mediated by task relevance and task usefulness, whose in¯ uence is contingent. Second, the negative effect of prior performance is more likely to hold for individual than group adoption processes.

## Implications for theory and research

Current research treats the innovation characteristics theory and the implementation process theory as two competing explanations for the successful implementation of IS innovations. Recent research extends the former theory and proposes that the effect of innovation characteristics is contingent upon a ® t between the implementation context and the context of classical diffusion theory (Fichman, 1992). This study extends and complements that research by proposing that the effect of implementation process is also contingent upon the context. Further, it distinguishes the level of analysis addressed by the two theories and integrates them within a uni® ed theory.

In addition to resolving the theoretical inconsistencies in the current literature, the uni® ed theory also provides an explanation for some of the null ® ndings for implementation process reported in earlier literature. Future research should carefully identify the implementation context being studied and distinguish the individual and group level impacts of the IS innovation. In particular, research is needed in Quadrants III and IV in Figure 2.

## Implications for managers

The ® ndings of this study have important implications for managers. The results show that, in the context of individual adoption of low task interdependence innovations, the relative contribution of innovation characteristics to implementation success is higher than that of implementation process. In which case, managers need to pay more attention to getting the innovation characteristics right, as compared to getting the implementation process right. Managerial in¯ uence in implementation of such innovations is higher at the design stage, when the innovation characteristics are determined, than at the implementation stage. In such cases managers need to focus on the process of design. User involvement and expert input are two of the strategies available for ensuring the appropriate innovation characteristics. Subsequent to the design stage, managerial decisions and behaviours have little in¯ uence on the implementation success of independent use IS innovations.

The second implication for managers relates to the processes involved in designing the innovation. The question is who is and, perhaps more important, who should be involved in the process of designing the innovation characteristics. The conventional answer is high user participation (Hartwick and Barki, 1994). In our study, in contrast, the system was developed in a highly autocratic manner with very little user involvement. However, the senior manager responsible for developing and implementing the system studied here had a long association with and deep knowledge about the HR function and also with the automation of the HR function. In this case, expert input appears to have substituted for the role of user involvement in designing task relevance and usefulness into the system.

## Conclusions

This research has developed a theory of IS implementation in which the contributions of innovation characteristics and implementation process are contingent upon the implementation context. A uni® ed theory is proposed which integrates the innovation characteristics and implementation process theories. This theory is tested within a particular implementation context characterized by high individual level impacts but low group level impacts. As hypothesized, innovation characteristics were found to make a strong contribution to implementation success. In contrast, the independent contribution of implementation process was small and nonsigni® cant.

The results have important implications for theory and practice. They suggest that implementation researchers need to account for the effect of implementation context and, in particular, distinguish between the individual level and group level impacts of IS innovations. For managers, the ® ndings suggest that they need to develop strategies, and allocate resources between the design stage and the implementation stage, contingent upon the implementation context. For low task interdependence innovations the design of the innovation is critical to implementation success and more attention needs to be paid to the design stage than the implementation stage. Conversely, high task interdependence innovations require a high level of managerial effort during the implementation stage.
