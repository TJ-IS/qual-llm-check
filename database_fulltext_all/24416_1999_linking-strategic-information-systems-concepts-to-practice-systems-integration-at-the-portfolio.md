# Linking strategic information systems concepts to practice: systems integration at the portfolio level

DOUG HAMILTON

School of Information Management and Systems, Monash University, Melbourne, Australia

Surveys during the past two decades have consistently shown that many of the most critical management concerns with information systems arise at the portfolio, rather than application, level. Architecture-driven planning with a view to the implementation of integrated information systems structures has been widely canvassed as a means of dealing with portfolio-level issues, particularly the problems of inconsistent data and uncoordinated processes which have affected many organizations in recent years. The bene® ts anticipated from this type of initiative have been such that many organizations have persisted with architecture-based strategies despite reports of high failure rates. This paper reports on some research into IS integration planning in the organization Telstra (formerly Telecom Australia)) during a period of 40 years. The research was conducted as an interpretive case study, with practitioners’ assessments and perspectives on IS planning being incorporated in the data analysis. The focus of the study was on four critical assumptions, which were identi® ed during an initial review of the theoretical literature as having to hold true for an organization to expect success with this form of planning. The study revealed that none of these assumptions held reliably throughout the period studied, a point fundamental to understanding why relatively little progress was made towards published integration targets. The paper then canvasses the view that though traditionally understood as blueprints for implementation, information systems architectures could be reconceptualized as knowledge assets, with independent value as sources of core planning concepts. The conclusion drawn is that their use in this type of role could facilitate the achievement of many, if not all, of the bene® ts anticipated from more comprehensive approaches.

## Introduction

This paper is concerned with the portfolio-level planning and implementation of integrated information systems structures (IS), driven by the development of information architectures. The research was based on a longitudinal case study of IS integration practice in the telecommunications organization Telstra (formerly Telecom Australia) during the period 1959± 98.

IS integration has strong appeal as a rational IS planning target, but research to date has indicated that the achievement of integration targets can pose dif® cult problems (Brittain, 1991; Goodhue et al., 1992; Brown, 1994). The value of prescriptive architecture-driven planning has as a consequence been questioned, though management surveys indicate that the development and implementation of information architectures continues to rank highly as an IT management concern (Brancheau et al., 1996). The aim in this study was to complement previous research by focusing on understanding the ways in which the basic concept of integration was interpreted and applied by different participants in processes of planning and implementation.

An initial review of the literature, analysed in relation to a logical model of IS integration processes, indicated that the successful implementation of an integrated IS structure must in practice depend on a number of critical assumptions holding well. The case study ® ndings were that only one of these assumptions held to any great extent even for part of the case study period, and that as a result many planning and implementation activities were largely ineffective.

The study indicated however that integration remained a guiding principle with practitioners involved with IS planning, even those who had been associated with at least one major planning failure. Feedback from the study participants was therefore analysed with a view to isolating some of the key issues involved, which were then utilized in the development of a revised process model. The revised model re¯ ects a major shift in perspective, based on the idea that IS architecture development and management should be decoupled from systems development and implementation processes. In this view, the IS architecture’s role would be that of a knowledge structure, valuable for representing the structural impacts of individual IS decisions, and as an aid in conceptualizing systems planning objectives. The architecture would not however, be developed as a blueprint for implementation, and rigorous integration (through the use of common de® nitions across all systems) would occur to an extremely limited extent only.

The research was conducted within the interpretive paradigm for disciplined inquiry. The approach taken was grounded in concepts which have evolved within the philosophical movement known as neopragmatism, an area of theory developed primarily by a group of American philosophers including W.V. Quine, Donald Davidson, Paul Feyarabend, and Richard Rorty (Murphy, 1990). Part of the impetus for the study was the researcher’s past participation in IS planning as Telstra employee, which resulted in a direct awareness of the practical dif® culties involved in generating effective action on the basis of what appear to be logically compelling concepts.

## The concept of information systems integration

IS integration can be understood in practical terms as the structural coherence of a set of applications and databases (Schwartz, 1970). Structural coherence can be achieved through the adoption of common data, process and technology de® nitions. The set of corporate information systems is conceptualized as one logical system, physically instantiated in a number of applications developed to conform to the set of standard de® nitions. Integration must in practice be a partial phenomenon, with the extent of integration achieved dependent on the scope of corporate de® nition achieved at any particular time, and the extent to which the corporate de® nitions have actually been embedded in systems in production. ## Critical assumptions

How viable is the architectural approach to strategic IS action? A number of researchers have suggested that the approach may be severely ¯ awed as a practical proposition (Earl, 1993), and that architecture-driven planning will only be useful within a severely constrained domain of action, and under very favourable circumstances (Goodhue et al., 1992). Survey ® ndings suggest however that many IS concerns are cross-portfolio in nature, and depend for their solution on an organization’s capacity to maintain information and process coherence across a number of applications (Brancheau et al., 1996). It has been stated that solving the information architecture conundrum is the most important issue confronting IT managers (Segars and Grover, 1996).

The approach taken to the study was predicted on the view that the development of a methodological approach to integration necessarily implied a view of the world in which the implementation of integrated IS structures was pragmatically possible. My analysis indicated that one explicit and three implicit presuppositions were generally evident in the methodological literature (Martin, 1990; Scheer, 1992; Spewak and Hill, 1993). The implicit assumptions were identi® ed on the basis of an absence of concern with issues that might otherwise be deemed to be relevant to major IT application projects. The relationships between these assumptions and an organizational decision to commit to a large-scale architecture-driven initiative are shown in Fig. 3.

Each assumption is required for the initiation of large-scale implementation action to be justi® able. The importance of the assumptions lies in their relevance to questions of achievability. While the concept of integration may legitimately be involved for its explanatory power in the analysis of IS portfolio problems (Martin, 1990), the logical strength of the concept does not necessarily imply the existence of means for the implementation of an integrated portfolio design.

The explicit assumption of data structure stability has been stated quite clearly in the theoretical literature, and amounts to the view that core organizational data, information, and associated process structures are suf® - ciently stable in the long-term for an integrated IS structure to be implemented, managed and maintained (Martin, 1982, p. 4, 1990, p. 16). There is a consensus among researchers that the development and implementation of an IS architecture are extremely timeconsuming activities (Goodhue et al., 1992; Earl, 1993; Segars and Grover, 1996), and the assumption of stability is required from the change management perspective. Volatility is incompatible with an initiative requiring years to complete.

The ® rst implicit assumption is that the initiative will add suf® cient robust organizational value to more than offset planning and implementation costs. This is often stated as an axiom rather than an assumption (Martin, 1990), but in practice business management usually requires something more formal (Willcocks and Lester, 1996). A considerable body of theory has developed around ways of estimating and measuring IS value-added (Parker and Benson, 1988; Willcocks and Lester, 1996), but there is little evidence that the innovative techniques recommended are as yet being widely adopted.

The second implicit assumption is that techniques adequate to translate information architecture concepts into realized structures are available to IS practitioners. What this reduces to as a concern for practice is the more speci® c assumption that processes for dealing with information architecture obligations at the project level can be integrated with techniques for the development and installation of individual applications and databases.

The third implicit assumption is that the technology required to enable an integrated structure to be realized exists in an accessible and applicable form. This is a widespread assumption in IS theory, perhaps most obviously in relation to business re-engineering theory, where IT is identi® ed as the unproblematic enabler of organizational change (Hammer and Champy, 1993). The critical aspect of the assumption is that it shifts IT issues outside the domain of the problem to be solved. The importance of the distinction between IT integration and integration of the technology infrastructure has not always been made fully clear in discussions of IT as an enabler (for instance in Hammer and Champy, 1993; Venkatraman et al., 1993; Venkatraman, 1994), and is therefore reviewed in a little more detail here.

## The integration of technology infrastructures

The distinction between IT integration and IS integration is important for several reasons. These include particularly the need to distinguish between interworking at the IT infrastructural level of networks, computers and operating software, and interworking at the applications level dealing with organizational business processes and information handling.

Essentially, the integration of a technology infrastructure is concerned with the provision of a seamless mechanism for the transmission, processing and storage of information (Broadbent and Weill, 1997). The scope of infrastructure planning is extremely broad in a modern organization; planning for data transmission alone may require consideration of the roles to be played by a range of telecommunications products including LANs, MANs, WANs, the internet, packet-switching networks, dedicated lines, broadband networks, voice networks and ISDN. Implementing a fully integrated structure, capable of being represented to users as a uni-® ed set of transmission and storage services, clearly poses great and continuing technical dif® culties. The aim is therefore conceptually straightforward, involving the provision of a facility which maximizes an organization’ s communication capabilities and options. An access link can always be inhibited as required, but there are no options if the link cannot be established in the ® rst place. The Internet is an example of the extent to which the extension of such structures can become a self-sustaining process. Once basic interconnection standards were established and some momentum had built up, the logic in favour of conforming to connection and transmission standards became self-evident.

IS integration presents a radically different set of issues. An information system is oriented towards the ef® cient performance of a set of speci® ed tasks, rather than the provision of a general-purpose enabling facility. Information systems are therefore about de® ning and restricting the space of possibilities of action. Even systems designed purely for support ® ts this mould; they represent the end result of a process which aims at reducing the amount of information that has to be considered in a particular context (Wildavsky,

1983). Information systems are therefore innately connected to concepts of control and order. IS integration at the portfolio level seeks to take the application of control principles a step further, and to impose an additional level of order beyond that possible with a single application.

Making this distinction was critical to developing an understanding of some ® ndings from the case study discussed later in the paper. Whereas IS integration in the study organization Telstra has proceeded in ® ts and starts over four decades, comparatively recent IT integration plans have been stunningly successful, saving the organization many millions of dollars (Broadbent and Weill, 1997; Butler and Weill, 1997). The reason most frequently canvassed for this vast difference in impact was based on drawing a distinction between the physical reality of an integrated information processing network, and the abstract nature of corporate data and process de® nitions. The logical arguments in favour of IT integration in Telstra were accompanied in practice by the emergence during the 1990s of a critical mass of opinion in favour of action, which led to the general acceptance of corporate IT standards and some dramatic improvements in IT costs structures. In contrast, at no time during the study period did such a critical mass emerge in the IS context.

## Interpretivism and neo-pragmatism

The research strategy involved undertaking an interpretive study of IS planning in Telstra during the fortyyear period 1959± 98. Studies of this type remain relatively rare in information systems research, though they are starting to become more frequent (Walsham, 1993). The interpretive approach, grounded in this case in neopragmatist concepts of community and the \`warranted assertibility’ of theoretical concepts, was seen to offer particular bene® ts in relation to the analysis of ® ndings on planning, where there is inevitably a strong element of subjectivity in evaluations of concepts and outcomes.

A major concern for planning researchers has been the dif® culty of isolating extrinsic from intrinsic factors in the analysis of issues. IS action at the strategic level occurs within a context in which a number of initiatives competing for scarce resources, including management attention, will normally be in train at any one time (Powell and Dent-Micallef, 1997). Because of this, it can be extremely dif® cult to determine the viability of strategic propositions in anything other than abstract terms. Plans may fail for a variety of reasons connected neither to the quality of the plan prepared, not to any defects in the triggering concept. Core concepts are not always subjected to critical evaluation, something which

seems desirable when the rates of failure in application can be high; more than 70% in the case of business reengineering (Martinsons, 1995), and perhaps even higher for architectural planning initiatives (Goodhue et al., 1991; Earl, 1993; Segars and Grover, 1996).

The interpretive approach to research provides a means of gaining in-depth insights into complex issues. The foundational assumption is that the use of hermeneutic processes which explore a range of different perspectives will lead to an enhanced comprehension of events in the round (Gadamer, 1989). One criticism which is frequently addressed to the interpretive approach is however that it can lead to a form of \`shallow relativism’ (Vattimo, 1997, p. 7), in which all interpretations are accorded equal value, or on some readings no value at all (Norris, 1997).

This view can be debated in a number of ways. Walsham, for instance, states that \`there are interesting and less interesting ways to view the world’ and that the importance of a theoretical perspective is related to the strength and size of the group for whom the theory is considered to be of value (Walsham, 1993, p. 6). The arguments put depend to an extent on the philosophical position adopted; Collinn distinguishes between a range of different interpretive stances stemming from theories of the social construction of knowledge, ethnomethodology, cultural relativism, phenomenology and hermeneutics among others (Collinn, 1997).

Neopragmatism directly confronts the issue of relativism by taking the denial of the existence of absolute truth to the logical conclusion that \`relativism’ is in effect a mistaken and misleading term. If there are no ahistorical truths, there is nothing absolute for any belief to be relative to. Rorty substitutes the concepts of \`solidarity’ and \`warranted assertibility’ for those of \`objectivity’ and \`truth’ , but denies relativism:

\`relativism’ is the view that every belief is as good as every other. No one holds the view. Except for the occasional cooperative freshman, one cannot ® nd anybody who says that two incompatible opinions on an important topic are equally good. The philosophers who get called \`relativists’ are those who say that the grounds for choosing between opinions are less algorithmic than had been thought.

(Rorty, 1982, p. 166)

Philosophical positions are essentially tendentious, and neopragmatist views are currently the subject of heated debate and criticism (for instance Norris, 1997). The strength of the neopragmatist position is taken here to be coherence it brings to the concept of a \`fragmented ad-hocracy’, a term frequently applied to the IS domain, where a pluralist approach to the selection of research methods has strong support (Banville and Landry, 1989; Landry and Banville, 1992). In the pragmatic view, a community (national, scienti® c, or others) is distinguished by a set of shared beliefs and practices, which may take any form which meets community standards for rigor and justi® cation:

to say that what is rational for us now to believe may not be true, is simply to say that somebody may come up with a better idea . . . the desire for objectivity is not the desire to escape the limitations of one’s community, but simply the desire for as much intersubjective agreement as possible, the desire to extend the reference of \`us’ as far as we can.

(Rorty, 1990, p. 23)

On this view, the issue of method is secondary to that of content in the development of understanding. When claims for truth are based on warranted assertibility, the growth of disciplinary knowledge stems from what the relevant community ® nds it reasonable to accept. The chief implication of neopragmatism for IS research was taken here to be the importance attached to breadth of inquiry and critique. An emphasis in past discussions on IS research methods has been that there should be a focus on relevance as well as rigor (Keen, 1991; Turner et al., 1991), and that theory and research should aim to bene® t practice. A complementary perspective is that practitioners should also be encouraged to contribute directly to theory development through the discussion and review of theoretical claims. The links between theory and practice are naturally close in IS, and practitioners frequently have very strong views on the theoretical foundations of their discipline.

## Research approach and methods

Four ® ndings from earlier research provided the initial rationale for adopting an interpretive case study approach. These were that:

long-term IS planning tends to proceed in a discontinuous manner, under conditions of uncertainty and ambiguity (Earl, 1993)

the timeframes required for IS integration plans to be prepared and to impact on the organizational environment are in the order of three or more years (Goodhue et al., 1992)

IS planning is conducted within a web of diverse organizational in¯ uences which will affect the directions and outcomes of planning activities (Earl, 1993)

l planning objectives and outcomes are not \`given’ an unequivocal data about the organizational environment (Mintzberg, 1994).

The complexity of the research data showed that there would be value in giving participants in the study a voice in the explanation of events. They were therefore asked in interview for their understanding of events, particularly with respect to the links between concepts, plans, and outcomes.

Data collection for the study was undertaken through a combination of interviews and document analysis. Agreement to the study was formally requested from and given by Telstra, with the proviso that the organization retained the right to scrutinize any material for publication, and remove anything deemed commercially sensitive. Since Telstra’s business situation was not an issue for the research, this agreement had no impact on the scoping or conduct of the study.

Thirty people were asked to participate in the study. All agreed to do so, and made themselves available for all interviews requested. Seven people were interviewed more than once, and a total of 40 interviews were conducted. Study participants were selected on the basis of extended experience with IS planning in Telstra, with the minimum period of involvement set at two years. Seven participants had IS experience which extended back to encompass work on the COMIS plan which commenced in 1959, and were thus able to bring a very long-term perspective to bear on the issues discussed. Each interview was tape recorded, and literally transcribed as soon as possible after the interview was completed. Interview times were generally not constrained, and interviews generally continued until the topics or the participants were exhausted.

## The Telstra case study

Empirical research was conducted during the period November 1995 to September 1997. The approach taken allowed for triangulation through a combination of documentary analysis and the interviewing of people involved in IS planning-related activities during the period. The timeframe selected allowed consideration of a number of separate planning activities; initiation of the ® rst (COMIS ± the computer operations and management information system) occurred in 1959, whilst the last in the series so far was still in progress early in 1998.

The case study presented an opportunity to consider and analyse IS integration over an extended period, in a number of different incarnations, and during different phases in the evolution of IT and IS. The vastly different technical environments for which the different plans were developed ruled out any attempt to directly compare outcomes, but it nevertheless proved possible to identify thematic connections between plans based on conceptual similarities.

There was a strong element of \`opportunism’ (Riemer, 1977) in the selection of Telstra as the case site, deriving from the researcher’s experience as a Telstra employee during the period 1970± 93. This included a period of direct involvement in IS planning, most notably with the \`TIME’ initiative during 1989± 91. In general terms, previous experience with the organization was seen to bring with it a number of research bene® ts including:

straightforward access to many of the people previously or currently involved with IS planning on the basis of past working and social relationships

knowledge of past organizational events shared with many of the study participants

direct knowledge of organizational processes and structures

direct experience of some of the major planning initiatives studied

l a good understanding of the organizational language in use.

The value of Telstra as a case site stemmed both from its lengthy involvement with the commercia application of IT, and from its ongoing concern with the design of effective IS structures at a portfolio level. The organization has become a sophisticated user of IT over the past four decades; a major reason for thinking that a study of integration activities in the company could be informative was an awareness that the topic had been the subject of often heated internal debate over a lengthy period, and that progress towards integration objectives had been slow, contested, and far from linear.

## Telstra’s IS planning history

Telstra is an organization which is in the habit of planning (Moyal, 1984). Good planning has always been a prerequisite for the effective design and management of the national telecommunications network, a Telstra responsibility for most of the twentieth century. Planning for the controlled introduction of the new technology of computing was therefore the continuance of an established pattern of behaviour when the ADP Exploratory Study was initiated in 1959 (PMG, 1961). Since then Telstra has undertaken a series of IS planning studies at the strategic level, with varying degrees of success.

An initial step in the case study analysis was a macrolevel analysis of major trends in planning modes. Strategic IS plans initiated during the case study are shown in Timeline 1.

The timeline shows a recurring attempt to impose structural constraints on all or part of the IS portfolio. COMIS, the Client plan, CINDA, TIME and OSA were all plans with a very strong emphasis on achieving controlled IS integration. One of the more striking points is the ebb and ¯ ow in the tide of blueprint planning, with highly prescriptive plans being followed by periods dominated by a more pragmatic focus on projects. Modern theories of organizational dynamics suggest that this type of alternation is characteristic of situations incorporating unresolved and unresolvable tensions between opposed imperatives (Stacey, 1996), in this case between a wish to control the direction and scope of IS activities on one hand, and on the other a requirement to be seen to be expediently responsive to emerging business needs. What the pattern also indicates is an ongoing concern with the synergy of the portfolio as a whole, rather than just with individual systems.

## The outcomes of IS integration planning

More often than not, the outcomes of strategic-level planning are diffuse and dif® cult to identify. The more abstract the concepts involved, the less likely it is that a clear link can be drawn between the original plan and concrete results in the form of implemented solutions (Marris, 1987, etc.). \`Success’ becomes a highly ambiguous construct in such circumstances, and the evaluation of the results of planning will vary with each individual’s attitudes, orientation, and experience.

IS integration is a complex abstraction in a number of ways. For the majority of people involved with systems, the question of underlying application and database structures is an irrelevancy. The systems simply do their job well or poorly. The case study showed further that there were still widespread misunderstandings about the distinction between interfacing and integrating systems, even where the long-term importance of structures was understood. For many people, the integration of two systems was perceived to require the implementation of an interface between them. The basic rationale for attempting to implement the more rigorous concept of common data and process structures across a core systems portfolio was in practice an objective held and understood by relatively few people, even within the IS area. A senior manager, who had previous extensive experience as an architect, explained the issue:

that’ s a maturity thing. The knowledge of being involved with architectures is just as important as being involved with projects. But if you’ve been in strategy for ten years without working on projects, then you’ ve got no idea to help them. On the other hand if you’ve been in projects, and just had to deliver the next release, and you haven’t tried to put two or three projects together with interfaces then you’re no better off either. So it’ s a combination of those things . . . a lot of people when you start talking about integration, they just think you mean interfaces.

Assessed against publicly stated integration targets, the long series of plans listed in Timeline 1 has had remarkably little effect on macrolevel IS structures. After 40 years of IS planning activity, Telstra’s core systems are still interfaced rather than integrated where data and/or process interdependence between core applications is a business process requirement. For many people this is unequivocal evidence that the plans have failed, and a review of the study ® ndings as a whole showed that past plans tend to be quickly buried and forgotten by the organization as a whole. One senior analyst, who joined the organization just a few months after the TIME initiative was terminated, commented that:

I have no knowledge of TIME except hearing the acronym from time to time, which usually resulted in mirth or angst depending on who you were talking to. It might have been a nonproject. It vanished without trace, whereas OSA is I think still talked about although it appears to have been quietly buried as a generic notion.

Yet integration planning has produced a number of positive effects. While application-level integration objectives were not achieved, some standardized data and process components are now in use across the organization. These include a customer management structure, a product management structure, a service location database, and some rationalization of key code-sets. These results do not, however, compare at all favourably with progress on the technology infrastructure.

Data analysis indicated that the most important long-term outcomes for IS in Telstra were in the form of enhanced professional learning and understanding, and therefore essentially intangible. Comments in this regard were often quite general as in the following extract:

it’ s interesting when you look back ± you can see the old cycle of things coming through in terms of \`I know we’ve been through this before’ and we obviously have to do it again. So if you go through it a little bit, you’re advancing each time. You’re just having to cycle around but you are actually improving.

## Linking strategic information systems

More concrete illustrations were also provided in some cases, in this example with respect to a new product area:

the ® rst thing we brought in was the billing system . . . when I saw the system the ® rst thing I said was \`the service order ± I want to change the address’. They said, \`why has it got to change?’ and I said \`it has to change ± there’s no point in arguing ± how much will it cost to change the address?’ So I changed the address to a 90% ® t to all those [core] systems . . . and it was only when one part of the address was too dif® cult to change in the time that I compromised on that, and even that I regret now . . . and you only get that stuff from the architecture experience in the ® rst place, you can’t teach it.

The ® ndings on professional learning posed something of a problem for the original study, where the topic was not a major focus. It is intended at the moment that the ® ndings will contribute to a followon study into the nature and value of professional expertise in this area, and will therefore be dealt with in subsequent papers.

## Discussion

Assessed against international performance and availability benchmarks, Telstra had a highly effective set of corporate systems at the end of the case study. Commenting on the fact that the most recent planning initiative (OSA) had not delivered on a number of objectives, one manager stated that senior business management was on record as saying that systems were no longer on the agenda for change, and that this was because we’re providing in excess of 99% availability and we’re providing response times that average about 2.5 seconds. What that masks is the content of the applications and how the applications in fact interwork with one another. Why would I disagree with the CEO and other people up there that IT service is not an issue? . . . the organization at least for its current products has a set of systems that provide a sustainable level of operational support.

The common thread in the views expressed was a belief that Telstra’s core systems are individually of high quality, but that planned attempts to improve the level of synergy at the portfolio level had generally not been successful. The outcome is a set of systems which now perform very well in operational terms, but somewhat less effectively as a coherent totality. A consensus emerged that planning processes were at least partly at fault, resulting in several cases in plans which were far too ambitious for effective realization.

The case study data showed that attempts to implement integrated structures were generally based on processes structured in accordance with the model pre sented earlier (Fig. 2). In other words, the aim at the start of each plan was to follow a linear progression from the formation of an intent to integrate, through the development of a corporate-level architectural design, and on to attempts to develop applications conforming with the architecture. These attempts were uniformly ineffective. Analysis of the relevant data in this area followed two broad directions. The ® rst focused on the critical assumptions shown in Fig. 3, and led to the conclusion that none were viable throughout the case study period, although the company was well on the way to establishing an integrated technology infrastructure by the start of 1998. The second involved the development of a process model able to account more reliably for the limited success experienced with IS structures during the case study period.

## The critical assumptions revisited

The study showed that people tended to work on one plan and then move on. One strategist commented that \`I spent three years on that bloody thing [the TIME initiative]. I’ve never been tempted to do anything like it again for a second’. The lack of continuity was reinforced by the organization’s tendency to discard one initiative and simply replace it with another rather than reviewing the experience. One strategist said:

I don’t think we ever needed to start from scratch. The big mistake we continually do when I’ve looked at it, is that we ® gure that we haven’ t learned anything, and that there’s no-one there with any history and so we continually re-examine it.

The result was that, broadly speaking, each new plan was developed by a new set of planners who relied heavily on published theory and consultants’ advice to provide the conceptual underpinnings for their activities. The interviews showed that the importance of the basic assumptions listed in Fig. 1 quickly became apparent in each instance. The quotes presented in the following discussion were selected as being representative of widely held views.

## Structural stability

The assumption that core data constructs are generally stable over time is clearly required for a long-term integration initiative to be viable. Data volatility implies that the architecture will become a moving target. Frequent architectural changes impede the development of fully consistent speci® cations at the application level. One of the dif® culties with the way stability has been theoretically asserted is the level of abstraction; while all organizations must have customers (Martin, 1990), it does not follow that the concept of customer is fully stable at all levels of detail. An IS executive commented that:

I was never convinced of that [the stability of data structures] because the underlying data is driven by business needs. So you also have the problem that not only is the underlying data driven by business needs, but it’ s also in¯ uenced powerfully by personalities in the customer areas, in that you can change a person in say the accounting area, and a subset of the business objectives changes.

The dif® culty of realizing high-level abstractions in implementable constructs was widely canvassed by IS architects. Commenting on the TIME initiative, one analyst pointed out that:

data structures are reasonably stable, but they’ re still not static, and unfortunately when you have those data structures embedded in the databases and the logic of your systems, even the slower rates of change can cause you major problems . . . there was a lot of effort spent on the actual metadata modelling at the high corporate level, and it came up with these ever-constant structures which could re¯ ect anything, but they turned out not to be useful. They came out not to be implementable, or not useful in an implemented form.

## The value of integration

The length of time required for successful architecturedriven initiatives would be less signi® cant if the assumption that an integrated structure would add suf® cient value to make the effort worthwhile held up. Continuing investment in a strategic initiative requires the maintenance of an organizational belief that the activity will be bene® cial. The signi® cance of IS costs is however increasing rather than decreasing (Willcocks and Lester, 1996), and is an issue with negative implications for strategic initiatives. One manager commented that:

every organization’s got a focus on costs now. Those are pretty simplistic notions. You’d never accuse the ® nancial markets of over-analysing things, but they can certainly drive some behaviour.

A number of participants pointed out that the abstract nature of IS assets was a major problem for the assumption of value. Architecture-driven IS activities were contrasted with IT-oriented plans. An IS manager put it that:

the problem I think is that the technology architecture is tangible; you can see the boxes. But with the architectures it’ s intangible. And that makes it a dif® cult thing to sell because people can’t really see the size, and I think it’ s just that nature of the product that is going to make software so much harder to model, to visualise, to see if you’re making progress. Really, you can’ t see it.

When returns cannot be quanti® ed, value is typically assessed relative to a set of beliefs. When beliefs are not shared, it can be well nigh impossible to achieve concerted action. An IS strategist who worked on the TIME and then OSA initiatives described a fundamental difference in perspective:

I really think what I lacked, and the guys lacked, which made it more dif® cult ± we were kind of TIME fundamentalists ± was we were very principleorientated and ethical and boring, and we were dealing with people who were just trying to make money . . . so then you’ve got two vendors in there, and they’ re in there trying to make money, and we’ re in there trying to patch it [OSA] up into this glorious building.

A review of the documentation on COMIS, CINDA, TIME and OSA showed that at no stage was architectural work seen to have any intrinsic value in its own right. In the absence of substantial visible progress in delivering conforming systems, the pressure to cancel an integration is thus ever-present.

## Technical capacity to deliver integrated IS

\`Scalability’ is a pervasive theme in IS and IT theory, and arises quite naturally in relation to IS integration. Because the logic underpinning IS integration at the portfolio level is identical in its essentials with that which underpins application speci® cation and development, there is a consequent tendency to assume that it should be possible to scale application level techniques up suf® ciently to deal with the broader problem. There are however some basic problems with this view. One is that a corporate architecture group is rarely if ever in a position to control the domain of action to the extent possible at the application level. Individual projects with speci® c goals and deliverables have dynamics distinct from strategic initiatives at a corporate level; though projects may see the logical force of meeting corporate obligations, those obligations are really someone else’ s problem. Marris notes this as a generic dif® culty for planners; \`planners do not characteristically carry out their plan; everyone else only looks for a plan when they are bewildered or frustrated’ (Marris, 1987, p. 76).

In the Telstra case these dif® culties drove deeply into methodological issues. Managing the realization of corporate requirements across a number of putatively conforming projects is a complex task with both

technical and managerial components. Ways of addressing the moving target issue discussed earlier were required, as was the technical awareness to know not only when corporate interventions in project lifecycles should be made, but also how to manage them. There was a consensus among those interviewed that there were many dif® culties, which could not be foreseen, or addressed solely on the basis of project experience, and for which there were no existing methodological guidelines. As the following comments show, while methodological problems were starkly evident during the COMIS plan of the 1960s, equivalent dif® culties were still being encountered with the OSA initiative almost thirty years later.

A former executive’s view on COMIS was that:

we didn’t know what we didn’t have. We had no depth in even our technical knowledge of what information systems was, or even what our technical capabilities were.

Similar problems arose with CINDA around 1980. Another comment from the same interview was that:

we got the CINDA thing started, but the problem with CINDA was that again it had to be followed through, and nobody had an acceptable methodology to move from the initial data statements and the interactions, to getting them through to the functions and then through to the applications.

Almost a decade later, the technical realization of TIME still required methods which seemed to be unavailable or untried. An IS strategist commented:

we were trying to come up with a technical architecture, where we weren’t too sure where we were going technically. We had the idea of atomic processes and separation of the levels, but we actually didn’t know how to achieve it, how to measure it and what we’ d get out at the end of it; what would be the freedom we’d get out of that.

Finally, similar dif® culties surfaced during the OSA initiative in the 1990s. An architect’s view was that:

the central premise on which OSA was built was ¯ awed, at least at the time . . . in terms of lack of data models, a data model on that scale is just enormous and I think the data modelling team in Integration Services struggled with the scale and the scope and they were never able to get their minds around it.

## Information technology

The most plausible assumption in retrospect was that an integrated IT platform could be designed to facilitate and support systems integration. Though technology dif® culties frequently stood in the way of progress in this regard, most of them were seen to be attributable to over-ambition and outright mistakes rather than any inherent limitations in the technologies available at any given time. A senior executive, formerly a systems analyst, traced the issue right back to the COMIS plan of the 1960s:

Telecom along the lines had a bit of problem because the visionaries have always been somewhat ahead of the technology, and the technology’s been slower in catching up than they thought it would, even back to the original Z system concept behind COMIS. If it had access to DASD [direct access storage devices] a lot earlier, then the \`Z’ system would have continued to develop and allowed more integration than occurred. So people are continuing to deploy stuff ahead of technology’s ability to realise the results in industrial strength form for them.

Technological issues identi® ed in interview included the lack of an active data dictionary at the time of CINDA, the failure of vendors including IBM to deliver on promised repository technology in 1989± 90, a technical inability to support distributed databases during the early 1990s, and the continuing lack of an effective cross-platform CUA (common user access) product from the mid-1980s to the end of the study period. The common theme was that the complexity of integration requirements tended to increase over time, as new business possibilities continued to emerge, and the technology continued to evolve. IT was therefore never \`just’ an enabler, but always an intrinsic, and demanding, part of the problem. It was however seen as a problem which could be solved in principle.

## A new process model for IS integration

The focus on assumptions reveals the depth of the issues which confronted Telstra’s IS planners. An outcome of their continuing frustrations was an ongoing series of attempts to ® nd alternative ways of realizing integration objectives. Some of these alternatives were tried from time to time without ever precipitating out as a clearly enunciated public policy. Figure 5 presents a process model for IS integration which represents the researcher’s synthesis of a number of these views.

The key element in this model is that IS architecture development and maintenance activities are decoupled from application and database provisioning. In this understanding the architecture is an information asset, which can be used both to assess the impact of new systems, and as the source for speci® c constraints on implemented application structures. The model re¯ ects the importance of case study ® ndings that Telstra’s most effective structural IS initiatives were always quite small in scope and highly speci® c. The implementation of a standard recursive structure for key attributes of the customer data entity was for instance seen as a major achievement of the OSA programme of action. According to one interviewee, the reduction in scope should be quite drastic:

I think you’ve got to sit down and talk in terms of the architecture, and decide what are the things you will never give away because you’ll never get them back, and what are the things you’re prepared to compromise on . . . we wasted our time on trying to win on all data elements when we could have won on the key eight or so, like customer-name, product, full national number and others.

Understandably, study participants still working on architectures at the time of interview felt that this type of approach could still result in quite comprehensive outcomes, and that the ideal approach would involve a creative combination of coercion and persuasion. This meant that:

if we can get people that want to do their own thing and say, \`well, do your own thing, but make sure that if you’re implementing this entity you conform to the standard’, then we’ve got a hope in hell whether it be the data warehouse level, whether some extracts, whether some report from client systems, of being able to bring this information together and allow management to look at the big picture.

Perhaps the biggest dif® culty for the formal adoption of this type of approach is its drastically reduced scope when compared with the standard concept. Anecdotal evidence based on informal discussions with IS architects from a range of companies suggests however that various versions of the approach are already in widespread use, a trend which theory has as yet failed to recognize.

## Conclusion

There was a consensus among case study participants that the standard theoretical view (Segars and Grover, 1996) was correct to the extent that some form of architecture-based planning at the portfolio level was desirable in principle, but ¯ awed in practice. One comment followed a discussion of the extent of Telstra’ s investment in core IS, informally estimated as being around A\$1b:

there is a lot of operations focus, and I think we have to actively argue against that, because operations is I feel the simple side of it . . . they have a predictable, managed, planned environment. We have an intangible product, an unpredictable management requirement, [and] we also have a lot more environmental variables that we’ve got to take into account . . . I’ m trying to see a means of planning which isn’t architecturally driven to some extent. I think it’ s needed.

The researcher’s perspective on the problems raised by different interviewees was that they very largely resolved to a general issue of disciplinary maturity. Interviewees who had worked directly on integration planning and IS architectures repeatedly commented on the steepness and unanticipated complexity of the learning curves involved. The \`intangible’ nature of systems work was a recurrent theme. Another IS strategist commented that:

I think architectures are really essential, but the dif-® culty is you try and relate [them] to something like the building trade, where you’ve got a history of architecture, you’ve got a history of project management, you’ve got a history of deliverable, and you can see the things. And you try and stack that up against an industry that’ s 40-years-old, which is trying to develop systems that can operate, that are modular, and do lots of other things. It’ s very very dif® cult, particularly since the product is not tangible.

The process model presented in Fig. 5 is based on the view that an IS architecture must have inherent value as a knowledge structure which is potentially relevant at all levels of IS planning. This value is independent of any additional value it may have as a blueprint for implementation, and thus persists even in the absence of conforming applications and databases.

## Linking strategic information systems

This research could be followed up on a number of fronts. The ® ndings on technical learning appeared, for instance, to be indicative of deep-seated problems with the acquisition and expansion of knowledge in this area of the IS discipline, problems which could be further explored. Purely from the IS planning perspective, it seems that additional intensive case studies into IS planning in complex organizations would be of most value. While it is argued that the model (Fig. 5) presented here as an outcome of the Telstra ® ndings is empirically plausible and hence could reasonably be extrapolated to other complex organizations, such a conclusion needs more support. Large organizations differ in many ways. It could be the case, for instance, that data structures in the banking industry are more stable than those in telecommunications, and therefore more amenable to prescriptive management. Further case studies, preferably based on organizations in industries other than telecommunications, would ® ll in a number of gaps.

Planning remains an under-researched area within the IS discipline. The rate of change of technology, in conjunction with changes in systems provisioning objectives and techniques, undoubtedly inhibits the establishment of any standard base from which to assess the value of planning. This study indicates however that professionals with lengthy experience in the area do form judgements on the basis of themes which persist over time. Taking practitioner perceptions and interpretations into account, ® ndings from the study suggest strongly that the prescriptive approach to architecture-driven planning at the portfolio level is fundamentally ¯ awed. Yet the threats and opportunities IT poses for organizations are not going away in the age of electronic commerce and the internet, and the belief that IS architectures have a major role to play in planning practice remains strong. The conclusions from this study are that IS architectures have intrinsic value as knowledge structures, and that their potential for use as planning and management aids has yet to be fully exploited.
