# Cyber-ethnography and the emergence of the virtually new community

KATIE J. WARD

Department of Humanities and Social Science, Staffordshire University, College Road, Stoke-on-Trent, ST4 2DE, UK

The aim is to demonstrate how cyber-ethnography has become the most appropriate tool in reaching a de® - nition of the virtual community. It is argued through the cyber-ethnographic examination of two virtual communities that, interpretative research methods traditionally associated with the social sciences enforce preconceived ideas and normative frameworks on to the virtual community. Cyber-ethnography allows a re¯ exive methodology to emerge, thus enabling the participants of virtual communities to de® ne their own reality and perimeters. It is propounded that there are two elements to the virtual community. First, it is emphasized that a hybrid space is rapidly emerging that is neither absolutely physical or virtual. Through its convergence with the physical the virtual community’s existence is apparent, though not unconditionally virtual. Secondly, the participants are depicted as having a transitory, unconditional relationship with the virtual community. That is; they will only participate for short periods when they require use of the resources that the virtual community has to offer.

## Introduction

In our computer-laden society with recent, with far reaching advances in computer mediated communication (CMC) it is possible to dispense with traditional understandings of space and time. Not only has ¯ ourishing communication via computers and the internet<sup>1</sup> led to instant communication that ceases to be restricted by such a priori structures, but it is also possible to identify the encroachment of the virtual realm on to our physical space.<sup>2</sup> Indeed, the physical and virtual realms are becoming increasingly dif® cult to separate due to the less frequent face-to-face contact. This idea is cognizant in many aspects of our changing society, for example communication via email is unlikely to be considered an \`unreal’ means of exchanging crucial information. Despite the virtual status of e-mail, it would not be regarded as ineffectual or any less profound or humane than other varieties of communication. E-mail in® ltrates and impacts on the way in which we fashion and conduct our lives in the physical world. Similarly, the phenomenon of Lara Croft in the game \`Tomb Raider’ clearly illustrates how the boundaries between the physical and the virtual are becoming more ¯ uid as individuals are interacting with digitally constructed entities. Lara Croft has no \`real’ counterpart in the wider social world and yet she has reached iconic status, become a role model and a new 1990s sex symbol; attributes usually associated with \`real,’ living individuals.

The merging together of the physical and virtual realms creates a new hybrid space that is neither wholly physical or virtual. Despite this, popular internet and computer culture and theorizing surrounding cyberspace continues to perpetuate the myth that the physical and virtual are somehow different entities. They are portrayed as existing in separation from each other and possessing thoroughly distinct characteristics. For example, in terms of internet culture, search engines are depicted as the tools by which the virtual world can be conquered. They are data bases which on the users’ command trawl through a massive amount of web pages searching for desired information. However, not only do they help to navigate the journey of discovery through the rhizomatic networking of computers, they aid in the social construction of the virtual world. For example, when a user utilizes the \`Lycos’ search engine he or she is warned: \`It’ s scary out there!’ This initially implies that when embarking on a trip around cyberspace one moves out somewhere, into another world that exists alongside the concrete reality of the wider social world, through which we live our everyday lives. The internet user is presented with a gateway to another environment, a virtual world, whilst also being advised that the contents that exist in this other world require the taming of a search engine. The internet user must not venture into this \`scary’ world and attempt to confront what lies ahead without the aid and shielding of the search engine. Similarly, the slogan that is used to promote Microsoft: \`Where do you want to go today?’ suggests that the virtual realm is a new place to be discovered via new computer technology.

So it transpires that the physical world and the virtual world are perceived as having different qualities. In a similar vein, much theorizing surrounding cyberspace has also contributed to, and perpetuated this idea that the physical and virtual occupy opposite positions in a dichotomous relationship. For many proponents of cyberculture, cyberspace represents a utopian environment. It has been construed as a world that has superior qualities, elevating its status above that of the physical world. These attributes include the notion that cyberspace with its lack of visual and audible cues has the potential to be a \`post gender, age, disability and ethnic’ environment. The idea of disembodied communication dominates the landscape and thus, communication in cyberspace has been cast as having accentuated egalitarian properties. This contrasts with the physical world where individuals, through visual and audible cues, can formulate preconceived ideas of the other (Benedikt, 1994; Heim, 1994; Tomas, 1994; Dery, 1995, 1996; Argile, 1996; Robins, 1995; Shields, 1996).

I suggest that such popular computer culture and cyber cultural theory arti® cially polarizes the physical and virtual realms. It has the effect to romanticize the virtual, in so far as it becomes depicted as a dimension retaining almost divine characteristics. Indeed, cyberspace becomes portrayed as a utopia as it escapes the restrictions that the physical world presents. Moreover, I propound that this myth can only be exploded through the study of the virtual community utilizing cyberethnography.<sup>3</sup> Cyber-ethnography differs from regular ethnography as it avoids holding any preconceived ideas concerning the existence of community. Rather than studying a group and assuming it to be a community, cyber-ethnography allows the participants to take the lead role in establishing the reality, status and principles of their group. Hence, the aggregation only becomes a community if they perceive it to be so, and experience the spirit of community. This abandonment of prescripted ideas is incisive as it avoids the enforcement of an inaccurate framework on to the virtual community by an outsider who may not fully understand all the social processes that constitute the virtual community.

Further, cyber-ethnography allows a more accurate picture of the virtual community to emerge. First, it deconstructs the idea of the wholly virtual community that has been popular among scholars (Rheingold, 1994; Reid, 1995; Bromberg, 1996). When traditional ethnographic research methods are applied to study virtual aggregations, they often have the effect to reinforce the dichotomous relationship that has emerged between the physical and the virtual. That is the virtual community becomes depicted as an entity that exists entirely within the virtual realm, as if in isolation from the physical; having played no part in the construction of our hybrid culture. To explode this myth I have carried out my cyber-ethnography in two virtual communities: The Cybergrrl Web Station and Women Halting Online Abuse (WHOA). It is demonstrated how the virtual community cannot exist entirely and absolutely within virtual space. The virtual community can never fully escape the con® nes of the physical; the physical, despite its apparent morphing with the virtual, continues to place restrictions on people’ s lives, and so a community that exists entirely in text based virtual space is implausible.

However, it cannot be denied that people use new technology to mimic traditional communities, to create a spirit of community that can extend across the globe. Secondly, the cyber-ethnography illustrates how virtual communities are increasingly transient and fragmented compared to traditional communities. Participants experience a ¯ eeting encounter with the virtual community and they feel no obligation to remain members over a long period of time. They have an unconditional relationship with the community as it is used to ful® l a particular set of requirements. Any virtual community may only have a core of ten regular visitors and a vast amount of transitory members who only frequent once. Such visitors move swiftly on, adding to the continuous stream of participants that sustain the virtual community. This inclusive, yet fragile state of the virtual aggregation does not make it any less of a community, on the contrary, it suggests that our understanding of community is changing. A community is no longer located in a speci® c place and it does not meet all of our needs in terms of surviving, working and socializing. Therefore, people can be members of many different communities that are all peripheral to their identities. This changing understanding of community can also be recognized in wider society where numerous different communities emerge and people are united by common understanding rather than proximity. These can take the form of fan clubs, animal rights groups, the student union or the CND movement. Such communities can be considered as \`imagined communities’ as the members may never meet face-to-face, but they consider themselves to be united by common interest (see Anderson, 1991 for a discussion of imagined communities).

The two web sites used are typical of this shifting conception of community as they provide the members with very speci® c resources, while expecting no long term commitment. The websites maintain these qualities even though they differ in structure and aim. The Cybergrrl Web Station is purely about making connections, whereas WHOA is de® ned through a political cause; the prevention of online harassment. However before it is possible to demonstrate how cyberethnography allows the virtual community to be constructed it is necessary to problematize the concept. Language is the central component in the construction of cyber-reality and the virtual community. Hence structures within cyberspace become far more ¯ exible and open to transformation, notably users can alter the structure of the virtual aggregation through changing how they organize their web of interaction. Virtual space is open to constant sculpting and resculpting, consequently a problem of boundaries and emerges as it becomes dif® cult to maintain a collective identity. Traditionally, in the Durkheimian (1984) sense the belief in collective ideals has acted as the agent that provides social cohesion and the bonds necessary for the group to function as a community. In the case of the virtual aggregation, anyone who has access to the technology can join and so there are no boundaries surrounding the community to provide the participants with a sense of communal identity. The virtual aggregation has the potential to spiral outwards encompassing a vast amount of virtual space and people in its rhizomatic development across the virtual landscape. It would seem, paradoxically, that the boundary-less freedom that dominates cyberspace is its only downfall when attempting to claim that its virtual aggregations are indeed communities.

## The virtual community: exploding the myth of the virtual utopia

Before further problematizing the virtual community, it is important to highlight the academic context within which the virtual community has emerged. The idea of community, in the broad sense, has always provided anthropologists and sociologists with much fuel for debate. This inception of this tradition in thought is located with Tonnies’ work (1957). The transition from Gemeinschaft to Gesellschaft was considered to bring a loss of community,<sup>7</sup> the inference being that community can only be located within pre-industrial, rural society. Consequently, community has become an ideal that is often depicted by urban planners as an entity that is threatened by rapid urban develop ments; hence it is perceived as a utopia to be preserved (Dennis, 1968; Glass, 1968). However more recent work has challenged the idea that there is a ® xed ideal of community. For example Young and Willmott (1957) in their study of Bethnal Green identi® ed a community within a conurbation. Similarly Gans (1962) identi® ed a community among the Italian-Americans within an inner city area of Boston called the West End. These ® ndings complicate the debate surrounding community as they suggest that a community is not dependent on the place being an idyllic, rural location. Furthermore these theorists are identifying a further important factor within the community: the networks of people that constitute the community. This adds new incentive to the debate as it suggests that the spirit of community or communion that is found among networks of people is far more important than having a sense of place.

The notion that the spirit of community is more important than a sense of place is compounded and re¯ ected within recent attempts to de® ne community and the swift, far reaching social changes that have taken place in our society. First in terms of de® nition; there is little unanimity among sociologists. Hillery (1955) proposes 94 de® nitions of community all in disagreement as to its constitution. The lowest common denominator that Hillery could draw is that communities all concern people, but otherwise there is no agreement. To further complicate the issue, Bell and Newby (1971) suggest that communities usually embody characteristics that pertain to a common consciousness, that is the activity revolves around shared understandings. However for all the scholars mentioned it seems that the only factor that is crucial to the perpetuation of community is the networks of people and the interaction that occurs among them.

Second, the idea that the spirit of community is the most important aspect of community is cognizant within recent and comprehensive changes in society. Over the last decade, society has become increasingly networked. The internet user is restricted by neither time nor space, indeed communication whether taking place in real or delayed time allows the participant to defy seemingly immovable structural forces. Within this context Scherer (1972) suggests that increased mobility plays a role in shaping social structure and hence the nature and de® nition of community is transformed. New communities can be based around common interest rather than shared territory or geographical location. Similarly, Schuler (cited in Jones, 1997) has noted, \`[t]he old concept of community is obsolete in many ways and needs to be updated to meet today’ s challenges.’ He regards \`traditional’ or \`old’ communities as \`exclusive . . . in¯ exible . . . and homogeneous . . . and new community [as] fundamentally devoted to democratic problem solving’ (p. 10, emphasis in the original).

It transpires that the development of new technology allows the idea of community to widen in scope making room for aggregations in text based virtual space to become communities. Initially the de® nition of the virtual community is rendered problematic as there is no commonality as to which form of virtual aggregation is a community. For example MUDs have been identi® ed as communities (Bromberg, 1996; Turkle, 1996), whereas others have introduced \`Usenet’ into the debate (Baym, 1995; Mackinnon, 1995).

Rather than negotiating the form of the virtual community, Rheingold has offered detailed ethnographic descriptions of The Whole World \`Lectronic Link’ (Well), a San Francisco based virtual conferencing system. He claims that people increasingly crave new communities as real public space gradually decreases and in his exploration of this virtual aggregation he de® nes virtual communities as \`Social aggregations that emerge from the net when enough people carry on those public discussions long enough, with suf® cient human feeling, to form webs of personal relationships in cyberspace (1994, p. 5).

Rheingold describes his experience of The Well as feeling as if he had walked into an \`authentic community’ (p. 2). He envisages it as community-built by the participants with its own agenda of norms and speci® c behaviour code. Rheingold argues that the behaviour of people on The Well is comparable to that of people’s real lives as they \`engage in intellectual discourse, share knowledge . . . gossip, feud . . . and fall in love’ (p. 3). Rheingold perceives the only difference to lie in the disembodied experience that cyberspace offers.<sup>8</sup>

However, despite the optimism of Rheingold, the factors of geographical proximity and sense of place, or the virtual community’s lack of, have led to controversy and scepticism within academic discussions. It has been questioned whether a delocalized website that fosters text-based discussion based around a particular interest can be considered a community. For example Wilbur (1997) notes that virtual aggregations can be perceived as not meriting the term community and he summarizes the negative approach that has been attached to the concept of virtual community, thus,

Virtual community is the illusion of community where there are no real people and no real communication. It is a term used by idealistic technophiles who fail to understand that authentic cannot be engendered through technological means. Virtual community ¯ ies in the face of \`human nature’ that is, essentially, it seems, depraved (p. 14).

Watson (1997) has moved away from the seemingly endless debate as to whether there are virtual communities and form in which they are realized. Instead, he asks why the debate has demanded so much attention and why the term community is so important. Watson begins with a recognition that certain groups of people who interact via the internet do so because their interests are akin. Importantly, Watson demonstrates that people who participate in virtual communities perceive their environment to be \`real’ , rather than an environment that is in competition with, and always aspiring to be the \`real thing’ . It is suggested that once this dichotomous thought has been abandoned virtual groups can be representative of certain political ideas and can thus emerge as a new and developing aspect of representative democracy.

## Cyber-ethnography: the re¯ exive approach to the virtual community

Despite all these attempts to clarify the de® nition of the virtual community and to establish its constitution, a successful de® nition of virtual community is yet to emerge. The problem surrounding the above attempts is that they do not perceive the physical and virtual as merging and creating a space that is apparently both physical and virtual simultaneously. Watson makes a positive attempt to move away from dichotomous thought. Nevertheless, he fails to perceive a new space; instead he sees the virtual as being \`as real’ as the physical. Thus he is not abandoning the categories, he is merely rede® ning them. The only conceivable way for an accurate presentation of the virtual community to emerge successfully is through the application of cyberethnography. In terms of carrying out research in the virtual realm, there is no established precedent as to how to collect data. Scholars have previously adopted the methods of traditional sociology and anthropology and applied them to the virtual realm. That is; researchers have employed participant observation (Reid, 1995; Bromberg, 1996), and informal interviews (Turkle, 1996).

These traditional ethnographic methods are fraught with problems when employed indiscriminately within the text-based virtual space as they have the effect to place a normative framework on to the concept of the virtual community. Traditional ethnography assumes the existence of a community and attempts to unfold, describe and analyse the social processes that constitute the aggregation; to make the strange familiar (Evans-Pritchard, 1969; Malinowski, 1978). To apply such methods to the virtual suggests an underlying assumption that a community exists and in effect the research methods construct an inaccurate depiction of the virtual community. Therefore preconceived ideas intrinsically bound to the research methods employed have the effect to present aggregations that are found in cyberspace as communities simply because ethnographers expect to ® nd and explicate communities. Subsequently, sociologists fall into the trap of enforcing preconceived ideas on to the aggregation they are studying rather than allowing the empirical evidence to drive the research.

The cyber-ethnographic method evades the implication of culture-centrism which arises through the enforcement of normative frameworks on to the aggregation under study. Nevertheless, to discuss the status of the virtual community the present research can be aligned with the approach of interpretative sociology, where the meanings the participants place on the social system are the driving force behind the research (Becker, 1963; Stanley, 1990, 1993). However, even though cyber-ethnography remains located within the tradition of interpretative research methods, it does involve some modi® cations to the associated methods. Cyber-ethnography is de® ned as follows,

Cyber-ethnography is a study of online interaction. It allows the subjects being studied to talk back even as the process is occurring. The talking back is part of the cyber-ethnographic process (Gajjala, 1997; see Cyber-ethnography website).

Within the traditional interview situation, as within Gavron’s \`Captive Wife’ study (1966), there are sets of expectations shared by interviewer and interviewee. The interviewer is expected to ask the questions or loosely de® ne the topic of conversation and the interviewee responds. Therefore, the interviewer is largely in a powerful position as he or she is expected to, and does de® ne the situation. Conversely, cyberethnography operates on the premise of a diologic process between the cyber-ethnographer. The lack of visual and audible cues causes a high degree of uncertainty for participants in terms of \`knowing’ the identity of the other. This ambiguity manifested itself in the research process, as participants asked me questions concerning my age, gender and ethnic background. As participants are in a position to ask questions of me, the power relationship between the researcher and researched changes radically. The researcher does not have so much control over the interview process.

Therefore the very nature and form of the cyberethnographic research method allows a unique perspective to emerge. The dialogue that emerges between myself as the cyber-ethnographer and the other internet users gives rise to a new type of data. Since the participants are in a position to ask a question and re¯ ect back on to the interaction, an alternative slant is placed on the data. The methodology is not so \`closed’ and so there are no de® nitive answers produced to the questions posed. In this sense the research always remains \`un® nished’ and re¯ exive. It is not the researcher who is in the position to have the ® nal word, indeed the interpretation remains open to constant renegotiation by both the researcher and the researched.

It is this openness or un® nished nature of the research that encapsulates the subjective understandings of the participants. This is not to say that the data are completely unmediated as any data are interceded, rather the participants are far more active in the research process. Consequently, the boundaries between the sociologist’s and lay discourse become increasingly blurred. To allow this diologic process to emerge it is necessary to employ research methods that enable the participants to drive and create the de® nitions of the concepts.

## Cyber-ethnography in practice

The practice of cyber-ethnography is not documented in text books, it only gains discussion in the cyberethnography website. Cyber-ethnography involves a degree of observation where the researcher observes the interaction on a particular website in order to gain a fuller understanding of internet culture. This method was employed during the explication of both the Cybergrrl Web Station and the WHOA website as much of the information, in both instances, is \`read only’. For example on the Cybergrrl Web Station there is an organization called Webgrrls and the information provided about this is presented in a read only format on the website. Webgrrls was established in April 1995 and is a support group who initially make contacts online within the Cybergrrl Web Station and subsequently meet of¯ ine. Thus, women who work with the internet, computers and technology have the opportunity to discuss careers, new media and technology. The aim is to \`create networking opportunities, job and business leads, form alliances . . . and [to] encourage non-competitive exchanges of information and experience’ (New York Times, 25 February 1996). Initially, the Webgrrls were a group of New York women, but more recently different \`chapters’ have opened worldwide, and now on a global scale, women are meeting and not only advancing their presence on the internet,

Even though the information is read only, it demonstrates how this type of facility opens the opportunity for community building that extends of¯ ine as it meets the needs of a group of individuals who are trying to achieve the same goal. In this sense the route is paved for the development of connections and hence, common consciousness.

A similar observation can also be applied to the WHOA website. Given that the website is dedicated to political campaigning as it aims to raise awareness about, and move towards preventing online harassment, the information provided typically illustrates that cyberspace is not always a \`safe’ place for women. For example a mission statement is provided, \`The mission of WHOA is . . . to create harassment free environments’. Similarly, WHOA makes its intentions clear in suggesting that it fully supports the right to free speech both online and off, but asserts that \`free speech is not protected when it involves threats to the emotional or physical safety of anyone. As such, we must begin to assert our power . . . (no matter what our backgrounds) toward the protection of all people against online harassment. . . . (WHOA website 1997). This information provides the foundations for the community in that it provides a common goal and consciousness to unite people. Similarly WHOA produces a bimonthly zine, \`A Cyber-Safe Room of our Own,’ that is delivered automatically to subscribers’ email addresses. This typically contains articles concerning online harassment and is used as a further method of raising awareness. The information contained suggests that the website owners who produce the zine consider their site to be a community. For example, the following extracts are from the zine,

WHOA provides empowerment and uni® ed support for persons who have been targeted for harassment and abuse (my emphasis).

We feel that we are providing a template for a future: a future which all people can work together . . . and not have to fear harassment and abuse and because we believe that the internet is both a re¯ ection of . . . society at large, we believe that in building such a world online, we can help to build it of¯ ine as well.

However, whether this does actually induce a spirit of community within people cannot be ascertained without the use of participant, interactive research methods. Participant observation as with regular ethnography is also a large part of cyber-ethnography, similarly other methods associated with interpretative research are applied and these include conversational interviews and focus groups. However, because of the nature of the cyber-landscape these methods cannot be realized as they would be in the physical world. Conversational interviews were used within the Cybergrrl Web Station, however they took place using the QM facility located within the Cybergrrl Village. Within this context I began discussing with the participants whether they perceived the Cybergrrl Web Station to be a community and if they experienced a sense of community. Typically the responses conformed to the following:

The Village is really a place for us all to share thoughts and ideas, meet people and really connect.

I ® nd the Cybergrrl site to be a very positive place for women to explore the internet and participate in the creation of a community.

I love the way we can all meet up here and there are so many people from such different backgrounds . . . it’ s so cool!

Its so weird it’ s like we’ re all just sitting at our computers and we have created this world. It’s almost spiritual.

It seems that people do experience a connection with others and crave this interaction that they can achieve through new technology. The second extract also seems to demonstrate that rather than diversity being a hindrance to a coherent community, it adds to the texture of the community. Instead of seeking closed communities that are constituted of people that are all of like mind, it seems that the participants want communities that allow them to broaden their horizons in terms of connecting with people from \`different backgrounds’. The third extract also indicates that a lack of geographical location does not prevent the emergence of community, on the contrary, the ability to connect, despite the distance seems to add to the interest and depth of the community.

However, not all websites have a QM facility or even a real time chat forum, where a similar technique could be employed, therefore other methods have to be used that can work in conjunction with the facilities available on the website. For example it is possible to carry out \`focus group’, style interviews using email lists.<sup>10</sup>

Even though this does not take place in real time, the researcher can post a question to the group and elicit many responses and subsequently encourage the participants to discuss the issue amongst themselves. In this sense this method closely resembles the focus group as the researcher is taking a \`back seat’ thus allowing the participants to play a dominant role in the discussion. Indeed the researcher only steps in to guide the conversation with a further question when the conversation loses its direction (see Berg, 1995, for a discussion of focus groups). This method has been applied to the WHOA website as in conjunction with the website, WHOA operates an e-mailing list. This typically is a forum where women can share their harassment stories and ask questions if they feel under threat, hence,

How does he know where I am, what e-mail client I’m using, and how does he know I’m me when I’m using an assumed name?

They can also gain advice if they feel that they under threat. For example, the following advice was given to the victims,

There is a very good chance he will get brave enough to track you down in real life and stalk you. You do not want to escalate it to that point. You need to get a good lawyer who will work on contingency and put a stop to this person now before it becomes more serious. . . .

Let us know what happens. And take precautions in the mean time: Don’t answer the door without knowing who it is ® rst. . . . Be aware at all times when you are outside your home

It seems that people connect with others on the basis that they are sharing problems and giving support and advice and this gives the group a sense of unity. As to whether the participants experience it as a community is more dif® cult to establish as the participants are primarily concerned with discussing harassment as opposed to community. Therefore the individuals’ experience of the group has to be deduced from the posts to the list. The following responses indicate that WHOA is considered to create a sense of community in the sense that its common goal unites people. Indeed they demonstrate how the community has become a place that people visit when they need help with something, or wish to bene® t from the resources that a particular organization has to offer. Participants can utilize the resources without obligation rather than perceiving it as a group that they are joining that they have to live, work and socialize within,

While looking for any resources to deal with this (nuisance calls as a result of personal information being posted to a message board) . . . I stumbled across WHOA. . . . I thought the group had merit.

Thanks so much to all of you who have replied to my request for help. I’m considering what each of you have suggested, and will probably make a decision on what actions I should take. . . .

Even though this website has a far more political agenda and clearly de® ned goal than the Cybergrr Web Station, it is not overtly stated and reiterated that this group is a community. However, they are united by their common goal even though the participants will never meet on a face-to-face level. Indeed this is clari® ed by the organizer of the WHOA website, Lynda Hinkle, \`It’ s a community in that we have certain community building programmes ± the discussion list, the newsletter, the safe site award etc.’

Given the ambiguity of the cyber-landscape and the modi® cations made to the interpretative methodology, new problems subsequently emerge. Both the above methods are problematic for sampling and ethics. Usually when a researcher wishes to gain a sample they gain it through randomly selecting people from a list such as, a GPs’ register. This is not possible in cyberspace as there is no complete and exhaustive list of all the internet users. Further, gaining a sample is also problematic in the sense that there is no time to become friendly with the participants and gain their trust as a researcher would normally (Oakley, 1981). In any virtual aggregation it is possible to meet someone once and then never speak to them again as they disappear into the realms of cyberspace. Therefore, the researcher has to become an opportunist and on a ® rst meeting become involved in a conversation about the participant’s feelings towards text-based virtual space. As there are no visual and audible cues, it would be possible for the researcher to carry out this research in a completely covert manner. It would be possible for me to communicate with people without revealing my true intentions. However, this type of research is deemed unethical by the British Sociological Association and so I found it necessary to reveal that I was a researcher studying social activity in cyberspace.

## The virtually new community: towards a working de® nition

As I was revealing information about myself this naturally aroused curiosity in people, given the lack of cues they could never be certain whether my intentions were pure. Many of the participants during the conversations asked me questions. However this adds to the beauty of the cyber-ethnographic method as it enables greater re¯ exivity from the participants, thus allowing their subjective understandings to drive the research. The dominance of the participants’ subjective understandings is crucial in reaching a working de® nition of the virtual community. As stated previously, traditional ethnographic methods have imposed normative frameworks on the virtual community. On the other hand,

## Cyber-ethnography and virtual community

the cyber-ethnographic method allows the virtual aggregation to \`speak for itself’ , in the sense that, if the participants perceive their virtual aggregation to be a community, then they have the power to de® ne it as such. The cyber-ethnographic method is the key in allowing the virtual community to emerge as it does not rely on older frameworks to inform new structures. It has been adapted so that the virtual community can emerge within its right, rather than as an entity that is constantly compared without dated concepts of community.

The virtual community has two major components; the ® rst embodies the notion that, the virtual community, whilst being a fragile social group is experienced by its ephemeral audience as a community. Indeed the members do not make a long term commitment to the virtual community, rather they will be more instrumental in their approach to the virtual community. They will utilize the resources it offers when required. This approach to community is also re¯ ected in the physical world where people are members of many different communities. It transpires that community is no longer central in the shaping of the consciousness. Instead, people belong unconditionally to many different groups, each serving a purpose and ful® lling a speci® c need in that individual’s life. The same is true of the virtual community, people drift in and out of numerous different virtual communities staying only as long as the virtual community is providing a solution or ful® lling a need in their life. On a ® rst glance this may seem as if it works against the grain of the public minded spirit of community. Examination of the two virtual communities suggests that this is not the case. It seems accepted by the people who frequent the two virtual communities that they are experiencing community, albeit in a manner that is far ¯ ung from the traditional ideals of what a community should be; networks of people working, living and socializing within a speci® c locale. It seems that people enjoy the diversity they experience within their virtual communities and similarly they overtly admit that they are being thoroughly instrumental in their choice of community.

The second important feature of the virtual community is that it is not an entirely virtual entity. Within our composite society where the physical and the virtual are becoming inextricably bound, it is hardly surprising that in the context of the virtual community, the physical and the virtual envelop each other. Given that the physical and the virtual are increasingly dif® cult to recognize as separate entities the limits by which we understand community have mutated. Our social space and political arena have widened due to the emergence of the virtual landscape and it is no longer applicable to refer to a true community in the physical or virtual sense. Due to increased mobility and the development of new computer technology the traditional \`Bethnal Green’ model of community is no longer a useful framework by which to measure the concept of community as people no longer remain united through the close vicinity of the neighbourhood.

It is no longer possible to speak of an authentic community, similarly it is not appropriate to discuss a truly virtual community. To consider the notion of the purely virtual community is fruitless as this suggests that there is an alien world that certain people inhabit who have managed to transcend the human state. The absolute virtual community cannot come to fruition as this would involve a metamorphosis rather like the one that the \`Lawn-Mower Man’ experienced. Lawn-Mower Man in the quest to become \`pure data’ became trapped in the mainframe computer. Despite the cyborg perfection he became powerless and could no longer yield any in¯ uence in the wider social world. This type of utopian vision seems to pertain to the fantasies of a science ® ction writer. The notion of a purely virtual community and the idea of the post-human are both extremely optimistic and rather grand given that the majority of people do not have the opportunity to live their lives as \`pure information’. Instead they continue to ® ght their social and political battles in the wider, concrete social world. Therefore the idea of a truly virtual community can never be identi® ed, indeed it can be aligned with other oxymorons, such as Eagleton’s (1998) \`Political Aesthetics’ or \`Military Intelligence’.

The physical continues to restrict people’s lives, whilst the virtual plays an increasingly larger role in the construction and understanding of our physical lives. The virtual challenges the boundaries that have traditionally been used to map our social space. Therefore it becomes more crucial to illustrate the notion that the virtual community cannot exist in isolation from the physical. The virtual community requires the physical to provide it with meaning and this factor becomes crucial in the criteria utilized to de® ne the virtual community. Therefore any individual virtual community must be perceived as being in a relationship with the wider physical world. Both virtual communities cited have different objectives and maintain a different relationship with the wider social world. The Cybergrrl Web Station functions to impact on people’s materiality or physical lives allowing them to change their social conditions, whereas the situation with the WHOA virtual community is more complicated. The bene® ts that this community brings to individuals’ physical lives are less palpable. The aims of the WHOA community are to prevent online harassment and it could be construed that their activity is con® ned in cyberspace. However the situation cannot be so neatly clari® ed, as WHOA in its campaign offers a wider, more inclusive approach to political problems and so has the potential to educate and raise the consciousness of a vast number of people on a global scale.

Given that both communities operate within a re¯ exive relationship with the physical it can be observed that neither exists in isolation; there is no truly virtual community. Rather the virtual extends our understanding of community so that we now have a conception of community that allows the potential of the virtual realm to be realized. This potential can be maximized as long as it continues to be af® rmed by the wider, social world; that is people can apply what they gain from the virtual realm to enhance their wider, social lives.
