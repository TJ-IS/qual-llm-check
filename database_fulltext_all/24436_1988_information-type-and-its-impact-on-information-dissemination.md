# Information Type and its Impact on Information Dissemination

AHMED S. ZAKI and RICHARD C. HOFFMAN

AHMED S. ZAKI is Professor of MIS at the School of Business Administration at the College of William and Mary. He holds a Ph.D. from the University of Washington in MIS with minors in Computer Science and OR. His publications appear in the Communication of ACM, the Journal of Computer and Information Sciences, Journal of Management Information Systems, the European Journal of Operations Research, the Computer Journal, the Journal of Computer Mathematics and other journals. He is a member of the Society of Management Information Systems and the American Institute of Decision Sciences.

RICHARD C. HOFFMAN is an Assistant Professor in the College of Business and Economics at the University of Delaware. He holds a Ph.D. in Management and Administration from Indiana University. His current research interests include strategic decision and information processes. He has published in Decision Sciences and Long Range Planning and has served as a research associate at IMEDE, Switzerland. He is a member of the Academy of Management and the Strategic Management Society.

ABSTRACT: A classification scheme for categorizing organizationally relevant information as either vital or supportive is validated empirically. The impact of the hierarchy on information dissemination was found to vary somewhat for both types of information. These results are based on a study of 118 managers at three hierarchical levels from 18 business firms.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: information types, information dissemination, relevance of information.

## 1. Introduction

THE PRIMARY OBJECTIVE of a well-designed information system is to provide or make available to managers at all levels the information necessary to perform their specific tasks. Halpin et al. [9] and Streufert [24] have shown that there is a direct relationship between the quality of information used by managers and the quality of their decision-making performance.

In order for data to be considered information, it must alter the utility $[20]$ or have a real or perceived value $[7]$ for its user at a given point in time. Information does not just happen; it is either user-generated or acquired from other sources. In other words, some user or potential user (recipient) has made a value judgement concerning certain data and concluded that it is worth the cost of trying to obtain it or disseminate it to others. Moreover, different items of information may have different values for the same user, depending on the role it is expected to play in the decision process. For example, certain information may be more valuable for problem definition, than either alternative generation or evaluation and choice $[22]$ .

Previous empirical studies have established that managers do assign values to the information they process. Jones and McLeod [11] found that top executives rate information they receive as having from little or no value to having a very high value. Three other studies [9, 16, 18] have indicated a direct relationship between “good” information and “good” decision making. Another investigation [27] revealed a direct relationship between the perceived quality of information (using six measures of quality) and its use in decision making. However, there has been little research investigating the specific values that managers actually assign to the information they process. Argyris [3] and Simon [22] have suggested a two-dimensional classification of information as: (1) favorable–unfavorable, and (2) important–unimportant. In an empirical study, Gee [7, 8] asked 49 middle managers to classify items of control information as either vital, important (used for reference), or background (items rarely used). The results showed that, of the 383 items of control information investigation, 32% were considered vital, 36% were considered important, and 32% were considered as background.

In formal organizations, information analysts, designers, and managers need to know: (1) What items of information need to be kept or sought? (2) Where and why should each item be routed? (3) When should the information reach the recipient?

Such questions can only be addressed if one can identify the role that each item of information plays in the recipient's decision-making process. Moreover, the value of the information to the manager's task should be specified to a greater degree than the previously used classifications of important-unimportant or little-high value. Classification of information should reflect the role it plays in the decision-making process. The classification proposed by Gee [8] with some modification appears to be a good way to distinguish between different types of task-relevant information.

## 2. Research Objectives

THE OBJECTIVE of this investigation is to explore whether managers assign utility/value to the information they route based upon the role they perceive it will play in the recipient's decision-making process. This study addresses only task-relevant information. Previous research has revealed that managers distinguish between relevant–irrelevant information; however, the information investigated was not confined to the manager's task. Thus, the interesting question here is whether managers make a clear distinction between different types of task-relevant information. As an initial step in answering this question, this study focuses on two specific objectives. The first concerns two modifications to Gee's classification of information types and its empirical verification. The second objective examines the flow of task-relevant information among three levels of management.

We propose to modify two of Gee's descriptors of information. First, he uses the descriptor “important” to describe information used for reference. Yet, the dictionary meaning of the term “important” and its conventional use in information science [16, 25] implies information that has much significance, consequence, relevance, or value. In other words, “important” is information that is essential and, consequently, vital in a decision setting that can cause semantic confusion between the vital and important classes. For this reason, we suggest the descriptor “supportive” instead of “important.” Secondly, we propose to use the descriptor “irrelevant” instead of “background,” since it specifically defines the role of the information to the manager’s task and conforms more with information science terminology. We, therefore, submit the following classification for the task-relevant information:

1. Vital—when information is perceived by the sender as absolutely necessary for the recipient's effective job performance. That is, without it the recipient cannot adequately perform his/her tasks.

2. Supportive—when perceived by the sender as not necessary for the recipient's effectiveness; however, knowledge of it will enhance the recipient's efficiency.

3. Irrelevant—when the information does not fall in the above two categories.

In order to test the robustness of the vital/supportive classification, we have chosen a riskier strategy of focusing only on relevant information. The risk is that we may find no clear distinction between these two; however, by also including irrelevant information, we would be fairly certain of identifying at least two types of information relevant and irrelevant. We, therefore, pose the following proposition:

Proposition 1: Information relevant to managerial tasks is perceived to be multidimensional by its sender. Specifically, task-relevant information will be represented by two independent dimensions reflecting perceptions of vital and supportive information.

The second objective of the study is to investigate the effect of the manager's hierarchical level on information flow. Prior research indicates that hierarchical levels do affect the pattern of organizational-level information flow. The status (level) of the recipient in the organization affects how frequently information is communicated to an individual [1, 5]. The hierarchical level of the sender of information affects the directionality of communication. Department heads have been found to communicate more with superiors and subordinates while subordinates communicate more with peers [4].

Research on the nature of managerial work suggests several reasons why information flow varies by hierarchical level. Top [12] and middle [26] level managers must deal with a multiple set of relationships with peers, bosses, subordinates, and outsiders to accomplish their tasks. Managers at lower levels are more dependent on upward relationships and, hence, communications [24] for task accomplishment. Finally, managerial roles at different hierarchical levels vary both in content and skills required [17] which affects their information needs.

The existence of dependent relationships and role differences among managerial levels leads one to expect that managers at different levels assign different utility/value to information and route information with varying frequency to other managerial levels. Information dissemination has been found to vary by type [utility/value] and level of the recipient. Two studies $[13, 16]$ have demonstrated that more favorable, more important and, therefore, more total information is sent laterally than either up or down the hierarchy. Based on the work of Bachrach and Aiken $[4]$ , our study proposes that the dissemination of different types of information varies with the hierarchical level of its sender. In order to examine the effect of the hierarchical level of managers on the dissemination of different types of task-relevant information, the following two propositions are offered:

Proposition 2: The frequency of disseminating relevant information is dependent upon the hierarchical level of the sender and also the type of information.

Proposition 3: The frequency of disseminating relevant information is dependent upon the hierarchical level of the recipient and also the type of information.

Propositions 2 and 3 examine the affect of the hierarchical level of both the sender and receiver on the frequency of disseminating vital and supportive information, respectively.

## 3. Methodology

DATA FOR THIS STUDY were acquired from 118 managers among three hierarchical levels in 18 large national organizations. The firms were evenly divided between the manufacturing and service sectors, and included two information systems/data processing firms. Data collection consisted of two phases. In the first phase, an introductory letter was sent to the CEO and then followed a week later by a phone call. The purpose of the phone call was to invite the firm to participate in the study and to have the CEO identify managers at different hierarchical levels who were heads of departments considered critical to the firm's success. At this point, $47\%$ of the firms contacted agreed to participate. Table 1 provides selected sample demographics.

During the second phase, the department managers were mailed a close-ended questionnaire concerning his/her department's information handling activities as well as certain managerial and organizational/departmental characteristics. Two mail follow-ups were used to encourage responses. The usable response rate for managers was 80 percent. On the average, six managers per firm (representing three levels of management) provided usable data. Diverse functional areas were fairly well represented in the sample.

Respondents were asked several questions to evaluate the variables examined in this study. The following paragraphs briefly explain the methods used for assessing the variables.

To avoid bias due to direct working, the questionnaire used the information descriptors type V and type F instead of vital and supportive, respectively. Dissemination of information was assessed with six items concerning the extent to which managers send types V and F information to superior, peer, and subordinate departments. Responses were made using five-point, Likert-type scales and definitions of type V and type F information were provided to the respondents. To examine the reliability of the measures, Cronbach's alpha test was calculated to the overall measure $[10]$ .

The hierarchical level of each respondent was determined from his/her responses to a question developed by Astley [2]. This variable distinguishes between hierarchical levels in terms of reporting relationships to the firm's company president. Three management levels were defined using this question. The first or top level included the president and all managers who reported directly to him/her. Second or middle level managers were those managers who were two levels below the president, while third or technical level managers were all those managers three or more levels below the president of the firm.

## Data Analysis

The first proposition concerning the types of information was analyzed using principal components factor analysis with varimax rotation. Factor analysis is a powerful method for assessing construct validity because it enables one to determine if the construct or abstract concept (versus tangible one) is undimensional or multidimensional [6]. To verify whether constructs are independent of one another, their convergent validity was assessed using the intercorrelation technique suggested by Campbell and Fiske [6] and Ives, Olson, and Baroudi [10]. This method suggests that multiple measures of the same construct should be positive and significantly correlated with each other, and more highly correlated than the correlations between the measures of different constructs.

The effect of the hierarchical level of the sender on information dissemination was investigated using one-way analysis of variance with post hoc, multiple-comparison tests: least significant difference method. Differences in information dissemination by the hierarchical level of the recipient were tested using paired T-tests.

Proposition 2: The frequency of disseminating relevant information is dependent upon the hierarchical level of the sender and also the type of information.

This proposition was partially supported. For each type of information, the effects of the hierarchical level of the sender were first examined for the total sample and then controlling for the level of the recipient (superior, peer, and subordinate). The analysis in Table 4 reveals that the hierarchical level of the sender did not significantly affect the dissemination of vital information overall, as noted by the factors identified in testing proposition 1, nor to recipients at any specific level in the hierarchy. However, there was a significant level effect for the overall dissemination of supportive information.

A post hoc analysis revealed that top managers disseminated supportive information significantly more than middle managers (x = 4.25 vs. 3.72). Most of the difference seems to be due to the dissemination of supportive information among peers. The original F-test for this difference almost reached significance (P = .07). These results suggest that the effect of the hierarchical level of the sender on information dissemination is contingent on the type of information sent. If the information is vital for task performance, it is sent with the same frequency by managers at all hierarchical levels in all directions. When the information is not vital, some discretion is exercised in its dissemination. Top managers continue to disseminate supportive information frequently, probably preferring that managers at lower levels receive all information that can aid their performance. Middle-level managers seem to exercise the most discretion and disseminate supportive information less frequently. However, the data suggest that most of this difference may be in disseminating less information to peers. Since peers are not in the manager's chain of command and, hence, do not impact directly on his/her performance, the manager apparently feels freer to exercise discretion in disseminating supportive information to them.

Proposition 3: The frequency of disseminating relevant information is dependent upon the hierarchical level of the recipient and also the type of information.

This proposition was supported. The effects of the hierarchical level of the recipient were first examined for the total sample and then controlling for the level of the sender. The analysis of the total sample revealed significant differences for both vital and supportive information (see Table 5). Vital information was disseminated more frequently to peers than subordinates, and supportive information was disseminated more frequently to superiors than subordinates. Controlling for the level of the sender, the difference in the direction of information dissemination exists at the technical level of management only. Top and middle-level managers seem to disseminate information with the same frequency throughout the hierarchy. Technical managers disseminate more vital and supportive information to peers than to subordinate departments. Controlling for the sender's level, the significant difference in disseminating supportive information shifted from the superior-subordinate comparison to the peer-subordinate one. Overall, these results appear quite logical. Once one reaches the technical level of the hierarchy, there are fewer departments subordinate to a given manager. Moreover, given the increased specialization at this level, it is not surprising that technical managers need to exchange information with greater frequency to accomplish their own tasks.

## 5. Conclusion

THIS STUDY established that decision makers view task-relevant information as consisting of two different types, based upon their perception of the role the information is expected to play in the decision-making process. This study builds upon and extends the work of Gee [8] in several ways. First, the information classification scheme based on the information's utility/value in decisions relating to managerial tasks was reconceptualized to include: vital, supportive, and irrelevant. Second, this classification scheme was found to be appropriate not only for control but also for all task-relevant information. Third, the usefulness of this information typology was extended to the tasks of top and technical as well as the middle-level managers. Finally, the frequency of disseminating task-relevant information was found to vary with both information type and the hierarchical level of its sender and recipient. Specifically, this study revealed that, as long as information was considered to be vital, it was disseminated with the same frequency by senders in all hierarchical levels. However, more discretion was exercised in disseminating supportive information by middle-level managers. The hierarchical level of the recipient affected information dissemination only at the technical level. Managers at this level disseminated both vital and supportive information more frequently to peers.

The findings of this study suggest that information systems managers and designers need to consider carefully the needs for different types of information at different managerial levels. Managers in charge of routing information largely influence the type and amount disseminated to different managerial levels. These factors must be considered and controls administered to insure that the proper type of information reaches the appropriate decision maker. Future research is needed to investigate the information recipient's perception of information types to complement the results of this study. Moreover, studies of information processes need to consider the hierarchical level of both the sender and the recipient of information in their research designs.
