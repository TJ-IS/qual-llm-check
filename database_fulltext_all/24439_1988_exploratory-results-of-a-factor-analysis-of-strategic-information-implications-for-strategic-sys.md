# Exploratory Results of a Factor Analysis of Strategic Information: Implications for Strategic Systems Planning

EDWARD J. SZEWCZAK

EDWARD J. SZEWCZAL is an Associate Professor in the Department of Management Information Systems at Canisius College, Buffalo, New York. He was previously on the faculty of the Southern Illinois University at Carbondale. His dissertation work in MIS and strategic management was completed at the Graduate School of Business of the University of Pittsburgh. His published work has appeared (or is scheduled to appear) in DATA BASE, Information & Management, Journal of Management Case Studies, Logique et Analyse, Long Range Planning, Organizational Behavior Teaching Review, and Omega, as well as in national and regional conference and symposium proceedings. His current research interests include the evaluation of information systems and the use of computer networking to support strategic management processes.

ABSTRACT: The usefulness of strategic decision support systems (SDSSs) has been a highly discussed, yet under-investigated issue. This paper attempts to help fill the gap between available subjective/argumentative theory and the necessary empirical evidence to evaluate and implement existing claims and design methodologies.

An empirical study is described that explicitly focused on the usefulness of information in a strategic decision-making context. Factor analysis was employed in the derivation of five orthogonal dimensions of the usefulness of strategic information. The results suggest that strategic systems planning $[20]$ may be preferable to historical, traditional approaches to providing useful information to strategic decision makers in a sDSS context. A research agenda is discussed that suggests avenues for studying strategic systems planning in the context of various planning processes contributing to sDSS development, of knowledge-based sDSSs, and of strategic expert systems.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: strategic information, strategic systems planning.

The author wishes to express appreciation to University Professor William R. King for his help in developing the questionnaire items used in this study, and to three anonymous reviewers for excellent critiques of earlier versions of this paper.

VARIOUS APPROACHES to designing information systems to support strategic planning have been proposed [11, 21]. These approaches generally assume that the information system, when properly developed, will deliver useful information to strategic planning decision makers.

More recently, the concept of a strategic decision support system (sDss) has gained credence $[20]$ . Exploiting the rapid development of computer-based technology and its proliferation throughout the organization, sDsss are developed to deliver information relevant to a variety of strategic decision-making contexts, with the purpose of improving the quality of strategic decision making.

An evaluation of the usefulness of the information provided is central to evaluating the effectiveness of sdsss to support strategic planning. This fact has been explicitly recognized by the strategic systems planning (ssp) process proposed by King [20]. In ssp an organization's mission, objectives, business strategies, and other strategic attributes form an “organizational strategy set” from which more specific organizational system elements may be derived. Sss may be performed for any organization, and the specifics of the organizational strategy set and derived strategy sets will be industry and organization specific. The organizational strategy set may be viewed as an organizational database that can be accessed to provide the basis for the development of more specific strategy sets.

This article reports on an empirical study designed to explore the efficacy of the ssp process by addressing the issue of the perceived dimensionality of the usefulness of strategic information. The methodology of the study is similar to the approach adopted in $[25]$ for evaluating a strategic planning information system in terms of key attitudinal factors and value perceptions of system users. The results of the study revealed five orthogonal (i.e., maximally uncorrelated) dimensions of strategic information. Generally speaking, empirically derived dimensions of information usefulness can be utilized to evaluate strategy sets (construed as organizational databases) to ensure that they are adequately fulfilling their function in terms of the usefulness of the data provided to strategic managers.

## Prior Research on the Dimensionality of Information

INFORMATION has been defined as data that have been “evaluated for use in a specific context” [19, p.5] Strategic information may be defined as “data that have been evaluated for use in the context of strategic management.” Strategic management has been defined as “a stream of decisions and actions which leads to the development of an effective strategy or strategies to help achieve corporate objectives” [9, p.5].

Previous empirical studies on the dimensionality of information are not directly relevant to strategic information. Part of the problem with previous studies is that they have been either too broadly or too narrowly focused with respect to task specification. Zmud [42] performed a laboratory study to address the issue of the dimensionality of information in general. In describing the task specified for subjects to complete as a decision scenario “of a general nature” (p. 190), the study’s task focus was too broadly defined for its results to be directly relevant to strategic information, although the study did suggest a useful approach to researching the issue. Larcker and Lessig [26] examined two hypothesized dimensions of information which they called “perceived importance” and “perceived useableness.” The task focus of the study was on the capital budgeting decision, but conclusions were drawn for information in general (even though the study subjects themselves suggested that the usefulness of information was different depending on the decision at hand). Neither study drew an explicit distinction between data and information in terms of the use to which the information was to be put.

Snavely [37] took a conceptual approach to the question of the dimensionality of information by viewing the usefulness of information in terms of a number of criteria, or attributes, that are related to each other in such a way as to form a hierarchy of meaningful relationships. With usefulness as the first-level criterion, second-level criteria of relevance, reliability, understandability, significance, sufficiency, and practicality were identified as comprising the meaning of information usefulness. Third-level criteria were also identified as comprising the meaning of each second-level criterion. For example, verifiability and freedom from bias were identified as comprising the meaning of reliability. Though this approach has intuitive appeal, it was never tested empirically. Also the approach suffered in its details (in particular, its third-level criteria) from an overemphasis on the usefulness of financial accounting information, and hence was not directly applicable to other areas of information usefulness such as strategic management. However, it was felt that the approach was helpful in thinking about the meaning of information usefulness in operational terms applicable to management concerns, and is extendable to the strategic management context without major conceptual difficulties. (See the discussion of the data collection instrument later.)

## Research Methodology

THE RATIONALE for the study was to explore the dimensionality of strategic information by focusing on a specific strategic decision-making task.

## The Subjects

The subjects were 155 MBA students taking an upper-level Management Policy course at a northeastern U.S. university. The Management Policy course was considered a “capstone” course in which students were expected to integrate and apply concepts from prior coursework in the analysis and solution of strategic management problems. All students had completed a Strategic Planning and Control course prior to entering the Management Policy course. Generally speaking, the task of strategic planning in organizations is reserved for those with appropriate knowledge, skills, and experience. However, there is some evidence that students and managers exhibit similar levels of performance when analyzing business cases (Bhaskar [3], as reported by Simon [36]).

The experimental study complemented the Management Policy course objectives in that it provided the subjects with an opportunity to engage in a strategic management decision-making process for a medium-sized company faced with the strategic choice of entering a new, potentially rewarding market for its innovative product. Subjects were not required to participate in the study, instead they freely chose to participate as part of a classroom exercise.

## The Task

Subjects were directed to evaluate the data in a strategic management case to decide which data were most significant in light of the strategic choice facing the case company. The case, “Sweco, Inc.” [5], was prepared as a basis for class discussion rather than to illustrate either effective or ineffective handling of an administrative solution. Subjects were instructed to think of themselves as managers in the case company responsible for making the strategic decision based on the data in the case. They were instructed to consider both quantitative and qualitative data in arriving at their evaluations of the case data as useful information. The case was chosen in light of four criteria judged relevant to the study: (1) its balance of quantitative and qualitative data, (2) its strategic decision-making focus, (3) the currency of its data in terms of contemporary subject matter, and (4) its moderate length.

The subjects, who had been directed to read the case beforehand, evaluated the case data using a prepared data collection instrument. The subjects' responses to the questionnaire items were not influenced by any feedback they received on their case performance, since feedback was provided only after the questionnaires had been completed.

## The Data Collection Instrument

Data on subject evaluations were collected using a 15-item questionnaire based on the Snavely conceptual framework of information usefulness, specifically, on the third-level criteria. However, since Snavely's framework had a strong financial accounting emphasis, the third-level criteria were adapted, where necessary, to fit the study's strategic management focus. In particular, the relevance criterion was adapted to reflect fundamental strategic management concerns (to formulate objectives, goals, and strategies; to formulate and evaluate strategic guidelines, directives, and control policies; and to evaluate strategic decision-making effectiveness [22]) in place of financial accounting concerns (to value the firm; to evaluate management; and to evaluate management's policies [37]). The significance criterion was adapted to reflect the concern with decision support [20] in place of a concern with the information's ability to “affect” decisions [37]. Finally, the practicality criterion was adapted to reflect the fact that strategic information may be used for more than one decision or in more than one environment [22, 27].

## Analysis of Results

Subject responses were factor analyzed using the BMDP statistical software package (program P4M), electing Kaiser's varimax rotation [18] and retaining all factors whose eigenvalues were greater than 1.0. Varimax rotation produces a factor structure in which each factor is orthogonal, i.e., maximally uncorrelated, with every other factor. (Strictly speaking, a “dimension” is an orthogonal factor.) With 15 items and 155 subjects, the ratio of observations to items is considered adequate to meet Nunnally’s [31] criterion of at least 10 observations per item to generate a statistically meaningful result using factor analysis.

The means and standard deviations of subject responses to each questionnaire item are presented in Table 2. Table 3 presents the varimax-rotated factor loadings, communalities $(h_i^2)$ , eigenvalues $(\lambda_j)$ and cumulative variance explained for each factor. A variable's communality is an $R^2$ measure (ranging from 0 to 1) based on a regression of a raw variable on the factors. It is equal to the sum of the squared loadings (with each factor) in that variable's row. A factor's eigenvalue is equivalent to the sum of the squared loadings (with each variable) in that factor's column. The sum of the $h_{i}^{2}$ equals the sum of the $\lambda_{j}$ (before rounding) [12].

## Discussion

## Multidimensionality of Strategic Information

The results of the study indicate that the usefulness of strategic information is a multidimensional concept. Though this idea is hardly new, and is present in the literature in various contexts (e.g., [8, 17, 23]), the results offer empirical support for five dimensions which are orthogonal, i.e., maximally uncorrelated, given the information contained in the strategic management case. The same general result concerning the multidimensionality of information was reported in [26] and [42], though the focus of these studies was not on strategic information specifically. It should be noted, however, that in addition to not addressing strategic information specifically, neither of these studies met Nunnally's [31] criterion of ten observations to each data collection instrument item for a factor analysis to be statistically meaningful.

The derived dimensions of the usefulness of strategic information can be assigned reasonable (albeit admittedly subjective) interpretations, as given in Table 4. Each of these derived factors has an empirical basis as well as intuitive plausibility in the context of strategic decision making.

Factor 1 (“strategic relevance”) is defined by items that deal with strategy formulation, strategy evaluation, and management policy. It also captures the consistency of information with what is expected in a strategic context, the flexibility of the information to be used for more than one decision or in more than one environment, and the worth of the information. From an intuitive point of view, this dimension is fundamental to information in a strategic management context. Not only is strategic information relevant to strategy formulation, strategy evaluation, and management policy, but it is also what is expected in a strategic management context rather than, say, a capital budgeting context. Also, since strategic management is concerned with the entire organization in its many environmental settings, the information is relevant to each of the many environmental settings and to each of the various decisions that must be made with regard to the organization in its environmental settings. Finally, since strategic management is critical to the long-term welfare of an organization, information relevant to strategic management is viewed as having considerable worth to the organization. It should be noted that the concept of strategic relevance as derived in this study may be construed as somewhat limited insofar as it does not address other important aspects such as managerial values, political aspirations, motivations, styles, etc.

Factor 2 (“factual overload”) is defined by items that deal with information simplicity (facts) and an overabundance of information. From an intuitive point of view, strategic management is characterized by an overabundance of facts available to an organization both from its external and internal environments. The factual overload presents a challenge to management to make decisions as to which facts are most important to strategic management, since the sheer volume of unevaluated facts will otherwise impede the progress, and hinder the effectiveness, of strategic management. The ability of management to focus on important information appears to be fundamental to the success of environmental scanning efforts. This dimension suggests that data can be evaluated to produce facts and that facts can be further evaluated as to their importance in strategic decision making. It may be noted that this result corroborates findings published previously in the literature on managerial information processing and predecisional behavior (e.g., [32, 33, 35, 41]).

Factor 3 (“quantitative insufficiency”) is defined by items that deal with quantitative information, information scarcity, and information support in decision making. From an intuitive point of view, this dimension suggests that quantitative tools (numbers and models) have a limited effectiveness in strategic decision making. Though quantitative data may be available, it alone is not sufficient for making strategic decisions but must be supplemented by qualitative data. For example, rigorous mathematical forecasting models must be supplemented by less rigorous forecasting techniques, such as scenarios and the Delphi method, before effective strategic decision making can be realized. To rely too heavily (or even exclusively) on quantitative data may result in the perception that even more quantitative data than what is at hand is needed, when actually what is needed is a judicious balance of quantitative and qualitative data.

Factor 4 (“‘verifiable uniformity’”) is defined by items that deal with the ability of the information to be verified and with the uniformity of the meaning of information across various items of information. From an intuitive point of view, this dimension suggests that strategic information is at least verifiable, i.e., it lends itself to cross-checking. Otherwise fundamentally important strategic decisions will potentially be made on an unreliable (and even false) information basis. This will add to the already uncertain nature of the decision making. Further, if the information is verifiable but is not understood in such a way as to be communicated uniformly, then decision making will progress very slowly (if it progresses at all), since the uncertain and perhaps conflicting meaning of terms will force strategic management to turn its focus away from decision making and toward seeking clarification for the data at hand.

Factor 5 (“timely accuracy”) is defined by items that deal with the timeliness of the information and the degree to which it is biased in a particular direction. From an intuitive point of view, this dimension suggests that strategic information is biased in a particular direction since the evaluator of data for use in a strategic management context is not impartial, but instead wishes to see certain organizational missions and objectives realized. This realization will occur in time, so to be useful strategic information is also timely, i.e., the strategic decision maker has it in time to use it effectively in decision making aimed at accomplishing the organizational mission and objectives.

It must be emphasized that dimensions derived empirically using factor analysis do not constitute reality $[31]$ but are merely suggestive of (or “point to”) what reality might be. The five-factor structure, though intuitively plausible, is based on the data in the strategic management case chosen for the study and may not be derived from other case material. What factors are derived in the evaluation of case data will be a function of the case material and perhaps even a function of the type of case users (students, managers) from which data are collected for analysis.

Other influences identified in the psychological decision theory literature are also relevant in this context. For example, Hogarth and Makridakis [15] review a number of biases (e.g., selective perception, illusory correlation, inconsistency, conservatism) that may impact a strategic planner's decision-making effectiveness. This study assumes a uniform utility for information dimensions which, while viable in this context, is still only an assumption that may not be valid in all contexts. Further, it should be noted that specific dimensions of information usefulness may evolve in an organizational setting. Since practical strategic planning involves the integration of several individual opinions, the influences on planners' opinions over time and their impact on perceived information usefulness remains an important topic for further research.

## Implications for Strategic Systems Planning: A Research Agenda

The results of the study are consistent with the primary objective of ssp; namely, the providing of useful information for the support of strategic decision making. In the context of the Sweco, Inc. case, it seems reasonable to infer that the process of ssp (i.e., the derivation of a market entry strategy set from an organizational strategy set) succeeds in providing an effective environment for the generation of useful information from available data, as is evidenced by the intuitive plausibility of the derived dimensionality of strategic information. Of course, this inference must be qualified in light of the fact that the results of the study are based on an idealized classroom situation using individual decision makers who are responsible for developing their own “mental” strategy sets. In a real world context, the various strategy sets will in all likelihood be the output of some formalized planning process involving groups of managers (supported by staff) representing various diverse interests in an organization [21].

The results of this exploratory study appear to support the claim that ssp is probably preferable to more traditional approaches to providing useful information to strategic decision makers, where the usefulness of the information is determined by the decision makers themselves during the ssp process. The methodology described in this article can be used to provide a good starting point for sdss development, particularly in terms of the representations to be included in the sdss. It can be employed to evaluate the perceived usefulness of the information on which the various ssp strategy sets are developed in terms of empirically derived dimensions.

These observations suggest a research agenda relevant to future developments in ssp. There are three primary areas for further research: (1) the effects of various planning processes contributing to sdss development; (2) the development of knowledge-based sdsses; and (3) the transition to strategic expert systems and the “value chain” [34].

## Various planning processes

The adoption of various planning processes may have an effect on the perceived usefulness of information. Applegate, Chen, Konsynski, and Nunamaker $[2]$ identify various planning processes for eliciting planner knowledge within the context of the PLEXIS planning system. Each of these processes may have an effect on the way people perceive information usefulness. Detailed experimental studies of each of these processes and their effects on the perception of information usefulness are suggested as fruitful avenues for further research.

Since most planning processes take place in a group context, the literature on group decision support systems (GDSS) is relevant here (e.g., [16]). Nunamaker, Applegate, and Konsynski [30] present the results of a study suggesting that group creativity can be facilitated in a GDSS context, specifically, by electronic brainstorming. Along the same lines Szewczak [40] suggests that various benefits may be achieved in industry using local- and wide-area computer networking in the development of “strategic data bases” [21], a process that was proposed based on traditional brainstorming. Additional research is needed to study whether (1) the planning processes themselves, (2) the GDSS technology, or (3) the synergistic interaction between various planning processes and GDSS technology is/are responsible for improved strategic planning effectiveness.

## Development of knowledge-based ssses

SSP is based on the development of various strategy sets which may be viewed as databases containing specific information relevant to strategic planning. Though not explicitly labeled as such in $[20]$ , this information may be construed as the “knowledge” which various strategic planners have brought to bear on their specific planning problem. Indeed, the nature of this information is predominantly qualitative/symbolic as opposed to quantitative/numeric. When viewed in this way, the strategy sets may be seen as forming a “knowledge base” (rather than a “database”) for strategic planning.

This terminological change, while not detracting in any way from the ssp concept, serves to link ssp to current knowledge-based systems thought. The derived factor “quantitative insufficiency” suggests that knowledge-based sdsss are desirable and a worthwhile avenue for further research. The fact that strategy sets may be derived logically from other strategy sets suggests that a knowledge-based systems design may be preferable to a traditional systems design $[29]$ in ssp.

## Transition to strategic expert systems

Knowledge bases are central to the development of expert systems, i.e., systems which mimic a person (expert) who can solve difficult problems effectively and efficiently [38]. Construing ssp strategy sets as knowledge bases, the integration of strategy sets into expert systems developed for the support of organizational planning appears a worthwhile avenue for further research. In particular, the qualitative nature of the strategy sets makes them good candidates for synthesizing and maintaining evolving organizational models of planning [7].

Sviokla [38] has indicated that expert systems can be tied to the concept of the “value chain” [34], insofar as it is possible to identify the types of expertise germane to a firm’s value-adding activity. The knowledge bases of expert systems developed around the ssp concept may benefit from using the evaluative methodology described in this paper. The methodology may be viewed as having value in a knowledge engineering context (e.g., [10]). Further research is needed to determine the extent of the methodology’s worth to a knowledge engineer in the development of strategy sets in value-added activity systems applications. It may well be that much of the value of such systems is directly attributable to the usefulness of the strategy sets forming the knowledge component of the systems.

## Summary

THE EFFICACY OF SSP was approached through a study of the dimensionality of strategic information. The usefulness of strategic information was shown empirically to exhibit five orthogonal dimensions based on the use of a strategic management case. Though these dimensions are the result of an exploratory study, they provide a reasonable basis for thinking that the SSP process may provide a “value-added” approach to structuring the perceptions of participants regarding information usefulness in an intuitively acceptable manner. A research agenda focusing on various planning processes, knowledge-based SDSSs, and strategic expert systems suggests avenues of possible study which may help to evaluate this claim in the context of current technological developments in management information systems.

## Appendix

This appendix presents the data collection questionnaire items. The items were constructed using a 7-point Likert scale with “Completely Disagree” = 1, “Neutral” = 4, and “Completely Agree” = 7 to record the subject’s opinion. The items were randomized in the creation of the questionnaire.

1. The case information is helpful in the formulation of management objectives, goals, and strategies.

2. The case information contributes to the formulation and evaluation of management guidelines, directives, control policies, etc.

3. The case information aids us in evaluating our decision-making effectiveness; i.e., helps in assessing how good our decisions are.

4. The case information can be verified to some degree; i.e., can be “cross-checked” if desired.

5. The case information is free from bias, i.e., accurately represents the way things are; is not slanted in a particular direction.

6. The case information is quantitative; i.e., can be usefully employed in a numerical context.

7. The case information is consistent with what we expect to receive; i.e., we understand how the information we have relates to our need for the information.

8. The case information is comparable to similar information provided about the same topic; i.e., there is a uniformity of meaning of words and numbers across information items.

9. The case information is simple enough to use; i.e., is not so complex as to be confusing or disordered.

10. The case information supports decision making or action; i.e., is instrumental or helpful in making choices.

11. The case information is insufficient insofar as there is too little of it to be useful.

12. The case information is too voluminous insofar as there is too much of it to deal with effectively.

13. The case information is worth more to use than it costs to obtain.

14. The case information can be used for more than one decision or in more than one environment.

15. The case information is available to us on a timely basis; i.e., it is not too “old” to be of use.
