# The Quality of User Documentation: An Instrument Validation

GHOLAMREZA TORKZADEH

G. TORKZADEH is Assistant Professor of Information Systems and Operations Management Department at the University of Toledo. He holds a Ph.D. in Operations Research from the University of Lancaster, England. A member of O.R. Society of Great Britain, TIMS, DSI, ACM, and SIM, he was involved in research programs pertaining to application of O.R. (in the public sector), distribution, resource allocation/reallocation, and mathematical modelling and has published in the Journal of Operational Research Society, Communications of the ACM, MIS Quarterly, and Information & Management. One of his current research interests is the management of the information systems function.

ABSTRACT: User documentation is an important tool for communication. It enhances the value of an application to the user and, in turn, improves user satisfaction. The impact of user documentation is even more considerable since interest in end-user computing is booming. Yet, there are very few practical managerial tools for measuring the quality of user documentation. This article examines two alternative forms of an instrument for measuring user perceptions of the quality of user documentation. The reliability and validity of these instruments are examined. Using a survey of 618 end-users from 44 firms, the author cross validates this instrument, conducts a factor analysis, and assesses the construct validity of the instrument. Furthermore, the reliability and validity of the instrument is assessed by nature and type of application. The results indicate a strong support for the instrument. With these instruments, management can more easily monitor and control the condition of their firm's user documentation.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: documentation, user documentation, user satisfaction, management

It is an educational as well as a training tool. Good user documentation has been considered an important component of “user friendliness” [13, 14], and has been associated with MIS development success [4] and user satisfaction [5]. Furthermore, professional literature has frequently cited the importance of user documentation [9, 15, 16, 17].

Good user documentation facilitates communication between users and analysts $[7, 12]$ , is important in improving user understanding $[8]$ , and enhances the value of applications for the user $[6]$ . Schaeffer $[15]$ suggests that user documentation is important for generating a satisfying user image of the systems department, making work more efficient, reducing costs and confusion, eliminating frustration, and improving management control and employee morale.

To measure the quality of user documentation, Doll and Torkzadeh [6] developed a comprehensive fifteen-item form and an alternative three-item short form. Using a limited sample of 81, they assessed reliability and validity of these instruments, and provided a tentative recommendation for using these tools. Due to the small sample size, they did not carry out factor analysis of the items; neither did they provide comparable reliability and validity results for all “types of applications,” nor provide quartile scores (standards) based on a large sample for evaluating the quality of user documentation. Furthermore, their sample covered only five different organizations, limiting cross-organizational validation.

Using a sample of 618 end-users, this article examines the reliability and validity of an instrument, assesses the dimensionality of the items proposed, provides standards for evaluating the quality of user documentation, and finally recommends a comprehensive seven-item instrument that is tested for reliability and validity. The results indicate that it can be used for both basic research and applied settings.

## 2. The Study

TO FURTHER EXPLORE THE INSTRUMENT, the questionnaire was administered in 44 firms. The sample was select rather than random; however, the large number of firms supports the generalizability of the findings. In each of these firms, the MIS director was asked to identify the major applications and the major users who directly interact with each application. The respondents were end-users who would interact with the application software to make decisions based upon the system's output. They also interact with the application software to enter information or prepare output reports. In the end-user computing environment these two roles are often combined [3]; the person who utilizes the system output also develops it.

To facilitate comparative analysis between earlier findings and those reported here, this survey used the same items and scores reported by Doll and Torkzadeh. The user documentation questions used a five-point scale: strongly disagree = 1; disagree = 2; neutral = 3; agree = 4; strongly agree = 5.

Data were gathered by research assistants who first interviewed users (using a structured interview process) and then administered the questionnaire. The structured interview questionnaire asked users how satisfied they were with the user documentation available, how it could be improved, and how important good user documentation was to them. Further, it instructed the interviewers to examine a copy of the user documentation and record their perception of its quality. The more qualitative interview comments were compared with the questionnaire data to identify respondents who did not complete the questionnaire carefully. Only eight responses were discarded because interview comments did not correspond with the questionnaire data.

A sample of 618 usable end-user responses was obtained. This sample represented two hundred fifty different applications with an average of 2.5 responses per application. The respondents were categorized by industry and position. The responses by industry were:

The respondents indicated that 41.9% of the applications were “primarily developed by an end-user” but only 91 (14.7%) had personally developed the application themselves. Twenty-five percent were micro whereas 75% were mini or mainframe applications. The applications were 37.6% decision support; 19.3% data base; 19.8% exception reporting; 19.9% monitor; and 3.4% other (e.g., word processing). Nine percent of respondents indicated that quality user documentation was non-existent for their applications, yielding 563 usable responses.

Cronbach's alpha was used to assess reliability. Kerlinger [10] cites two methods of construct validation, correlations between total scores and item scores, and factor analysis. The first approach assumes the total score to be valid; thus the extent to which the item correlates with the total score is indicative of construct validity for the item. In this study each item score was subtracted from the total score in order to avoid a spurious part-whole correlation [2]; the result is a corrected item total (sum for 14 items) which was then correlated with the item score.

Factor analysis was also used to assess construct validity. The data were examined using principal components analysis as the extraction technique and varimax as a method of rotation for factor analysis. The ratio of sample size to number of items (38:1) was well above the minimum 10:1 ratio suggested for factor analysis by Kerlinger [10]. However, in this case a large sample was considered essential in order to generate data across organizations and enhance the generality of the results. To assess reliability and validity by nature and type of application, users were asked whether their application was: end-user developed; micro or mainframe; or monitor, exception reporting, inquiry, or analysis [1].

A measure of criterion-related validity [12] was also examined to identify how closely items, as well as the fifteen-item overall scale, were related to the quality of the documentation construct. In this survey, a separate criterion question (“Overall, how would you rate the quality of the user documentation available for this application?”) was used. The criterion question used a five-point scale: 1 = non-existent; 2 = poor; 3 = fair; 4 = good; 5 = excellent. This global item, measuring perceived overall quality of the user documentation for specific application, was considered to be valid measure [10] and was used as a criterion scale. The extent to which each item was correlated with this criterion scale provided a measure of criterion-related validity.

Table 2 presents data on construct and criterion-related validity. The individual items show a high correlation with corrected item total correlations (a measure of internal consistency) ranging from .65 to .81. The correlations between the individual items and the criterion ranged from .51 to .78. The factor analysis resulted in only one factor indicating a single construct that explained $58\%$ of the variance with item loading ranging from .63 to .85.

Doll and Torkzadeh [6] also provide a short three-item alternative for a summary evaluation of the quality of user documentation—questions 1, 3, and 6. These three measures captured the notions of “complete,” “well-written,” and “describes step-by-step how to use the software.” Using the large sample size, reliability (alpha = .84) and criterion-related validity (.82) were calculated for this three-item instrument. While criterion-related validity was comparable with earlier findings (.83), the reliability was found to be lower than previously reported (alpha = .90). This would suggest that the three-item scale is reliable for use in basic research but not for use in applied settings where important decisions are made with respect to test scores [11].

To obtain a short form that could be used in applied settings, the author examined Table 2 to identify items that had highest factor loading, and Table 1 to assess the description of measures. User documentation is conceptualized as having one inherent attribute (i.e., how well it is written) and three functions (i.e., helping the user understand what the software does, how it works, and how to use it) [6]. Items with the highest factor loading and descriptions capturing the essence of good user documentation were selected. The results suggest a seven-item instrument with measures capturing notions of “complete,” “easy to use,” “describes step-by-step how to use the software,” “well written,” “provides relevant information,” “concise,” and “satisfactory.” These correspond to questions 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 14, and 15 in Table 1.

The eight remaining measures in the fifteen-item instrument are somewhat repetitive of the same, or very similar, notions covered by these seven items. For example, “The user documentation is well written” captures a similar notion expressed by the questions: “The user documentation is presented in a simple and straightforward way” or “The user documentation is well organized,” or “The user documentation is written in non-technical language” or “The user documentation is written from a user’s perspective.”

All seven items have factor loadings of .78 or more, and overall they have high reliability (alpha = .92) and criterion-related validity (.86). These are comparable with the corresponding results for the fifteen-item instrument. The corrected item total correlation for this scale ranged from .74 to .81 and the correlation with criterion ranged from .69 to .78.

Reliability for both the seven- and fifteen-item scales is consistently well above the .80 minimum standard for basic research, and is consistently above the .9 standard suggested for use in applied settings where important decisions will be made with respect to specific test scores [11].

## 4. Discussion and Conclusions

USER DOCUMENTATION plays an important role in maintaining user satisfaction, improving user understanding, and enhancing communication between users and analysts during the systems development process. The effective use of MIS/DSS in organizations requires that users understand how their application works as well as “how to use it.” This effectiveness is more critical in an end-user computing environment where users become more dependent on documentation and less dependent on the interaction with analysts and programmers.

Management needs reliable and applicable instruments to assess the condition of their user documentation. Doll and Torkzadeh have provided an instrument with two alternative forms. This paper provides a cross validation of that instrument and reports the reliability and validity of two tools for examining quality of a firm's user documentation. A three-item scale was examined for reliability and validity and found to be useful for basic research but not for applied settings. A fifteen-item scale and a seven-item scale for measuring the quality of user documentation have been described.

The results suggest that both these scales have adequate reliability and validity and might be used with confidence across a variety of applications for applied settings where important decisions will be made. However, the seven-item scale seems to provide a comprehensive and adequate tool rendering the use of the longer instrument less plausible.

With this instrument, it becomes practical to monitor and control the condition of a firm's user documentation. One application's or vendor's documentation can be compared systematically with that of another. Where poorly documented applications are identified, the instrument may suggest where improvements can be made. Such efforts may improve the quality of user documentation and protect a firm's investment in information technology by enhancing the value of the application for the user.

Finally, it is worth noting that use of a structured interview approach in this study proved to be a useful practice for explaining to the user/respondent the definition of user documentation and the type of application. A number of respondents indicated that either they did not have documentation or they were not familiar with the concept. The written definition plus the interviews helped to explain the concept. It is recommended that such a definition be included in the instrument. Without the structured interview effort and/or a written definition, the reliability might be slightly lower.
