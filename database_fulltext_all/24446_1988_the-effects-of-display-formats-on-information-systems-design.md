# The Effects of Display Formats on Information Systems Design

MATTHEW J. LIBERATORE, GEORGE J. TITUS, and PAUL W. DIXON

ABSTRACT: A field experiment was conducted to investigate the effects of differences in task, display, and viewer characteristics on interpretation accuracy, viewer preference, and subjects' utility ratings of the displays. Practicing engineering managers at Lockheed-Georgia company were the subjects. Display format influenced interpretation accuracy only through its interaction with task-related variables. Although improvement in interpretation accuracy occurred over time, no comparative advantage of graphs over tables was found. No clear viewer preferences for graphs or tables were expressed, except at the conclusion of the experiment, when tables were favored for finance-related reports. Tables were generally perceived to be more useful in answering interpretation accuracy questions, although the results were not as definitive in the detailed analysis. Personality type, as measured by field-independence/dependence, was unrelated to differences in interpretation accuracy and viewer preference.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: Graphics in information systems, information media.

## 1. Introduction

THE ADVANCES IN COMPUTER TECHNOLOGY offer systems designers a wide range of options to structure data and present information. Systems designers are utilizing graphical and color-enhanced reports ostensibly to improve the ease and quality of decision-making. However, the benefits of color and graphics are often exaggerated in some popular publications, while the results of the few experimental studies are generally mixed or conflicting. Evidence to date suggests that the selection of suitable display formats must consider such factors as the nature of the tasks themselves, the preferences and performance requirements of the user, the impact of learning and experience, and the characteristics of the organization and its environment.

In late 1983, the Lockheed-Georgia Company considered expanding its Management Information and Decision Support (MIDS) System beyond senior executive users by including middle-level engineering managers within a new MIDS subsystem (see Houdeshel and Watson [11] for a detailed discussion of MIDS). In this context, many design issues were raised, such as the selection of display formats to match the type of information viewed and the anticipated task requirements of the users. To address these design issues, a field experiment using practicing engineering managers was proposed. A mockup of a MIDS engineering subsystem was developed for this experiment.

The purpose of this paper is to present an experimental framework and the results of a field experiment which investigated the effects of: (1) information display formats, (2) task characteristics, (3) type of information, and (4) cognitive style, on interpretation accuracy, viewer preference of display formats, and viewer ratings on report utility. The experimental conditions were designed to approximate the actual decision-making activities of experienced managers as closely as possible. The experiment was conducted from June 1984 through September 1984 in an engineering project management environment at the

Lockheed-Georgia Company, Marietta, Georgia.

The next section reviews the literature on display formats and cognitive style. This is followed by a description of the research framework and its application for the design of the study. The results and data analysis are presented, and the paper concludes with a discussion of the findings, their applications, and the suitability of the experimental framework and design.

## 2. Background

## Presentation Mode

The mode of information presentation has received considerable attention in the literature $[5, 18]$ . Experimental evidence to support the claims that graphics improve managerial performance and productivity are quite limited and tenuous at best $[13, 6, 3]$ . In an extensive review and analysis of the literature, DeSanctis $[6]$ found that previous management-oriented studies which compared graphs and tables on interpretation accuracy and viewer preference were inconclusive. One reason for these conflicting results is that comparative studies of tables versus graphs do not fully consider differences in the task, type of information viewed, and the interactions of these variables with display format. For example, since a variety of tasks are used across experimental studies, it may be inappropriate to compare the results in one task environment with those in another $[15]$ . As several authors suggest, the best method of data display may be a function of the characteristics of the tasks to be accomplished by the user $[6, 7, 4]$ .

## Cognitive Style

The process behavior of obtaining and interpreting information (as opposed to decision-making capability) is termed cognitive style in the literature $[16]$ . Field-independence/dependence has been used by many management and information systems researchers as a measure of cognitive style and personality differences (e.g., $[19, 9, 1, 2, 3]$ ). A reliable measure of the degree of field independence is the Group Embedded Figures Test $[21]$ , used by the above-mentioned studies.

The field-independent dimension can be described as the ability to separate an item from an organized field or to overcome an embedded context $[20]$ . Multi-line or multi-bar graphs can be viewed as complex fields which are embedding information. Field-dependents are comparatively passive receivers of information and tend not to structure or restructure a field, given situational demands. One would expect these individuals to be disrupted most when the display format does not match a particular preference. However, in previous studies $[19, 3]$ , no interaction between information presentation type and cognitive style when measuring performance was found. The relationship between cognitive style and display format as they jointly influence viewer preference and interpretation accuracy may exist only in some specific, well-defined task environments.

In summary, the studies reviewed above emphasize the comparison of “tables versus graphs” and investigate which is better for the dependent variable(s) of interest. This approach does not fully consider the differential effect of other independent variables, such as task differences. For this reason, we present a research framework and describe a field experiment that investigates other factors which may affect interpretation accuracy and viewer preference.

## 3. The Experiment

THE FRAMEWORK WHICH SUGGESTED the design of this experiment is presented as Figure 1. It builds on the work of others (e.g., [6, 10]) and identifies classes of variables that influence interpretation accuracy (as well as other measures of performance) and viewer preference. The components of this framework—namely, task, display, and viewer characteristics—as well as experimental/environmental conditions are briefly described below as they were applied to this study.

## Task Characteristics

The basic task for the project manager in this experiment is to interpret correctly project or activity status using financial $(F)$ , manpower $(M)$ , and schedule $(S)$ information. In this experiment, each subject was required to view a series of 17 episodes. Each episode provides a “glimpse” or “snapshot” of the project or one of its activities. An episode consists of three displays of F, M, and S information at a particular point in the project’s history. The 17 episodes comprising the experiment were not presented to follow the chronology of a project’s progress. This was done to eliminate the need for the subjects to remember or use the past history of the project.

Figure 2. Sample Interpretation Accuracy Questions

The same sequence of episodes was viewed by each subject.

For each display the subjects were asked to interpret accurately the status of the project or one of its activities. These interpretation accuracy questions were presented in an objective, multiple-choice format, where only one of these choices was a correct answer. The wording of the interpretation accuracy questions was essentially identical regardless of the type of information $(F, M, \text{or } S)$ viewed. This was possible because in all cases the questions compared budget or planned performance to actual.

The type of questions asked can be characterized as simple $(S)$ , range $(R)$ , and integrated $(I)$ . Simple questions require the subject to determine whether the project or activity is on, ahead, or behind schedule or budget, not started, or completed. Range questions probe the size of the variance, such as 15 to 30 percent over budget. Integrated questions require interpreting information contained in two consecutive displays to identify the source of the variance (if any). If the two consecutive displays pertained to schedule and finance information, the first question would be a simple or range type concerning schedule. A choice in the second question might be costs are over budget due to schedule delays. This integrated question would be characterized as both schedule and finance related. Sample interpretation accuracy questions are given as Figure 2.

In addition, the subjects were asked to rate the utility of each display viewed. This rating measured the perceived value of the report in answering the interpretation accuracy question. A five-point Likert scale was used, where 1 denoted least useful, and 5 most useful.

## Display Characteristics

Graphical and tabular display formats were used to present information to each subject. As in the Benbasat and Dexter [3] study, the design of the tabular and graphical displays was done in concert to minimize differences in information content, use of color, and report layout. The graphical formats followed the guidelines suggested by Integrated Software Systems Corporation [12], and incorporated the standard features and color conventions used in MIDS. For example, each display contained a screen number, title, date of last update, source of the information presented, and a telephone number of the source.

The graphical display for financial information $(F)$ was a variation of a horizontal bar chart design of Jarett [14]. His design was modified to show “earned value,” the budgeted amount of work completed. A series of line charts was used to display manpower information $(M)$ , while schedule information $(S)$ was presented using a standard Gantt or bar chart.

## Viewer Characteristics

The criteria for the selection of subjects were:

1. a minimum of five years' experience in engineering project management, i.e., senior engineers, project engineers, and engineering supervisors; and

2. the full cooperation of the immediate supervisor of each subject, due to the length of the experiment.

A questionnaire was administered to the subjects prior to commencing the experiment to elicit their familiarity with, and usage of, computerized information systems. Computer skill level was not included as a variable in the analysis, since nearly all of the subjects were inexperienced computer system users. The Group Embedded Figures Test (GEFT) was used to measure field independence/dependence for each subject.

## Experimental/Environmental Conditions

The site for this field experiment was the Lockheed-Georgia Company's Marietta, Georgia facility. The subjects viewed all displays on an Intelligent Systems Corporation stand-alone workstation. All subject responses were collected on-line, and only the ten-key pad functioned during the experiment to

make the system more user friendly.

The experiment had three distinct phases:

Phase 0: During this phase, the subjects received a hands-on demonstration of the computer equipment, and were provided background information on the hypothetical engineering project that formed the basis of the experimental tasks. In addition, the subjects were introduced to the experimental format and procedures using three practice episodes. These episodes also served to highlight problems or inconsistencies in display design that were corrected prior to the start of the next phase.

Phase I: The subjects viewed the questions to be answered before selecting the graphical or tabular display formats. The subjects could view only the selected display format. A 45-second time limit (found to be comfortable and adequate during Phase 0) was allowed for viewing this display. After viewing the display, the subjects answered the interpretation accuracy question and gave the report utility rating.

Phase II: This phase was identical to Phase I in all respects, except that display format, whether graphical or tabular, was selected by the experimenters, not by the subjects. Viewer preference was an independent variable during this phase.

Phase I consisted of eleven episodes or 33 displays, while Phase II had six episodes or 18 displays. A major objective of the experimental design was to balance the number of questions in the S, R, and I categories. Eleven of each of the S, R, and I questions were viewed in Phase I, while six of each were viewed in Phase II. The design was balanced to the extent possible to provide an equal number of information-type $(F, M, S)$ questions in Phase I. An equal number of graphical and tabular display formats were viewed for each F, M, S and S, R, I combination in Phase II.

## Performance/Preference

As previously discussed, performance was measured by the correct or incorrect responses to the interpretation accuracy questions in Phases I and II. Viewer preference was measured by the subjects' selection of either the graphical or tabular display formats in Phase I only. Finally, the report utility ratings were measured for each display format viewed in Phases I and II using a five-point Likert scale.

## Experimental Procedures

As a group, the 23 subjects were relatively inexperienced in the use of computerized information systems at the beginning of the study. Eighteen rated themselves as very inexperienced computer users. As in previous studies [19, 9, 1, 2, 3], subjects who scored 14 or more on the GEFT were classified as field-independent. This resulted in classifying 9 subjects as field-independent, and 14 as field-dependent. These 23 subjects collectively viewed 1,173 displays (23 × 51) in Phases I and II, and recorded 759 preferences (23 × 33) in Phase I. A summary of the experiment and its procedures is given as Figure 3. More details on the experiment and its procedures followed can be found in the Lockheed-Georgia technical report [8], which can also be obtained from the authors.

## 4. Results and Analysis

SINCE ALL DATA COLLECTED in this experiment are categorical in nature, nonparametric statistical methods were used in the analysis. The Mann–Whitney test was used for analyzing report utility rating relationships. The chi-square goodness-of-fit test was used to investigate the influence of task, viewer, and display characteristics on interpretation accuracy and viewer preference. The influence of time on the dependent variables of interest is considered elsewhere [17]. The principal findings discussed below still remain significant after a detailed analysis of the time-series data.

Tables 1, 2, and 3 summarize the results associated with interpretation accuracy. No statistically significant differences were found between information display format and interpretation accuracy in Phases I and II and overall, as seen from Table 1. Learning did occur as shown by the improvement in interpretation accuracy from 61.5 percent correct in Phase I to 71.7 percent in Phase II.

The participating managers were accustomed to using tables for financial information and graphs for manpower and schedule information. As expected, information type in conjunction with display format does affect interpretation accuracy, but in a manner not always consistent with the managers' experience (see Table 2). Interpretation accuracy was significantly higher for finance-related questions when tabular formats were viewed by the subjects during both phases of the experiment. The use of graphical charts is seen to be related to higher levels of interpretation accuracy for manpower-related questions, especially in Phase II. However, no significant differences were found concerning schedule-related information, even though the Gantt or bar chart is commonly used in engineering project management.

Type of question and display format were found to jointly influence interpretation accuracy in certain situations (see Table 3). For simple questions, no differences were found. For range questions, performance improved when tabular formats were viewed. A possible explanation is that it was easier for the subjects to estimate the percentage ranges directly from the numbers than from the bar charts. Integrated questions required the use of limited information from a previously viewed display to be integrated with information on the current display. As the experiment progressed, the subjects' performance improved when the current display used to answer the question was graphical. These results suggest that graphs may not lead to better performance in every situation, but may in some decision-making settings require more information processing and integration. Unfortunately, approaches for identifying situations favoring graphs appear to require detailed analysis and understanding of the users' needs and task environment.

The relationships among interpretation accuracy, display format, and personality type were analyzed. There appears to be no statistically significant relationship between personality type and interpretation accuracy if display format is not considered. This result holds for both phases of the study and for any information or question type. For subjects of a given personality type (i.e., either field-independent or dependent), the relationship between interpretation accuracy and display format was analyzed. ## Viewer Preference

Viewer preference was measured during Phase I of the study only. As can be seen from Table 1, minimal, if any, differences in viewer preference were found in the overall study; 51.4 percent of the displays selected were graphical, while 48.6 percent were tabular. When viewer preference was measured for specific information or question types (see Tables 4 and 5), no significant differences were noted.

Personality type was not found to influence viewer preference for graphs or tables. The same result was also found when the data were analyzed by phase and information and question type.

At the conclusion of the experiment, each subject was asked to indicate his final preference for display format by information type. A strong preference for the tabular format was indicated for finance-related information, and no clear preferences were expressed for manpower or schedule-related information (see Table 6). The preference expressed for displaying financial information in tabular formats is at odds with the subjects' selections during Phase I, and may reflect their increasing experience with both display formats.

## Report Utility Ratings

Tabular reports were perceived to be more relevant in answering the interpretation accuracy questions throughout both phases of the experiment (see Table 7). However, this result was not as definitive when the data were analyzed according to the levels of question and information types. For instance, no significant differences were found for simple and integrated questions, nor for manpower-related questions in Phase II. It is important to note that the subjects did not select the report formats (graphical or tabular) during Phase II, which may partially explain these results.

The higher subjective ratings for tabular reports appear to be more strongly related to field-dependents (especially in Phase II), as can be seen from Table 7. These results tend to support the notion that field-dependents may not be satisfied with a display format which is not of their own choosing, especially if the display is graphical and the information type is finance-related. However, as mentioned above, no differences in interpretation accuracy or viewer preference were found when comparing field-independent to field-dependents in Phase II.

At the conclusion of the experiment, the subjects were asked to indicate a comfort rating concerning their usage of the computerized information system. The average rating was 3.43 (on a five-point Likert scale), indicating a rating of moderately comfortable to comfortable. This is noteworthy since a majority of the subjects rated themselves as being inexperienced (1.39 average rating) in using computerized information systems at the start of the experiment.

## 5. Summary and Conclusions

THIS STUDY PRESENTED AND APPLIED A FRAMEWORK to investigate the effects of differences in task, display, and viewer characteristics on interpretation accuracy, viewer preference, and perceived report utility in a computerized information system. Display format was found to influence interpretation accuracy only through its interaction with question and information type. No clear preferences for display format were expressed under any circumstances during Phase I of the experiment.

However, a preference of tables for finance-related information was shown after Phase II was completed, indicating an effect of learning as the experiment progressed. Tabular formats were generally perceived to be more useful than graphs in answering interpretation accuracy questions, although the effect was somewhat weaker in Phase II. Field-dependents tended to rate the utility of tabular formats higher than field-independent, especially when they were unable to select the display format in Phase II.

The results presented indicate that information systems designers must consider the type of information viewed, question type, and the interactions of these factors in determining the display format. This presents a complex environment for the designer. We suggest that systems designers permit potential users to experiment with several display options to help determine which task characteristics and display formats reinforce each other. This approach (comparable to prototyping) may enable the designer to better match the display format with the task, and thereby help to improve interpretation accuracy or other performance measure(s) of interest. This study contributed to the evolution of Lockheed-Georgia's executive information system by improving the understanding of the interactions between task and display characteristics.

The research approach presented is a useful construct for identifying and categorizing variables that should be considered in similar field studies. For example, this approach is broad enough to encompass: display characteristics, such as color, three-dimensional graphics, and voice output; measures of performance, such as decision quality; measures of cognitive style, such as Myers-Briggs; and task and experimental conditions, such as a game or simulation. Comparative studies of graphs versus tables are limited in scope because they do not explicitly consider a broad range of task-related effects and their interrelationships.

Although field experiments are somewhat difficult to organize and control, they offer significant benefits. The data collected represent the responses of practicing managers, minimizing the problems associated with transferring the results to the workplace. Field experiments also provide direction for the design and execution of follow-up studies. The favorable comfort ratings given by the subjects at the conclusion suggest that experiments which simulate a computerized information system can serve to train users and assist in finalizing system configurations. Further research is needed to apply and enhance our experimental approach and procedures in various organizational settings and task environments.
