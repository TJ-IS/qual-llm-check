## Introduction

DECISION SUPPORT SYSTEMS (DSS) BEGAN TO BE USED in organizations in the mid to late 1970s. These systems combine data, models, and an easy-to-use interface to help managers and professionals analyze poorly structured decision-making tasks. Dss have become increasingly prevalent in the 1980s with the emergence of more powerful PCs, inexpensive and easy-to-use software, enhanced database and data communications capabilities, organizational efforts to encourage and support their use, and a growing awareness of their potential value.

To some extent, DSS activity can flourish with little or no organizational support. For example, a financial analyst with an IBM PS/2 and Lotus 1-2-3 may be able to build and use a DSS with no external help. On the other hand, there are instances where assistance is needed. A user may require a software routine or at least help in extracting and downloading data from a corporate database. A manager may need to be trained in the use of an existing DSS. New DSS products may have to be evaluated for potential company-wide adoption. These are just some of the types of organizational support for DSS that might be required.

Sprague and Carlson [24] declared that an organization that “has decided to create a general dss capability using the staged approach” must first create a dss group. They continued: “A conscious, purposeful dss development effort will require a group of people to take the responsibility for managing it [24].” Similarly, staff support for dss headed Young’s [29] list of the “seven major components a dss strategy must deal with.” Hurst, Ness, Gambino, and Johnson [14] also stressed the importance of supporting dss: “Perhaps even more than standard data processing systems, the support of the organization is critical to the success of dss.” More recently, Epich [3] concluded that the two characteristics that separate “the successful cases from the failures” among “companies that have installed decision support systems in the past few years” are: “the degree of top-level commitment to the spread of this new technology” and “the quality of the support groups in information services (is) and user organizations”[3].

The two characteristics Epich associated with successful decision support systems are not entirely independent. An organization's arrangements for supporting dss activity reflect “the degree of top-level commitment to the spread of” dss to some extent [3]. However, there is no consensus about exactly how companies should support dss activity. In fact, relatively little is known about how organizations are supporting DSS activities. For this reason, the authors recently conducted a survey of 55 organizations that use DSS to answer the following questions:

\- Is Dss being formally supported?

\- Which organizational unit(s) has support responsibilities?

• What types and frequency of support are provided?

\- Which organizational units receive support, and how often is it provided?

\- What levels of management receive support, and how often is it provided?

• What is the perceived quality of the support given?

The purpose of this study was to investigate current practices of organizations in which DSS are built and used to determine whether industry practice coincides with the recommendations cited above and, if so, how companies have implemented these very general guidelines. Organizational support for DSS was examined only from the perspective of DSS groups. Members of DSS groups were also asked to rate the effectiveness of DSS support, as perceived by themselves, users, and management.

Our findings provide insights about organizational practice in the area of support for DSS. They also suggest that arrangements for supporting DSS have practical implications for organizations. This is important information for practitioners who are involved in making decisions about supporting DSS and for researchers who might want to explore this area further. Finally, educators can incorporate these findings into their discussions of current industry practices.

## Previous Research

KEEN POINTED OUT THAT DECISION SUPPORT, a radical concept in 1976, is now part of the mainstream [16]. Watson and Carr [28] concluded that DSS activity can be found in most large firms, and that one or more organizational units typically support DSS activities. Case studies of specific decision support systems abound [1, 9, 25, and others], and users of specific DSS as well as users of DSS generators have been surveyed [8, 10, 12, 17, 20, 23]. Hough and Duffy [13] studied the familiarity of top managers in South Africa with DSS concepts, as well as top managers' use of DSS. However, the extent to which American companies provide formal or informal DSS support and where DSS support groups are currently most likely to be located in organizations have not been investigated.

Sprague and Carlson [24] listed five possible sources of DSS support: (1) a special purpose team of applications systems analysts, (2) a reoriented tools group, (3) a management science or operations research group, (4) the planning department, and (5) a staff analysis group from one of the functional areas. They theorized that the best location for a DSS group would be within the information services division. However, if the information services division were merely a relabeled eDP department, then a highly placed executive staff group or the finance area would be more suitable locations. More recently, Watson and Carr [28] noted that DSS support could also be the responsibility of a formally chartered DSS group, the information center, or a DSS group within end-user services. They discussed the advantages and disadvantages of eight arrangements for Dss support. Hogue and Watson [12] found that a planning department or specialized staff analysis group were the most popular choices in 1980. Watson and Carr [28] observed that these arrangements for supporting Dss continue to predominate. Several researchers have noted the popularity of placing Dss support in an information center under the control of information systems (is) [7, 22, 26]. Both Harris and Thachenkary [7] and Keen [16] noted that Dss support is frequently subsumed under end-user computing (eUC). In their study of organizational support for end-user computing in fifteen large corporations, Harris and Thachenkary [7] found that the information center has become “one of the primary vehicles for supporting not only end-user computing but Dss.”

Sprague and Carlson [24] mentioned that a Dss group's size could range from two to three individuals to twelve to fifteen. These figures appear to be based on casual observations rather than systematic study. Harris and Thachenkary [7] profiled the formal training of end-user support personnel supporting Dss activity. A majority of end-user support personnel studied had formal training in MIS or computer science, but those with training in functional areas, MS/OR, or decision sciences were also represented.

Many researchers mention common functions of DSS groups $[3, 7, 15, 24, 28]$ . Only Harris and Thachenkary $[7]$ have investigated what services DSS groups provide, and no one has surveyed DSS groups to determine how frequently various services are requested and by whom.

Various researchers have investigated who builds DSS, who interacts with them, and who uses their output $[8, 10, 17, 23]$ . Top management involvement in the building of and interaction with DSS was reported to be infrequent. Sanders and Courtney $[23]$ found that financial or planning analysts and middle management used DSS most frequently. Hogue $[10]$ determined that middle managers generally built and tested the DSS, and that intermediaries and middle management worked with specific DSS most frequently. Similarly, Keen $[17]$ found that while top management was most likely to request an IFPS application, staff analysts most frequently built the DSS. Usually staff analysts operated the terminal, but middle managers also performed this task. Hill $[8]$ found that most DSS users were middle managers or lower-level managers. She reported that staff analysts served as DSS intermediaries most frequently, while lower-level managers did so less frequently.

Mittman and Moore [20] found that even among senior executives who used computers directly, median use of DSS was categorized as slight. Dss use for decision implementation predominated over DSS use for decision making. Most executive users of DSS studied by Mittman and Moore built their own relatively unsophisticated DSS and did not run complicated DSS built by others.

Hogue [10] examined user satisfaction with DSS and frequency of use of a DSS when use was voluntary. In his study, Hogue discussed Welsch's evaluation of DSS success as another measure of DSS effectiveness. Like Welsch, Sanders and Courtney [23] investigated DSS success and concluded that the level of top management support, user training, and length of DSS use were correlated with DSS success. However, all of these evaluations of DSS incorporated the viewpoint of the user. As

Watson, Boyd-Wilson, and Magal [27] pointed out, there are actually three perspectives involved in the evaluation of Dss groups: those of management, users, and Dss groups. Each group has unique and shared interests in how Dss is supported in an organization. In contrast to the studies cited above, this study investigated the effectiveness of Dss support as perceived by the Dss group.

## Study Method

IN ORDER TO OBTAIN DATA about how various organizations are supporting DSS activities, a mail questionnaire was prepared. (A copy of the questionnaire appears in the Appendix.) The authors wanted to conduct a large, cross-sectional study and felt that the issues to be addressed and the terminology involved were sufficiently well understood that practitioners would be able to answer the questions without difficulty.

None of the existing research or development frameworks was deemed appropriate for guiding the design of the questionnaire. Instead, general topic areas and questions were developed based on the literature dealing with organizing for DSS support. Most important to this effort were the studies and writings of Harris and Thachenkary [7], Sprague and Carlson [24], Watson, Boyd-Wilson, and Magal [27], and Watson and Carr [28].

The initial version of the questionnaire was pretested with five DSS managers and professionals who were known by the authors. These people had attended DSS–XX meetings and were thus judged to be representative of the population of DSS–XX attendees. The questionnaire was mailed to those participating in pretesting. Each recipient was asked to complete the questionnaire and to indicate whether any questions asked or issues addressed were unclear. The authors examined the responses to see if questions were answered as they expected and to see if any items were not obvious to this group of respondents. Changes made to the questionnaire as a result of the pretest were minor. For example, they included adding choices that pretest participants had listed under the “other” category. The revised questionnaire was mailed to 300 attendees of the DSS–85 and DSS–86 conferences who were not identifiable as academics.

Recipients of the revised questionnaire were asked to pass it on, if there was someone more appropriate in the organization to respond. Follow-up calls were made to a number of nonrespondents to improve the response rate and to check for the possibility of nonresponse bias.

Fifty-five usable questionnaires (18.3 percent) were received and are believed to be representative of the population surveyed. Questionnaires were considered usable if respondents indicated that DSS were used in their companies. (Several respondents other than the 55 mentioned above indicated that no DSS were being used in their companies. Since the population surveyed was biased toward users of DSS, the percentage of respondents using DSS was not considered to be indicative of the extent of DSS use in organizations. ## Study Findings

ONE WOULD EXPECT TO FIND HIGH LEVELS OF DSS activity and support for DSS in organizations that sent personnel to DSS-85 and DSS-86. Consequently, it is not surprising that 35 of the 55 responding organizations have recognized the need to support DSS activity formally somewhere within their firms (see Table 2). Even though 20 respondents indicated that formally chartered DSS support is not in place at their firms, 44 indicated that informal DSS support is provided, suggesting that DSS activity has matured to the point of necessitating some form of support. $^{1}$ Twenty-nine organizations provide both formal and informal DSS support. Only five organizations in which DSS are used offer neither formal nor informal DSS support. For this reason, the number of respondents to questions involving DSS support drops to 50 (or less, in the case of missing responses to specific questions) for questions about DSS support groups (Tables 6–9).

On the average, companies now offering formal support for DSS first offered DSS support about a year earlier than companies that are not now offering formal DSS support. Similarly, companies now offering both formal and informal support for DSS first offered DSS support about one and a half years earlier than companies that are not now offering both formal and informal DSS support. $^{2}$

Survey participants indicated that DSS support is present in a variety of locations within their firms (Table 3). A number of the companies have created formally chartered DSS departments. However, most DSS support is clearly in specialized staff departments such as corporate planning and staff analysis groups in a functional area. The approach of distributing Dss support among functional areas, which Harris and Thachenkary [7] advocated, seems to have been adopted by a large number of companies. The high incidence of Dss support in these areas, in conjunction with the placement of Dss groups in information centers and end-user services, suggests that Dss is primarily an end-user computing activity, as opposed to an MIS function. The relatively low percentage of companies listing MS/OR departments as providers of formal or informal Dss support may be attributable to the dismantling of MS/OR departments in many organizations [4] or to their reorganization and renaming, as described by Calderone [2].

## Unit Characteristics

While the concept of DSS emerged in academe in the early 1970s, few applications were being supported in industry prior to 1975 (see Table 7a). In the late 1970s, DSS support began to mushroom, and by the mid 1980s many others had come on board.

As might be expected, many DSS support groups tend to be small (see Table 7b). Surprisingly, however, more than a third indicated a staff of more than 20 employees. Undoubtedly, employees of large DSS support units provide DSS support as one of many tasks. The total job responsibility of a staff analysis department in a functional area, for example, extends beyond the performance of DSS services.

## Nature of Support Services Provided

To determine the types of services provided by DSS support units and the characteristics of the support service users, respondents were provided with descriptions of eleven possible support services and asked to indicate the frequency with which their units provided each service. Responses to this question are compiled in percentage form and presented in Table 8a.

The most frequently provided services include: providing hardware, software, and data; promoting the use of DSS; providing DSS consulting services; and offering technical support. Other services in frequent demand are: planning, organizing, staffing, directing, and controlling DSS activities; building DSS for end users; and operating the DSS for decision makers. Some of the services included in the survey were rarely performed. Most units never develop DSS for sale to other companies, and one-third evaluate DSS products only on an annual basis, if at all. Other services were clearly very significant: DSS consulting is performed with some level of frequency by 100 percent of the reporting units, and DSS applications training is performed by more than 90 percent of the respondents.

Table 8b indicates that staff/professional employees are the heaviest users of DSS support services, but Dss are being used by all levels of management and by other knowledge workers, as Sprague and Carlson [24] recommended. In earlier studies of Dss users, middle management and intermediaries were found to be most involved with Dss development and use, while top management and lower management involvement was negligible [8, 10, 17, 23]. Much of the use of Dss by lower management, middle management, and staff-level employees probably occurs as a result of requests by top management for a subordinate to serve as a “virtual Dss” for top management.

Marketing departments make use of Dss support group services almost as frequently. The high use of Dss support services by finance and marketing departments is not surprising, since most of the activities performed by these departments lend themselves to the type of support that Dss provides. In addition, many popular Dss generators were designed specifically for supporting financial decision making (e.g., IFPS, System W, Lotus 1–2–3) and marketing decision making (e.g., EXPRESS). What is surprising is the lack of Dss support used by engineering and production departments. Engineering departments request Dss support less frequently than any other area except human resources. Engineers clearly have the aptitude and training to make use of Dss. Several possible explanations for the low level of Dss support services requested by engineering departments come to mind. Software appropriate for engineering tasks may not exist. It is also possible that the use of Dss by engineering departments is transparent to the Dss groups because of the self-sufficiency of engineering users. Finally, it is possible that many of the organizations surveyed do not have engineering departments at the locations at which the responding DSS groups are based. After all, 76.4 percent of the companies surveyed had corporate headquarters at that location, and many organizations are in industries that do not necessarily employ engineers (see Table 1a).

## Perceived Effectiveness

The respondent from each Dss support unit surveyed was asked to rate the unit's effectiveness as perceived by users, management, and the Dss group itself. It seems that Dss support units are perceived as being effective (Table 9). Interestingly enough, support group personnel viewed themselves as being more effective than they perceived users and management to consider them. Clearly, respondents feel that management is least convinced that the Dss support provided is effective.

Measuring the effectiveness of DSS support from all three perspectives would involve surveying all three groups. A research design that also included surveys of users and management about DSS group effectiveness was rejected for two reasons. First, the primary purpose of this research was not to examine the effectiveness of DSS groups but to investigate how organizations are supporting DSS activity. Consequently, the effort required to identify both users of the services offered by the DSS groups surveyed as well as management personnel in charge of these groups was considered excessive. Secondly, of the three stakeholder groups, DSS group members are most familiar with the functioning of their units. Through their regular interaction with users of DSS support services, they receive direct feedback about the usefulness of the services provided. While it is possible that DSS support groups are positively biased with regard to the effectiveness of their work and how others perceive it, the continued existence of a DSS group and the continued use of DSS depend on the perception of users and managers that the DSS group and DSS are valuable to the organization. As a result, the continued existence of a DSS support unit may be the most objective measure of users' and management's perceptions of the group's effectiveness.

Further investigation of the effectiveness of Dss support from the perspective of the Dss group may suggest policies for managing and motivating members of a Dss group. Surveying members of Dss groups could also be useful in determining whether new Dss tools are needed to accommodate the distribution of Dss resources among various locations.

## Conclusions

NEW TECHNOLOGY IS ASSIMILATED IN STAGES. This is certainly true of DSS. In the early 1970s, DSS was in an initiation stage [6, 18], as the conceptual foundation was formulated and the first applications were developed. In the late 1970s, many organizations became aware of the potential for DSS, and vendors offered a growing number of DSS products. This growth or contagion stage continued into the early 1980s. Throughout the 1980s, DSS has moved through the control and even into the maturity stage in many organizations. $^{4}$

One aspect of the late stages of technology assimilation is the institution of formal structures and procedures for managing the technology. Providing support for the use of the technology is an important part of this evolution. This study explored how Dss is supported in a number of companies. An analysis of the survey data provides generalizations about Dss activity in these organizations:

\- Most organizations in which DSS are used provide formal support for DSS, informal support, or both.

\- If formal dss support is not provided, informal support is generally available.

\- Dss support is often found in many locations.

\- Specialized staff departments are the most common providers of DSS support.

\- Dss is primarily an end-user computing activity.

\- Dss support began in the late 1970s and has spread during the 1980s.

\- Dss support groups tend to be small. Their members are usually well educated and have considerable work experience.

\- Various DSS support services are offered. Those most frequently available are: providing hardware, software, and data; promoting the use of DSS; providing consulting services; and offering technical support.

\- Staff and other organizational professionals are the heaviest users of DSS support services.

• Finance and marketing departments most frequently receive DSS support.

\- Most DSS support groups consider the DSS support they provide to be either moderately or highly effective.

Dss support typically begins as an informal activity in the department(s) in which the first users of Dss are located. As Dss use expands to other departments, informal support may also be provided at those locations. Since informal support for Dss is usually not publicized, many potential users of Dss may not be aware of its availability. Personnel who provide informal support for Dss in their own units may also not be prepared to provide Dss support services to personnel in other departments. An organization that decides to support Dss activity formally may formally acknowledge the role of those who previously provided informal support, it may create and staff a separate unit to support Dss (and often other EUC activities as well), or it may do both. Considerations such as the computer sophistication of users, the level of Dss use, and the location of Dss users in the organization determine whether Dss is supported and what arrangements to deliver such support are instituted.

These findings and resulting generalizations are descriptive rather than prescriptive in nature. That is to say, they describe current practice rather than suggest what it should be. Nonetheless, for organizations that are in early stages of DSS evolution, the practices of other, more advanced firms should be of value in planning for the future. Even organizations with considerable DSS experience may find it valuable to compare the practices of other firms with their own in order to discover differences, and consequently, to provide a basis for rethinking their current practices.

## NOTES

2. Only 34 respondents provided the year in which DSS support was first offered in their companies. As a result of this reduced sample size and relatively high standard deviations, differences in the number of years companies have offered DSS support were not found to be significant. The few respondents from companies now offering only informal DSS support did not indicate when DSS support was first provided by their companies. Consequently, companies now offering only informal DSS support could not be compared with companies not now offering informal DSS support with respect to the number of years their companies have offered DSS support. (Informal DSS support is least likely to be documented and publicized. One respondent, whose company offers both formal and informal support located in staff analysis departments in two functional areas and a formally chartered DSS department, commented that there are “probably many others that I’m not aware of.”)

3. These findings do not corroborate the positive association between years of Dss generator use in a company and the number of functional area Dss support groups within major departments suggested by Harris and Thachenkary [7]. It is likely that other factors besides years of support for Dss (such as the company's degree of centralization and the nature of the company's business) affect the number of functional areas that provide Dss.

4. While Nolan has expanded the Gibson and Nolan four-stage model to include six stages [21], the expanded model appears to describe the process of assimilating transaction processing and MIS into an organization better than it describes the process of assimilating DSS technology.
