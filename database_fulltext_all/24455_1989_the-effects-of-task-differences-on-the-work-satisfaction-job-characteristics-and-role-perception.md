# The Effects of Task Differences on the Work Satisfaction, Job Characteristics, and Role Perceptions of Programmer/Analysts

DAVID K. GOLDSTEIN

DAVID K. GOLDSTEIN is Assistant Professor of Management Information Systems at Boston University's School of Management. He received his Ph.D. in Management Science from Sloan School of Management at MIT. He also received a Master of Engineering degree in Information Science from Harvard University and a B.S. from McGill University. He has extensive MIS teaching experience in MBA, doctoral, and executive education programs at both Harvard Business School and the School of Management at Boston University. He has published articles in Management Information Systems Quarterly on factors affecting the job satisfaction of information systems personnel and on qualitative research methods in MIS. His current research examines the impact of information technology on the effectiveness of managers, focusing on the marketing function.

ABSTRACT: A measure for classifying programmer/analysts based on the tasks they perform is proposed. A questionnaire, including the measure and measures of work satisfaction, job characteristics, role perceptions, and user interaction characteristics, was administered to over 250 subjects in a manufacturing firm. The results indicated that the task differences measure could be used to classify programmer/analysts into four groups—development/programmers, development/analysts, maintainers, and supporters. In addition, significant differences among the groups in skill variety, role ambiguity, and amount of user interaction were found. Implications of the results for those interested in redesigning the jobs of programmer/analysts are discussed.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: Information systems personnel, job satisfaction, systems development, maintenance, end-user computing.

## Introduction

WITH ORGANIZATIONS INCREASING THEIR USE OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS, the work carried out by programmer/analysts has become increasingly critical. As such, the factors that affect programmer/analysts' satisfaction with their work has become a rising concern among information systems managers. This concern is heightened by research indicating that job satisfaction is an important determinant of turnover among programmer/analysts [4]. Turnover is of special relevance for information systems managers, due to the shortage of experienced programmer/analysts and the high cost of training new hires. Indeed, Ives and Olson [13] and Rockart [21] have found that the difficulties of hiring and training programmer/analysts are among the most important problems faced by information systems managers.

At the same time, systems development groups in large organizations are undergoing major changes, taking on more diverse and specialized tasks. With the growth in the number of existing systems, the task of maintaining systems is occupying a large portion of programmer/analysts' time $[15]$ . In addition, the increase in end-user computing has caused information systems personnel to spend more time providing assistance to workers with limited technical knowledge $[19, 22]$ . One outcome of the specialization of programmer/analysts' work is that researchers and managers cannot treat these workers as if they were a homogeneous group. Many researchers, however, continue to do so $[3]$ .

There are two objectives to this study. The first is to determine if there are differences among programmer/analysts, based on the tasks they perform, in satisfaction and in variables that have been shown to be determinants of satisfaction in previous studies $[8, 9]$ . Toward that end, differences between maintainers and developers, between analysts and programmers, and between user support personnel and other programmer/analysts are examined. The variables identified as determinants of satisfaction include job characteristics $[11]$ and role perceptions $[14]$ . The second objective is to illustrate the use of a measure of task differences at one company.

Several benefits can accrue to both researchers and practitioners from examining differences among programmer/analysts who perform different tasks. If such differences exist, then the least satisfied group might be more apt to quit. For example, if a practitioner finds that workers who spend most of their time maintaining existing systems are less satisfied with their work, the manager might want to initiate a work redesign program to improve satisfaction in that group. In addition, if differences exist, researchers can carry out studies examining the effect of alternative organization structures or of changes in career paths on job satisfaction or job performance among different groups of programmer/analysts.

## Overview

THIS RESEARCH BUILDS ON TWO SETS OF STUDIES—those that measure task differences among programmer/analysts and those that examine work-related correlates of satisfaction among all programmer/analysts. Since most of the studies that present task differences measures build on the job satisfaction research, the latter body of research will be examined first.

## Job Satisfaction Studies

Programmer/analyst job satisfaction research has built upon studies of job satisfaction in the organizational behavior area (see Locke [16] for a review of this research). Specifically, researchers have used Hackman and Oldham's [11] Job Characteristics Model (JCM) along with Kahn, Wolfe, Quinn, Snoek, and Rosenthal's [14] role conflict and ambiguity constructs as the principal determinants of satisfaction among programmer/analysts.

Both models provide critical information for the study of factors influencing job satisfaction in programmer/analysts. The JCM captures aspects of the tasks performed that influence satisfaction. The performance of tasks such as analyzing a problem or revising a program are important parts of programmer/analysts' work. The role variables, in contrast, capture aspects of the relationship among workers. Working in a design team or with a user to determine changes to a program are also important parts of programmer/analysts' work.

The JCM specifies that five characteristics of the tasks carried out by programmer/analysts—skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, and job feedback—are positively related to several outcome variables, including job satisfaction. The model has been validated in studies with many different types of workers $[11]$ . The JCM, however, was designed to study work carried out independently. Hence, it does not take into account the effect of interactions among workers on job satisfaction.

The role conflict and ambiguity constructs permit the researcher to examine the effect of interactions among workers on their job satisfaction. Role conflict is defined as “the degree of incongruity or incompatibility in the expectations or requirements communicated to a focal person” [5, p. 92]. Role ambiguity is “the degree to which desired expectations are vague, ambiguous, or unclear, thereby making it difficult for the person to fulfill the requirements [of his or her role]” [5, p. 93]. In their study of various jobs, Kahn et al. found role conflict and role ambiguity to be high among people whose jobs required being innovative or involved boundary spanning—dealing with people outside the person’s work group. Organizational behavior researchers have found a negative relationship between role variables and job satisfaction in many different types of jobs and organizations [12, 17, 20, 23].

In research involving programmer/analysts, Couger and Zawacki [8] found statistically significant positive correlations between Hackman and Oldham's five job characteristics and programmer/analysts' job satisfaction $(.26 < r < .40)$ . Bostrom [5] found statistically significant negative correlations between job satisfaction and both role conflict $(r = .34)$ and role ambiguity $(r = .45)$ among programmer/analysts. Baroudi [1] also found significant negative relationships between role perceptions and job satisfaction. He found that, through the intervening variables of job satisfaction and organizational commitment, role perceptions were significantly negatively related to intention to quit. Goldstein and Rockart [10] examined the relative importance of job characteristics, conflict and ambiguity, and the quality of leadership provided by supervisors and peers as correlates of job satisfaction in programmer/analysts. This study found that all three sets of variables explained a significant amount of the variance in satisfaction (total $r^2 = .56$ ).

## Task Differences Studies

Several researchers have examined the effect of task differences on job satisfaction or on related variables. Couger and Zawacki [8] considered differences between analysts, programmers, and programmer/analysts using job title as a means of classifying subjects. They found programmers were more satisfied than analysts, even though analysts scored more highly on each of the five job characteristics. Baroudi [1] found positive correlations between the amount of analytic duties performed by a programmer/analyst and three variables—boundary spanning, role conflict, and organizational commitment. He found negative correlations between analytic duties and intention to quit.

In separate papers, these researchers also examined differences between maintainers and developers. Couger and Cotler [7] found a negative correlation between the percentage of time spent maintaining systems and both motivating potential score and role conflict. Baroudi and Ginzberg [3] found a negative correlation between percentage of time spent on maintenance and satisfaction. They found no significant correlation between maintenance percentage and either organizational commitment, role ambiguity, or role conflict.

There have been four weaknesses in the previous studies of task differences among programmer/analysts. First, the methods used to measure task differences may not have been not reliable. Couger and Zawacki $[8]$ used job title to classify programmer/analysts. Job titles, however, do not necessarily reflect accurately the work done by programmer/analysts. For example, it is unlikely that all programmers spend the majority of their time programming, that all systems analysts spend the majority of their time performing analysis tasks, or that programmer/analysts spend half their time programming and half performing systems analysis tasks. Other researchers used a single measure to determine the extent of maintenance as opposed to development activity, or programming as opposed to analysis activity $[1]$ . However, the reliability of a measure cannot be assessed with one question.

Second, interactions between programmer/analysts and users were not considered in these studies. Examining these interactions allows us to capture relevant aspects of the work carried out by programmer/analysts that should be correlated with job satisfaction. We might hypothesize, for example, that higher levels of feedback from users lead to higher levels of job satisfaction. In addition, the type of tasks performed by programmer/analysts should affect the quality and quantity of interactions between programmer/analysts and users. We might expect, for example, that subjects who spend most of their time doing analysis for new systems interact more with users than those who make programming changes to existing systems.

Third, the studies examined task differences along a single dimension. They either assess differences between analysts and programmers or between maintainers and developers. Hence, it cannot be determined whether interaction effects exist between the dimensions.

Fourth, the studies do not consider user support as a distinct function. Without a user support category, the members of the systems development group who spend most of their time providing assistance to non-information systems personnel cannot be accurately classified.

This study seeks to bridge these research gaps. Multiple measures are used to classify programmer/analysts into up to five job categories—development/analysts, development/programmers, maintenance/analysts, maintenance/programmers, and user support personnel. Variables that assess user interactions, as well as job characteristics and role perceptions, are included in the study.

## Variables to be Studied

THREE SETS OF VARIABLES ARE INCLUDED IN THIS STUDY. The first consists of one variable that divides programmer/analysts into the groups discussed above. This variable differs in two ways from task differences variables used in previous studies. First, it includes measures to both classify subjects as analysts or programmers and as maintainers or developers. Second, it includes measures to classify subjects as user support personnel.

The second set consists of one variable that measures the programmer/analysts' satisfaction with their work. Work satisfaction [25] was chosen because the variable can be adequately measured and it is an important outcome measure in a study of programmer/analysts. A work satisfaction variable was selected instead of the job satisfaction variable used in some other studies (e.g., Goldstein and Rockart [10]), because the work satisfaction variable measures only satisfaction with the work and, therefore, should be more highly related to differences in tasks than the more general job satisfaction variable. The job satisfaction variable also takes into account satisfaction with pay, promotions, and other job-related factors.

The third set consists of three groups of variables—job characteristics, role perceptions, and user interaction characteristics. Three of the four groups (all but the user interaction characteristics) have been shown in previous studies to be correlated with job satisfaction. The user interaction characteristics variables measure the quality and quantity of user–programmer/analyst interactions. They are defined below:

(a) user feedback—the degree to which the users of the system on which the programmer/analyst is working provide the programmer/analyst with direct and clear information about the effectiveness of his or her performance.

(b) user interaction—the degree to which the programmer/analyst's job requires him or her to work closely with users in carrying out the work activities.

These variables were modified from Hackman and Oldham's [11] “job feedback” and “dealing with others” variables. The user feedback variable measures the degree to which users tell the programmer/analysts how well or poorly they are doing their jobs. The user interaction variable is more general, capturing the quantity of interactions between programmer/analysts and users.

## Research Hypotheses

THE RESEARCH CARRIED OUT BY COUGER [7, 8] AND BAROUDI [1, 3] suggests that differences exist among programmer/analysts when pairs of groups are compared. This research expands on their results by examining overall differences among several groups simultaneously, adding user support personnel and examining both analyst/programmer differences and maintainer/developer differences. There are two hypotheses concerning overall differences among five groups of programmer/analysts—development/programmers, development/analysts, maintenance/programmers, maintenance/analysts, and user support personnel.

H1a: Significant differences in work satisfaction exist among the groups.

H1b: Significant differences in the variables identified as determinants of work satisfaction—job characteristics, role perceptions, and user interaction characteristics—exist among the groups.

This research also both broadens and retests their results by examining differences among pairs of groups of programmer/analysts. Toward that end, a hierarchy of groups was established. First, programmer/analysts were split into supporters and non-supporters, permitting a comparison of subjects who performed traditional systems development tasks to those who performed the relatively new task of providing user support. Next, the non-supporters were split into those who maintained existing systems and those who developed new systems. Finally, the developers and maintainers were split into programmers and analysts. This allowed examination of differences between analysts and programmers controlling for maintenance/development differences.

In contrasting supporters and other programmer/analysts, we predict:

H2a: Supporters have more autonomy than other programmer/analysts, because they have more discretion in determining how to carry out their work.

H2b&c: Supporters interact more and receive more feedback from users, because they work more closely with users.

H2d: Supporters view their work as less ambiguous. Working with users closely allows supporters to have a clearer sense of why they are performing certain tasks.

H2e: Supporters are more satisfied with their work, for the reasons stated in H2a–d.

In contrasting maintainers and developers, we predict:

H3a–f: Developers experience higher skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, job feedback, and less role conflict than maintainers [7].

H3g: Developers are more satisfied with their work [3].

In contrasting analysts and programmers, we might expect the following differences to exist:

H4a–f: Analysts experience higher skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, and job feedback [8], and more role conflict [1] than programmers.

H4g: Analysts are more satisfied with their work, for the reasons stated above.

## Subjects and Procedures

DATA WERE COLLECTED FROM 292 PROGRAMMER/ANALYSTS at three locations (one in the Midwest and two in Canada) of a large manufacturing company. Of the 292 subjects, 38 were excluded from the study; they were either contract programmers, users assigned to the MIS department, trainees, or cooperative students. The 254 programmer/analysts in the final sample were all fulltime, permanent employees of the company.

Participation in the study was voluntary. Subjects were contacted by the information systems management at the company. Approximately 80 percent of the programmer/analysts asked to participate in the study filled out the questionnaire. Most non-participants were either on vacation or off-site at the time the survey was administered. The questionnaire used to collect the data was administered in a classroom setting to groups of between 10 and 30 subjects at a time and took about 45 minutes to complete. Subjects filled out the questionnaire anonymously.

Subjects' job titles included junior, assistant, associate, and senior programmer/analyst; associate and senior programmer; junior and senior systems analyst; team and project leader; and associate support analyst. The subjects were organized into groups that were assigned to a specific systems development project or to maintaining a specific set of systems. In one of the Canadian locations, an information center was established to provide assistance to users in applications development. The demographic variables, which provide a profile of the programmer/analysts in the sample, are in Table 1.

## Measures

FOR VARIABLES OTHER THAN TASK DIFFERENCES and user interaction characteristics, standard measures found in the organizational behavior literature were employed. Hackman and Oldham's Job Diagnostic Survey (JDS) [11] provided measures of the five job characteristics. The “role conflict” and “ambiguity” scales were based on the eight- and six-item scales developed by Rizzo, House, and Lirtzman [20], as modified by Bostrom [5]. The “work satisfaction” scale is taken from the Job Descriptive Index developed by Smith, Kendall, and Hulin [25]. The “user feedback scale” consists of three items that are similar to the JDS’s “feedback from job” scale. The “user interaction” scale consists of three items that are similar to the “dealing with others” scale found in the JDS. These last two scales are presented in Table 2.

Six scales are used to classify programmer/analysts based on the tasks they perform. Three scales place subjects into either the maintenance, development, or support category (the MDS scales). Three others classify the developers into either the analysis or programming category (the AP scales). Supporters were not divided into analysts and programmers, because they do not perform either traditional analysis or programming tasks.

Maintainers were not divided into analysts and programmers, because very few subjects (14 of the 128 maintainers) were placed in the former category. In addition, those 14 spent, on average, more time on programming tasks than the development/analysts. Therefore, when testing H1 only four groups of programmer/analysts were considered—maintainers, development/programmers, development/analysts, and supporters. In addition, when testing H4 only differences between development/programmers and development/analysts were examined.

## Results

BEFORE CONSIDERING DIFFERENCES IN WORK SATISFACTION and the explanatory variables, demographic differences among the groups and summary statistics for the variables are presented. The only demographic differences among the four groups were that supporters tended to have worked at the company on average about 2.5 years longer than the other subjects and had about 2.5 years more information systems experience than the others. Table 5 presents summary statistics for the dependent variable (work satisfaction) and for the explanatory variables (job characteristics, role conflict and ambiguity, and user feedback and interaction). The statistics for the explanatory variables indicate that the sample is similar to the four companies studied by Goldstein and Rockart [10]. All but one of the internal consistency reliabilities for the explanatory and dependent variables are within the range deemed acceptable by Nunnally [18] (Cronbach's alpha .6). Further, all but one of the correlations between the explanatory variables and work satisfaction are significant ( $p < .05$ ) and in the appropriate direction. The exception in both cases is the user interaction variable.

Univariate and multivariate analyses of variance were used to assess the differences among the four groups of subjects (see Table 6). Differences among the groups in work satisfaction approached significance $p = .11$ , and hence H1a was not supported. Maintainers were the least satisfied group and the other three groups were approximately equally satisfied. A multivariate analysis of variance indicated that overall differences existed $(F_{9,234}=2.11, p<.01)$ among the four groups in the explanatory variables, supporting H1b. A second set of analyses were performed with the 14 subjects who were classified as maintenance/analysts excluded from the sample. The results obtained were similar to those presented here.

Univariate analyses of variance indicated that significant differences existed among the four groups in three independent variables—skill variety, role ambiguity, and user interaction. Skill variety was highest among development/programmers and lowest among maintainers. The difference between the groups was not significant $p < .05$ , however, when a Scheffe test was applied. Development/analysts perceived their work to be the most ambiguous and development/programmers the least. This difference was significant $p < .05$ when a Scheffe test was applied. Similarly, development/analysts interacted most with users and development/programmers least. The Scheffe test indicated that both development/analysts and supporters interacted with users significantly more than development/programmers.

In general, there were few differences between supporters and subjects who developed and maintained information systems (Table 7a). An analysis of variance revealed no significant difference in work satisfaction, and hence H2e was not supported. A multivariate analysis of variance revealed no overall significant differences in the independent variables, although this result approaches significance ( $F_{9,236} = 1.65$ , $p = .10$ ). For eight of the nine explanatory variables, supporters viewed their jobs more positively than non-supporters. The univariate ANOVAs showed that supporters had more autonomy (supporting H2a) and they had more interactions with and received more feedback from users than non-supporters (supporting H2b&c). There was no evidence that supporters perceived less role ambiguity (providing no support for H2d). They did, however, view their jobs as more significant.

Significant differences in work satisfaction were found when maintainers and developers were compared, supporting H3g (Table 7b). In addition, the multivariate analysis of variance indicated that overall significant differences in the explanatory variables existed between these two groups ( $F_{9,194} = 2.07$ , $p = .03$ ). In examining the individual explanatory variables, the data indicated that developers used a significantly wider variety of skills, but no other predicted differences were found (supporting H3a, but not H3b–f).

There were no significant differences in work satisfaction when analysts and programmers, who performed only development activities, were compared, providing no support for H4h (Table 7c). There were, however, overall significant differences in the explanatory variables ( $F_{9,66} = 2.19$ , $p = .03$ ). The univariate ANOVAs indicated that analysts received more job feedback (H4d), interacted more with users (H4e), and found their jobs less ambiguous than programmers (providing support for H4g). The other hypotheses (H4a–c,f) were not supported.

## Discussion

THE RESULTS PROVIDE ONLY PARTIAL CONFIRMATION of the hypothesized differences among pairs of groups of programmer/analysts. When contrasting supporters and traditional programmer/analysts, the former worked more autonomously and worked more closely with users and hence had a clearer understanding of their role. Those differences, however, did not lead to differences in satisfaction. Few differences were found between maintainers and developers or between analysts and programmers. While maintainers were significantly less satisfied (confirming [3]) and there were overall differences in the explanatory variables, the satisfaction difference was not the explanatory variables (skill variety, providing only partial confirmation of Couger and Cotler's [7] findings). Programmers were no less satisfied than analysts. While overall differences exist in the explanatory variables, when single variables are studied, significant differences are found in only job feedback (higher for analysts), user interaction, and role ambiguity (both lower for analysts). Differences in job characteristics were generally smaller than those found by Couger and Zawacki [8], which suggests that their results could be confounded by maintenance/development differences.

When contrasting the four groups, it is evident that significant but not large differences exist. On a close examination of the results, a pattern emerges. Maintainers are the least satisfied group and their lack of satisfaction can be traced to lower scores on job characteristics (they rank lowest on three variables and next to lowest on the other two), and to relatively less feedback from users and a less clear understanding of their role. Since most programmer/analysts are in contact with colleagues who are performing different tasks, and since many of them move from one task to another, even small differences could become apparent and could lead to negative consequences. For example, programmer/analysts who move from support to maintenance tasks might perceive that their jobs are less satisfying and be more likely to quit or to reduce their productivity.

The relatively lower levels of satisfaction among maintainers suggest a possible need to redesign these workers' jobs. One strategy for redesigning the maintainers' job is to make it more similar to the job of supporters. That is, the maintainers could work with the user to set priorities for the changes to be made, make the needed modifications to the system, and inform the user that they were made. This change in the maintainers' jobs could be accompanied by training in the user's business and in consulting skills.

The primary effect of this recommendation would be a change in the relationship between users and maintainers. Currently, user contact with maintainers is either through a written request for change or through the maintainers' supervisor. User contact with supporters, in contrast, is more direct. If a user has a problem with a system, the user would contact the supporter who is responsible for the system. The supporter would attempt to answer the user's question or to obtain the information that the user wants.

This proposed redesign would have several benefits for maintainers. First, the maintainers would have more autonomy, since they would work directly with users in planning and scheduling their work. Second, in becoming more familiar with the uses of the systems on which they are working, maintainers would understand more fully the significance of their work.

Unfortunately, the research results provide less information that could be used to improve the work satisfaction of supporters. They indicate that although there were no differences in work satisfaction between supporters and non-supporters, supporters perceived that their work had higher levels of task identity, task significance, autonomy, user feedback, user interaction, and lower levels of role conflict and role ambiguity than maintenance and development work. Other factors than those measured by this study could be negatively affecting the satisfaction of this group.

There were no differences in satisfaction when analysts and programmers were compared, contradicting the finding presented by Couger and Zawacki $[8]$ . One possible explanation for the difference in findings is that Couger and Zawacki combined both maintainers and developers in their sample. They did not consider the possibility that analysis/programming differences could be due to the fact that maintenance activities require a larger percentage of programming than development activities and that there are significant satisfaction differences between developers and maintainers.

There were, however, small but statistically significant differences between analysts and programmers in two of the explanatory variables. The analysts perceive that they interact more with users and that their jobs are less clear than programmers' jobs.

The work redesign for analysts could focus, for example, on reducing the amount of ambiguity perceived by these workers. Methods suggested by Baroudi [2], including clarifying job priorities and reporting relationships, could be employed toward that end. Role ambiguity could be further reduced by increasing both the analysts' training in the users' business area and the users' training in information systems. This would facilitate communication between users and analysts and, possibly, reduce the amount of ambiguity perceived by analysts.

Two notes of caution should be made concerning the above recommendations. First, the data on which these recommendations were made were collected at only one company. If the recommendations are to be applied to another company, it would be prudent to administer the questionnaire used in this study to the target company to ensure that similar results are obtained.

Second, even though the purpose of the recommendations is to improve the work satisfaction of programmer/analysts, they could have other significant consequences. For example, a change in the work performed by programmer/analysts would probably affect their job performance as well as their work satisfaction. Therefore, the possible effect of work redesign on job performance should be considered.

The results also provide insights for information systems managers interested in planning the career paths of programmer/analysts. Since maintainers appear to be less satisfied than other programmer/analysts, moving a programmer/analyst from an analysis, programming, or support task to a maintenance task could lead to disenchantment and possibly to a desire to leave the job. There is no evidence that managers need be concerned about moving programmer/analysts among the other four types of tasks.

## Areas for Future Research

The results of this study point up the need for a more detailed study of the support role. Several researchers have noted that increases in both end-user computing and in the use of personal computers will lead to an increase in the need for supporters $[22]$ . One extension to this study would involve interviewing supporters to determine the strengths and weaknesses of their work. A study could then be carried out and recommendations could be made for improving the work satisfaction of these individuals.

In addition, the results point up the need for qualitative research that addresses the relationship between organizational strategies and work satisfaction in programmer/analysts. Companies take many different approaches to organizing their information systems function. For example, some have separate development and maintenance staffs, while others organize their staffs with all the developers and maintainers for a given user group working together. Case studies of companies that are successful in managing their information systems development and maintenance staffs could provide important insights into these organizational issues.

The findings of this study have implications for both managers and researchers. The observations that programmer/analysts cannot be treated as a homogeneous group and that differences in tasks are important determinants of differences in work satisfaction should lead to further studies concerning the organization, tasks, and career paths of information systems professionals. In addition, the task differences in work satisfaction and in several of the explanatory variables between maintainers and other information systems workers provide insights for those interested in redesigning the work of programmer/analysts.
