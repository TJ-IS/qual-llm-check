# Relationship of the Rank of Information Systems Executive to the Organizational Role and Planning Dimensions of Information Systems

BHANU RAGHUNATHAN and T. S. RAGHUNATHAN

BHANU RAGHUNATHAN, Ph.D., is Assistant Professor of Accounting in the College of Business Administration at the University of Toledo. She received her doctorate in Business Administration from the University of Pittsburgh. Her current research interests include Information Systems planning and strategy, auditing and accounting information systems.

T. S. RAGHUNATHAN, Ph.D., is Assistant Professor of Information Systems and Operations Management in the College of Business Administration at the University of Toledo. He received his doctorate in Business Administration from the University of Pittsburgh. His current research interests include Information Systems planning and strategy, end-user computing, system design and analysis, and expert system building.

ABSTRACT: The organizational rank of the senior Information Systems (IS) officer has been suggested to influence the effectiveness of the organizational IS effort. As IS continues to gain increasing recognition as a key organizational system, a study of the various factors that contribute to its effectiveness should be particularly useful. This research explores the relationship between the reporting level of the senior IS executive and two specific aspects of IS, namely, IS organizational role and IS planning. The analysis is based on the responses of IS executives to a survey instrument. Results support the existence of significant linkages between the rank of the IS executive and the IS aspects addressed in the study. This suggests that the reporting level of the IS executive may be a key variable that management should focus on in efforts to increase the effectiveness of their information systems.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: Rank of IS executive, IS organizational role, IS planning.

THE ORGANIZATIONAL RANK OR REPORTING LEVEL of the senior Information Systems (IS) executive and its relevance to the success of the IS function is a subject that has been discussed in the literature for quite some time now [3, 6, 8, 15]. Despite widespread interest in the topic, there has been little empirical work addressing this issue.

Ein-Dor and Segev [10], while proposing a paradigm for MIS, identify “senior executives” as one of the seven elements of their model linking MIS success to a set of MIS variables. They suggest that the degree of association of these executives with

MIS and their rank, functions, and capabilities are variables that are relevant to the effectiveness of MIS, but note that the amount of research in this particular subject is “virtually nil.” They further point out that the level of involvement of these senior executives is dependent on their appreciation, understanding, motivation, and perceptions of IS. These are qualities that can be fostered by an IS manager who is able to have direct access to these senior executives. Such access would be easier, the higher the level of management to which the IS manager reports. The reporting level of the IS manager can, therefore, be a significant factor in influencing the effectiveness of the organizational IS.

Recently, there has been a revival of interest in the role and rank of the IS executive and an upward movement in the IS manager's position in the organizational hierarchy [6]. This upward movement in the rank of the IS functional head is attributed to the increasing recognition that information is a strategic resource and the newly emerging information technologies can be used to gain a competitive edge.

The present research is an attempt to explore further the effect of the rank of the top IS executive, in an empirical setting, and in the context of certain aspects of the organizational IS. The major question addressed here is: Is the organizational rank of the IS head a significant element in influencing the way in which IS tasks are undertaken in organizations? That is, are there any significant relationships between the management level of the IS head and the emphasis placed on different aspects of IS? This study is an attempt to identify the existence and strength of such relationships by focusing on two specific aspects of IS. The choice of the aspects addressed in this study is based on the two IS issues that have been and continue to be increasingly the subject of research and practitioner interest in the 1980s [5, 9, 13,], namely, (i) the organizational role of IS and (ii) IS planning. The former addresses the strategic role of IS in the organization, while the latter looks at an array of IS planning dimensions. Data for the study consist of the responses of 192 IS executives to a questionnaire survey of key IS issues. Respondent organizations were classified into three groups, based on whether their IS executives were one, two, or more ranks below the level of the CEO. The differences between these organizations were analyzed in the context of the two IS aspects referred to earlier. Results indicate that the rank of the IS executive has significant linkages to the organizational role of IS and to the emphasis placed on various dimensions of IS planning.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The next section presents a discussion of the suggested importance of the organizational rank of the IS functional head. This is followed by a discussion of the two IS aspects—organizational role of IS and IS planning dimensions—addressed in this research, and our expectations as to the nature of their relationship with the IS executive's rank. The research methodology used in this study to test these proposed relationships is then described. This is followed by a discussion of the results and conclusions.

They recognize that it has the potential to provide opportunities for exploiting information-based competitive advantage by altering the definition of industry structure, by redefining corporate strategy, and by reorienting the company's business strategy [1, 4, 20, 21, 25].

The organizational theory literature views the organization as a system of positions and their associated roles $[27]$ . The specific characteristics of an organizational position, such as job or reporting level, can be important in affecting decisions, attitudes, and responses of organizational members $[28]$ . The hierarchical organization structure confers formal authority and constitutes a base of power $[12]$ ; the higher the hierarchical level, the greater the authority and power to affect and influence organizational actions. In the current context of the increasing recognition of IS as a strategic resource, the leadership of the IS function in an organization and its importance in the organizational hierarchy could be viewed as a powerful, and perhaps critical, element in determining the organizational impact of the IS function. In other words, the rank of the head of IS in the organizational hierarchy could be an important factor affecting the success of IS and its use as a strategic, competitive weapon.

Such effective use of IS requires that IS managers make concerted efforts to inculcate “IS awareness” within the senior management team. This involves keeping them continually appraised of critical issues and trends affecting information management. Such awareness could help senior management appreciate and understand both the potential and problems associated with IS, and thus facilitate appropriate IS responses to organizational demands. The closer the IS manager is to top management in the organizational hierarchy, the more feasible it would be to maintain such IS awareness.

In a related context Ein-Dor and Segev [10] have proposed that the “likelihood of a successful MIS effort declines rapidly the lower the rank of the responsible executive, and is virtually negligible more than two levels below the chief officer of the particular organization the MIS serves.” They point out further that “as information systems become more sophisticated and more closely enmeshed in fundamental organizational structures, the need for top executive involvement will probably increase, rather than decrease. It would be of considerable interest and utility if more data were available both on current practice and on the relationships between ranks of officers involved and success, especially as these develop over time.” Other studies [3, 7, 15, 19] have also suggested that computer system efforts have a better chance of success when the head of the function is hierarchically close to the chief executive.

The current interest in the strategic role of IS and in IS planning makes the study of their relationship to the organizational rank of the IS executive particularly relevant. Research to date has not tested these relationships in an empirical setting. This focus on the IS executive's rank is also further justified by evidence of a growing awareness of the importance of the rank of the IS manager, particularly among practitioners. This is very different from the situation that existed less than a decade ago when, as Synnott and Gruber [30] point out, “surprisingly few information managers make a strong effort to get time with top management . . . perhaps because few information managers, as yet, report directly to the top." The relationships tested in this study are associative and we are not imputing any causality. For example, while we propose that there is a direct positive relation between the rank of the IS manager and the strategic nature of the organizational IS, we do not attribute any cause-effect relationships. This is because, as IS evolves in an organization, one can influence and also be influenced by the other. The higher ranking IS manager may, because of her/his position, be able to get more attention to focus on certain aspects of IS. On the other hand, the recognition of the growing importance of IS may prompt top management to raise the reporting level of the IS manager to ensure that appropriate attention is paid to key IS issues. This study tests for the strength of these relationships.

## Organizational Role of IS

WHILE THE CURRENT INTEREST AMONG PRACTITIONERS and researchers alike has tended to highlight the increasing strategic role of IS, there may be some organizations where, quite legitimately, IS may not play a strategic role. For IS executives in these organizations, notwithstanding anecdotal stories in the popular press, the upward movement of IS executives to the higher management echelons may not generally happen. Cash, McFarlan, McKenney, and Vitale [7] have summed up this situation very succinctly when they point out that “for some organizations IT activities represent an area of great strategic importance; for other organizations they play (and appropriately will continue to play) a cost-effective, useful, but distinctly supportive role. It is inappropriate for organizations with supportive IT to expect that the same amount of senior management strategic thinking should be devoted to the IT organization as in organizations of the former type.” They also point out that where IS is considered strategic, IS management’s reporting level “should be very high” and where IS is regarded as supportive and low-strategic, the reporting level “can be low.”

Thus a link between the IS head's rank and IS's strategic role can be proposed. Companies with higher ranking IS heads can be expected to have more strategic orientations to their IS; conversely, companies with lower ranking IS heads can be expected to have a less strategic orientation to their IS. In terms of the classifications of companies in the sample into three groups, this would mean that there are likely to be more high-strategic companies in group 1 than in group 2, and relatively more such companies in group 2 than in group 3.

## Is Planning Dimensions

IS PLANNING HAS BEEN CONSIDERED BY BOTH IS practitioners and researchers as one of the more important IS management issues addressed in the 1980s [2, 5, 9, 13, 17]. This increased attention given to the planning aspects of IS by both practitioners and researchers has helped to highlight a number of variables that affect planning. Pyburn [22] has, for example, identified factors such as style of senior management decision making, the volatility of the business, the complexity of the IS organization and management task, and styles in approaching IS planning, as affecting IS planning success.

We now describe a set of factors that can be presumed to represent different aspects of IS planning in the organization; they are referred in this study as the planning dimensions. It is expected that, in general, there will be significant differences among the companies in the three groups along each of these dimensions. The choice of the IS planning dimensions taken up for consideration is based on an attempt to capture some broad measures of planning importance and acceptance. The literature in the strategic planning area has been extensively used in the choice of the variables and in the formulation of questions to capture the underlying constructs. Kanter [15] provides a framework in this context by focusing on what he refers to as some “underlying principles” of planning. They are (i) IS must be consistent with the corporate business plan, (ii) management involvement and commitment are essential, (iii) planning is everybody’s job, and (iv) it takes several cycles to institutionalize a planning process. This conceptualization closely parallels the choice of the IS planning dimensions used in this study. The planning dimensions chosen for investigation, and our expectations as to their links with the IS head’s rank in the organization, are set out below.

In general it can be assumed that the greater the importance of an activity or function in an organization, the higher would be the rank of the executive entrusted with managing that function. Extending this assumption to the IS function, the expected relationships between the IS head's rank and each of the planning dimensions listed above can be hypothesized using the link provided by the organizational importance of IS.

Overall acceptance of IS planning: Literature in both the IS and organizational theory areas have emphasized the need to reduce organizational resistance in order to facilitate the effectiveness of any organizational activity. Schultz and Slevin [29] discuss, for example, organizational validity in the context of implementation of OR/MS models. Organizational acceptance can be expected to be influenced by the organizational importance of the activity under consideration. Based on the assumed link between rank and importance of the IS function, it is proposed that the higher the rank of the IS executive, the greater the degree of acceptance of IS planning in that organization. In terms of our three-group classification of respondent organizations, group 1 companies can be expected to have a higher score for the acceptance of IS planning than group 3 companies, with the group 2 companies falling in between. ## Research Methodology

## Data Collection

The data used in this research were obtained by using the mail survey technique. A self-administered questionnaire instrument was mailed to 800 information systems managers. These were chosen at random from a mailing list of 3,000 is executives. A total of 202 responses were received, 192 of which were usable and comprised the sample of companies for this study. Table 1 provides a classification of the sample by industry type. Table 2 provides information on company revenues. Companies with revenues in the ranges above 50 million are well represented in the sample. The results of this study may therefore be more appropriately relevant to companies in these size categories.

## Operationalization of Variables

Organizational rank of the IS manager: The empirical test in this research involves an analysis of the relationship between the IS manager's organizational rank and the two IS aspects identified earlier. The rank variable was operationalized by asking the respondents (to the questionnaire survey) to indicate the number of levels below the CEO that the head of the IS function was located. These were ranked one (one rank below the CEO), two (two ranks below the CEO), etc., so that rank one would, for example, refer to a higher organizational level than rank two. We are not, however, controlling for the different “flatness” of management ranks across organizations, that is, these are not normalized ranks. Table 3 presents information on the number of companies falling into each of the rank categories. For analytical purposes these companies were then classified into three groups. Companies in rank 0 and rank 1 were grouped together as group 1 companies, companies in rank 2 as group 2 companies, and companies in rank 3 and below as group 3 companies. Table 4 presents information on the classification of respondents into these three groups based on company revenue. This table suggests that the most common hierarchical level of the IS executive appears to be two ranks below the CEO, irrespective of the size of the company.

Organizational role of IS: Operationally, the companies in the sample were located along a high–low strategic continuum by adapting the Cash et al. [7] classification scheme for classifying IS as high or low strategic. They suggest that for some organizations, IS activities represent an area of great strategic importance, while for other organizations they play—and quite appropriately may continue to play—a cost-effective and useful role but one that is distinctly supportive in nature. Further, while the IS function may not currently have strategic importance, the thrust of its applications portfolio may be such as to have great significance for the future. The opposite could be true where IS plays a strategic role in the company’s current operations, but future applications do not seem to offer the same payoff or significance.

This research aims to test empirically the relationship between the organizational rank of the IS head and each of two sets of variables, the organizational role of IS and IS planning dimensions. The variables whose relationship to rank were tested are:

## Results

THE RESULTS CAN BE ANALYZED FROM TWO PERSPECTIVES. One, the existence of significant group differences with respect to each of the IS strategic role and planning dimensions and two, the direction of these differences, based on a comparison of the mean values for each group.

Viewing these results from the first perspective, the F values indicate support for our expectations regarding differences between the groups for both strategic role variables and for all but one of the IS planning dimensions. That is, these results suggest that the importance given to the position of the IS functional head is closely linked to various aspects of the organizational IS. Specifically, these results suggest that there are significant relationships between the rank of the IS manager and two aspects of IS, namely, the strategic orientation of the IS and the importance given to planning. In the absence of any imputations of causation, these results can be interpreted to indicate that the IS head's role significantly influences and is influenced by the emphasis given to various aspects of the organizational IS.

For the strategic impact variables, the mean values for groups 2 and 3 were so close that it appeared likely there might not be a significant difference between these two groups. To explore this further, t tests of group differences were conducted between groups 1 and 2, between groups 2 and 3, and between groups 1 and 3, for all the variables. Results (presented in Tables 7, 8, and 9, respectively) showed that while there were significant differences, generally, between groups 1 and 2 as well as between groups 1 and 3, there were no statistically significant differences between groups 2 and 3 with respect to any of the variables under study. This empirical result suggests the very interesting interpretation that, while literature has generally addressed the importance of the rank of the IS manager, the really crucial impact appears to be when the IS head is directly reporting to the CEO. Below that level there appears to be no significant impact, whether the IS head is in rank 2 or rank 3 or below. This result allows for a further refinement of the distinction proposed by Ein-Dor and Segev. They suggested that the lower the rank of the IS executive, any significant rank effects decline rapidly, and that they would be “virtually negligible” more than two ranks below the CEO. The results of this study suggest that such usefulness may decline significantly even when the IS head is more than one rank below the CEO. These results point to some interesting implications for the organization and its executives, which are discussed in the concluding section.

## Conclusion

THIS STUDY ANALYZED THE NATURE OF THE IMPORTANCE of the rank of the IS manager in terms of its relationship to certain IS variables. The results suggest that there are indeed differences in the way certain aspects of IS are emphasized among organizations whose IS managers are at different levels of the organizational hierarchy, and allow us to conclude that these aspects of IS would benefit by having IS managers at higher reporting levels.

These results have interesting implications from the perspective of (i) the organization as a whole, (ii) the IS function, (iii) the IS executive's job, and (iv) the senior management group.

Viewed from an organization's perspective, the implications of these findings should be of particular value to organizations planning to use their IS in more strategic ways. A number of considerations such as technology, competitive environment, and resources have been suggested to be important to an organization as it moves to make its IS more strategic. The results of this study point to the importance of an additional factor, i.e., to the importance of looking at the reporting level of the IS manager, and making necessary changes in the reporting hierarchy to ensure that the proper fit exists between the organizational importance of IS and the organizational rank of the chief information systems officer. This recognition of the importance of the role of the IS executive by the business world is already becoming apparent. Business Week [6] reports, for example, that “there’s a new breed of manager surfacing in the executive suite. As countless chief executives come to grasp the importance of information to their business, they’re seeking people . . . and assigning them a high-stake mission: figuring out how to fashion a confusing array of often-incompatible computer and communications equipment into a cudgel that can clobber the competition. Some members of this new information elite sit behind such recognizable nameplates as senior vice-president, vice-president for information services, or information resources manager. Others are beginning to get a higher-sounding title to reflect their new status: chief information officer, or CIO.”

From the perspective of the IS function or department, having its executive reporting at the highest levels can have a significant impact on its organizational credibility. It has been said that the CEO and senior management are generally thought to be less informed about the IS function than about any other major function in the business $[15]$ . Quite often this can lead to what Cash et al. $[7]$ refer to as a “mismatch,” i. e., a situation where IS and senior management perceive the role of IS differently. In the particular instance referred to by Cash et al. $[7]$ , it required an outside review of the organization's overall strategy to convince senior management that they had misunderstood the role of IS and should treat it as strategic. Such mismatches in perception can potentially be avoided if IS has a powerful spokesperson with direct access to the highest levels of management.

The results of this study also have implications for the nature of the IS executive's job. Rockart [26] presents a profile of the successful IS executive based on interviews with IS executives who were considered as highly successful both by company and industry standards. The profile is of an individual who reported to a key senior executive, viewed him/herself as a manager rather than a technician, saw a constantly evolving structure for the role, and focused more on effectiveness than on efficiency considerations. These IS executives interviewed for Rockart's study also listed the ability to maintain communication links with top management as a critical success factor. The results of the present study suggest that such communication can perhaps best be achieved by having IS executives report at the highest management levels. Rockart's description of the successful IS executive also fits the description of an executive at the hierarchical level at which the present study suggests that the IS executive should report. This study thus finds support for some of the implications following from Rockart's analysis. IS executives who aspire to the higher hierarchical positions should move away from a purely technical orientation and focus on long-term IS strategy and planning within the broader context of their company's overall business mission.

Finally, the proposed importance of the IS executive's reporting level has implications for senior management as well. Kanter [15] suggests that the dramatic changes taking place in the ways in which IS is being used in today's business world both excite and worry the senior management of business enterprises. They excite because of the new ways in which IS is creating the potential to establish strategic product and market uniqueness. At the same time, senior managers worry lest their lack of knowledge of the new technology gives others opportunities for inroads into their markets. They ask themselves questions such as: Is the technology running away with us? How does our IS function compare to our competition? Do we have a comprehensive long-range direction and strategy? Are we treating information as a resource? Answers to these questions may decide the direction the organization's IS will take and, in turn, influence the effectiveness of the IS function. The results of this study suggest that an IS executive working close to senior management can help senior management gain the understanding necessary to answer these and similar questions. A direct line of communication between senior management and the IS executive could substantially enhance senior management's ability to utilize fully the potential of its IS as a strategic resource.

As a final remark it should be noted that the results of this study may not allow us to generalize our conclusions with respect to IS aspects other than those specifically addressed in this study. However, prior literature has addressed, in theory, the importance of the rank of the IS head for the likelihood of a successful MIS effort. It would therefore be a reasonable extension of our earlier conclusion to suggest that, if future research were to address other aspects of the organizational IS, these findings of the positive impact of the IS head's rank will continue to be supported. Future research can also extend the current research findings by addressing the impact of other variables in the organizational environment such as the organization's size and industry type, the functional area the IS executive reports to, and the stage of IS growth.
