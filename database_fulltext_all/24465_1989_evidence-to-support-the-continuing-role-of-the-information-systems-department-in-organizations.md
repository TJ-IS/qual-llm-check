# Evidence to Support the Continuing Role of the Information Systems Department in Organizations

MARIUS A. JANSON

MARIUS A. JANSON is Associate Professor in the Department of Management Sciences and Information Systems at the University of Missouri–St. Louis. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Minnesota. His research interests focus on methodologies for systems analysis and design, managerial issues of end-user computing, and the application of exploratory statistical techniques to problems of information systems research. His articles have appeared in JMIS, Information and Management, MIS Quarterly, and Omega: The International Journal of Management.

ABSTRACT: Advances in computing technology, end-user computing, and commercial software have greatly increased managers' discretionary use of information resources. This development has profoundly affected the status of the information systems (IS) department. Some academics and practitioners have even predicted its demise. This article refutes this prediction by showing that it is based on inappropriate reductive reasoning. Analysis of three diverse case experiences demonstrates that the IS department continues to contribute essential services to the success of information systems operating in environments where great user autonomy exists.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: Management of MIS, strategy formulation, competitive information systems, end-user computing, information systems case studies.

## Introduction

END-USER COMPUTING ENCOMPASSES THE DEVELOPMENT, implementation, maintenance, and use of information systems by individuals outside the information systems (IS) department. Its emergence and rapid growth during the 1980s has attracted the attention of both business professionals and academics. Benjamin's survey [2] found that end-user computing continues to gain in importance, and he predicted the processing capacity dedicated to end-user computing will increase from 40 percent in 1980 to 75 percent in 1990.

End-user computing, however, exposes organizations to serious risks $[1, 6]$ . Davis $[6]$ contends that the reluctance to test, document, and validate user-developed systems and the tendency to omit audit trails and operating controls are important risks of end-user computing. The growth of end-user computing and its attendant dangers have also attracted management's attention. A 1987 survey $[4]$ of top MIS management personnel ranked the role of IS and end-user computing as fourth and sixth out of 26 “key management issues for the 1980s.” Although organizations have responded to end-user computing in different ways, many managers agree on the need for some form of centralized planning and control. In many cases these are provided by the information center, usually controlled by and as a part of the IS department [3].

Some practitioners and academics, however, propose to manage end-user computing differently. The literature reports instances where the users insist on a singular control over their data processing operations $[14]$ , avoid even the implied control by the corporate IS department $[15]$ , or have supplanted the IS department altogether $[18]$ . No one advocates this approach to managing end-user computing more forcefully than Dearden $[8]$ , who predicts the demise of the centralized IS department in all but the largest business organizations. Dearden envisions an environment characterized by complete user control over individual information systems, software selection, and data management. Even though Dearden does not specifically use the term “end-user computing,” his comments are appropriate in this context, because in the absence of the IS department all computing by definition turns into end-user computing.

Despite the outlandish way in which Dearden presents his recommendations, these recommendations are clearly meant to be taken literally. Dearden's 1972 [7] and 1987 [8] articles share the same satirical tone and advocate similar radical views toward the IS department. His forceful writing style has a great deal of allure because the user-manager is given clear and unambiguous directives for managing a very complex environment. Dearden commands respect, and his recommendations warrant close study.

## The Issue: Who Should Control Information Systems?

THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN THE IS DEPARTMENT AND THE END USERS is an important managerial concern. It continues to be a subject of discussion that often results in contradictory advice $[10, 12, 13]$ . Whereas most see a need for some form of IS control of end-user computing, others do not $[3, 5, 6, 8]$ . I will contribute to this discussion by relating several cases involving very different information systems. The first case, the most complex in the series, describes a highly competitive business environment requiring close communication between separate organizations. The second case, a computerized automobile dispatching system, concerns the implementation of off-the-shelf software. The third case describes off-the-shelf information system software for energy conservation in public buildings that failed despite its modest complexity. Drawing on these case experiences, I will demonstrate that real-life situations give rise to complex problems not answered in terms of absolutes, but call for more adaptive approaches to resolving problems. To fix ideas, I shall first describe Dearden's $[8]$ views, which reflect his role as a prominent advocate of total user autonomy over the information resource.

Dearden's point of departure is that the locus of authority and responsibility should be the user-manager. Only then can the user act most effectively in the face of an increasingly competitive business environment without being hampered by an IS department with goals that almost invariably differ from those of the user. From this perspective, the user is in the best position to prioritize applications development and hardware acquisition, and to accept blame in cases when things go awry.

A complete decentralization of control, elusive in the past, can now be achieved by placing the user-manager at the locus of authority. This is possible because of recent software developments. Among the most important of these are the availability of fourth-generation languages and commercially designed software. Such software is claimed to be lower in cost and superior in quality to applications developed in house. Lower cost results from spreading development expenses over multiple users. Improvements in software quality arise because developers specialize in particular types of business applications software.

Dearden [8] cautions that placing the user at the locus of control will be resisted by IS departments on the grounds that it will result in (1) software duplication, (2) hardware and software incompatibility, and (3) a lack of systems integration, which in turn inhibits on-line data sharing. As Dearden sees it, however, these concerns lack substance because if standard software is produced by specialty companies, then duplication is by definition impossible. Second, hardware incompatibilities are presently already being resolved by hardware manufacturers. Third, Dearden claims that data problems should be rare because 80 percent of corporate information is not needed at more than one location, and the 20 percent of the data needed at multiple locations need not be shared on-line. Finally, data inconsistencies between different applications can be resolved by the users involved without the intervention of an IS department. In short, the situation Dearden depicts is one of many users with information systems that largely operate independently of one another with only occasional interactions occurring in the most circumspect ways.

## Case 1: An Interorganizational System

CERTAIN LINES OF INSURANCE SUCH AS PERSONAL PROPERTY, health, and travel, involve large numbers of customers who each take out relatively small dollar amounts of coverage. Processing insurance claims requires a great deal of information, usually supplied in written form by the insured party, which is then manually entered into the company's database. Often information on a single claim comes from multiple sources (e.g., a car accident may involve claims for auto repair, hospitalization, and personal liability). Filing claims is frequently complicated because many insurance accounts originate at independent insurance agents. Much of this information is transferred by public mail, which is slow and is becoming increasingly more expensive. Reducing the paperwork flow between the insurance company and its customers and independent agents is an important cost-cutting strategy for the insurance firm: it simplifies the reporting tasks for the independent agents and customers, and it provides the customer with a speedier claims settlement. Because of these advantages, linking independent agents to the insurance company's information system support functions will induce independent insurance agents to favor that company when signing up new customers for insurance. This obviously benefits the company because it obtains a strategic advantage over its competitors.

With this in mind the Finnish insurance firm SAMPO started a pilot project to link its mainframe-based information system with the PC-based information systems of car dealers and trucking firms. The car dealers provided insurance to new car buyers, and the trucking firms purchased insurance for their trucking fleets. The software required in these interorganizational information systems was designed by the insurance firm with the cooperation of, but at no cost to, the participating organizations $[19]$ .

This business operates in a highly competitive environment and its information system is considered to be essential to achieving its corporate goals. Management would be averse to buying off-the-shelf software if this software would negate the company's advantage over its competitors. Furthermore, the application requires the exchange of large quantities of data between the insurance company's information system and the information systems of its independent agents. Linking the many different information systems requires a centralized information systems group and cannot be left in the hands of individual users. In short, making the user the locus of control and responsibility does not work in this instance.

## Case 2: An Information System for Computer-assisted Dispatching

A POLICE CAR DISPATCHING SEQUENCE TYPICALLY BEGINS with a call from a citizen for police assistance. The call is answered by a complaint evaluator who selects one of four courses of action: (1) no need exists for a police response (e.g., the caller requests the local town hall's address), (2) no need exists for a police response but a police report is required (e.g., the caller reports his car stolen), (3) a police response and a police report are required (e.g., a crime is in progress), and (4) a police response but no police report are required (e.g., police involvement is limited to directing traffic in an emergency situation). Only in the last two cases is a police car required, and the complaint evaluator places the request for assistance in a queue, where it awaits processing by the next available dispatcher.

Computer-assisted car dispatching (CAD) systems have been operating successfully for nearly 20 years [11]. Thus, when the St. Louis Police Department required such a system, it was natural to look for standard off-the-shelf software that could operate on the department's mainframe. However, even though police car dispatching procedures follow a similar pattern, police departments differ in how they perform this day-to-day task. A second complicating factor is that police information systems, rather than being isolated entities, communicate and exchange data with regional and national information systems. With these issues in mind, the department's database administrator, accompanied by several members of the police force, visited four cities with CAD systems similar in specifications to those needed in St. Louis. They selected a system in use in Kansas City because it used dispatching procedures in close agreement with those followed by St. Louis dispatching personnel. The only obstacles to implementing an exact duplicate of the Kansas City system in St. Louis were the dissimilar database systems of the two applications. The database administrator planned to bridge these differences by a partial rewrite of the software, which was not thought to affect the success of system implementation.

To ensure a smooth implementation, police personnel initially operated the modified system at approximately 20 percent of its intended capacity alongside the existing manual dispatching system. The entire cycle of dispatching a car requires information displayed on four different screens. In the modified system the dispatcher had to look at two screens at a time; thus the completion of a dispatching task required alternating between screens. The time needed to alternate between screens was only a few seconds, but the St. Louis dispatchers considered this time to be objectionably long because it had the potential of placing police officers in undue jeopardy during crisis situations. Displaying all four screens simultaneously on one video display terminal, however, required significant changes in hardware and software.

This case illustrates the problems that may interfere with successful off-the-shelf software implementation at one location, even though it is operating successfully at other locations. First, no two computing environments are exactly alike. In the case of the CAD system, the database environments differed between St. Louis and Kansas City. Second, users' system operating requirements may differ. In contrast to Kansas City, St. Louis dispatchers insisted on a system that enabled car dispatching without screen changes.

The system modifications required to bridge these differences and, thus, to bring about successful system implementation, may exceed the technical expertise of most end users. In the CAD system, the database administrator possessed a unique combination of firsthand experience with police work and with information system and database design. First, his experience with police work helped him understand that even small delays in car dispatching can place police officers in jeopardy. Second, his technical expertise enabled him to make the system modifications required to eliminate the dispatching delays. Successful implementation of the car-dispatching system and favorable experiences with its day-to-day operation stimulated requests for additional information systems. These new applications, in combination with a growing need for software maintenance, necessitated hiring additional personnel.

## Case 3: An Information System for Energy Conservation

ADEQUATE INFORMATION ABOUT ENERGY CONSUMPTION BY END-USE TYPE is crucial in any effective energy conservation program. Dickson and Janson [9] relate the failure of an energy information system installed by a midwestern state to support the energy conservation effort for its publicly owned buildings. The energy agency purchased standard off-the-shelf software that had already been used extensively in other states to target buildings for energy consumption measures. The software was part of a system consisting of data collection forms, system operating manuals, and instruction manuals for staff members at individual buildings to help them complete the self-administered data collection forms. The agency collected data on approximately 300 variables for several thousand buildings and prepared reports indicating whether energy conservation measures were desirable. Subsequent analysis of these results by agency personnel revealed that the recommendations contained in the reports were erroneous and the system was abandoned.

This application seems a fitting candidate for commercial software because (1) demand for similar information existed at other state energy agencies, thus making the availability of standard off-the-shelf software likely, and (2) no data exchange with other systems was required. Then what were the causes of its failure? First, the energy agency was unaware that its objectives differed from those of the software house. The software house wanted a database for its own future use that contained as much information as possible on the energy consumption of publicly owned buildings. Thus, the amount of data collected was far in excess of what was needed for the energy agency's limited goal of targeting buildings for energy conservation measures. The data collection and data entry processes became unnecessarily complex and were sources of many data errors. The resulting data of poor quality demonstrate that end-user computing without adequate attention to data integrity can place an organization at considerable risk [1].

Second, the software contained an engineering model for identifying buildings that consumed excessive amounts of energy compared to buildings with similar structural and operational characteristics. Because the model was incorrectly calibrated for climatic conditions and the building characteristics were unlike those found in the midwestern state, grossly inaccurate predictions resulted. The problems with the model and the data were uncovered after an in-depth study that required expertise on energy conservation, information technology, and statistical techniques for improving data quality.

This case illustrates that implementing standard off-the-shelf software can be disastrous. It demonstrates that differences between the objectives of system developers and users affect commercially acquired software and in-house developed software to the same degree. It is evident, even in the case of seemingly routine applications, that the user can benefit from the advice provided by a centrally located organizational entity such as the traditional IS department or the information center.

## Case Analysis and Discussion

THE THREE CASES REPRESENT A RANGE OF TYPICAL ENVIRONMENTS that differ in terms of organizational, system, and software characteristics (Table 1). For example, organizational size ranges from large to small, system requirements from well known to uncertain, and software acquisition from in-house developed to purchased commercial software. The first case illustrates the complexities of an interorganizational system arising from the need to (1) coordinate the actions of the various independent organizational entities, (2) transfer data over a communication link, and (3) store the data for future use to gain a sustainable competitive edge. The system's complexity and its application as a competitive weapon preclude placing the user at the locus of control; rather, they dictate locating control at a higher organizational level. The competitive purpose of the system also precludes purchasing off-the-shelf software because in that case the system might also be used by competitors, thus negating its competitive advantage.

The automobile dispatching system was not intended to gain a competitive edge, and off-the-shelf software was available. However, extensive software and hardware modifications were required to implement the system successfully. Software changes were planned at the start of the project. Unforeseen changes in hardware (i.e., the need for different video display terminals) arose in response to unanticipated user requirements that surfaced during implementation. The data gathered during day-to-day operations would eventually be used in other applications and thus had to be accessible on-line. The unique mix of skills of the database administrator was pivotal in bringing about successful implementation. He had both expertise in database and information system design and experience with police work. The importance of this skill combination has also been stressed by Rouse and Hartog [16, 17]. Thus, this person quickly gained the confidence of the users and actively marketed the supporting role that information systems can play in day-to-day police work. This, in turn, led to user requests for additional information systems. However, the most striking observation is that the expertise needed to locate and evaluate commercially available software to effect the necessary modifications and to ensure successful system implementation precluded placing the user in the locus of control and required the involvement of the IS staff.

The energy conservation system appears to be the most likely candidate for complete user control because the system is relatively simple, off-the-shelf software was available, and data, so it seemed at first, did not need to be shared with others. However, unknown to the users, the software house wanted access to the data and it designed the system accordingly. This is a case where the system eventually failed because the needs of the user and those of the provider of the software differed.

These cases demonstrate that for systems of strategic importance to an organization, off-the-shelf software is nonexistent. Second, commercially available software may, and often does, require extensive hardware and software modifications. Third, in all but the simplest systems, data are shared in ways difficult to specify in advance, but can have a profound impact on the success of the system. These cases suggest a wider set of applications that are similar because they do not operate in isolation. Thus, vesting the end user with sole control over data, design, and implementation decisions is not a viable alternative. In summary, whereas Dearden [8] claims that the IS department is superfluous, particularly in small organizations, these cases suggest a continuing need for an IS function irrespective of organizational size.

## Implications for Information Systems Management

TABLE 2 SHOWS HOW ACTUAL AND POTENTIAL IS CONTRIBUTIONS to successful system implementation differ from case to case. With respect to the interorganizational information system, the IS department's primary function was providing technological leadership, designing the information system, linking the geographically distributed systems by a telecommunications network, and creating and maintaining the information system's database. The organizational and technological complexity of the task placed the IS department at the locus of control.

A different relationship existed in the case of the automobile dispatching system, where IS staff members and users shared control equally. The IS department's primary role was selecting, testing, modifying, and implementing a commercially available application software package, and designing the application's database. The users participated as equals in specifying system requirements, system implementation, and operating procedures.

Finally, in the case of the energy conservation system, all control was in the hands of the users who took charge of system selection and implementation. Had the IS department been involved, it could have assisted the end user in selecting and testing the commercial software package and the database. The IS department's contributions were essential to the success of the interorganizational and automobile dispatching systems; its involvement with the energy information system would have reduced the probability of failure.

The observed shifting of control away from the IS department and toward the users is indicative of the growing trend in the United States and Europe toward greater user autonomy. It occurs in response to a realization that information technology should be supportive of knowledge work $[5, 20]$ . The function of information technology in this context is to add “value” to the work done by people and it can do this by granting the user of this technology the greatest possible discretion in its application. However, as the cases presented here demonstrate, designing and implementing these information systems successfully requires continued involvement by the IS department. The process appears deadlocked, because the possibility of greater user autonomy is thwarted by a concomitant rise in IS involvement and control. This deadlock can be broken by focusing on two closely connected issues that, nonetheless, can be addressed separately: the development of a technology and its day-to-day application in a specific environment. With respect to end-user computing, the deadlock can be resolved by assigning the IS department the task of developing the technology and granting the user of this technology the greatest possible discretion in its application. In short, what needs to be managed is not the user but the information technology that makes it all possible.

## Conclusions

THESE THREE CASES ILLUSTRATE INFORMATION SYSTEMS THAT DIFFER GREATLY in their organizational impact and complexity. One case illustrates a need for gaining a sustainable competitive advantage that could not be met by using commercially available software. The second case describes an application where standard commercial software was appropriate but only in a modified form. The third case relates the failure of an information system implemented with commercial software acquired and installed by the end user. The contributions of the IS department were essential to the successful implementation of the first two systems. In the third case the IS department's involvement could have greatly increased the probability of success. These cases indicate the complexities brought about by the availability of commercially available software and end-user computing. These developments have resulted in the discretionary use of information resources and greater levels of user autonomy, which have profoundly altered the role of the traditional IS department. Yet, consequently, to argue that the IS department is superfluous is a dangerous and reductive line of reasoning based on an oversimplification of a very complex situation. A more effective approach would be to organize the IS department around the task of providing the user with a technological infrastructure that ensures the greatest possible user discretion in the deployment of information resources.
