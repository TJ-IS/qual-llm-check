# Building Large, Dynamic Hypertexts: How Do We Link Intelligently?

ERRAN CARMEL, WILLIAM K. McHENRY, and YESHAYAHU COHEN

ERRAN CARMEL is a Ph.D. student at the University of Arizona Department of Management Information Systems. His research interests include the management of “messy” data, hypertext, negotiation support systems and software engineering. Prior to joining the Ph.D. program he spent several years in industry—in design and development of large systems, and later in software marketing.

WILLIAM K. MCHENRY is an Assistant Professor in the School of Business Administration, Georgetown University. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Arizona MIS Department in 1985. His research interests include hypertext systems, group decision support systems, and the economic, political, and social issues concerned with the globalization of computing. He has recently served on and co-authored a report of the National Academy of Sciences Committee to Study International Developments in Computer Science and Technology. Other publications include an article on the absorption of management information systems in the USSR which appeared in the Communications of the ACM.

YESHAYAHU COHEN is a visiting scholar at the University of Arizona MIS Department. His interests include systems analysis and design. He has over ten years of industry experience in information systems.

ABSTRACT: Hypertext systems will have difficulty growing into large hyperbases because of the problems of link creation. This paper introduces the concept of a Large Dynamic Hyperbase (LDH). Information Retrieval indexing methods, indexing assistants, quality evaluation methods, and organizational methods are examined as means to help ameliorate the linking problems. Numerous examples from AAIS (a large-scale Text Based Management System) are used to inform the discussion.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: Hypertext, hyperbase, information retrieval systems, links in hypertexts, indexing of hypertexts.

## 1. Introduction

CONSIDERABLE EXCITEMENT HAS BEEN GENERATED by the hypertext paradigm. Hypertext—a term coined by Ted Nelson [22], surveyed in [4, 20]—is the concept of structuring text in a non-linear format. Hypertext systems are proliferating rapidly. Some claim that hypertext will capture a vast wealth of knowledge in many domains by the 1990s; public and multi-organizational hypertext systems will become widespread, like the current boom in public databases. $^{1}$ Hypertext is now moving from its pioneer beginnings $^{2}$ to more complex applications. We can now speak of hyperbases; hypermedia systems storing large amounts of data and having characteristics that are similar to Text Based Management Systems (TBMS). Applications are appearing today in research and analysis environments: Fortune 500 business intelligence, military intelligence, and in academic research institutions. NASA, for example, is currently investigating the feasibility of a hypermedia system for the Space Station Program, which is anticipated to capture data at the rate of two gigabytes per day.

Figure 1. Hypertext Screens with Buttons Representing Links

These hyperbases are the focus of this paper. We refer to them as Large Dynamic Hyperbases (LDH). These large systems are receiving information from many sources on a continuous, dynamic basis. An LDH, in fact, moves us one step closer to Ted Nelson's vision of world literary systems. Contrast this type of environment with the common hypertext systems we find today: Apple's Hypercard applications tend to be small, intended for a single user. More advanced hyperbases, such as a hypertext encyclopedia [16] or hypertext dictionary [26] are built in parallel by several users and involve complex links, but have static releases, such as CD-ROM. On the other hand, LDHs are in constant use, even while being updated. Typically, hyperbases of this kind deal with “messy” data (a term introduced by McHenry et al. in [19])—small pieces of information (reports, articles, vendor summaries, statistics, technical data) that are of little interest by themselves, but are significant when combined with other pieces of data.

With so much excitement about hypertext in the air, many users may start to build hyperbases without realizing what will happen as the amount of information they store grows larger. Their small hypertext systems are so successful that they will want to put large amounts of information into the systems, expanding them in multi-user settings. However, popular packages offer little assistance in managing all this information. Just at the point when sophisticated cross-analyses become interesting, the hyperbase may sag under its own weight until maintaining it becomes daunting for a typical user. Many researchers have been aware of the problems of “scaling-up” the hypertext systems for use with large amounts of information, but have been hindered in their research by the difficulty of acquiring large-scale hyperbases on which to experiment.

One of the central problems of building LDHS is making the links between nodes. In most systems each screen window (node) displays one node that has a set of links set up between it and other nodes. Links permit the user to move from node to node according to the set of associations that the links form. The end points of the links can often be seen on the screen in a graphic form, such as icons or buttons (see Figure 1). “Browsers” make movement between nodes possible, and can also display menus, tables of contents, and hypertext topology maps that show the overall structure of the text without too much detail. Browsing is an unstructured exploratory activity performed by the user that is especially appropriate for ill-defined problems and exploring new task domains. Browsing is one of the chief reasons for all the excitement over hypertext, because it seems to offer the novice user a painless way of finding interesting items, without learning a query language.

This paper will explore the problem of inserting data into the hyperbase. How do we link intelligently? What mechanisms can we use to allow many users to update, in parallel, a growing hyperbase and not create chaos? The first section introduces the Arizona Analyst Information System (AAIS), a complex text based management system (TBMS) that provides considerable insights into the linking problems. The next two sections use the AAIS experience to explore the linking problems themselves and some methods that can be used to ameliorate them.

## 2. The AAIS: A Vantage Point to Large Dynamic Hyperbases

THE ARIZONA ANALYST INFORMATION SYSTEM offers a unique vantage point to examine LDHs in the organizational context, although, as we shall explain later, it only partially meets the definition of hypertext. The AAIS is a mature TBMS that has been in active use by a large number of users over the course of seven years. Furthermore, it is a large system in which, like hypertext systems, every textual node has been carefully selected and indexed.

The AAIS was developed at the University of Arizona MIS department $[18, 19]$ and has been used since 1982 by a large group of researchers to study international computer and technology trends. The main database in the AAIS family is International Computer and Technology Trends (ICTT). $^{3}$ Five other databases have also been started. Currently ICTT has more than 32,000 screen-size nodes of textual information. It has grown fairly steadily at 200–500 nodes per month since 1982.

Like Notecards [12], the AAIS was initially designed to facilitate research projects by storing “chunk” size pieces of text that may range from abstracts and small excerpts to pieces of the entire text of a document. It includes tools to support all stages of the research life cycle: from data selection and entry, through analysis and query, to production of papers, including bibliography generation. The AAIS is built on top of a relational database management system, therefore providing a rich environment for query formulation.

There is a natural relationship between hypertext and Information Retrieval (IR) systems $^{4}$ making it appropriate to use the results from the latter to examine the former. All relationships between nodes in both IR and hypertext systems can be termed links. IR links (e.g., indexes) are implicit while hypertext links are explicit. Using another terminology—IR links are “soft” and hypertext links are “hard” (i.e., the link button is physically placed on the screen).

Given this view of links, AAIS shares much in common with hypertext (see Table 1). Each AAIS chunk of text has a variety of indexes attached to it. Some of the indexes are semantically differentiated by using predefined categories. AAIS predefined categories include people, places, and organizations. Indexes that do not fall into these categories are generic keywords. A screen-oriented program permits the user to browse through retrieved sets of nodes; but without doing some sort of query, even if it is as simple as “retrieve everything,” no browsing can take place.

AAIS does not fit the classical definition of hypertext because there are no “hard” links between nodes. However, AAIS has a hierarchical structure that uses the file folder metaphor. This structure is similar to many hypertext browsers. A file folder contains a set of textual nodes that share a “meta-topic,” or any specific topic of interest. File folders can be arranged hierarchically within a pre-assigned subject tree (see Figure 2). Textual nodes are assigned to any folders that reflect their content. Although the branches of this tree are rigid, the nodes themselves can be assigned or de-assigned to file folders easily. The hierarchy is shared by all users with some users taking responsibility for a section of the hierarchy that they have created. The tree changes infrequently; however, each user who may enter data can make changes as he or she sees fit.

## 3. The Hypertext Linking Problem

CONSIDER THE PARAGRAPH IN THE HYPERTEXT WINDOW OF FIGURE 1. Once this text appears as a node, it must be linked intelligently to other nodes or to the index structure. The indexer $^{5}$ requires a great deal of knowledge to link this node correctly. Will the indexer translate Fed into Federal Reserve as a keyword? Should the indexer create a link from the word Tokyo to a street map of Tokyo? Probably not—but should it create a link to a node about monetary policy? Will the linker catch the logical link to the Nikkei index? The 1 percent fall in the Tokyo market is actually a 1 percent fall in the Nikkei index. Is it important? Perhaps it is important to one user of the system, and not to another. Should hyperbase vendors therefore include all possible links of interest? $^{4}$

One of the issues this example demonstrates is that the link logic is influenced strongly by the domain of the system. If the system focused on global economic news, it would have different links than if it focused on U.S. political developments. Similarly, two users may be examining related problems and wish to make use of subsets of each other's nodes. Will they properly link their own nodes so that they can be found by other researchers?

As hyperbases grow in size, five link problems manifest themselves:

1. Incomplete Links. Indexers overlook important connections and cannot foresee what they will need to be in the future. Raskin [25] also argues that people are inherently lazy and will not bother making links, especially when they are hot on the trail of some information.

2. “Too Complete” Links. Links are created at too fine or too general a level of granularity, or there are too many links assigned to a single node.

3. Inconsistent Links. Indexers link nodes differently.

4. Ambiguous Links. No one remembers why a link was made.

5. Obsolete Links. As the system grows and changes, certain links are no longer meaningful or useful. Perhaps the link is obsolete because of historical changes in word usage (does anyone still use the term “stand-alone” computer?).

Creating links is a difficult intellectual task: it falls somewhere between an art and a science. When the hypertext system grows, maintaining an effective system for creating links, whether automatic or manual, is a difficult challenge. If the system is manual or partially manual, it places a severe burden on the human indexers, which, if too great, could cause them to abandon using the system. As in any structure that relies on human input, there may be large variations in indexing quality. As the size of the hyperbase grows, link quality may degrade.

## 3.1. The Linking Problem and the AAIS Experience

The AAIS experience has provided insights into all of the five linkage problems mentioned above. First, consider why there are incomplete links when the hyperbase is large. How will a user go about identifying the nodes to which a new node should be linked? Placing the node into a hierarchy may not be too difficult, but will this yield the kinds of associative links that distinguish hypertext? Once the system is large, there could be hundreds if not thousands of candidates to evaluate for links. In the ICTT database, an extremely common concept is “SOFTWARE”; 3,632 chunks of text currently share “SOFTWARE” as an index value. Clearly it would be impossible to compare a new chunk with 3,632 others to see if any links were appropriate. Just comparing it to 363 candidates is out of the question; even 36 may be excessive. There is a large risk of incomplete links.

On the other hand, there may be too many links associated with each node (the “too complete” problem). Experience with ICTT shows that the average number of indexes for each node in the database is about 12. $^{7}$ From an information retrieval point of view, more indexes increase the chances that the node will be found; if these are also sufficiently specific, the chances that only the “right” set will be retrieved also go up. In contrast, more links in hyperbases may ironically decrease the chances of finding something. $^{8}$

Links may be inconsistent or ambiguous. Indexing a chunk of text in AAIS is similar to the act of linking a new chunk into the hyperbase, but there is considerable variation in indexing quality. Some index values are much too specific, others too general, and others suffer from morphological variations (for example IBM 4341 versus IBM-4341). Of 200,000 general keywords in ICTT, the percentage of terms used only once is 17 percent. $^{9}$ Similar findings are observed in the ISTH database (ISTH is another database in the AAIS family that concerns issues of International Science, Technology, and Health; see also Figure 3). While 17 percent may seem at first to be excessive, it is difficult and prohibitively expensive for most user groups to come up with an entire thesaurus in advance, in order to ensure that a controlled vocabulary of indexing terms is used. Indeed, one of the advantages of LDH systems is the ability to discover the subject area as the hyperbase grows. The problem of inconsistency may grow as new generations of indexers introduce their own vocabularies [29]. Finally, the problem of obsolete links shows up in AAIS particularly because of its technological subject matter.

Our collective experience with a mature system sheds light on indexing behavior as it differs among indexers. Indexers perform similar tasks to users of hypertext systems who are linking data. Table 2 summarizes AAIS indexer data for all indexers who have indexed at least 100 nodes. $^{10}$ The most striking observation is that for textual nodes that are roughly constant in size, the range of average indexes per node is large—from 5.85 to 19.9—so that the most prolific indexer generally found about three times as many indexes as the least prolific. The AAIS environment includes both junior and senior members; junior members generally focus solely on indexing, while senior members are both researchers/end users and indexers.

Why is there such a wide range in indexing behavior in the AAIS? Few statistically significant relationships were found in the data, and they only explained a small amount of the variation. Neither “months worked” nor “total nodes entered,” which would presumably capture experience and perhaps knowledge of subject domain, were good predictors of indexing behavior. Another measure, which attempted to characterize the type of information being entered, also failed as a predictor.

Intuitively, it would seem likely that the wide variety of content in the nodes is the single most important factor that determines indexing behavior. Other factors worth investigating may be the form in which the information arrives, and who selected the material. In the case of ICTT, some of the information is translated by others, some is translated and entered directly by users, some is abstracted or summarized, and some comes in the form of abstracts. Summarized information may require more indexes per character of text than if the full text of the document is being indexed.

Considerable differences in indexing were found in the extent to which index values were chosen by more than one person [19]. There is nevertheless some evidence that individual indexing styles are responsible for some of the variations noted in the table. For ICTT, the group of indexers falling within one standard deviation above the mean (in Table 2) consists primarily of junior people who were hired to enter information for others; on the other hand, the group within one standard deviation below the mean consists mainly of those who were adding data themselves and using it in their own research, or indexers who were working closely with these people. The former group would be more likely to take a shotgun approach, hoping somehow to catch the right indexes, whereas the latter group has a better feel for what really needs to be indexed. Alternatively, the former group may be indexing with everyone's needs in mind while the latter group may be indexing primarily for themselves. For ISTH, where the average number of indexes per node is 19 (significantly higher than in ICTT), all of the indexers so far have been junior people entering data for others.

We have also found that where structures are clearly set up in advance—as with predefined category keywords—indexer variation is relatively small. AAIS has three such predefined category structures: countries, persons, and organizations. It should be emphasized that this does not represent a controlled vocabulary, but simply a design that allows the indexers to pick out easily those key terms and place them in their respective structures.

The AAIS experience sheds light on many problems that we see in structuring and linking LDHs. Creating an index is easier than creating a hypertext link, because in the latter case it is also necessary to find the correct node to which to link. In the next section we offer a framework for attempting to solve some of the LDH linking problems.

## 4. Possible Solutions for the Linking Problem

WE SUGGEST FOUR WAYS IN WHICH THE LINKING PROBLEM IN LDH environments can be addressed: indexes should be used to supplement hypertext links and indexing assistants can be built to aid the indexing process; domain restrictions need to be applied; quality measures may be developed; and organization-level solutions can be instituted.

## 4.1. Supplementing Hypertext with Indexes

The idea of using indexes (so-called “soft links”) to supplement hypertext links is not new. For instance, the File Retrieval and Editing System (FRESS) [32] at Brown University was in use for more than a decade and permitted keywords to be attached to links. $^{11}$ Designers of several well-known modern hypertext systems are either considering, or currently adding, indexing capabilities to their systems. As the systems approach the dimensions of an LDH system, the limitation of links for retrieval are becoming apparent. Halasz [12] concludes that “navigational access by itself is not sufficient” in Notecards. Notecards and other well-known systems—KMS [1], Intermedia, Hyperties, and Hypercard—have full-text search features. Although the larger hypertext systems (primarily in research) have implemented keyword capabilities, most of the commercial systems have been slow to add indexing capabilities; it is likely that most of their customers do not (yet) need them.

There are two main reasons to supplement hypertext links with indexes. First is the huge amount of labor needed to link each new hypertext node (recall the SOFTWARE example from the previous section). This act requires more work than the act of indexing, because pairs of nodes must be evaluated for relevance to each other. $^{12}$ In a basic hypertext system, which has no indexing or retrieval mechanism other than the links themselves, there is no other way to be sure that all relevant links have been made. Indexing allows the user to avoid looking at the rest of the nodes, because the addition of an index term automatically adds an implicit link to all other nodes with that term. Secondly, indexing permits an important distinction to be built right into the hyperbase. Indexes are derived based on objective similarities in content between nodes. If indexes are present, hypertext links can be used for only those areas of the hyperbase where a rigid structure is mandatory and only for associations that would not occur on the basis of content. $^{13}$

Because linking is so laborious, there is a good chance that too few links or inconsistent links will be created. If it can be carried out successfully, indexing should ameliorate both of these problems. If the set of rules used by the indexer is known, then ambiguous links could also be avoided. Before considering how to index, we discuss how indexes and links can complement each other in a hyperbase.

## 4.1.1. Combining Indexes and Links for Retrieval

Two general schemes are suggested here: index-to-link and link-to-index. It should be clear to the reader by now that a user cannot begin a search in an LDH from a hypertext hard link. One needs to narrow the search considerably before the browsing that hypertext provides becomes feasible and economical. Thus in the index-to-link scheme, the beginning of the search must begin with standard query mechanisms (i.e., full-text search, Boolean combinations of indexes, or a sequential menu). Next, the user is presented with a set nodes, which (as is often the case) is too large to sort through. This is the problem described by Frisse [10]; the hypertext medical handbook solution is to store term frequencies of all documents, and, depending on the query, a hypertext browser is created dynamically, given the frequencies and their weights. As a result, each user has a personal set of links created for every search. This is both the appeal and drawback of such a solution; dynamic tree creation in large hyperbases may be prohibitively expensive.

The link-to-index approach uses the file folder hierarchy (as described for the AAIS in section 2). Here, a hypertext link points not to an individual node but to a file folder, or a packet of nodes that share some content. Now that the user has arrived at the folder by traversing a link, he is presented with a menu of nodes in the folder, or uses standard search mechanisms to narrow the search (a conceptually similar approach using compositions is presented in [11]). In addition to reducing the cognitive load on search, link-to-index combinations improve the act of indexing because the file folder hierarchy is known, is relatively small, and is fairly rigid. This mitigates the “incomplete links” problem. In addition, partitioning the sets of text chunks reduces the number of possible links the user might follow, hence addressing the problem of “too many links.”

## 4.1.2. Automatic Indexing

Information scientists have been studying the indexing question for over twenty-five years, and much research has focused on the relative merits of automated and manual indexing (e.g., [3]). The motivation for automated indexing is intuitively clear; consistency is maintained, and the cost of labor is (presumably) reduced. Three dominant approaches to indexing automation are:

1. Full-text search, which can be efficient [7], but provides inappropriate recall and precision. $^{14}$

2. Statistical techniques, which are in fairly wide use [29], but may not work well with the small amount of specific material in a hypertext node (and certainly will not work for hypermedia objects such as pictures).

3. Artificial intelligence techniques, which show promise but have not yet reached commercial viability (e.g., [15, 27]).

Even if statistical techniques are deemed applicable for hypertext nodes, there is still a long-standing debate regarding the relative effectiveness of automatic versus manual indexing. Blair and Maron [3] state that when high recall is desired, manual indexing is preferable to full-text searching. Salton [28] favors using the statistical methods, asserting that using just five “‘blueprint rules’” is often superior to conventional intellectual indexing systems. He does point out, however, that certain domain-specific knowledge is needed to handle high-occurrence keywords on an automated basis. Croft and Ruggles [5] state that for automatic indexing, the “‘simplest techniques are the most effective’” and suggest focusing on a standard suffix removal technique (e.g., the techniques used by Frisse [10]). McHenry et al. [19] have argued for the importance of manual, “‘intellectual’” indexing, because no reasonable amount of automation can fully capture the inferences drawn from the text itself.

## 4.1.3. A Hybrid Solution: The Indexing Assistant

Experience suggests that a hybrid of manual and automated methods may provide the best current solution for creating indexes. An interactive semi-automatic indexing procedure can be designed by which the computer suggests possible index terms. For instance, inverse term frequency may be used to highlight promising terms. The computer suggests indexing terms and links, but the final decisions are made by human indexers. AAIS currently has a facility that identifies any terms in incoming text that have already been used as index terms. A hybrid system includes lists of synonymous terms. For example, AAIS allows the country names U.S.A., U.S., United States, and America to be treated synonymously.

The Indexing Aid System (IAS), described by Humphrey [14], is an indexing assistant based on a frame-based representation of a controlled vocabulary for indexing medical literature. Each frame has a slot for the name, several values for the entity links, and can automatically inherit, retrieve, suggest values, and confirm values that have been suggested by the indexer. During data entry, the indexer may request a display of allowable values for any slot. Unlike AAIS, IAS has a tightly controlled indexing vocabulary. $^{15}$

Creating a linking assistant, as opposed to an indexing assistant, is a more formidable task. In our view, only artificial intelligence techniques will be able to provide the tools necessary to find potential links that are not found when content alone is evaluated. It could be possible to develop “content profiles” of the various hypertext regions and compare them to the new node. This could help to reduce the number of nodes the user would have to scan while linking the new node. However, there is a risk that the very kinds of associative links that make hypertext so exciting will not be made if these conventional content-based mechanisms predominate.

If manual indexing is chosen as a part of the hypertext system, designers must be careful not to overburden the indexer, especially when the indexer is likely to be the end user as well. It is a great temptation to design a system using named or multiple types of links in order to support the user with the most “advanced” searching and retrieval means. $^{16}$ But if first the user has to find all possible links and then classify them, he or she may simply not use the system.

## 4.2. The Need for a Domain-Specific System

Most of the well-known hypertext systems are generic shells, meaning that they are designed to handle any application—from travel guides to literature on forensic anthropology. Domain specificity is anathema to the original, somewhat utopian, vision of hypertext, which envisioned traversing from one potentially unrelated topic to another just as the human mind can jump from one thought to another. However, domain-specific (or application-specific) design seems inevitable in LDH environments, and is already the modus operandi in the many educational and informational systems that have proliferated in the last two years. $^{17}$

We have found that limiting the scope of the hypertext system can improve indexing. In a limited domain, human indexers are more likely to understand nuances in the data (recall the Nikkei stock index example). As Salton [28] pointed out: domain-specific information may be needed to improve automatic indexing techniques. The AAIS has an indexing assistant, which uses a domain-specific heuristic algorithm to control the entry of new organizations (organization names are often misspelled, abbreviated, or corrupted when entered from translations). Reimer and Hahn [27] constructed a hypertext system called TOPIC that uses AI techniques to parse, condense, and link textual nodes automatically. The semantic net for this system is a rich domain-specific knowledge base (in the case of TOPIC, the domain of knowledge is computers). It is hard to imagine an automated indexer that uses a controlled vocabulary not delimited by domain. $^{18}$

In summary, domain specificity can address some of the linking problems. It permits the creation of tools that help to eliminate inconsistent indexing. By permitting the definition of a few types of nodes, it may help to solve the problem of “too complete links” by dividing them appropriately. It may be possible to define norms for certain domains to serve as a guideline for how many links are needed.

## 4.3. Evaluating Link Quality: Audits and Measurement

The concept of linking quality needs to be embodied in LDH systems, although a good measure for linkage quality is difficult to come by. On one hand, the more links a node has, the more likely it is that someone will find it. However, some links may be superfluous and lead to information overload, and, as pointed out previously, link structures are subject to various deficiencies.

A category of measurements can be derived from the structure of the hypertext itself. Using formalisms from graph theory, an LDH can find unlinked nodes, circular links, and substructures that are all but cut off from other parts of the hypertext. Topological anomalies are produced and are manually inspected and fixed if necessary. If the LDH also has indexes (soft links) associated with each node, the number and type of indexes per node can be evaluated, and cases with very few or very many links may be examined.

In traditional information retrieval systems, the classical form of evaluation is to formulate a set of queries and compare the retrieved items against the entire database. Although time consuming, this is the most comprehensive way to determine how well indexing performs. In basic hypertext systems, where browsing is the chief form of information retrieval, the concept of a query is more fuzzy, and we cannot definitively evaluate the relevance of each node visited.

Nevertheless, some usage frequency measures need to be introduced. The frequency of access to each node can be summarized in a system administrator report. The time spent on a node could also be used as a surrogate measure for relevance and value. Nodes that are rarely accessed over a fairly long period of time can be evaluated both for relevance and for quality of linking structures. The collection of such statistics must be built into the system from the start. These statistics should allow both for garbage collection (identifying obsolete links) and for permitting the system administrator to analyze search strategies and patterns of retrieval.

## 4.4. Organization-Level Solutions

Finally, as in any information system, system solutions in and of themselves are not sufficient to address control issues without the appropriate management solutions. Hypertext may be particularly susceptible to data control problems, since users want the freedom to create or not create links as they see fit. For example, Halasz describes the “problem of premature organization” in Notecards, in which he observed that users are reluctant to define formal structures at early stages of the research process $[12]$ . ICTT has gone through three major iterations in changing and expanding the underlying file hierarchy, and a fourth review is under way now.

Figure 4. Work Flows in Large Dynamic Hyperbase Environments

Organizations seeking to build LDHs need to provide for sufficient personnel to fill the various roles that are required to avoid chaos. Figure 4 shows a scheme of people and tasks in an LDH environment. Even if some of these jobs are performed by the same person, it is important to recognize the delineation of responsibilities. Text Entry may be as simple as scanning, or as difficult as choosing valuable excerpts from largely worthless documents. The Indexer must have a broad view of the existing hypertext structures and linking conventions. The Hyperbase Administrator (HBA) has overall responsibility for the integrity, consistency, and safety of the hyperbase. Like a DBA, this person is knowledgeable about the information already in the system, the linkages between the system components, and the norms for using information in the organization. The End User may range from a novice doing simple queries to an experienced researcher who may also enter, index, and maintain text in the system. The end user/researcher often has the most expertise in the domain area, but may not be aware of—or adhere to—the organizational standards for linking.

Certain organizational formalisms can be introduced to the users and to the system. First, to promote sharing of information, new information needs to be flagged and forwarded, and the hyperbase needs to be seamlessly integrated with mail systems. AAIS uses the file folder hierarchy to share information; each AAIS user has his or her own hot file, which is like a mailbox to which other users “send” interesting nodes. Routing can help to avoid the problems of too few and inconsistent links by passing the burden of linking to those users who are most knowledgeable and have the greatest stake in a certain area of the hyperbase. Second, inconsistencies can be avoided by checking and approving indexing and linking of all inexperienced users. Formal procedures are introduced, whereby text chunks are not inserted into the hyperbase until they have been approved by the HBA, or some other trained person.

## 5. Summary and Conclusions

ALTHOUGH HYPERTEXT HAS SUDDENLY RECEIVED ATTENTION as a “new” paradigm for organizing information, the concept has strong ties to traditional information retrieval systems. Experience with systems such as AAIS, which has features of a combined IR-hypertext system, can and should be applied to hypertext. One of the tenets of hypertext is to allow greater freedom for both the reader and the author, but there may be a large price to pay down the line for allowing the user to be unsystematic in how links are created.

Particularly when multiple indexers are involved, hypertext links are subject to all sorts of problems. They may be incomplete, or there may be too many links. Links may be ambiguous, incorrect, or obsolete. All of these problems are apparent in the AAIS, which is one of the largest systems of its kind. Traditional indexing methods are now being investigated in a hypertext context, but automatic indexing techniques may not be particularly applicable. Combinations of indexes and links, such as the AAIS hierarchical file folders, are one way to reduce the indexing burden and retain some advantages of hypertext. In addition, it may be advisable to limit the domain of the hyperbase. Then specific algorithms can be built for specific domains, which help the user to make linking decisions. Recognition and division of roles, explicit sharing of the linking burden by distributing nodes to individuals for further processing, and other organizational formalisms can help to improve linking performance.

More research is needed on the entire gamut of issues related to hypertext links. Specific directions could include comparing hypertext linking behavior to indexing; performing retrieval experiments with LDH to find out how well links perform; testing new indexing and linking assistants; and developing better quality measures.

As we migrate to large dynamic hyperbases, the designers of these systems need to apply the technical and managerial concepts that have already been learned from experience in information retrieval systems and in database management systems. The true litmus test of large dynamic hypertext systems is how they will perform over time: How well are the links managed after thousands of new nodes are added to the system, by dozens of indexers and users?
