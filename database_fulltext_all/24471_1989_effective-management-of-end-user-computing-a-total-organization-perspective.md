# Effective Management of End-user Computing: A Total Organization Perspective

CAROL V. BROWN and ROBERT P. BOSTROM

CAROL V. BROWN is an Assistant Professor of Management within the Krannert School at Purdue University. She holds an A.B. from Vassar College, an M.Mus. from Northwestern University, and an M.B.A. from Indiana University, where she is currently completing her Ph.D. in MIS. Her prior work experience includes a nine-year tenure as a user manager with IS liaison responsibilities. Her current research interests encompass organization and management of the IS function issues, including end-user computing.

ROBERT P. BOSTROM is an Associate Professor of Management Information Systems within the Department of Management at the University of Georgia. He holds a B.A. and M.B.A. from Michigan State University, an M.S. in Computer Science from SUNY at Albany, and a Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Minnesota. Besides numerous publications in leading academic and practitioner journals, he has extensive consulting and training experience in the areas of MIS management, MIS design, organizational development, and high-performing MIS professionals. His current research interests are focused on high-performing individuals, user-designer relationships, computerized collaborative work support systems, end-user computing, and effective design of organizations via integrating human/social and technological dimensions.

ABSTRACT: Using the constructs of strategy, structure, and performance from organizational theory, the authors provide a framework for critically reviewing the previous research on end-user computing (EUC) management from a total organization perspective. While the growth of EUC and the need for its effective management have been well documented, our knowledge about what EUC management infrastructure is most effective for a given organization at a given point in time is incomplete and without a common theoretical basis.

Adopting the assumption that EUC is an organizational innovation, the authors develop a two-cell model of EUC management effectiveness that synthesizes selected MIS and innovation management literature. An exploratory investigation of the validity of the primary assumptions of this model is described. Interview data from IS managers in five organizations are presented as mini-cases. A revised model of EUC management effectiveness, which incorporates both the findings from this field study and previous “conflicting” EUC literature, is then proposed for further research.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: End-user computing, organizational innovation, contingency models.

## 1. Introduction

THE NEED FOR ORGANIZATIONS TO FORMULATE an appropriate “strategy” and “tactics” for the management of EUC has been repeatedly emphasized in the MIS literature $[1, 13, 16, 23, 24, 25, 28]$ . Yet to date an absence of explicit EUC “strategies” seems to be the overwhelming norm $[10, 13, 14]$ .

On the other hand, this situation is not surprising if one subscribes to the assumption that the development of business applications by end users is an organizational innovation. That is, if EUC is an organizational innovation, organizational learning is required in order to formulate a “strategy” for the management of EUC, and many organizations are still at an early point on that learning curve. In addition, EUC by definition is the dispersion of functions traditionally performed by MIS professionals to non-MIS personnel within the organization. The management tasks required for EUC therefore cross functional boundaries and may not readily fit within current work unit structures. That is, new and/or substantially revised organizational designs may be required for effective EUC management “tactics.”

Under the assumption that EUC is an organizational innovation, the purpose of this paper is to contribute to our knowledge about what EUC management infrastructure is most effective for a given organization at a particular point in time. First, a framework for addressing the primary EUC management tasks from a total organization perspective is presented. Next, a preliminary model of EUC management effectiveness that synthesizes prior MIS and innovation management literature is developed, and an exploratory investigation of the validity of its primary assumptions is described. A revised model of EUC management effectiveness, which incorporates both the findings from this field study and previous “conflicting” EUC literature, is then proposed for further research.

## 2. The Framework

FIGURE 1 DEPICTS A FRAMEWORK FOR ADDRESSING EUC MANAGEMENT from an organization theory perspective. Although an organization may not have an explicit “strategy” for EUC, the importance of taking into account the strategic choice factor [15] when evaluating the effectiveness of a particular structure is reflected in the framework. Further, an organization’s strategy/objectives for EUC would appear to be influenced by organizational factors such as industry type, organizational size, the overall IS objectives, as well as the perceived organizational design alternatives.

Here, the term EUC “structure” refers to the allocation of job roles and mechanisms to integrate and control the EUC management tasks across functional boundaries. The organizational choice of an EUC “structure” needs to take into account the following elements:

(1) the people (in this case, end-user developers and their functional area managers),

(2) the technology to be used by these people, and

(3) the data required for end-user application development.

As presented in Figure 1, four primary infrastructures [16] are required to manage EUC effectively. The first three elements correspond directly to the above three elements:

(1) a people support infrastructure, which includes tasks such as training and education, problem-solving through hotlines, ongoing consulting, and periodic communication mechanisms;

(2) a technology infrastructure, which includes scanning, selection, and acquisition tasks; and

(3) a data infrastructure, which includes data structuring and storage, data collection and maintenance, and data access tasks.

Frequently, a separate organizational unit (for example, an Information Center) has been created for personnel that perform the above EUC management tasks, at least temporarily $[5, 29]$ .

However, as pointed out by recent researchers $[1, 16]$ , attention to these three infrastructures is not enough for effective EUC management. An organization also needs to have a fourth management infrastructure for planning and evaluation to establish EUC goals for the organization, allocate resources, assign roles and responsibilities, develop policies and procedures, and assess performance across functional boundaries within the organization. According to the literature and our own field experience, a Steering Committee that has both IS and user representation—and sometimes both management and end-user developer representation—has frequently been created to perform or oversee these tasks, at least temporarily.

Finally, the performance variable of interest here is the effectiveness of EUC management. An organization's EUC management effectiveness is assumed to be a function of its level of performance for the management of the four EUC task areas.

## 3. Previous EUC Research

WITHIN THE MIS LITERATURE, SEVERAL STUDIES have addressed the EUC phenomenon with a macro-organization perspective compatible with the above EUC management framework. Although no empirical study has investigated relationships among all three EUC constructs in our framework—strategy, structure, and performance—empirical studies that address some aspects of EUC structure and performance, as well as conceptual studies that address the EUC structure with a “strategic choice” assumption, have recently appeared.

## 3.1. Strategy and Structure: Evolutionary Models

Several MIS researchers have proposed an evolutionary approach to EUC management based on the assumption that the organization's EUC strategy should change over time to match the stage of EUC assimilation within the given organization [1, 16, 17, 18, 20, 23, 24, 26]. Of these, three evolutionary models provide the most comprehensive guidelines for the EUC structure [1, 16, 23] and are therefore the most relevant to our total organization framework.

The four-stage, normative model for EUC management developed by Henderson and Treacy [16] is based in turn on McKenney and McFarlan's innovative information technology assimilation model [22]. A primary, unstated assumption of this model is that the organization's overall EUC management objective is to maximize the pace of EUC growth (or rate of EUC diffusion) within the organization. An additional assumption specific to Henderson and Treacy's model is that the relative importance of four critical managerial “issues”—the support infrastructure, technological infrastructure, data infrastructure, and evaluation/planning—changes over time. Different strategic perspectives and structuring mechanisms (for which they use the terms “structure” and “control mechanisms”) are therefore prescribed for each of their four stages (Implementation, Marketing, Operations, Economic). Henderson and Treacy observe, however, that organizations frequently impose the highly controlled and “centralized” Operations perspective “too early” in the EUC growth curve (p. 13).

Taking a descriptive rather than prescriptive research approach, Munro and Huff $[23, 24]$ have proposed a $2 \times 2$ model of four strategies defined by the degree to which the organization applies what they categorize as “control” and “expansion” levers. Like Henderson and Treacy, these authors found some organizations selecting a high control/low acceleration approach (here called “Containment”) early in the EUC implementation process. However, Munro and Huff view this “Containment” strategy as an appropriate early strategy, dependent on the organization’s choice of EUC growth pattern. That is, the authors propose that the degree of control is chosen by the organization in relation to its specific EUC objective, which could be either an accelerated or less accelerated pace of EUC growth.

Alavi et al. [1] build on the two somewhat “conflicting” models discussed above, as well as exploratory interviews the authors conducted with five companies. Five EUC strategies are profiled according to the implementation or non-implementation of four categories of EUC management “activities”: planning, policy setting, support, and control. The authors then prescribe an evolutionary progression of these strategies based on the organizational learning-curve assumption of McKenney and McFarlan’s model [22]. Like Henderson and Treacy, therefore, these researchers assume that an organization’s EUC objective is to maximize the rate of EUC growth. These authors also concur with Henderson and Treacy’s evolutionary progression in that a high-control “Containment” strategy is prescribed for a late phase of EUC implementation, just prior to a mature “Operations” strategy.

Implications. As a group, these three evolutionary models suggest the need to take into account two contingent factors when explaining or predicting the effectiveness of a given organization's EUC structure. First, all three share the assumption that effective management of EUC requires a “fit” between the EUC “strategy” (or growth objective) and the EUC structure. Second, two of the models also assume a “fit” between the organization’s current level of organizational learning (or stage of EUC implementation) and the EUC structure.

Unfortunately, none of these models has been empirically tested beyond the authors' initial validation efforts.

## 3.2. Structure and Performance: Empirical Studies

The establishment of a separate organizational unit to house the people support, data, and/or technology management tasks is a management action well documented in both the practitioner and academic literature. Several field studies investigating the roles of the Information Center (IC) staff, end-user tools and resources, the types of applications developed by IC users, the critical success factors for IC management, and real-world implementations vs. normative IC models have been published within the past few years [4, 7, 19, 21, 29, 30, 31]. Conceptual models of (1) organizational factors affecting the success of an end-user computing facility [8], and (2) individual and organizational factors affecting the satisfaction of end-user developers [27], have also recently been published. Finally, a preliminary investigation of the relationships between extent of EUC and various structuring “mechanisms”—both within and outside of an IC structure—has also been reported [33].

However, of more relevance to the EUC management framework here are three recently completed U.S. dissertations that empirically test hypothesized relationships between EUC “structure” variables and variables that measure EUC management performance. Amoroso [2] hypothesized positive relationships between (1) levels of “support” for EUC and levels of performance, and (2) levels of “helpful” policies and performance. He studied these relationships within an overall investigation of his model of organizational, technical, and individual end-user variables based on an EUC literature review. At the individual level of analysis, he then measured the relationships between these variables and two performance measures from the perspective of the end-user developer only: (1) user satisfaction with and (2) utilization of a specific end-user developed application. Amoroso only found strong support for the hypothesized relationship between end-user support and application utilization.

Crawford [10] also hypothesized a positive relationship between levels of EUC “support” and performance, and a negative relationship between levels of “control” and performance. He measured several indicators of “support” and “control” from IS managers and then collected performance measures from both end-user developers and their managers. None of the hypothesized relationships were supported.

Finally, within a larger study of the adoption and utilization of spreadsheet software from an information technology innovation perspective, Brancheau [3] undertook an exploratory investigation of the relationship between levels of support (expansion) and control for EUC and the diffusion of spreadsheet software within an organization. Similar to Crawford, he hypothesized (1) a positive relationship between levels of “expansion” tactics and the organization’s rate of spreadsheet adoption, and (2) a negative relationship between levels of “control” tactics and the organization’s rate of spreadsheet adoption, but his data did not support them. However, Brancheau also cautions against the generalizability of his results. In most of the organizations he studied, Information Centers were established too late to have an effect on the early adopters of this technology.

Implications. The lack of statistically significant results in the two studies that focused on EUC implementation $[2, 10]$ could be due to the lack of control for the two contingent factors suggested by the evolutionary models discussed in section 3.1: (1) the organization's position on the EUC learning curve (that is, its “stage” of EUC implementation), and (2) the organization's overall objective for EUC growth (that is, high vs. low pace of EUC growth). It is our viewpoint that these two factors—EUC implementation stage and EUC growth objective—are contingent factors that need to be accounted for when measuring relationships between EUC structure and EUC performance.

We believe that the above conceptual and empirical studies provide only partial answers to our research question. First, although the developers of all three of the evolutionary models could be said to share the perspective of EUC as an organizational innovation, none of these models specifically builds on prior management of innovation research. Second, no empirical study that measures the three EUC constructs in our management framework (strategy, structure, performance) is known to the authors. Third, also lacking within the previous EUC literature is a common agreement as to what are the key “structure” variables for the EUC management tasks.

In the next section we develop a preliminary model that directly builds on some of these prior studies and begins to address these lacunae.

## 4. A Preliminary Model of EUC Management Effectiveness

THE PRIOR LITERATURE REVIEW IDENTIFIED TWO CONTINGENT FACTORS that need to be considered when developing a model of EUC management effectiveness from an innovation management viewpoint. The first, the organization's strategic growth objective, was already included in our EUC management framework. The second, the organization's current “stage” of EUC implementation, needs to be specified as a contingent organizational factor.

As for the third deficiency, prior studies on the management of innovation from the reference discipline of organization theory provide some direction as to what EUC structure or organizational design factors may be significant determinants of EUC management effectiveness. Beginning with Burns and Stalker [6], management researchers have found considerable evidence for a positive relationship between rate of innovation adoption by a given firm and the degree to which it exhibits an “organic” (vs. “mechanistic”) structural form. In addition, three organization design variables (centralization, formalization, and complexity) have become widely accepted as the key components of the “organic” and “mechanistic” forms [9, 15].

Although the above management studies are primarily concerned with innovation adoption rather than sustained innovation implementation, some management researchers have developed structural models that include innovation implementation $[11]$ . Of these, the theoretical framework elaborated on by Duncan $[12, 32]$ appears to us to be especially useful for grounding the EUC evolutionary models of Henderson and Treacy $[16]$ and Alavi et al. $[1]$ in existing theory from a structural perspective.

More specifically, Duncan has proposed a two-stage normative model for the management of innovation, in which an “organic” structure is most effective in the “initiation” stage of the organizational innovation diffusion process, but a “mechanistic” structure is most effective in the “implementation” stage. $^{1}$ It should be noted that Duncan’s “ambidextrous” model also implicitly assumes that the organization’s objective is to maximize the innovation diffusion rate, an assumption shared by the above MIS models. Duncan’s definitions of the three key organizational design variables follow, along with the authors’ operationalizations of the variables for the domain of EUC management.

Centralization refers to the degree to which decision making is dispersed in terms of (1) the locus of authority for decision making and (2) the hierarchical levels of the organizational employees who participate in the decision process. Low centralization (decentralization) is a characteristic of the “organic” form; high centralization characterizes the “mechanistic” form.

As discussed in section 1, the management of EUC requires the dispersion of tasks traditionally the responsibility of the MIS professional. The measurement of the locus of authority for EUC management decision making should therefore include the degree to which authority is shared by the IS department and the user departments as well as the hierarchical locus of authority. Organizations with very high centralization of the EUC management tasks would therefore have the decision-making authority in the top management levels of either the IS department alone or user departments alone, and would not involve user-developers or first-line supervisors in decisions such as EUC resource prioritization, the development of policies and procedures for data “sharing,” and the creation or dissolution of an Information Center as a separate organizational unit.

Formalization refers to the extent to which appropriate rules and procedures for performing a job task exist in formal written statements, and the extent to which there is an organizational emphasis on following them. Low formalization is a characteristic of the “organic” form; high centralization characterizes the “mechanistic” form.

Organizations with very high levels of formalization would be characterized by specific operating rules and procedures, in written form, for the personnel performing the EUC people support, data, and technology tasks. In addition, the norm within the work units of these personnel would be to adhere rigidly to all rules and procedures.

Complexity refers to the number of occupational specialties and the degree of differentiation in the task structure. The degree of complexity is measured by the reliance on separate units to perform the tasks, the degree of discretion that personnel have in performance of the tasks, and the degree to which division of labor has occurred. High complexity is a characteristic of the “organic” form; low complexity characterizes the “mechanistic” form.

Organization designs with very high levels of complexity would be highly reliant on separate units for the performance of the EUC management tasks (for example, an Information Center for people support, data, and technology tasks; a Steering Committee for EUC planning and evaluation tasks). In addition, the personnel performing these tasks would have little direct supervision and the freedom to tailor their job positions.

If one adopts the assumptions that (1) EUC is an organizational innovation and therefore a stage/structure “fit” is required for EUC management effectiveness, and (2) organizations have an objective of a maximum EUC growth rate, a model of EUC management effectiveness that builds on the above cited research has just two cells—as shown in Figure 2. The first cell models organizations in an initial phase of EUC implementation as being effective when they implement an “organic” structure for the EUC management tasks. The second cell models organizations in an integrated phase of EUC implementation as being effective when they implement a “mechanistic” structure for the EUC management tasks. $^{2}$

## 5. Exploratory “Testing” of the Model’s Primary Assumptions

DURING THE SUMMER OF 1987 ON-SITE, EXPLORATORY INTERVIEWS with managers of five different organizations were held with one of the authors. The primary purpose of these interviews was to explore the validity of the two primary assumptions of the preliminary model of EUC management effectiveness: (1) the assumption that effective management of an EUC innovation presumed a maximum EUC diffusion rate, and (2) the assumption that a stage/structure “fit” would be required for effective EUC management. The small sample was justified by the fact that the existence of a single disconfirmation of the above two assumptions would require a revision of the model.

As shown in Figure 3, the sample included organizations from the private, nonprofit, and government sectors, but was one of convenience. In all but one of the organizations, only one manager was interviewed. The estimated number of user-developers within the organizations ranged from 10 to 300.

Each of the managers was interviewed individually for approximately 90 minutes in what was characterized as an information-gathering session for the purposes of comparing real-world approaches to EUC management with preliminary models of effective EUC management. The managers typically first described their own organizational position and EUC responsibilities, and then provided information on how EUC “got started” and has been managed. Qualitative notes were taken on a structured questionnaire organized according to the constructs in the EUC management framework (Figure 1). If a manager did not discuss a given item on the questionnaire, the interviewer raised the open-ended question. In some cases relevant documents were also shared with the interviewer (e.g., organization charts, manuals, policy statements, job descriptions).

## 6. Revised Model of EUC Management Effectiveness

SINCE THE IMPLICIT ASSUMPTION THAT EFFECTIVE MANAGEMENT OF EUC presumes a maximum EUC diffusion rate appears to be an invalid one, the authors expanded the preliminary model to include both a high rate and low rate of EUC diffusion as an EUC objective for both the initial and integrated implementation phases. The MIS and organization theory literature as well as the above exploratory interviews were used to model the structural form that would be associated with high EUC management effectiveness under the EUC objective of a low rate of diffusion.

As seen in the revised model in Figure 5, it is proposed that organizations in the initial implementation phase that have a low rate of EUC diffusion as their objective should implement a more “mechanistic” structure than organizations with a high rate of diffusion objective. The primary rationale for this proposition is that high-level decision making (high centralization), extensive procedural controls (high formalization), and little task discretion for those performing the EUC people support, technology, and data tasks (low complexity), will inhibit organizational learning and therefore EUC growth.

One additional important assumption of the revised model should also be made explicit: that is, the organization's objective for the pace of EUC growth takes into account specific organizational variables such as the level of top management support for the diffusion of EUC and the extent of EUC resources available.

## 7. Conclusions

THE CONTINGENT MODEL OF EUC MANAGEMENT EFFECTIVENESS PROPOSED HERE (Figure 5) is based on both prior MIS and management of innovation research, as well as exploratory interviews with managers in five organizations. It synthesizes conflicting elements in prior EUC conceptual studies, provides a potential explanation for unexpected findings in prior EUC empirical studies, and builds on innovation management research from a structural perspective.

For MIS managers who are faced with the selection of structural alternatives, our EUC management framework and contingent model may serve to increase awareness of the need to attend to (1) the EUC strategy/structure “fit” and (2) the “fit” between EUC structure and the organization’s stage of EUC implementation, when designing or redesigning the EUC management infrastructure.

Finally, the authors propose that both MIS managers and researchers consider evaluating the effectiveness of an EUC management infrastructure from an organizational design viewpoint. That is, we advocate that more attention be given to the degree to which EUC decision-making authority and participation is dispersed (Centralization), the degree to which written guidelines exist for EUC support personnel (Formalization), and the extent to which separate units and specialized job roles are relied on (Complexity) for effective EUC management.

## Mini-Cases

Of the three private sector organizations, the two service industries (A and B) were also highly information-intensive. Organization A was in the early part of an integrated implementation phase, with micro-mainframe linkages accessible to end users. The EUC objectives included constrained growth with standardized products. Although quarterly “payback” reports on user microcomputer purchases had just been discontinued, the cost-control orientation of the organization as a whole was seen by the Director to contribute to cost concerns about EUC resource expenditures. Centralization and formalization of EUC management were currently both high. Both the director and IC manager also characterized their initial implementation approach as having been even more tightly controlled, with the result that growth in the utilization of microcomputers may have been more highly contained than may have been in the organization's best long-range interests.

Organization B was also in the early part of an integrated implementation phase, with micro-mainframe linkages accessible to end users. The EUC objective appeared to be relatively high EUC growth, with the availability of excellent support service serving as an incentive for the adoption of standardized products. Centralization and formalization appeared to be at a medium level. The IS manager also placed a high value on the “service” attitude adopted by the EUC support unit in achieving end-user satisfaction with EUC management.

Organization C, from a manufacturing industry, was in an initial implementation phase with primarily stand-alone applications. The manager interviewed emphasized operational efficiency concerns and the need for EUC resource sharing due to low utilization rates. Centralization and formalization appeared to be medium high. The IS manager also suggested that a “laissez-faire” managerial approach would be considered by organization C’s top management to be “mismanagement”; the conservative, controlled approach adopted for EUC management matched the organization’s overall emphasis on cost management.

Organization D, from the government sector, was in an initial EUC implementation phase, with micro-mainframe linkages accomplished by Information Center staff. Within the organization's formal documents, the EUC objectives were stated in terms of operational goals, including efficiency concerns; the manager characterized the approach as “controlled” but spoke of effectiveness as well as efficiency objectives. Centralization and formalization of EUC management were both high. As an example of the latter, a “user certification” program for mainframe, mini, and micro tools was part of an IC position's job description. The IC manager of D considered the “highly regulated environment” of the organization's industry sector (government) to be a major influencing factor for both the EUC management structuring and its slow implementation. According to the manager, government tends not to be an “innovator” and the IC “fits the mold.”

Organization E, from the non-profit sector, was also in an initial implementation phase, with micro-mainframe linkages accomplished by IS staff. The IS director characterized their current EUC management approach as “laissez-faire.” Centralization and formalization were therefore at the low end of the continuum. Although this “laissez-faire” characterization initially appeared to be contradictory to the acquisition review authority that had been assigned to the director six months previously, it was actually being used as a communication mechanism; i.e., the user departments were permitted to “get what they wanted” but they were advised prior to purchasing whether or not the IC intended to provide support for their acquisitions. This choice of approach was seen to be appropriate due to the loosely coupled divisional structure of organization E as a whole. Although the IS director seemed to expect high EUC growth in the future, short-term growth objectives seemed to be diminished due to other organizational priorities.
