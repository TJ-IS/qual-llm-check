## 1. Introduction

THE LONDON STOCK EXCHANGE'S BIG BANG on October 27, 198,6 marked the arrival of sweeping and long-awaited deregulation. Numerous changes occurred simultaneously, including elimination of fixed commissions, marked increase in the number of market participants, change in the structure and ownership of trading firms, and, perhaps most importantly rapid movement of stock trading off the floor of the Exchange. This remains the most rapid and complete regulatory reform of any market, and the most striking example to date of a regulatory event engineered to benefit the local financial industry. $^{2}$ The market's changes were, in large part, accomplished through the application of Information Technology (IT), and the introduction of a screen-based dealing system. Although Big Bang was largely driven by a lawsuit challenging the anti-competitive practices of the Exchange in Britain, the timing was excellent, since the effort was effectively supported by the Thatcher government's privatization program. Since the overhaul of established securities trading practices, London's financial markets have undergone considerable change while continuing to operate smoothly; a number of benefits have been realized.

Big Bang resulted in major changes in the structure of the market, and the nature and number of participants [38]. Where previously there had been only two or three jobbers per stock, now twelve to sixteen market makers compete for the most active issues. $^{3}$ Just three major jobbers (Smith Brothers, Wedd Durlacher, and Akroyd & Smithers) controlled 75 percent of all market making. Together with a handful of brokers they were the equities portion of the Exchange. On Big Bang day, 36 equity market makers were open for business; there had been 13 previously. In gilts, where previously eight jobbers operated and two or three firms dominated the market, there were 27 market makers after Big Bang.

Despite the benefits to London as a financial center, the changes associated with Big Bang appear to have been counter to the interests of many stakeholders, including both the partners in the dominant houses and the most senior and successful floor-based traders. Pressured by overseas competitors and by imminent government regulatory action, in June 1985 the Exchange was able to get the 75 percent majority of its 5,400 individual members required for ratification of the Big Bang changes. There are fascinating issues of organizational change involved:

\- How was the Exchange able to get the necessary approval from an overwhelming majority of individual members, who might be potentially adversely affected by the proposed changes?

\- How does the membership view the changes that have now occurred?

Throughout Big Bang, technology played a fundamental role as the enabler of the process. Information technology makes possible the electronic trading that has replaced the Stock Exchange floor. It facilitates handling the greatly increased volumes and number of market participants that resulted. And, by improving surveillance, it enables some of the regulatory relaxations associated with Big Bang.

Four major forces were acting on stock exchanges at the time of Big Bang, and continue to be a factor in determining the evolution of securities trading:

\- There is increasing reliance on and use of information technology. Market mechanisms are now driven by information technology, and the demands of market participants for greater liquidity, more hedging techniques, and low transaction and settlement costs [10].

\- There is increasing competition among exchanges as technology removes or erodes geographic barriers [12].

\- There has been increasing internationalization of investor behavior [17, 19, 39]. Foreign gross activity in U.S. equities rose from \$26 billion in 1975 to \$277 billion in 1986. U.S. gross activity in foreign equities rose from \$3 billion in 1975 to \$101 billion in 1986. Despite a temporary interruption after the October 1987 market crash, internationalization continues to grow. The total cross-border investment in securities in 1988 was higher than in 1986.

\- And deregulation or liberalization of major exchanges has continued: The Paris Bourse is undergoing its own Mini Bang, with significant improvement to trading and after-trade settlement, and with the development of active derivatives markets. Tokyo has lowered commissions, has allowed the introduction of equities index futures, has opened a financial futures exchange, and is planning to introduce index options. And Canada commenced its own gradualist approach to deregulation with its Little Bang on June 30, 1987, ending separation of the four sectors of its financial services industry and allowing foreign ownership of securities trading firms. $^{5}$

As a financial center with centuries of experience, London enjoys a most favorable position. Additionally, since Big Bang, London's International Stock Exchange (ISE) has been at the forefront in exploiting the four trends listed above. Big Bang required three years to prepare for massive regulatory, technological, and organizational change. The ISE's use of technology produced a revolutionary change in London's market structure and trading practices. London's trading floor was rapidly abandoned, and a centuries-old tradition of face-to-face dealing was rapidly replaced with electronic trading. Within a week, between two-thirds and three-quarters of the trading had moved upstairs.

The introduction of new technology has been associated with a significant improvement in London's competitive position, and a resulting increase in trading volumes. There was a notable increase in liquidity, and in the market's ability to handle large and very large trades. And there was a substantial rise in the volume of non-U.K. equities traded in London.

## 2. Big Bang

BIG BANG ENTAILED MAJOR RESTRUCTURING OF MOST ASPECTS of London's securities market; it was far more than the automation of existing trading practices:

\- Industry players were restructured, merging, consolidating, and engaging in new activities.

\- Trading practices were restructured. Floor-based face-to-face trading was replaced by an electronic system of competing market makers.

\- The Exchange itself was restructured. It merged with ISRO, the International Securities Regulatory Organization. And The Securities Association (TSA), a self-regulatory organization associated with the Exchange, was established.

## 2.1. Major Changes Associated with Big Bang

The following four major changes were associated with Big Bang: unfixing of stock trading commissions, screen-based trading, dual capacity operations of member firms, and opening of the Exchange to new members.

## 2.1.1. Unfixed Commissions

A minimum scale for commissions was eliminated and rates were open to negotiation. This was especially significant for large bargains, and half of equities turnover after Big Bang was done net, with no commissions at all. According to a survey of firms, 1986 commission rates for small bargains rose slightly; for instance, the average commission on a £5,000 trade rose from 1.26 percent to 1.60 percent. Institutional rates fell about 30 to 40 percent; for instance, bargains of £100,000 were charged 0.39 percent on average before Big Bang and 0.28 percent just afterward [36].

## 2.1.2. Dual Capacity Operations

Prior to Big Bang, firms could participate either as brokers or as jobbers. A broker was an agency trader, representing clients wishing to buy or sell shares. A jobber stood on the Exchange floor and bought from brokers with customers who wanted to sell, sold to brokers whose customers wanted to buy, and made a spread, the difference between the price at which he bought and the price at which he sold shares. All trades involved a broker, with fixed commissions, and a market maker, who took his spread. With Big Bang, the prohibitions against performing both functions within a single firm were eliminated, and most firms chose to operate in dual-capacity, with both agency broker operations and market maker functions performed in a single firm. Shortly after Big Bang, close to half the institutional trading was self-brokered, with the institutional client contacting the market maker directly.

## 2.1.3. Opening of the Exchange to Outsiders

Restrictions on Exchange membership were lifted and 187 ISRO members and foreign firms were allowed to acquire existing member firms or become members themselves. Considerable restructuring resulted. Prior to Big Bang, a number of expensive and well-publicized acquisitions and mergers took place: $^{6}$ Barclays Bank acquired de Zoete & Bevan, a broker, and Wedd Durlacher, a major jobber with strong equities and gilts operations; the merchant bank S. G. Warburg acquired jobber Akroyd & Smithers, broker Rowe & Pitman, and government broker Mullens & Co.; Smith Brothers, the major equities jobber, merged with broking firm Scott Goff Layton to become Smith New Court; and Security Pacific Bank bought broker Hoare Govett and jobber Charles Pulley. $^{7}$ There were nearly 350 member firms in the London market after Big Bang, up from about 200 previously. Additions included major foreign powerhouses such as Nomura Securities and Merrill Lynch.

## 2.1.4. Screen-based Trading

Firm bid and ask quotes were announced by competing market makers $^{8}$ in alpha and beta stocks. $^{9}$ SEAQ, the Stock Exchange Automatic Quotation System based on NASDAQ, was developed as an electronic, screen-based system for market makers to publicize their prices. Alternative trading mechanisms were considered, and this was selected after careful study. $^{10}$ Trading screens were made available both downstairs, on the floor of the Exchange, and upstairs, in the offices of member firms; it was therefore possible to trade by selecting a market maker based on announced prices and then contacting the market maker, either by telephone or by walking to his trading pitch on the floor. Despite member firms taking out three-year leases on 224 floor positions, the floor trading crowd disappeared.

## 2.2. Planned Improvements

Several enhancements, not implemented as part of Big Bang, are planned for the Exchange's trading and settlement systems. Some have encountered major delays.

\- SEAQ Automated Execution Facility (SAEF) was repeatedly delayed, but came online in February 1989. It supports automatic execution of trades under 1,000 shares; it is expected that bargains of up to 5,000 shares will soon be accommodated. All market makers are required to participate, paying for SAEF equipment and receiving SAEF orders when eligible.

\- TAURUS, a proposed automated settlement system, has been repeatedly delayed. It was originally intended to provide for dematerialization. $^{11}$ The TAURUS proposal has been revised frequently, and most recently the Exchange has agreed to limit itself to a 40 percent stake in a venture majority owned by a consortium of banks and securities firms. It is not certain how this will be resolved.

\- Talisman is already in use for automated settlement of inter-market maker trades. It will likely be the basis for TAURUS, and will be linked to SAEF. It is hoped that trades with SAEF execution and Talisman settlement will cost £5, down from the current cost of £4 for dealing and £20 for settlement.

## 3. Background

## 3.1. Factors that Affect a Market's Attractiveness

Several factors combine to make a market attractive [12, 29]:

\- Liquidity is perhaps most important in determining the attractiveness of a market. It is a measure of a market's ability to convert readily between securities and cash, and to do so rapidly and with minimal changes in the market price. Counterparties must be available, and arrival of investors' sell orders should not depress the market price, and buy orders should not raise it unduly.

\- Depth measures the market's ability to absorb very large trades.

\- Efficiency has two components. Price efficiency describes the market's ability to offer fair prices, and to adjust prices to capture available information. Execution efficiency describes the cost and speed of execution, and thus the relationship between the price in the market and the price actually paid or received by the end customer.

\- Information and information access are the characteristics most readily affected by technology. How widely will information be shared or disseminated? Will all players have access to information comparable to that enjoyed by the insiders? How quickly will information be available?

\- Fairness is a characteristic critically important to retail investors, and most readily influenced by regulation. It reflects the rights and privileges of the non-member, or non-insider, compared to those of more powerful or better connected traders.

## 3.2. Role of Technology

Technology has long played a role in changing securities trading. Communications technology, in particular, has had a major impact on trading practices. Phone lines from brokers to the New York Stock Exchange floor were installed in 1878, just two years after the telephone was first demonstrated. Garbade and Silber showed that a New York–Philadelphia telegraph link in 1846 and a New York–London connection in 1866 quickly cut price differentials between the cities' markets in currencies, bonds, and stocks [21]. Information technology has several valuable roles in securities markets:

\- Quicker information flow supports price efficiency, rapid adjustment of prices to reflect supply, demand, and available information. By allowing arbitragers to exploit price differences among markets, information flow also facilitates efficient price setting across markets.

\- The ability to be informed away from the floor, with greater dissemination of information to participants wherever they are located, improves efficiency, and can have a significant impact on liquidity.

\- More efficient settlement reduces transaction processing costs and settlement delay, with associated reduction in opportunity costs and risk.

Additionally, technology supports effective market surveillance, which improves fairness: Surveillance computers linked to SEAQ can detect trades executed at other than the best price. Brokers risk losing their licenses by arranging client trades at disadvantageous prices. In effect, dual capacity is enabled by automated surveillance. Additionally, surveillance computers are being used by compliance departments to identify large-scale or irregular share activity prior to a merger or acquisition. This occurs both within member firms, and within the various regulatory bodies attempting to identify insider trading. $^{12}$

Technology was essential to all aspects of London's deregulation, from making possible screen-based trading with greatly increased numbers of market makers, to making politically feasible ending the separation of broking and jobbing functions.

Several other applications of technology to securities trading have been attempted, with far less striking effects.

\- The Intermarket Trading System (ITS) was implemented in 1978 to link five regional exchanges and the New York and American exchanges. Although 1,200 stocks are jointly listed, ITS trading supports just 4 percent of the shares' aggregate volume. In addition, a 1981 study by the SEC found no significant impact in reducing spreads [15].

\- Instinet, a Reuters subsidiary, is a “notice board” system for exposing investor orders to the market. In 1988, Instinet accounted for less than 0.2 percent of the total trading volume in 1,788 NYSE-listed stocks [35].

\- The Cincinnati Stock Exchange (CSE), although an earlier and more extensive user of information technology than the dominant NYSE, has had only limited impact [15]. Before Merrill Lynch, its primary supporter, withdrew in 1983, CSE trading was just 0.6 percent of composite volume in its 188 eligible stocks.

## 3.3. Competitive Impact

London appears to have intended to capture order flow from Continental exchanges, as articles entitled “Stock Exchange plans to lead world trading in international shares” [33] indicate, and to some extent they have succeeded. Tokyo does not appear to have been affected, and at present it is hard to demonstrate an impact on the New York Stock Exchange.

Continental exchanges were particularly vulnerable, due to problems with trading practices, liquidity, and execution and settlement efficiency. The Paris Bourse operated as a periodic call market. $^{13}$

The Madrid market was plagued by incredible settlement difficulties [41]. Colin Grimsey, vice president of Chase Manhattan Bank in London, noted that “we have maintained a presence in Madrid for over a year now and we are still unable to clearly identify how the settlement systems work." Although the Milan market has recently improved settlement, horror stories abound.

Swedish authorities doubled their country's turnover tax to 1 percent on all securities transactions, paid by both the buyer and seller. Traders responded by moving volume off the Stockholm exchange for the most liquid Swedish issues, and prior to the October 1987 crash the most attractive stocks were five times more liquid in London than on their home Stockholm exchange [34].

The problems faced by these European exchanges had the effect of making each, in its own way, less attractive. London is more efficient than Paris in setting prices, than Milan and Madrid in execution and settlement, than Stockholm in transaction costs. SEAQ disseminates price and trading information widely and rapidly. The two together bring in more trading, which makes the market more liquid, which itself brings in more trading and more liquidity. As George Hayter of the ISE notes, “London is more attractive. And information systems take geography entirely out of the account.” $^{14}$ London, therefore, has been able to capture order flow from Continental exchanges. Significant turnover of major issues has moved from Frankfurt and Paris, and major Swedish issues are now more liquid in London than on their home exchanges.

Most of the smaller exchanges that compete with major exchanges have attempted to respond to technological threats, at least to retain listings and order flow for domestic companies. Le Petit Bang has begun in Paris. $^{15}$ Milan has improved settlement procedures. And Toronto has attempted to use technology to recapture at least trading in Canadian issues away from the NYSE [18].

New York is a very different market from London, and has not been adversely affected. The NYSE is currently committed to its specialist system, retaining a single market maker in a near-monopoly position on the exchange floor. The specialist faces increasing competition from regional exchanges. In return, the specialist has an “affirmative obligation” to maintain a fair and orderly market in each of the Big Board’s 1,788 traded issues. This means maintaining a principal position and limiting price fluctuations by selling or buying when order imbalances occur. Automation has been essential to expand the capacity of the NYSE. New York handled more volume in the week of October 19, 1987, than in an entire year only a decade earlier! NYSE automation has largely been directed at automating clerical details of the existing practice and trading mechanism [1] and little structural change has been attempted. Through its international market, London is equipped to trade overseas stocks, and if overly restrictive regulatory controls were imposed in the wake of the October Crash, there was a threat that trading volume in major U.S. stocks would have shifted to London. So far, New York has not needed to respond to London.

Tokyo is a unique market. The Tokyo Stock Exchange has protected its order flow through regulatory expedients: it is illegal for Japanese to sell Japanese shares other than on Japanese exchanges, and it is illegal for Japanese to own shares in Japanese companies in ADR form. It remains the most regulated major market, and although commissions have been lowered they remain fixed; for retail investors commissions are higher than in other major markets. Only a very small percentage of the trading in Japanese shares has moved to other exchanges, and it has not been necessary for Tokyo to respond to London.

## 4. Principal Findings

THE ABANDONMENT OF LONDON'S TRADING FLOOR was astoundingly rapid and astoundingly complete. Many firms prepared for both floor-based and upstairs trading. As confirmed in articles and through our interviews with several senior equities traders, many firms took out three-year leases on floor pitches as insurance in case SEAQ adoption was slow. Most firms had expected floor trading to remain, at least for a transition period of two to three years [14]. Although 28 firms had trading pitches on Big Bang day, compared with 19 previously [40], during the first week only a quarter to a third of all trading was on the floor [32]. The floor crowd fell to between 200 and 300 after the first week, down from 1,800 to 2,000 before [40].

This rapid and complex change has several explanations, which are explored below.

## 4.1. Speed of the Demise of the Floor

The speed with which trading left the floor was clearly a surprise. An investment of several million pounds was made to upgrade the floor. Firms took three-year leases on their trading pitches in preparation for Big Bang, and competed fiercely for available space. Yet the floor's share of bargains and turnover, and trader head count on the floor, all dropped rapidly in the first week of screen-based trading. In fact, at the end of the first week, movement off the floor and its resulting death were being discussed, and alternative uses of the floor, ranging from a dance hall to returning full circle to a coffee shop, were openly suggested in the press [6, 7]. By January, the Financial Times published an article entitled “The Hexagonal Wine Bar,” referring to the shape of the floor jobbers' pitches. The death of the floor was already complete.

Traders were astounded by the speed. Anthony Lewis was chairman of Smith New Court, which included Smith Brothers, one of the three largest and most successful floor-based jobbers before Big Bang. Not surprisingly, he was the most active advocate of preserving the trading floor, and he insisted that any screen-based system be designed so that floor-based trading remained a viable alternative. His firm made only minimal provisions for an upstairs alternative to the floor. Yet, his colleague Anthony Abrahams notes: "Within five minutes of Big Bang, on Monday morning, it was clear to me that the floor was dead. I'm not bragging. I was the last person in the City to figure it out!" $^{16}$

As John Wilson, head of equities at James Capel, added: "The speed of transition to screen trading surprised me. I thought all the information you needed was on the floor; eye contact, sweat, movement. You could always tell from the eyes of the junior trader whether his boss was long or short, and how badly they wanted to get out of their position." $^{17}$

And traders were clearly appalled by the changes. Wilson continued: "I was horrified. Horrified. Absolutely shattered. It had been a great club." $^{18}$

Mr. R. Wilson Stephens, Honorary Market Official at the International Stock Exchange, added: "I was horrified. What I miss about the floor is the supportive friends you develop there. They would know if you were having a bad day or a good one." $^{19}$

Smith New Court's Abrahams and Montagu's Pepper $^{20}$ echoed the sentiment mourning the demise of the club. Abrahams clearly did not welcome changes from the practices that had enabled his firm to prosper.

Not all traders were astounded or unprepared. Bernard Leaver, now chief equities trader and managing director at Shearson Lehman Hutton in London, anticipated the changes several years in advance. After rising from his junior position into a partner position in a small specialty jobber, Charles Pulley, Leaver evaluated potential acquirers, and arranged the firm's merger with Hoare Govett and Security Pacific, where he became head equities trader before departing for Shearson.

## 4.2. Reasons for Abandoning the Floor

There are several reasons, or partial explanations, for the demise of the trading floor and the rapid adoption of SEAQ technology.

Role of firm price quotations for alpha and beta stocks. Firm screen prices meant that you could get beaten up quite badly if prices moved against you. If you were occupied handling trades on the floor, and were not monitoring your quote in SEAQ, you could then be obligated to deal with the next person who said “Done!” Upstairs you could be insulated by the telephone, affording you time to alter a stale quote before the trader could reach you. This was first explained to us by Anthony Abrahams, and was reinforced by Gene Lockhart, CEO of Information Systems, Midland Bank.

Role of improved ergonomics upstairs. Some third-party vendors are actually able to supply changing price information faster than the Exchange. Increasing importance of additional information is obvious when viewing multi-million dollar upstairs dealing rooms, where each equities traders is equipped with four, five, or even more screens and banks of telephones. As Bernard Leaver, Managing Director of Shearson Lehman Hutton, notes: "Information plays a far greater role in trading decisions than it did. Trade figures, Reuters data, and other information are available more easily in the office than on the floor." $^{22}$

Real time feeds from newswire services and reliance on computer modeling are already factors. Eventually computerized trading strategies will also be a factor.

Role of improved access to colleagues upstairs. The design of upstairs dealing rooms supported much better interaction among firms' employees. Sales personnel, selling to customers by telephone, had improved access to the market research staff since they were together on the same dealing floor. Similarly, sales staff, supporting the firm's broker function, had immediate access to the firm's market makers. Brokers have a fiduciary obligation to ensure that a customer's order executes at the best price available in the market, whether this is quoted by the broker's in-house market maker or not. In practice, orders from preferred customers are seldom transacted outside a dual capacity firm; rather, the market maker, contacted by his firm's sales personnel with a buy or sell order, will usually match the best price displayed $^{23}$ in the SEAQ yellow strip. $^{24}$ Major firms like Barclays de Zoete Wedd designed and redesigned their trading floors, attempting to find the optimal configuration to support interaction among their staff members [8, 16].

Role of self-brokered institutional clients. Market makers expected a significant portion of their institutional clients to self-broker. Their best customers were expected to make their own investment decisions, to bypass brokers, and to deal directly with a firm's market-making personnel. As Mr. Jeremy Tigue, Director of Foreign and Colonial Pensions Management, notes, many fund managers considered it "macho to self-broker." $^{25}$ The dissemination of price information through SEAQ made it possible to self-broker, and the expectation was that this in fact would occur.

In the three months after Big Bang, about half of all institutional orders were being done net [36] (i.e., at no commission, with the market maker profiting only from spread). By July 1987, self-brokered trading volume was down considerably to 27.1 percent of total U.K. equities volume [37]. Although Exchange rules require that investors receive the best available price on SEAQ whether or not they are trading net, brokers that charge commission are often able to improve upon the screen price by negotiating with market makers. And the broker does provide valuable information. Smaller investment managers, unable to afford full market research facilities, often choose to continue to pay commissions in order to receive the research that the best brokers can provide. $^{26}$

Thus, self-brokered trades are not the factor that they were expected to be. Still, the expectation that larger accounts would choose to be self-brokered, requiring improved telephone access to market makers from outside, no doubt contributed to the move off the floor.

Role of self-reinforcing trends. A confluence of factors, from changed trading rules to the expectation of self-brokered trades, moved much of the crowd upstairs. Then, "You couldn't defy it—you had to follow the crowd." $^{27}$ You can't trade by yourself! Smith New Court was among the most successful of the old jobbers, and its chairman Anthony Lewis was certainly most committed to remaining on the trading floor [6]; yet within days of Big Bang, Smith New Court had decided to move off the floor, and did so as soon as dealing facilities were available in their offices. $^{28}$ Since traders benefit from exposure to as many orders as possible, the floor is valuable only if a sufficient number of traders use it. As more traders moved more of their volume upstairs, they rapidly tipped the preferences of others.

In summary, numerous factors acting together, ranging from the interaction between screen-based trading and the requirements of the new trading rules, to response to the behavior of other traders, rapidly moved all equities activity off the floor.

## 5. Managing the Organizational Change

THE CHANGES MADE ON THE SINGLE DAY OF BIG BANG exceeded the regulatory changes made in New York over a decade. New York appears wedded to the concept of the specialist market; given the vested power of the specialists in the Exchange, and the attractiveness and profitability of their position, this is not surprising. $^{30}$ Some would say it is nearly impossible to make comparably sweeping changes in New York today.

Certainly vested interests were affected. Smith Brothers, Akroyd & Smithers, and Wedd Durlacher accounted for three-fourths of the jobbing market, and were certain to lose share. The entrance of new competitors, especially well-capitalized foreign players such as Merrill Lynch, Goldman Sachs, Citicorp, Security Pacific, Credit Suisse, First Boston, and potentially Nomura and Daiwa as full equities market makers, had to have been seen as a threat!

As noted above, the changes encompassed in Big Bang required a vote of 75 percent of the individuals who were members of the Exchange. Why was London's membership able to agree to these changes?

A partial explanation can be found in the pressures facing London. The Exchange needed to settle a restrictive trading practices lawsuit brought against the Exchange by the British Office of Fair Trading. And there was pressure from Bank of England, which wanted to increase the number of gilts market makers. In October 1983 the suit was settled. The ending of fixed commissions was mandated by the settlement; other reforms at the same time were to permit dual capacity operations, and to install an improved price dissemination system.

London also faced competitive pressures. The de facto upstairs institutional market, and the threat of Reuters to support that market and ISRO to provide both the rules and the mechanism, had to be taken seriously. The Exchange's share of trading in U.K. stocks was eroding. As George Hayter notes, "An Exchange has no God-given right to exist, ordained from on high. An Exchange is a business like any other." $^{31}$ Unless you are the most efficient, most attractive place to trade, you will be destroyed. And, as John Wilson notes, there was a real threat from American and Japanese markets: "If we don't change, the Americans and the Japanese are going to come in and do it their way." $^{32}$

But the ISE was seizing opportunities as well as responding to threats. Foremost was the opportunity to compete with “Gilbert and Sullivan” stock exchanges on the Continent. Additionally, the ISE was able to profit from London’s centuries-old position as a world financial capital: London has a long tradition as a trading center, it is the focus of the Eurobond market, and \$90.0 billion of foreign currency was traded daily in the U.K. in 1986. Comparable currency trading volume in 1986 was \$58.5 billion per day in the U.S. and \$48.0 billion in Japan. And timing created the opportunity to profit from a wave of new investors associated with the Thatcher government’s privatizations and popular capitalism effort, intended to boost the ownership of shares by individuals.

Additionally, members of the Exchange were ready, even eager, for change. Brokers wanted to be jobbers, jobbers wanted to be brokers. A former jobber we spoke to assured us that there was widespread belief that “Brokers make money without taking risk,” while a former broker assured us that “Jobbers make money without having to know anything, without having to sell, without having to pay for research analysts.” John Wilson summarized it tersely: “The grass is always greener on the other side. Dealers wanted the easy job of jobbing. Jobbers were enthusiastic about talking directly to clients.” $^{33}$

And, as the Exchange and its member firms had grown over the previous twenty years, personnel had begun to specialize. There were now professional, full-time floor-based trading staff; partners and senior personnel were already upstairs, selling over the phone. They might walk an order over to the floor, but were no longer based on the floor. The move upstairs could actually have given senior managers better control over more intricate operations and a feel for a more complex market. $^{34}$

Finally, the Exchange was well positioned to make changes. It enjoyed an excellent systems group, with both the technical skills necessary to implement changes and the vision to determine what was going to be required to compete. And there was an excellent ad hoc strategic planning function in place to prepare for settlement of the Unfair Trading Practices lawsuit.

## 6. Conclusions

## 6.1. Summary—Benefits to London

London does appear to have enhanced its competitive position. Our earlier work has suggested that it is difficult to use information technology to gain and defend competitive advantage, unless certain conditions are met:

\- The innovation should play to some intrinsic strength, so that competitors cannot readily duplicate it. In particular, it is useful if the strength is not in information technology, but represents some portfolio of assets that competitors cannot readily acquire; in this case, duplication of the system will not provide competitors with comparable benefits [10].

\- Or, competitors should be unwilling to match the innovation, or uninterested in doing so. This provides the lead-time needed to seize market share [10].

\- And some form of switching cost should be present, keeping customers from defecting, and preserving initial marketplace gains [9].

London has long been a global money center and a financial capital for Europe, and Big Bang effectively leveraged this strength. And high quality investment research is a noted strength in London. When a London broker, Savory Milln, issued a “buy” recommendation for Peugeot in 1986, it pushed the share price up. Similar advice from Paris analysts had failed to move the price [26]. Additionally, competitors ranging from major exchanges such as the New York and Tokyo Stock Exchanges to far smaller Continental exchanges, appeared uninterested in comparable restructuring. $^{35}$

There were at least two sources of switching costs to protect London's gains. When trading operations are established, there is of course a significant cost associated with moving them to another country. And liquidity follows liquidity; that is, there is a cost penalty associated with trading in less liquid markets, which, other things being equal, keeps investors trading in established trading centers. $^{36}$ Thus, in the absence of self-destructive regulation or other change in London, there should be sufficient switching costs to provide sustainable competitive advantage.

Benefits to London, all documented in quarterly Quality of Markets Reports, include the following:

\- increased trading volume, liquidity, and depth,

\- reduced spreads and institutional commissions,

\- increased trading of foreign securities on the London Exchange. $^{37}$

The last gain noted includes both systematic and persistent increases in volume captured from Continental exchanges, and expedient use of London to trade when an issue's home exchange undergoes a temporary disruption or suspension of trading. $^{38}$

A considerable increase in liquidity followed Big Bang. Unfortunately, while most market participants and students of the markets agree that liquidity is the most important characteristic determining the attractiveness of an exchange, it remains an elusive concept and extremely difficult to measure. $^{39}$ John Maynard Keynes recognized liquidity as being “vague . . . having no absolute standard,” but also as being necessary “in estimating the comparative attractions of having different forms of wealth” [28]. In the years since Keynes, the lack of a single measure for liquidity has been noted by a number of other authors [5, 24], and a number of surrogate measures have been proposed. Those applicable to London include:

• average daily turnover, $^{40}$

\- the number of market makers and the number of investors holding each security [22],

\- size of the bid–ask spread [3].

Average daily turnover in London was £643 million for the first 9 months of 1986. In the first three months after Big Bang, average daily turnover nearly doubled to £1,161 million [36]. The depth of the market also increased. Larger transactions have become more common and have a less severe impact on prices than before. Bargains greater than £1 million rose to 25.2 percent of equities turnover value three months after Big Bang, up from 12.2 percent of value 3 months before Big Bang [36]. And there has been a marked increase in trading volume of non-U.K. issues. Estimates of non-U.K. trading are imprecise because, prior to Big Bang, member firms did not have to report trades in foreign equities except when they dealt with other member firms [38], and, subsequent to Big Bang, trading through SEAQ International by former ISRO members, who were not yet members of the ISE, was not reported; still, estimates are that daily volume increased from £290 million before Big Bang to £1 billion after [12].

As noted above, there were 13 equities floor jobbers before Big Bang, of whom only 5 had any significant share of the market. After Big Bang, the number of market makers had increased to 36. Additionally, Thatcher's privatization and popular capitalism programs had significantly increased the number of shareholders in the U.K., from 3 million in 1979 to 9 million in 1988 [38].

Amihud and Mendelson argue convincingly that “illiquidity can be measured by the cost of immediate execution,” and that the bid–ask spread is a major component of execution cost $[3]$ . And they show empirically that less liquid stocks command a liquidity penalty: investors require a higher rate of return to entice them to hold illiquid securities. Since the spread generally increases with the size of the bargain in London, and increases as well with the move from alpha (those universally considered most liquid) to gamma (least liquid), we are encouraged to consider the spread as another liquidity measure for London. The average touch (the difference between the best bid and ask quotes) dropped slightly in all classes of equities after Big Bang, as shown in Table 2. In none of the three classes was the average spread between the largest quotes in SEAQ after Big Bang worse than the average touch before $[25]$ .

## 6.2. Implications for Other Exchanges

There are lessons for New York and other established exchanges. Since information systems can reduce the importance of geography, and of geographically protected franchises, people will ultimately trade in the most attractive markets. “This is a bit like water finding its own level. Exchanges will adapt, and meet somewhere in the middle.” $^{41}$ Exchanges that cannot adapt are likely to lose order flow, and ultimately to lose listings. There is danger in unilateral, or inappropriate, U.S. regulation: Transnational trading has made the notion of restrictive national rules and regulations a mirage [20]. “We cannot have effective circuit breakers if markets can move offshore to a free-for-all environment. We cannot control program trading if London or Tokyo encourages it” [23, p. 3]. And London is gratefully getting ready to accept order flow in U.S. equities and derivative instruments, if inappropriate or excessively restrictive new regulations are enacted in the United States. $^{42}$
