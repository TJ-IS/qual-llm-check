# In Search of Sustainability: Reaping Long-term Advantage from Investments in Information Technology

DAVID F. FEENY and BLAKE IVES

DAVID FEENY is a Fellow in Information Management at Templeton College, Oxford, and a member of the Oxford Institute of Information Management, which has pioneered research and teaching in the subject within Britain. His own research and writing has centered on the use of IT for strategic advantage, and on organizational arrangements that encourage effective exploitation of IT. He holds an M.A. from Oxford University and an M.B.A. from Harvard. Prior to joining Templeton College he was a senior marketing manager with IBM.

BLAKE IVES is the Eugene J. and Ruth F. Constantin Distinguished Professor of MIS at the Edwin L. Cox School of Business at Southern Methodist University. He received his Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Minnesota in 1978. He has held appointments at Dartmouth College, Harvard Business School, and Templeton College, Oxford. He is on the editorial board of Information Systems Research. His research interests include information for competitive advantage, information in support of customer service, global information technology, and the senior manager's perspective on information systems.

ABSTRACT: We have raised executive management's awareness, and expectations of the strategic role of information systems technology. Teams of managers are commonly involved in workshops or planning processes designed to identify such applications. Conceptual frameworks to assist in such “idea-generation” sessions are widely cited in the literature and used in practice. Less well understood is the process used to evaluate the sustainability of proposed applications. We present a framework for evaluating sustainability based on the competitor's anticipated response time, differences among competitors, and the potential of the application to preempt competitive responses. Such an analysis is proposed as an evaluation tool for intended strategic applications of information technology.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: Information systems for competitive advantage, sustaining competitive advantage, strategic IT applications.

## 1. Introduction

SINCE 1983 A HOST OF ARTICLES [4, 31, 33, 36], BOOKS [24, 40, 47], AND SEMINARS have championed a new competitive weapon—Information Technology (IT). Although the IT for strategic advantage idea appears now to be well accepted, there is less understanding of how such applications are identified. Within this paper we present a model for accessing the sustainability of competitive advantage applications of IT.

Within the remainder of this section we briefly review the literature focused on IT for competitive advantage and then consider work that has looked at the sustainability of IT. Section 2 describes a model for examining the sustainability of a competitive advantage application of IT. In Section 3 we illustrate the model using a case example. The paper ends with our concluding remarks.

## 1.1. Background: IT for Competitive Advantage

The IT for competitive advantage message has been conveyed to researcher and practitioner in many ways. The message has included descriptions of how a small number of companies successfully deployed their IT weapons for strategic advantage $[13, 21, 26, 39, 42]$ . Perhaps most compelling to the practicing manager are tales of the firms such as Frontier Airline $[41]$ and People Express $[5]$ , that have been beaten down by information technology. The message has included frameworks, such as those proposed by Ives and Learmonth $[18]$ , Parsons $[33]$ , Porter and Millar $[36]$ , and Wiseman $[47]$ , intended to help managers identify applications that can bring competitive advantage to their own business. Rackoff et al. $[37]$ describe the use of such a framework within an organization, while Vitale, Ives, and Beath $[44]$ describe alternative approaches to identify strategic applications. Some investigators have looked at specific types of strategic applications, including customer service $[25]$ , after-sale service $[20]$ , and strategic pricing opportunities $[2]$ . Others have looked at differences across industry. McFarlan et al. $[30]$ , for instance, have suggested via their strategic grid model that IT will have varying applicability across industries, a notion that has been verified empirically by others $[23]$ . Copeland and McKenney $[15]$ have traced the history of competitive advantage applications of IT within the airline industry.

Others have considered the role of telecommunications [9] or database [28] infrastructure in contributing to strategic applications. Cash and Konsynski [7] following on the work of Barrett [1], have drawn attention to the strategic possibilities of inter-organizational systems, and both Clemons and Kimbrough [11] and Malone et al. [29] have proposed using Williamson's [46] theory of transaction costs to further investigate the economic underpinnings of such systems. The realities of interorganizational systems are empirically explored by Runge [38]. Runge also demonstrated the crucial role often played by a project champion in identifying and implementing strategic applications, a finding that has been reconfirmed by others [27, 45]. Methods for fostering the involvement and cooperation of such champions have been explored in Beath and Ives [3].

## 1.2. Background: Sustaining the Advantage

A review of the academic literature and the last five years' business press confirms that the concept of IT as a strategic resource now appears to be widely recognized. But much less well accepted and understood is how IT can be a source of sustainable competitive advantage, providing pioneering companies with rewards substantial enough to justify the costs and risks associated with being the prime mover. After all, the technology is increasingly within the grasp of even the smallest follower company, either directly or through well-developed IT service supply segments. Management requires clearer insights into the relative merits of pioneering versus following with particular IT initiatives. Prime movers face the traditional risks associated with innovation, and the changing economics of IT also guarantee the later entrant a lower-cost equipment platform.

Vitale [43], for example, warns that some supposed strategic applications of IT may provide limited advantages to the innovator before being readily copied by competitors. The result, he worries, may often be “an extension of the current competitive situation at an increased level of cost” [43, p. 333].

There has been little attention in the literature to the sustainability of strategic IT applications. A notable exception is the work of Clemons and his associates at Wharton [10, 11, 12, 14]. Clemons [10] notes the importance of imposing switching costs on customers to ensure sustainability. Clemons and Row [12] propose that applications taking advantage of “unique structural characteristics of the innovating firm” will be more likely to contribute to sustainability. Customer adoption rate has also been proposed [12] as critical to sustainability for systems that establish customer switching costs. Recognizing that a particular application may be necessary but not sustainable, Clemons and Knez [14] have described several approaches for evaluating whether a given application should be developed independently or as part of consortium of competitors.

## 2. A Framework for Determining Sustainability

MUCH OF THE PREVIOUS WORK HAS FOCUSED ON FRAMEWORKS to support “idea generation,” but “idea filtration” may be a considerably richer target of investigation. There is an extensive cost–benefit literature and a smaller risk analysis literature, but there is no comprehensive analytical tool for assessing the strategic implications of IT-based competitive edge projects.

Drawing on our own experience, some theoretical underpinnings, and the work of Clemons and others, we have sought to provide a useful conceptual model. We propose the pillared framework depicted in Figure 1 as a means to assess the sustainability of IT-based competitive advantage. The first and most obvious support pillar involves analysis of the project development cycle to understand what we term the “generic lead time” faced by a follower company.

The second pillar identifies the extent to which the project is protected through “competitive asymmetry.” This analysis is based on the theory of competition popularized by Chamberlin [8], and is used to assess what handicaps, if any, each competitor will face in attempting to respond to the pioneer. Finally we turn to supply chain analysis and the ideas of McMillan [32] to consider the “preemption potential” of the proposed application. The three pillars suggest the questions:

1. How long before a competitor can respond to our idea?

2. Which competitors can respond?

3. Will a response be effective?

In the following three sections we examine each of these questions.

## 2.1. Project Life Cycle Analysis: How Long Before a Response?

A “sustainability analysis” for an IT application can produce a much different profile than a risk analysis. An expensive system that takes two years to build may be high risk indeed, particularly if the payoff is dubious. But a high-risk system is unlikely to be copied by a competitor unless the motivation is compelling. And, if the application is a large one, there may be considerable delay for the build itself. As Merrill Lynch demonstrated with their cash management account, a large high-risk application that bears fruit can provide a nicely biased playing field for pleasant years of increased profitability or market share growth $[47]$ .

The time line in Figure 2 depicts the components of generic lead time—the time it would take the competitor to duplicate approximately the application, given similar starting points. The prime mover must go through some preliminary stages before the application can be built. A planning process, customer request, emerging vision, or similar motivator leads into a project approval and resource allocation process. Interestingly, we now understand that applications with strategic or competitive implications often manage to sidestep traditional project approval processes [27, 38, 45]. But, whatever the process, the resources are allocated and the application build initiated. Depending on the nature of the application, the build may be a lengthy and complex process—particularly if new hardware—software infrastructure is required or if implementation requires installing systems in unfamiliar environments. Eventually, the application will be launched and market advantages begin to accrue. As competitors respond, system enhancements provide an opportunity to keep the originator's application out in front.

![](/api/attachments/72U8QWV9/fulltext/images/fb6449e8870d94f83b457e9e2d991fbae1bbb86a63826294b4afc1119c9a518a.jpg)  
Figure 2. The Components of Generic Lead Time: How Long to Respond

Lead time is the amount of time from project launch until a competitor or competitors have a substantive response in place. Perhaps all that is necessary is to modify a few parameters within an existing software application or the installation of an off-theshelf package. Or the competitor may be able to give the customer (or supplier) access to an existing internal application. Then the response may be nearly immediate and lead time nonexistent. But lead time is usually far longer. The actual length of time will be dependent on the variables discussed below.

## 2.1.1. The Awakening

How does a follower respond to the initiative of a prime mover? Followers require no vision or planning process. Instead, they become aware of the change in the playing field and aroused as they begin to recognize the costs associated with its unfavorable tilt. But they still must ascertain what is causing the hemorrhage in revenues, profits, goodwill, et cetera. And then someone must be designated to repair the damage. Each stage can be problematic. Each can contribute to the innovator's lead time.

The first hint of a competitive initiative can come quickly. A new employee reports on a systems project at her old firm, newspaper advertisements suggest that company X is looking for people with experience in a leading-edge technology, or the field salesforce may bring back rumors from the field. If the initiative is not picked up prior to project launch, it will probably be spotted when the application goes live, particularly if it is a basis of differentiation that will need to be sold to the customer. But, if the application is focused on an internal process, such as a database for deriving market intelligence, an expert system for authorizing bank loans, or an executive information system that permits worldwide customer analysis, it may take considerably longer to realize that something unusual has happened.

However, even when the competitive initiative has been recognized, the follower may not respond until sales have fallen off, profits declined, or customers have clamored for comparable capabilities. This seems particularly likely if the prime mover is a small player or a new entrant rather than a feared major competitor.

There may be still further delay as the follower organization decides who is responsible for responding. One solution, turning the problem over to the information systems group, may result in a poorly crafted or inappropriate approach that inadequately addresses the range of potential business responses. Feeny [16] describes how competitive response to a pioneer in the UK Travel Industry was misdirected for three years.

Once the problem is recognized and someone assigned to lead the defense, there can be problems deciding how to respond. There may be issues of proprietary technology, unavailable data, or an unknown solution. For example, Fidelity Investments was the first mutual fund to introduce hourly pricing to the marketplace. Prior to Fidelity's innovation, mutual funds had been priced once a day. Once Fidelity announced this new capability, it became obvious to the followers what the customer implications were. It took somewhat longer to determine the extent of the damage attributable to the uneven playing field. But even as competitors began to see market damage, it was not a simple matter to understand the rather complex mechanisms required to implement hourly mutual fund pricing.

## 2.1.2. Winning Approval

Now funds will need to be allocated to mount a defense. If the follower believes it is being hurt, the monies will be quickly allocated, even for a project that had previously been sidelined. For instance, the IT manager of a UK firm some years ago proposed to senior management that the firm introduce point-of-sale (POS) terminals in their retail outlets. The suggestion died an inglorious death during an early round of that year's application portfolio deliberations, and met the same fate the following year, and again the year after that. But when the London Times published an account of a major competitor's intention to roll out POS terminals during the next twelve months, the attitude of the follower's senior management team changed dramatically. The original proposal was quickly revitalized, enthusiastically endorsed, and generously funded.

In another industry, a prime mover's management team, understanding their competitor's conservative attitude about IT, went ahead with an application that they knew could be easily imitated. Past experience suggested that their competitors would not respond until the new application was demonstrated to be a commercial success in the marketplace. The resulting lead time, which proved to be over two years, guaranteed them significant gains in market share before the competitive response was put in place.

## 2.1.3. Building the System

The system build may present an opportunity for the follower to make up lost time. This is particularly true if the application depends to a large extent on off-the-shelf packaged software or expertise readily brought in from the outside (perhaps from previous employees, vendors, or former consultants of the prime mover). The follower has opportunities that were not available to the prime mover. The follower begins from a well-understood starting point—the original application developed by the prime mover. The follower can also consider whether joining a syndicate of other competitors might be an appropriate way to meet the prime mover's initiative (thus decreasing the costs but probably increasing the lead time).

The prime mover will be in a stronger position at this stage if no shortcuts exist for the follower. This is more likely to be the case if the application is built around an existing data or knowledge base.

## 2.1.4. Project Launch

Project launch may again offer time advantages to the follower, because the system's intended users will now understand the value of the application. If the users are customers, distributors, or suppliers, they are likely to be aware of the application's potential due to the successful efforts of the prime mover. If the users are internal, competitive pressures will have likely paved the way for the new system. For instance, if the expert system for approving loans has increased the quality of the prime mover's loan portfolio, then the follower will eventually see that they are losing the best risks.

## 2.2. Competitor Analysis: Who Can Respond?

Projects that rely on generic lead time for advantage are at risk. Word may leak out sooner than expected or a competitor may find a shortcut solution. Delays in the initial build or problems during implementation may give the competition an opportunity to field a timely response. Worse, the competitive response may leapfrog the original initiative, particularly if they have greater resources [43]. Sustainability is much more likely either where the prime mover earns a decided advantage by going first or where competitive asymmetry makes it difficult or impossible for a competitor to respond. In this section we discuss competitive asymmetry, and focus in the following section on ways the prime mover can preempt subsequent challenges.

Chamberlin [8] used the term “monopolistic competition” to describe industry rivalry between businesses that possess significant unique features within their resource and asset bases. He demonstrated that monopolistic competition was associated with the achievement by some industry participants of superior financial returns for sustained periods. The implication is that heterogeneity is a potential source of competitive advantage, and companies should seek to base their strategies around such distinctive capabilities. Thus, a new application that leverages dissimilarities between the prime mover and likely followers makes it difficult or impossible for followers to mount an effective response. In such a situation the follower is required not only to build the new application, but to also address the fundamental dissimilarity that the system relies on for success.

We have experienced three categories of such differences that can be usefully exploited with information technology. The first focuses on the firm's scope of operations, the second on issues revolving around the firm's chosen organizational structure and culture, and the last on the firm's existing information base. Each is described below and depicted in Figure 3.

## 2.2.1. Competitive Scope

Michael Porter claims that competitive scope can have "a powerful effect on competitive advantage" [35, p. 55]. In essence, scope is the range of markets, locations, channels, and products within which the firm chooses to compete. Porter describes four types of scope:

Geographical Scope refers to the geographical area that a firm chooses to compete in or the places that it chooses to locate particular types of work.

Segment Scope refers to the breadth of products sold and the buyers served.

Vertical Scope is the extent to which the firm has elected to pursue backward or forward integration strategies.

Industry Scope is “the range of related industries in which the firm competes with a coordinated strategy” [35, p. 54].

Differences in scope can provide powerful starting points for leveraging IT. Merrill Corporation, a financial printer, has located its production operations in a headquarters facility in St. Paul, Minnesota. Their primary competitors historically have located both production and sales facilities in local offices in cities across the United States. A communications network ties Merrill's sales offices to St. Paul, where inexpensive and well-educated labor is easily obtainable. Merrill, unlike most competitors, farms out much of its printing to other printers [34]. Merrill's competitors, saddled with much higher costs for labor, equipment, and rent, have invested heavily in facilities in their present offices and face huge cost penalties if they elect to redo their current infrastructure. Tellingly, in the downturn in the financial printing business that followed the stock market crash of October 1987, Merrill fared well due to its low fixed costs [22].

Merrill has taken what was once a geographical advantage of its competitors—downtown locations near their prestigious clients—and used IT to turn a disadvantage into an advantage. Similarly, firms with a worldwide presence and corporate network often have opportunities to move workloads and expertise across time zones, giving them an advantage over firms located in a single country. World-class negotiating experts in Boston, for instance, can be preparing tomorrow's negotiating ploys, while the sales staff wine and dine the hapless customer in Paris.

Large multinationals can use worldwide electronic networks to take advantage of their geographical scope, but special familiarity with a local market also can provide unique opportunities, as RHP Ltd. has demonstrated. Initially RHP, a UK manufacturer of ball bearings, found themselves at a disadvantage when a multinational competitor established electronic linkages to the offices of major customers. RHP responded with a system specially tailored for their own UK-based customers. The system took advantage of technology readily available in the UK that was not available worldwide. RHP's offering quickly won them back lost market share. Moreover, the multinational competitor, facing the constraints of developing a common system for a geographically dispersed marketplace, had little incentive and less latitude to respond properly.

Segment scope and product breadth can also provide exploitable opportunities. Thomson Holidays, the largest participant in the UK holiday industry, was not the industry leader in providing direct links to the offices of travel agents. But, unlike the smaller competitor who initiated the concept, Thomson offered a wide enough assortment of tours and constituted a big enough share of the travel agent's business so that the agents quickly accepted the new system. Thomson's market position was so great that they could downgrade service on manned phone lines, thus forcing the travel agents to use the system and further improving Thomson's cost position.

The now defunct airline, People Express, was victimized by information technology that took advantage of the airline's narrow product scope. People Express offered one price for every seat, while higher-cost competitors such as American Airlines used information technology to fine tune the prices for a much broader set of products. The sophisticated yield management software of the major carriers turned People's low-cost advantage into a fatal flaw; Donald Burr, founder of People Express, described the role of IT in the demise of his airline: "The reason People Express didn't roll on . . . had everything to do with information technology. . . . The goal [of the competition] was to sell every seat at every price point" [5, pp. 62–63].

As Clemons and Row [12] have noted, variations in vertical integration can sometimes be exploited. Otis Elevator, a firm that competes within several elements of the overall supply system, had such an opportunity. Otis manufactures and services elevators. In the former business they have few competitors, but in the latter they have hundreds—including many set up by former Otis employees. Otis recently began embedding a remote diagnostic computer within the elevator that was connected via the in-car phone to an Otis repair facility. The closed-loop system establishes an entry barrier for the independent service operators. Going in the other direction, the Otisline service database provides information on elevator reliability that will help Otis to design and build better elevators in the future [39].

When a firm competes in numerous related industries, advantages in industry scope may provide opportunities to exploit information technology. For example, a unit of a larger industrial company equips its sales force with portable computers containing marketing information on products from across other units of the business; now the sales person calling on a customer to sell one product can intelligently talk about others. The sales force becomes considerably more useful to the customer, perhaps differentiating what had previously been a product bought strictly on the basis of price and availability.

## 2.2.2. Organizational Base

Competitive scope takes an outward look at the firm, focusing on its products, target markets, and degree of integration. Looking within the organization, however, can lead to other unique attributes that can be leveraged. Differences in organizational structure, culture, or asset base all provide exploitable opportunities.

One of the most powerful competitors damaged by American Hospital Supply's ASAP order entry system was Johnson & Johnson, a manufacturer and distributor of hospital supply products [42]. Unlike AHS, J&J had for many years maintained a very decentralized organizational structure. This meant, among other things, that each J&J business unit had its own outgoing logistics operations. Responding to the AHS order entry system was therefore a difficult challenge. After considerable heart searching,

J&J set up a hospital services company in 1982 to centralize its order-taking and distribution functions across the product companies. A decade after the AHS initiative, the J&J response is still perceived as a tentative one by industry observers.

The firm's existing asset base can similarly constrain the range of responses available to the follower. Mercury Communications currently enjoys such an advantage over its far bigger rival, British Telecom. Mercury is a relative newcomer in the recently deregulated UK telecommunications industry. They have differentiated themselves from British Telecom by providing call-detail reporting on customer bills. British Telecom, whose switching equipment was installed years ago, is unable to collect the required information and must still charge by metered time, with customers receiving a lump-sum bill every quarter.

## 2.2.3. Information Resources

Competitive scope and organizational base provide likely sources of exploitable competitive asymmetry. The firm's existing information resources provide another. The existing technological infrastructure, applications inventory, data resources, and knowledge base all provide potential opportunities.

Recurring investments in distinct technological infrastructures can produce significant and exploitable differences among firms. In their history of the airline industry, for instance, Copeland and McKenney found that, "the multiprocessor systems that today belong to American and United, as well as TWA, Delta and Eastern/Texas Air, make them different in kind, not just degree, from the uniprocessor systems common to the rest of the airline industry" [15, p. 367]. Such differences permitted these large carriers to implement sophisticated yield management software, frequent flyer programs, and the sale of reservations services to those very same competitors. Copeland and McKenney conclude that, "technical competence is a necessary requirement for gaining competitive advantage from information technology" [15, p. 368].

But adherence to a technological architecture can also present a potential Achilles heal to competitors. For instance, for years prominent information technology consultants strongly suggested that firms settle on an IBM-based architecture and thereby simplify life. A company that ignored that seemingly sound advice, perhaps by choosing instead to go with the Unix operating system or Apple microcomputers, now finds itself with a computer architecture far different from its competitors. If the uniqueness of these architectures can be translated into a business advantage, it may be difficult and extremely expensive for a competitor, deeply invested in an alternative technology, to mount an effective challenge.

Uniqueness in the application's inventory can be another source of potential advantage as Commercial Union, a UK insurance company, recently demonstrated. When new regulations required UK life insurance brokers to choose between complete independence or alliance with a single underwriter, those insurance companies with an advanced IT applications base prospered at the expense of their rivals. Many companies were still running on batch-based centralized systems. But Commercial Union was able to offer a range of customer-service applications such as online policy inquiry and the downloading of customer information to the broker's office—because these facilities were extensions of the facilities already developed for their own branch offices. Such capabilities helped Commercial Union to sign up a big UK bank and 500 smaller firms as dedicated agents, giving them thousands of new salesmen for their products.

Existing databases present another rich source of leveragable uniqueness. The International Trucks division of Navistar, for instance, had for some years been collecting information from existing customers so as to give their engineers some understanding of how the trucks were holding up in use. Eventually, it was discovered that this database could be used to provide analytic tools of use to the fleet owners that Navistar often sold to. Personal computer-based models were developed that assisted fleet owners in determining appropriate staffing levels for maintenance facilities or investments in spare parts inventories. Competitive response was slowed by the lack of a comparable historical database upon which to base the models $[20]$ .

A similar approach was taken by Hartford Steam Boiler Insurance Company, which took a database of information on boiler operations, originally developed to assist their underwriters, and made it available to their customers' boiler room managers [17]. And, in the future, as expert systems become more commonplace and as more expertise is captured within their knowledge bases, we expect to see them presenting innovative firms with similarly exploitable opportunities.

## 2.3. Supply Chain Analysis: Will Copying Help?

Lead time and competitive asymmetry have been discussed as two contributing factors to sustainable advantage. “Generic lead time” tells us how long we have until competitors can put together the same application and “competitive asymmetry” tells us if they will be able to copy the application. The final question is whether, once the competition has the application in place, it will do any good. Here we are concerned with the potential of the prime mover to seriously preempt retaliation by the follower.

Some of the best-known exemplars of using IT for competitive advantage involve preemption—perhaps that accounts for their fame. The Merrill Lynch Cash Management Account (CMA) was targeted at the wealthy individuals of North America; competitors struggled to respond, and eventually brought out equivalent offerings [4]. However, Merrill Lynch retained its customer base; five years after the introduction of CMA, Merrill's nearest rival was reported to have only 10 percent the number of accounts as Merrill Lynch [47]. In 1986 Clemons [10] reported that Merrill's market share was still over 50 percent, due to the account stickiness of the CMA. Similarly, American Airlines and United Airlines between them signed up nearly 60 percent of U.S. travel agents for their systems, causing severe discomfort for airlines such as Frontier and People Express. These and other examples illustrate the three components of successful preemption, which we have labeled:

• finding the exploitable link,

• capturing the pole position,

\- keeping the gate closed.

## 2.3.1. Finding the Exploitable Link

Whereas Chamberlin [8] and the concept of competitive asymmetry focus our attention on the asset base of the business contrasted with that of competitors, McMillan [32] proposes that we search for preemption potential within the upstream and downstream elements of the supply chain. There we should look for a concentration point within the supply chain where resources are finite and where a limited number of participants control the link in the chain. Examples of such exploitable links could be restricted supply sources for a key input, concentrated distribution channels, or a limited number of customers within the target segment. In Merrill Lynch's case, the qualified customers represented the exploitable resource; in the airline industry it was first the travel agents (for the computer reservations systems) and then the customers (for the frequent flyer programs). For the John Hancock Insurance Company, described below, it might be the employee benefits managers within their corporate customer set. For paint manufacturer Benjamin Moore, it is the major outlets retailing paint to customers.

## 2.3.2. Capturing the Pole Position

Having identified such target groups within the supply chain, we must seek out IT applications that can propel the business into a unique and superior relationship with that group. IT will provide a superior relationship if, in return for the benefits provided by the application, the customer establishes the prime mover as a preferred business partner. This relationship must be unique in the sense that the customer will not want more than one such partner—there is only one pole position, denoting clear and unchallenged leadership. The leader may be displaced in time, but he will not be joined. Thus no customer wants or needs two cash management accounts—that would damage the whole service concept; he either has a Merrill Lynch account or transfers to a competitor. In providing the retailer with a service that mixes paint to the precise color of a customer's sample, Benjamin Moore achieved the same position, while increasing sales of paint to the stores using the system by 5 to 10 percent [6]. The paint retailer, having gained benefit from the provision of a new service, is unlikely to elect to incur the expense, space loss, and set-up time required to acquire a second and essentially redundant machine from a rival paint supplier.

In true preemption, the prime mover's continued leadership position is secure against any competitive response that is equivalent to his own offering. He must guard against leaving room for a follower to come in with an application that is clearly superior in the sense that it offers significant extra benefits to the user. Only then will the user be prepared to incur the switching costs, which are the subject of the next section.

## 2.3.3. Keeping the Gate Closed

From the earliest days of computing, it has been clear that users perceive there to be significant tangible and intangible costs incurred in moving from one system to another. As a result, COBOL programmers generally have resisted using the more powerful C or Ada programming languages, due to the investment they have made in COBOL. Programmers skilled in the use of mainframe computers similarly often resist developing systems for personal computers, and professionals and secretaries alike resist transferring from one word-processing package to another. Such switching costs represent the prime mover's initial base for sustaining advantage, the gate that bars the way of the follower. Clemons argues that, "most external or customer-focused systems have relied on customers' switching costs to defend initial gains in the marketplace" [10, p. 134].

The switching costs associated with today's IT applications can be considered under three headings. These focus on the application interface, the database, and the community of users. In the first case, even the simplest IT application requires its user to learn and remember an interface comprised of a sign-on procedure, password, command language, et cetera. Feeny [16] found in his investigations of the UK package holiday industry that travel agents who had grown accustomed to the Thompson Holidays' system were not prepared to learn new interfaces in order to use a follower's system in parallel. But when a competitor, Horizon Holidays, introduced a system that looked to the travel agent to be identical to that of the prime mover, Thompson's advantage began to erode.

Transfer of allegiance may also involve the user in loss or recreation of data. For instance, a U.S. travel agent transferring from American Airlines SABRE to United's APOLLO loses his sales history file; or a customer moving from Merrill Lynch's Cash Management Account has to transfer all the information that describes his probably complex financial status. Additional switching costs are incurred in having to learn the rival's system. In total, they decrease the likelihood of switching unless the follower offers substantial advantages over the prime mover, or unless the relationship between user and prime mover has seriously deteriorated.

A newly established “community” of users represents a third potential source of switching costs. For example, in the John Hancock system, the users are the employee benefits managers of different corporations. In addition to accessing John Hancock for insurance services, they use the system to communicate with each other via electronic mail on matters of common interest. If one user were to switch from John Hancock to a rival, he would be separated from the circle of his fellow professionals. Similarly, foreign exchange dealers would find it difficult to move from the Reuters monitor system and still have enough people to trade with. In cases like these, the prime movers’ systems have become part of the infrastructure of the supply chain. Individual defections to a follower system become most unlikely; only wholesale defection makes sense, and that will only be organized if the user community as a whole loses faith in the prime mover’s system. Note, however, that in fostering a community of users, such as John Hancock’s benefits managers, there is a new potential risk—as a community, they may eventually wield considerably more collective power than they did as a disassociated set of customers.

## 3. An Example: Wingtip Couriers

THE SUSTAINABILITY MODEL PROVIDES POWERFUL INSIGHTS REGARDING a proposed application or extensions to an existing system. The following case study illustrates the use of the sustainability model.

Wingtip Couriers is a Dallas-based provider of local courier service [21]. Using a fleet of small trucks, Wingtip's couriers provide local package delivery service to, primarily, professional firms around the Dallas–Fort Worth metro area. Each truck is equipped with a small computer terminal that communicates to a computer in headquarters via radio waves. Customers calling for package delivery reach a customer service representative, who enters the relevant details into the computer system. A dispatcher then assigns the call to a particular driver. The necessary information about the call is sent, over radio waves, to the terminal in the truck, where it may join other delivery requests that the driver has already been assigned. As the driver completes activities, such as delivering a package, s/he notifies the dispatcher by keying in appropriate codes on the computer system. The computerized system gives Wingtip a decided advantage in driver productivity over their competitors, while also providing a basis for monitoring their service to particular customers. Long-term contracts are written with customers that provide for penalties for each late delivery.

Below we use the sustainability model to assess the long-run potential of the computer system at Wingtip.

## 3.1. Generic Lead Time for Wingtip

Wingtip was a pioneer user of onboard computer technology. As a consequence, system development took longer and cost much more than they anticipated. Through a joint venture with a software developer, they developed the necessary applications. The lead time required to duplicate Wingtip's system was relatively short. The radio-controlled computer technology was available off the shelf by the time Wingtip's system was installed, and there were people familiar with the specific application who could be called upon to develop it. In fact, Wingtip and its partner put the software up for sale to interested parties. Although they probably would not be willing to sell to an immediate competitor, the software was, nonetheless, publicly available and its functionality readily examined. Wingtip's drivers, customers, and dispatchers were, of course, also intimately familiar with the functionality of the application, and presumably one might readily have been recruited to help in the specifications of a new system.

## 3.2. Supply Chain for Wingtip

One exploitable link in the supply chain for Wingtip was the limited number of prestigious professional services firms that are heavy users of courier services. These lawyers, architects, and accountants often pass the costs of the courier service on to their own customers, sometimes appending a handling fee. They are less cost sensitive than some segments of the courier service business, but expect reliable, efficient, and professional service.

To capture the pole position with these customers, Wingtip invested in new, readily identifiable vehicles, a trained and well-educated workforce, and the onboard computer system. The vehicles and trained couriers provided professionalism on the customer's premises. The computer system ensured a mechanism for tracking reliable service and professional processing of customer orders and requests for follow-up. It also provided the means to help customers pass on charges to their own clients.

Computer-to-computer communications and transfers of magnetic tapes permit Wing-tip to forward billing information for use by their customers' own billing software. From the customer's vantage point, there are obvious advantages to using only a single courier service if that courier provides such additional services.

It is less obvious how well Wingtip has succeeded in keeping the gate closed. Some of the services Wingtip initiated can be, and have been, readily copied by competitors. Some of the services provided by the computer system, such as billing, do not require onboard terminals. Others can be duplicated, though at some cost, by alternative means. Moreover, there are limited switching costs imposed on the buyer. A few bad deliveries in a row, and the customer may move to a competitor. Wingtip has tried to be as easy to use as possible, but so have their competitors. There is little required to shift to a new supplier—just a different phone number to call. Wingtip does store the many addresses often used by a particular customer to designate specific pickup and delivery points. But these stored addresses are primarily a convenience to Wingtip rather than the customer. They also represent a switching cost that may not be recognized by the customer until after the decision has been made to switch.

The secretaries and shipping clerks who actually place phone calls for Wingtip's services present another potentially exploitable link in the supply chain that may offer future potential interest to Wingtip or a competitor. On the other hand, it may be very difficult for any of the players in the industry to keep the gate closed on new technologies, such as facsimile, which potentially could have widespread negative consequences for the industry.

## 3.3. Sources of Asymmetry within the Courier Industry

An analysis of Wingtip relative to its local competitors is revealing. Most share Wingtip's geographical scope, though several only handle deliveries within downtown Dallas or Fort Worth. Many competitors, however, are much smaller in this fragmented marketplace. Wingtip's focus on professional firms places it within a particular segment of the marketplace, and thus provides opportunities for differentiation. Perhaps the most relevant difference concerns vertical scope. Unlike most of their major competitors in 1985, Wingtip owned its own vehicles and paid its drivers a wage. Competitors typically subcontracted out the actual delivery to independent contractors who often used the family car in making the deliveries.

As a consequence, Wingtip's organizational base differed markedly from its competitors. There are obvious differences in both culture and management approach between a firm that employs young drivers, trains them, and pays them a wage, versus a system that contracts with independent operators, often retired workers, and then pays them commission. The consequences of the differing asset base are also important to consider. Who would be willing to put a computer system costing several thousand dollars into someone else's automobile? For competitors to copy Wingtip's initiative requires them to give up their current mode of operation and to purchase vehicles. Only then could they install the computer systems and train drivers to use them.

Wingtip also had a head start in their use of information technology. This is not the same as introducing a new strategic application in an industry that is already a sophisticated user of information technology. Wingtip only employed a part-time programmer, but their competitors had none. While Wingtip's CEO was well versed in the possibilities of information technology, the heads of major competing firms appear to have been learning all they cared to about the strategic possibilities of information technology only by watching his progress.

Wingtip installed their computer system in December of 1984. By 1989 a major competitor had installed a similar system. The competitor first bought vehicles and then converted from independent to employee operators. A source was easily identified to provide the required technology. It is unclear, however, how much use the competitor is actually making of the new system, which would require substantial changes in internal operations.

## 4. Conclusions

MOST LARGE ORGANIZATIONS ARE NOW ALERT TO THE CONCEPT of using IT to achieve a competitive edge, particularly by providing new services to customers and distributors. But ideas in this area need careful analysis before investments can be made with any confidence. The framework we have proposed and illustrated allows management to consider the probability that an application, if successful, will provide advantage for long enough to justify the investment required.

The framework can also give insights into the actions that will lead to an increased chance of sustainable advantage, either from new applications or enhancements to old applications. For example, if an application is seen to be solely dependent on generic lead time, it will be important to ensure that it is developed in a secure environment so that competitive reaction is triggered as late as possible. A series of upgrades should be planned from the start so that leadership can be consistently renewed and follower companies disoriented. A firm might also attempt intentionally to lead competitors to believe that it is pursuing a far different approach. Competitive asymmetry provides other opportunities. We can be confident that the business as a whole invests in monitoring competition, but how many IS departments have invested in building a picture of the competitor's IS capability that can be the basis of understanding what initiatives the competitor can respond to? It may also be possible to find ways to use IT to take an existing structural disadvantage and turn it into an advantage. Finally, if the application represents an attempt at preemption, two questions are particularly important. Can the application scope be expanded to more completely meet the user's need, thereby denying followers the superior response that might displace the prime mover? And can switching costs be built up by adding database facilities, increasing functionality—and thereby the complexity of the interface—and/or establishing the type of community of users we have described?

Greater attention to the analysis of sustainability can, we believe, help to restore the credibility of IT as a competitive weapon at a time when many are wondering whether the concept is the most recent example of IT industry hype $[10]$ . And if the analysis shows that an application, despite offering attractive benefits, cannot be defended against the competition, management should forego the excitement (and cost!) of pioneering the idea and settle for a fast, cheap, and effective follower role.
