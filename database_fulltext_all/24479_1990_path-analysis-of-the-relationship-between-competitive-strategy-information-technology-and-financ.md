# Path Analysis of the Relationship between Competitive Strategy, Information Technology, and Financial Performance

STEVEN. W. FLOYD and BILL WOOLDRIDGE

STEVEN W. FLOYD is Assistant Professor teaching strategic management in the School of Management, University of Massachusetts at Amherst. His research focuses on the use of information technology in strategy and the strategic decision process. Other recent work appears in the Strategic Management Journal.

BILL WOOLDRIDGE is Assistant Professor teaching strategic management in the School of Management at the University of Massachusetts at Amherst. His research interests center on the strategy formation process and the use of information technology in strategy. He has published papers recently in the Strategic Management Journal.

ABSTRACT: This study evaluates the impact of competitive strategy on information technology (IT) and of IT on organizational performance. In addition, the research model incorporates indirect performance effects from strategy and IT alignment. Data is drawn from the survey responses of 127 banks and interviews with 68 bank CEOs. Path analysis separates direct, indirect, and spurious effects. Interview data support the quantitative analysis. The results lend statistical support to the strategy–IT relationship described in case studies, and in addition, provide an explanation for inconsistent findings in previous IT performance research.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: Information systems for competitive advantage, information technology and corporate performance, alignment of information technology and business strategy.

RESEARCH HAS NOT DEMONSTRATED A CONSISTENT RELATIONSHIP between investment in information technology (IT) and organizational performance [5, 13, 20, 38, 52]. In light of the strategy–IT literature [6, 9, 10, 11, 16, 24, 25, 26, 32, 34, 44], however, these inconclusive results may not be surprising. Several writers suggest that returns on IT investments depend on the system's strategic role [41, 47, 51, 54]. Perhaps significant performance effects should be expected only for IT investments that support strategy. If this is true, it would help to explain why the statistical results in IT investment research have been equivocal.

This paper presents a study that develops and tests a model of how strategy and IT jointly affect organizational performance. The purpose is not to make normative judgments regarding specific strategy–IT configurations, but rather, to test three propositions about the strategy–IT performance relationship.

In the first section of the paper, the theoretical basis for the model is reviewed and hypotheses proposed. Following this, a path diagram makes explicit the expected links among seven strategies, two types of IT, and profitability. Path analysis tests the significance of (1) strategy's direct effects on IT, (2) IT's direct effects on performance, and (3) any indirect performance effects of strategy–IT alignment, using data from 127 retail banks. Qualitative data from interviews with a subsample of 68 CEOs are explored to help validate the statistical results. Finally, conclusions and suggestions for future research are discussed.

## Theoretical Background

THE THEORETICAL BASIS FOR IT'S ROLE IN COMPETITIVE STRATEGY lies in the technology's effect on industrial organization [39, 40, 41]. IT applications have the potential to lower costs or create differentiation across a wide array of activities that constitute a firm's value chain. When applications create a differential advantage, they affect the structural characteristics of the industry and become important to successful strategy and organizational performance [23, 39]. Under such circumstances, firms will seek to align their IT resources to position themselves favorably relative to rivals, buyers, suppliers, substitutes, or potential entrants.

## IT for Strategic Necessity or Competitive Advantage

In some circumstances, applying IT to a value chain activity is essential, even where it provides no lasting competitive advantage. The technology becomes a strategic necessity “because failing to attend to it results in strategic disadvantage” [10, p. 101]. In this sense, IT becomes more threat than opportunity. The costs of adoption may yield little or no return, but the costs of not adopting are sufficiently high to justify the investment.

For instance, automatic teller machines (ATMs) are necessary to survive in retail banking. Most banks have ATMs with the same basic services, and these systems have significantly enhanced customer access to funds. An empirical study $[3]$ , however, recently concluded that ATM deployment among bank branches had little or no effect on realizing greater deposit collection. Apparently, ATMs are “strategic necessities” $[10]$ , but provide no real competitive advantage to any particular bank.

To provide competitive advantage [4, 11, 23, 25, 26, 53], the effect of the technology must be distinctive [39]. If rivals employ the IT with the same or similar effects, as with ATMs, then no competitive advantage exists. Moreover, for the advantage to be lasting, the application of IT in the value chain must be difficult to imitate [10]. Otherwise, any competitive advantage may be transitory.

The presence of inherent “first-mover” benefits $[31]$ provides the basis for the sustainability of an IT advantage. Inevitably, the technology itself can be imitated. But given sufficient time before followers catch up, a firm's leadership position may allow it to use the technology to unique advantage. In addition, innovators may achieve advantages that continue even after the technology is widely diffused [39]. For example, Merrill Lynch's early introduction of Cash Management Accounts apparently achieved a level of market visibility and an image unmatched by followers. The company continues to enjoy a dominant market share [10].

## Misalignment of IT and Strategy

While there are many examples of IT supporting strategy, the potential for misalignment between strategy and IT cannot be ignored. Misalignment results in IT effects on the value chain that are inconsistent with firm strategy. In such cases, the misaligned IT undermines strategy and decreases organizational performance.

For example, a large stationery company whose marketing strategy relies on differentiation recently implemented an automated order entry system. The system was designed and justified on the basis of reducing costs per order. The new technology succeeded in reducing costs, but sacrificed the personalized service that large, wholesale customers had come to expect. The system not only fails to create competitive advantage, it actually sabotages an otherwise successful strategy.

Warner [51] documented other cases where IT became a “competitive burden,” and Vitale et al. [50] noted examples of misperceptions of IT advantage in the airline industry. Warner’s example of a manufacturing firm spending millions to automate warehousing, and a year later, adopting just-in-time inventory management, makes an important point. What is appropriate strategy today may become inappropriate tomorrow, and technical advances provide a continuing source of change in IT. Thus, aligning strategy and IT occurs in a dynamic environment, and even organizations who plan carefully may find IT resources becoming misaligned.

Finally, it is important to note that strategic alignment may be irrelevant for some IT applications. Administrative systems, such as payroll, word processing, and the like, are frequently enhanced by new technology, but the changes may be only remotely relevant to strategy [10]. Even though the effect of such applications is usually positive for a specific function, the benefits may not translate into an enhanced “bottom line” [8, 47].

In sum, IT that is aligned with strategy, that provides sustainable competitive advantage, and/or that is required to avoid strategic disadvantage, enhances competitive position. IT's effects on organizational performance seem to range along a continuum from strongly positive in cases of competitive advantage, to modestly positive or neutral in cases of strategic necessity or non-strategic IT, to negative in cases of strategic misalignment.

## Hypotheses

It can be argued, therefore, that the inconsistent findings regarding the IT-performance relationship may reflect a more complex association that considers organizational strategy. The links among these three variables are suggested by theory [10, 39, 40, 41]: industry structure causes strategies to form; multiple strategies cause multiple forms of IT adoption; and these strategies and technologies jointly affect firm performance. For research in a single industry (where industry structure does not vary), this causal logic suggests the following hypotheses:

H1: Competitive strategy has direct effects on the adoption of information technology.

H2: Information technology adoption has direct effects on organizational performance.

H3: In addition to any direct effects, strategy also indirectly affects organizational performance through its effects on information technology adoption.

H1 would be expected to hold whether strategy's effect on IT results from management's intention to pursue competitive advantage or from strategic necessity. While it is conceivable to specify the particular form of strategy–IT relationships for certain technologies in a given industry, this goes beyond the purposes of the present study.

H2 is designed to test the relationship between IT and organizational performance. Like previous studies $[5, 13, 20, 38, 48]$ , the hypothesis suggests a direct association between level of IT investment and organizational performance.

H3 extends prior research by identifying a specific interrelationship among strategy, IT, and organizational performance. Essentially, it proposes that strategy's positive effects on performance can be enhanced by linking it with appropriate information technology. If, on the other hand, the technology is inappropriate to the strategy, the indirect effects may be negative, even when the direct effects of strategy are positive. Thus, the hypothesis allows for the effects of strategy–IT misalignment. In addition, if the strategy itself is inappropriate and direct effects on performance are negative, then indirect effects through IT could magnify, or even offset, strategy's direct effects. This hypothesis has not been examined in prior statistical research.

## Methodology

## A Path Diagram of Strategy, IT, and Performance

Path analysis was introduced in the 1930s [56] and has been used in biology, economics, and the social sciences [29, 37]. Recently, Woo [55] used the approach to test relationships between market share, business strategy, and risk. Venkatraman and Prescott [49] employ path analysis to test the stability of direct and spurious market-share effects on profitability.

Hypotheses are made more explicit through the construction of an arrow diagram depicting the expected causal sequence. Not only are relationships between the variables of interest specified, but also their relationship to other variables is made explicit [12]. In this study, the technique provides a holistic approach for evaluating H1, H2, and H3 in an integrative framework.

Path analysis is especially appropriate for investigating propositions about variables with complex direct and indirect effects such as those in H3 [27]. For example, since direct and indirect effects can assume opposite signs, they can cancel each other, yielding insignificant zero-order correlations. Path analysis, however, separates these effects and makes them explicit. Further, since path analysis separates indirect and spurious effects from regression coefficients, the technique provides more information than regression alone. Thus, the approach is useful in both the formulation and testing of hypotheses [2, 12, 27].

To construct a path diagram, the researcher begins with clear preconceptions about causality [12, 36]. The causal sequence (strategy–IT–performance) in this study is suggested in previous strategy–IT work [10, 25, 39, 41]. Moreover, the cause–effect relationships are grounded in the literature of strategic management, which emphasizes the effects of the environment, strategy, and organization structure on financial performance [30]. Operationally, these factors may be represented by industry variables, competitive strategies, value chain activities (including IT), and profitability.

Two factors serve to constrain the number of variables in the path diagram. First, since the sample is drawn from a single industry, environmental variables are controlled by the research design and can be omitted. Second, because the focus is on the strategic role of IT, value chain activities unrelated to IT are outside the study's scope. Thus, three sets of variables are included in the model; competitive strategy, information technology, and organizational performance.

Given this definition of the model's scope and the banking industry context, the initial path diagram shown in Figure 1 was created in four steps. First, measurement procedures based on Dess and Davis [14] produced seven dimensions of competitive strategy in banks $(CS_{1-7})$ . These dimensions are listed and described on Table 1. Second, a list of information technologies used in banking was created. For the sake of parsimony, the list was classified into product $(T_8)$ and process $(T_9)$ IT as shown in Table 2. Third, return on assets was chosen as a measure of organizational performance. (A more detailed discussion of the measures follows below.)

Finally, the expected relationships were mapped onto the variables. Figure 1 was drawn to allow for as many links among variables as interpretation of theory allowed but as constrained by the need for an identified model [27].

The resulting diagram shows strategies as exogenous variables affecting the level of IT adoption and ROA. Strategies are deemed to be exogenous because they are determined by industry structure, which is outside the scope of the model. Product and process IT along with ROA are endogenous variables that are explained partly by strategy, and in the case of ROA, IT variables. Both direct and indirect strategy–performance connections are shown. The paths drawn are in accord with the effects anticipated in the hypotheses.

As with all models, this one necessarily simplifies for the sake of parsimony. For example, it indicates a set of linear relationships with causal arrows going in only one direction. Feedback effects are not explicitly considered. While performance may affect future strategy and/or technology adoption, such effects are best modeled by taking time lags into account $[12]$ and are thus outside the scope of this research.

## Sample and Data Collection

Over a three-year period, the authors have surveyed and interviewed several hundred bank executives attending an annual, residential development program at a major university. Out of 419 bankers in the program, 130 (31 percent) agreed to participate and volunteered to complete the questionnaire. Responses were screened to ensure that the respondent held a key position in the bank and was knowledgeable concerning the bank's strategy, IT adoption, and performance. Almost all respondents expressed an interest in the results, suggesting their motivation to provide accurate data. A copy of the questionnaire is available from the authors.

In addition to the questionnaire, semistructured interviews were conducted with a subsample of 68 bank CEOs. Questions focused on the relationship between IT and bank strategy, the process involved in IT decision making, and the justification for IT investments.

For the most part, the sample is composed of relatively small retail banks located in seven Western and Midwestern states. In addition to consumers, commercial customers of these banks included small and medium-sized businesses. A statistical description of participating banks is shown in Table 3.

Beyond reducing the complexity of the model, sampling from a single industry was advisable for other reasons. First, the design controlled for industry context, which is a potential confound to the strategy–performance relationship $[19, 22]$ . Second, the measures of strategy and technology were developed specifically for the banking context. As a result, the questionnaire and interview could be written in terms easily recognized by practicing managers, and this approach helped to enhance the meaningfulness of responses.

## Measures

Competitive Strategies.- Seven dimensions of competitive strategy were incorporated into the path analysis. Six of these were measured by asking respondents to assess the relative importance of nineteen items adapted from Dess and Davis [14]. The large number of items required that they be condensed into a meaningful and manageable data set. This was accomplished through principal components factor analysis. This technique assumes that related variables describe a common underlying dimension. Factor analysis is widely used in management studies and has been used in previous banking studies [e.g., 46].

Table 4 presents details of this analysis. As the table shows, the 19 items reduced to six multi-item strategy factors. Each factor represents a unique strategic dimension, and its meaning is based on the items that make it up. (See Table 1 for the definitions assigned to them.)

The seventh dimension of competitive strategy—product breadth—represents the scope of product/service offerings relative to competitors [40]. To measure this variable, a list of thirteen specific bank products was developed based on discussions with a leading bank consultant who directed the executive development program. The list was “tailor made” in that it contained representative products from each major line of business offered by the sampled banks (retail consumer, commercial lending, real estate, and investment banking). Products where IT was inherent (e.g., treasury workstations) were omitted in order to avoid redundancy with the product IT measure. Since this did not necessarily reflect all products offered by the bank, the measure was intended to assess relative (rather than absolute) product breadth. Respondents were asked to indicate whether the product was (1) currently offered, (2) being considered, (3) rejected after consideration, or (4) prohibited by regulation. A scale calculated as the number of products currently offered constituted the product breadth measure. The specific products listed were NOW accounts, money market accounts, stock brokerage, investment banking, real estate mortgages, home equity credit lines, credit card issuance, commercial asset financing, consumer retirement accounts, relationship banking, electronic payroll deposits, home banking by phone, and drive-up banking.

Information Technology.- The specific technologies measured were chosen based on a review of the bank literature, e.g., [7, 45], and through discussions with several bank executives. The ITs were classified into product and process technologies (see Table 2). Respondents were asked to assess on a scale from one to six for each of the specific technologies whether the technology was: (1) not under consideration and not operational; (2) under consideration but not planning for implementation; (3) being planned for implementation; (4) operational in a pilot program; (5) being implemented on a widespread basis; or (6) operational on a widespread basis. These descriptions were derived from stage theories of IT adoption found in the literature [17, 33, 58] and were arrayed in order of increasing IT investment. An additive scale representing the adoption level for each of the IT types was created by summing the item scores according to the classification scheme in Table 2. As with product breadth, this was a relative measure of IT investment. $^{1}$

Performance.- Organizational performance is a multifaceted construct that defies measurement by a single number [42, 57]. The choice of return on assets (ROA) as the dependent variable for this study was based on two considerations. First, profitability is the most frequently used measure of firm performance in the strategic management literature [30]. Second, ROA and return on equity (ROE) are widely used measures of financial performance in banking [1]. Since ROA has been shown to be the best substitute for multiple performance measures [18], it was selected over ROE. Accordingly, respondents were asked to provide the average annual rate of return on assets over the last three-year period.

An examination of the residuals for each of the equations suggested that there were no serious violations of the requirements that the error terms (1) be independent, (2) of equal variance, and (3) have a mean of zero. Missing data reduced the number of cases to 127. Parameter estimates are provided by the standardized regression coefficients (beta weights).

Path analysis breaks down zero-order correlations between independent and dependent variables in the model into simple and compound paths. Simple paths represent direct effects (like the path from product IT to ROA) while compound paths represent effects through third variables. Compound effects from third variables that intervene in the causal sequence between independent and dependent variables are called indirect effects (like the path from product breadth, through product IT, to ROA). Compound effects from the mutual correlation between independent and dependent variables with factors not in the causal sequence are called spurious effects. For example, the correlation between product IT and ROA from the paths running “backward" through the seven strategy variables to ROA is spurious, because it results from compound paths outside the causal sequence. Thus, the total correlation between variables in the model can be recapitulated into direct, indirect, and spurious effects [2].

## Does Competitive Strategy Affect IT Adoption?

To test H1, estimates of the parameters for Equations 1 and 2 were obtained and are shown in Table 5. Adjusted R-square statistics for both equations are significant, providing support for the hypothesis that competitive strategies affect IT adoption.

The effects of strategy on IT are quite distinctive. Product breadth is the only significant predictor of product technology, but the effect is quite large. Similarly, segmentation proved most significant in process technology, and again, the effect is large. In both equations 1 and 2, a single strategy stands out. This is consistent with references in the theoretical literature to the context-specific character of the strategy–IT relationship. Consider an example outside the study. If the participants had represented commercial banks serving large corporations, treasury workstations would have been meaningful as part of the product technology measure. Thus, one would expect a significant correlation between segmentation and product IT, because IT was a means for achieving segmentation of the client base. Reasons for the distinct strategy–IT effects of the present sample are described in the interview results below.

## What Do We Learn from the Distinction between the Direct and Indirect Performance Effects of Strategy and IT?

To test H2 and H3, the effects of strategy and IT on ROA were decomposed into three categories: direct, indirect, and spurious. The estimates for direct effects are the path coefficients (beta weights) for the independent variables in Equation 3. Estimates for indirect effects are computed by tracing in the path diagram from competitive strategy $CS_{1}$ to $CS_{7}$ through the two types of technology $T_{8}$ and $T_{9}$ to ROA [2, 27]. (Note that product and process IT are specified in the model as having direct, but no indirect, effects on ROA.) Thus, for example, the indirect effects of $CS_{1}$ are computed according to this “tracing rule” [2] as: $(p_{r8} \times p_{81}) + (p_{r9} \times p_{91})$ (see Equations 1, 2, and 3 above). Finally, the correlation remaining after direct and indirect effects have been subtracted from the total zero-order correlation is classified as spurious. The results are shown in Table 6.

As the table shows, the direct effects of product IT are stronger than those of any other independent variable, and this provides support for H2. On the other hand, the effect of process IT is insignificant, and its sign is negative. Thus, direct performance effects were in evidence for product IT, but not process IT. Again, it is important to keep in mind that the relative magnitude of these specific IT-ROA effects is not generalizable beyond the population of smaller retail banks.

The magnitude of the spurious effects for both forms of IT indicates that, unless strategy variables are specified in the model, the direct impact of IT on ROA will be underestimated. In other words, the coefficients obtained in Equation 3 without the strategy variables would be biased. In both cases, the spurious effects are opposite to the direct effects, so that the zero-order correlation by itself would lead to an erroneous impression of the IT–ROA relationship.

Overall, the regression results provide moderate support for H1 and H2 and limited support for H3. Strategy appears to have direct effects on IT, and IT has direct effects on ROA. The path analysis also shows, however, that zero-order correlations between IT and ROA can be misleading. In addition, the analysis suggests that IT can play a meaningful role in the strategy–ROA relationship.

Given the methodology and the limited nature of the sample, however, caution must be exercised when interpreting the particulars. The magnitude of effects is not generalizable beyond the population of smaller retail banks. The analysis is intended to test statistically certain theoretical relationships, rather than provide normative guidelines for specific strategy–IT choices.

How Do the Quantitative Results Compare with Data from the CEO Interviews?

In the following discussion, qualitative data from the CEO interviews are used to help validate the quantitative findings. Attention centers on three linkages: product breadth and product IT, segmentation and process IT, and IT adoption with ROA.

Interviews suggested that the effects of product breadth on product IT were more the result of strategic necessity than the pursuit of competitive advantage in this sample. As one CEO commented, “ATMs were originally offered because the technology was available, not because the market demanded it. At this point, however, they’ve become a necessary part of doing business. The customer expects ATM access. We pass on network fees, but even then, they [ATMs] don’t pay off.”

Segmentation's effects on process technology can be seen in the adoption of decision support and online management reporting systems to analyze the needs of various customer groups. In addition, the adoption of online transactions processing and personal computers helps support processes that are important to specific target markets.

For example, to seek competitive advantage in the risky agricultural loan business, several bank CEOs reported investing in microcomputer software. These systems supported loan officers in projecting and monitoring financial statements and cash flows for agricultural borrowers. As one CEO put it: “the system gives both of us [the bank and the farmer] a better understanding of the business.”

The path estimates between technologies and performance are also interesting to compare with interview data. Since product IT was measured largely in terms of ATM adoption, its performance effect is inconsistent with the findings of Banker and Kauffman [3], and even contrary to the perceptions of many interviewees. But, if product IT is adopted out of strategic necessity (as the interviews suggested), not adopting may have powerful negative effects. Alternatively, perhaps high performing banks had the slack resources to adopt a technology with no real payoff.

The negative coefficient between process technology and ROA was not expected. Since the total correlation is trivial and the direct effect is insignificant, no definite interpretation can be made about process IT's effect on financial performance. The result does at least suggest the possibility, however, of negative effects due to strategy–IT misalignment in this sample.

System implementation difficulties are one explanation for misalignment. For example, a CEO described investing heavily in an online transactions processing system. It was designed in part to help loan officers control loan losses. After two years with the system, the bank found no appreciable increase in loan quality. Apparently, bank personnel had ignored “flags” generated by the system. As the CEO said, “The problem is not really the system, but the people that work with the system.” Other banks had had similar experiences. One CEO commented, “Processing time has not decreased as expected. Our people don’t have the time to learn what’s needed to take full advantage of the system.”

In sum, the interviews provided support for and insight into the statistical findings.

The relationships suggested by path coefficients seem to have counterparts in the bankers' experiences.

## Discussion and Conclusions

AS CONCLUSIONS ARE SUMMARIZED, THERE ARE THREE LIMITING characteristics of this research to keep in mind. First, the survey method does not provide as rich a database as might be desirable. However, the measures and statistical procedures are enriched by the theoretical support for the nature and direction of the relationships in Figure 1. In addition, item construction differed markedly between strategy and information technology variables, and performance was measured objectively. These measurement approaches help to dampen the potential for mono-method bias [12]. Finally, interviews of a subsample helped to validate results.

A second potential limitation involves the representativeness of the sample. Clearly, any inferences based on the results are restricted to relatively small retail banks. Moreover, since nonrandom sampling was employed, the sample may not represent the population of retail banks in all respects, although statistics in Table 3 do not suggest any systematic biases.

Finally, strategy-driven models of technology adoption are not without their detractors. Hayes [21], for example, suggests that managers develop technological resources first and then identify strategies to fit capability. Rockart and Scott Morton [43] use the Foremost McKesson case to show that “the ability to use IT to create new business strategies is potentially very important” (p. 94). In a similar vein, Vitale et al. [50], argue that the top-down approach to identifying strategic IT has many limitations, especially in turbulent environments, and that technical knowledge is crucial to strategy making. Admittedly, therefore, the present model is limited by the causal assumptions embedded in the theory that supports it. Further, the regression analysis is meant to assess the fit of the data with the theory, rather than to demonstrate causal directionality.

Despite these caveats, three conclusions seem to be warranted. First, the strategic context makes a significant difference in the correlations observed between IT adoption and ROA. Without strategy variables in the model, the correlations would have been underestimated. This finding might help explain why some studies have found no relationship [48] or a skewed relationship [13]. Additional research is needed to identify other factors that impinge on the IT-performance link.

Second, the effect of competitive strategy on IT so often embraced by theorists and described in case research is reflected in this study's statistical results. The observed correlations seemed to be industry specific and consistent with what CEOs said in interviews about the role of IT in their banks. Certain effects that might have been expected were not observed, however—such as the effect of cost reduction strategy on process IT. Future research could explore statistical strategy–IT associations in other contexts with the aim of confirming (or disconfirming) prevailing industry beliefs.

Third, successful strategy–IT alignment in some cases seems to be more complex than choosing a technology to enhance the effects from good strategy. Evidence for alignment of this sort would have been cumulative positive correlations for both direct and indirect effects. Instead, the analysis found small and negative direct effects on ROA combined with larger and positive indirect effects through IT. Without product IT, product breadth strategy impacts ROA negatively. This suggests that adoption of IT may sometimes be a necessary condition to successful strategy.

Alternatively, the combination of direct negative and indirect positive effects can be seen as the “shaping” impact that IT has on strategy $[53]$ . This interpretation is inconsistent with the results here, however, since the combined ROA effects would be negative if IT were modeled as the cause of strategy. In other words, reversing the causal arrow between IT and strategy leaves their combination with negative ROA effects. Perhaps, certain strategies and technologies are so conceptually and operationally interconnected that cause–effect analysis is impossible $[43]$ .

Many questions remain concerning the conceptualization and analysis of strategy–IT relationships. What alternatives are there to the model of strategy's indirect effects through IT (H3)? How else can the concepts of “support” and “alignment” be translated into researchable hypotheses? Would a technology-driven model find support in a statistical study?

For bankers and other practitioners, the study reinforces the importance of new product technologies. ATMs are not unique in their status as strategic necessities. Many manufacturers, for example, face demands from customers for service enhancements via electronic data interchange (EDI). The conventional wisdom often holds that such technologies do more for the customer's bottom line than for the adopter's. That may be true. In this study, however, the adoption of product IT does make a difference to profitability. Thus, even though ATMs are strategic necessities, not adopting them appears to create a competitive disadvantage.

Few topics are of such strong practical relevance as the strategic uses of IT. Validated, normative theory in the area would be invaluable. This study is an initial step toward validation of the concepts found in the rich descriptions of case studies. It is hoped that the results will stimulate others to reformulate questions, test alternative views, and move the field closer to a theory of IT strategy.
