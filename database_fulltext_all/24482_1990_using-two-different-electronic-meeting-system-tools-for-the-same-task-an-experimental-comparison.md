# Using Two Different Electronic Meeting System Tools for the Same Task: An Experimental Comparison

GEORGE K. EASTON, JOEY F. GEORGE,
JAY F. NUNAMAKER, JR., and MARK O. PENDERGAST

He received his master's degree from the American Graduate School of International Management in 1978 and his Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Arizona in 1988. His research interests include Electronic Meeting Systems (EMS) and Computer-Aided Software Engineering (CASE).

His current research investigates the effects of electronic meeting systems (EMS) use on the group work process and its outcomes, EMS use in non-American cultures, the effects of computing on decision authority in organizations, and the effects of extensive computerization in work groups.

He received his Ph.D. from Case Institute of Technology in systems engineering and operations research. He is Chairman of the Association for Computing Machinery Curriculum Committee on Information Systems.

His research interests include data communications, local area networks, and electronic meeting systems.

ABSTRACT: Various aspects of the design and use of Electronic Meeting Systems (EMS) have been investigated in laboratory and field studies, but until now no one has systematically investigated the role of EMS software on group performance. The current study compares two different EMS software tools in a controlled experiment.

Dependent variables are decision quality, number of unique alternatives generated, satisfaction, and consensus. The study found that one software tool produced better quality solutions to a combination creativity and intellective task, but the other helped generate more unique alternatives. Each tool worked best on the task for which it was designed. The findings support the authors' premise that there should be a match between the EMS software tool and the task to be performed. The findings have several implications for the design of EMS software.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: Electronic meeting systems, group decision support systems, software tools, group performance.

## 1. Introduction

ELECTRONIC MEETING SYSTEMS (EMS) ARE NO LONGER A NEW TECHNOLOGY. The first decision room, built at Execucom, was already operating in 1980 [14]. Many additional systems have been developed and installed through the decade, in both industry and academia. Currently, there are several major EMS operating throughout the U.S., and the body of literature on EMS is growing rapidly, with much work being done in both laboratory settings and in the field.

Much of the research to date has concentrated on particular aspects of McGrath's conceptual model of group interaction [18]. Many studies have compared groups using EMS to free interacting groups without EMS support [2, 11, 13, 31, 33]. Others have varied group size [28, 30, 32]. Still others have manipulated task difficulty [11], investigated the role of facilitation [8], or studied the role of group member dispersion [3, 12, 16, 27]. Most of this research, however, has taken the EMS technology as a given. No one has investigated the role of individual software tools used to work on a particular problem.

Yet the software may be an extremely important factor in the EMS equation. For example, a particular software tool may have been designed for a particular type of task (e.g., intellective vs. creativity [18]), but in the course of EMS use, such a tool may well be used for another type of task, much in the same way some people use spreadsheet software for word processing. The tool may perform reasonably well, but one would assume the tool would perform best on the task for which it was designed. We thought that this same type of mismatch between a software tool and the task for which it was used could help explain our findings from a previous experiment [13]. We had found no differences in the quality of the solutions reached by EMS-supported and non-EMS-supported groups, even though the literature supports the expectation that the performance of EMS groups should be superior. One post hoc explanation for our findings was that the software used was not a good match for our experimental task. We conducted the current study to test our explanation.

The next section documents relevant findings from the EMS literature and deals with our previous experiment in some detail, describing the experiment and the software used. This is followed by a discussion of a research model, the variables of interest in the current study, our hypotheses, and a description of the study. The last sections of the paper are concerned with our findings, their meaning and implications for EMS design, and our conclusions. We found that the software tool used does indeed make a difference in group meeting outcomes, at least for one type of task.

## 2. Groups and Performance

THE GENERAL CONCLUSIONS FROM THE VAST LITERATURE ON SMALL GROUPS and performance are that group performance depends on the task [18]. For some tasks, such as decision making, groups sometimes do better than individuals. For other tasks, such as idea generation, groups do not perform as well as their best individual member. The synergistic effects hoped for in groups do not always occur because of many problems that arise in the group setting. These problems include dominance of the meeting by one individual, member influence based on member status rather than the quality of the contribution, intolerance of unpopular views, the inability to access information not physically present in the meeting room, and a relative overemphasis on social rather than task-oriented activities [7]. These problems often get in the way of optimal group performance, resulting in process losses [25].

EMS have been designed to support group meetings and to help overcome the problems inherent in them. In general, past EMS studies found that groups using EMS outperformed groups not using EMS $[11, 15, 17, 24, 32]$ . (It is not clear, however, if EMS use allows the group to outperform its best individual member.) In addition to the ability of EMS to deal with the problems listed above, several other reasons have been given for this difference in performance. For example, Gallupe et al. $[11]$ wrote that EMS use helped groups consider issues and alternatives objectively, focused the discussion on the task, imposed an implicit agenda on the group, acted as a group memory, and in general, enabled the group to consider more alternatives in an in-depth manner. According to Jarvenpaa et al. $[15]$ , groups using an electronic bulletin board outperformed other groups because of the shared group focus the technology allowed. This focus may also have allowed the groups to scrutinize more closely and tune more finely their ideas. Groups that used workstations outperformed groups with no computer support, because their members participated more equally in the meeting and focused more on the task. Studies of EMS conducted in the field demonstrated increased efficiency on the part of groups that used them $[5, 29]$ . In one study, as a result of EMS use, users reported average savings of 55 percent of the time required to complete projects $[19]$ .

It would seem, then, that EMS use can lead to increased group performance. However, some experimental studies found that groups not using EMS perform equally as well as EMS groups $[2, 9, 13]$ . One possible explanation is that inconsistencies among experimental studies are due to the use of students as subjects. However, as DeSanctis $[6]$ and others pointed out, students are not necessarily a poor source of subjects, provided generic, salient tasks are used and the students have a long-term stake in their performance (e.g., through grading). Based on our knowledge of the experiments cited here, we do not believe using students was a problem. An alternative explanation is that the tasks used in some of the experiments were of insufficient complexity $[29]$ . The implication of such a view is that in experiments where EMS use

## 2.1. Motivation for the Current Study: A Previous Experiment

Our previous experiment was conducted in the winter of 1987/88 at the University of

Arizona. The research design was a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ factorial, with the three independent variables being communication condition (EMS support vs. no EMS support), anonymity (anonymous vs. non-anonymous interaction), and assigned leadership (assigned vs. not assigned). Since anonymous interaction without EMS support seemed artificial to the investigators, the two cells of the design that would have involved these conditions were not investigated, so there were only six cells (see [10, 13] for more details).

Group size was held constant at six, and there were five groups per cell, for a total of 180 subjects ( $6 \times 5 \times 6$ ). Subjects were students drawn from upper division MIS classes at Arizona. The experimental task was the “Parkway Drug Company” case, a combination idea generation (creativity) and intellective task. In this case, two salesmen are vying for the lucrative sales territory of a retiring salesman. Subjects must generate possible solutions to the case, and then decide on the one best solution. All possible solutions to the case can be evaluated in terms of sales commissions, hence, there is a best answer (the highest commissions possible), and all solutions can be ranked according to the amount of commissions each would produce.

Subjects were instructed to use a predetermined process for working on the case (Figure 1). The process was structured the same, whether the groups used EMS or not. Those using EMS utilized, in a specified order, three different tools from the University of Arizona GroupSystems' toolkit: the Electronic Brainstorming tool (EBS) to generate possible solutions, Issue Analyzer (IA) to list and consolidate them, and the Voting tool to try to agree on a single solution. If the group did not reach consensus, they were then allowed to use EBS again for further discussion, followed by another vote. This procedure could be followed again if necessary, but the group was stopped 30 minutes after their initial vote, whether they had reached consensus or not.

The experiment found no main effects for anonymity and assigned leadership, but it did find main effects for communication condition. Groups using EMS were less likely to reach consensus and took longer to reach a final decision than groups without EMS support. This was not surprising, given similar results in the literature $[23, 26]$ . The experiment also found that members of EMS-supported groups participated more equally in the group process, which was also not surprising, since it agreed with previous findings $[17, 20, 33]$ . Given the demonstrated ability of EMS to increase group performance, however, we were surprised to find no differences in decision quality between EMS and non-EMS groups. Post hoc reflection pointed us to a mismatch between the task and the software, which the current study was designed to investigate.

## 3. EMS Research Model

THE CURRENT STUDY WAS DESIGNED WITHIN THE FRAMEWORK of a model for the study of EMS developed by Dennis et al. [4] and reproduced below (Figure 2). It is an integration of many of the models used as a basis for EMS research. The model presents four classes of independent variables, each of which is hypothesized to affect group process and group outcomes directly. In addition, the independent variables are hypothesized as having an indirect effect on outcomes through group process, which mediates between the independent variables and outcome.

## 4. Hypotheses

OUR BASIC PREMISE HERE IS THAT A GOOD MATCH BETWEEN the software tool being used and the task being performed should lead to a high level of performance for the group. Therefore, we hypothesized that EDS use would lead to better-quality solutions than use of the three-tool sequence, because the electronic conversation provided by EDS would allow the group to explore more fully the issues related to any proposed solution. Such discussion would help them more readily see the strengths and weaknesses of solutions. It should also help them reach consensus. On the other hand, the three-tool sequence should help groups generate more unique alternative solutions, since that is what tools like EBS have been designed to do.

We expected groups using EDS to be more satisfied with the process than groups using the three-tool sequence, because the tools should provide them with the type of support they need, making them more satisfied than groups using mismatched tools. Having to bend the tools for the task in the three-tool sequence might lead to frustration and confusion, which would decrease the satisfaction group members felt with the process.

Therefore, our hypotheses for a combination creativity and intellective task, for small groups, are as follows:

Hypothesis 1: Groups using EDS will produce better quality solutions than groups using EBS, IA, and voting in sequence.

Hypothesis 2: Groups using EDS will generate fewer unique alternative solutions than group using EBS, IA, and voting in sequence.

Hypothesis 3: Groups using EDS will be more satisfied with the process than groups using EBS, IA, and voting in sequence.

Hypothesis 4: Groups using EDS will be more likely to reach consensus than groups using EBS, IA, and voting in sequence.

## 5. The Study

THE CURRENT STUDY INVOLVED ONLY ONE INDEPENDENT VARIABLE, with two treatments: groups using EDS and groups using EBS, IA, and voting in sequence. Five groups of six members each were exposed to each of the two treatments, for a total of 60 subjects ( $6 \times 5 \times 2$ ). The “Parkway Drug Case” was not used in the study, since most of the potential subject pool had been exposed to it in other experiments or in classes. Instead, a task of the same type (combination creativity and intellective) was used. The case, called “Video/Video,” deals with a fictional American company that manufactures VCRs. The company has learned that its Japanese competitors have developed a breakthrough technology, which will make their existing product line obsolete. The company’s management must decide on the best approach to meeting this threat. The correct answer for the case was determined by expert ranking. The setting for the study was the same as that for our previous experiment: the PlexCenter facility at the University of Arizona. The PlexCenter research facility was designed for the study of decision making and planning processes [1, 4]. Sixteen networked microcomputers are positioned around a large U-shaped table to provide for the interaction among participants. A facilitator’s microcomputer/file server is also on the network. The room includes a large screen projector, which permits the display of aggregated information. The decision-making strategy was the same as that used in the previous experiment (Figure 1). Subjects were all drawn from upper division MIS courses at the University of Arizona and were randomly assigned to groups. The groups were then randomly assigned to treatments, which controlled for other variables such as age, sex, educational experience, and work experience. The dependent variables were decision quality, number of unique alternatives generated, satisfaction, and consensus. Decision quality was determined by comparing each group’s final decision to solutions considered optimum by the group of experts. The experts ranked their solutions from best to worst. Each of their solutions was then assigned a point value, with the best solution having the most points, the second best having the second most points, and so on, such that higher values reflected better solutions. If the group reached consensus, its joint decision was assigned the appropriate point value. If the group did not reach consensus, the value of the decision quality was calculated as follows:

In order to determine the number of unique alternatives generated, the lists of alternatives produced prior to each vote were analyzed. Redundant alternatives were eliminated, leaving only unique solutions. Satisfaction was measured by a post-treatment questionnaire given to each participant. The instrument consisted of 16 questions whose answers were recorded on five-point Likert scales. A composite satisfaction score was first determined for each participant in the group by summing the values for all sixteen questions. A satisfaction average was then determined for the group by summing the individual member satisfaction scores and dividing by six. Cronbach's alpha for the scale was 0.8249, indicating an acceptable level of reliability. Consensus was achieved when all of the group members agreed on a single solution to the problem, as reflected in the group's voting. It was coded as a dummy variable: the group either reached consensus or it did not.

## 6. Findings

T-TESTS WERE RUN FOR EACH OF THE FIVE DEPENDENT VARIABLES to determine if there were any statistically significant differences between the treatments. The results are reported in Table 1.

The results show that there is a statistically significant difference for decision quality $p < .1$ , and for the number of unique alternatives generated $p < .05$ . Since the higher the decision quality value, the better the decision, Table 1 shows that groups using EDS reached better decisions than groups using the three-tool sequence. This supports our first hypothesis. Table 1 also shows that groups using the three-tool sequence generated more unique alternatives than those using EDS, which provides support for Hypothesis 2. There were no statistically significant differences for satisfaction or consensus, so there is no support for Hypotheses 3 or 4. It is interesting to note, however, that twice as many groups (4 out of 5, or 80 percent) reached consensus using EDS as reached consensus using the three-tool sequence (2 out of 5, or 40 percent).

## 7. Discussion

OUR FINDINGS SUPPORT OUR GENERAL PREMISE THAT THERE SHOULD BE a match between the EMS software and task. We found that EDS, which can be used to simulate a face-to-face conversation, although electronically, enabled better quality solutions for the groups using it for this type of task, than groups that used EBS, IA, and voting in sequence. Those using the EBS tool sequence, on the other hand, were able to generate more unique solutions than groups using EDS. These findings are as one would expect, and they can be interpreted as demonstrating that each software tool did what it was designed to do.

EDS, configured for a small group, with only one file being worked on at a time, comes very close to an electronic conversation. Every group member has access to all comments generated by every other group member for the entire process. Not only can a group member see and read every comment being entered as it is being entered, he or she can also read back through previous comments that were made earlier. Such a tool provides at least two advantages over verbal conversations: the ability to have everyone participate at once and still be understood, and the ability to read verbatim comments made earlier in the process. An electronic conversation provides a clear focus on the task at hand, which is important for an intellective task where the group is searching for a best answer, and for a process that is supposed to lead to consensus, where all group members have been charged with producing a unanimous group decision.

An electronic conversation is not well suited for generating unique ideas, however. The dynamics of the process point to a focus or a convergence, whereas idea generation profits from a divergence of views $[21]$ . Idea generation works best when different ideas are dispersed and each is allowed to become the starting point for a unique stream of additional ideas. This is the way EBS was designed to operate. Our findings show that it works better for idea generation than does the electronic conversation of EDS. The divergence of ideas that EBS supports so well, however, makes it difficult to use for tasks with a best answer or for reaching consensus. There are at least two reasons for this: the interaction among group members is fragmented due to the $N + 1$ files being used, making the process more difficult to follow; and, since EBS helps individuals generate more ideas, there are more alternatives for the group to consider, making it more difficult for the group to agree on any one alternative.

It is probably not a coincidence, then, that groups using EDS were more likely (though not statistically so) to reach consensus, since they had fewer unique solutions with which to deal. They were able to concentrate on just a few ideas and were hence more likely to find one that satisfied everybody.

It is interesting that groups using EDS were no more satisfied than groups that used EBS, IA, and voting. We had expected them to be more satisfied, since the tools they were using better fit the task they were asked to work on. It may be that fit between tool and task has no effect on the satisfaction of the user, even though a good fit allows him or her to obtain better outcomes. Or it may be that all of the software tools used in the experiment were easy to use, so that any frustration or confusion that might have occurred was minimized and thus had little effect on satisfaction.

It is possible that the differences we found between EDS and the three-tool sequence were due to a simple but basic difference between these software methods: EDS is one tool and the sequence consists of three tools. Users in EDS could stay within the tool, whereas users of the three-tool sequence had to switch back and forth between tools. We do not believe this is enough to account for the differences we found, however, because switching between capabilities in EDS and switching between EBS, IA, and voting was controlled by a facilitator who was not a member of the group. Moving between functions in EDS required about the same amount of facilitator intervention and caused about as much disruption as moving between tools in the three-tool sequence. EDS can be configured so that users decide when to move between functions, but we intentionally configured EDS so that only the facilitator could switch functions. This made EDS and the three-tool sequence more closely comparable.

## 8. Implications for Design

WE FOUND THAT THE EMS SOFTWARE USED MADE A DIFFERENCE in the level of group performance on a particular task, such that a better fit between the software and the task was associated with better performance. This was true for both the creativity and intellective parts of the task we used. McGrath [18] identifies six other types of group tasks, listed and described in Table 2. Based on the findings from this and other studies, and on informed speculation, we can categorize McGrath's task types by whether EBS or EDS would be most suitable for them.

Past studies of EBS [20] have indicated that it works well for planning. The current study, and at least one other [17], have demonstrated that brainstorming software also works well for creativity tasks. EDS should work as well for decision-making tasks as it does for intellective tasks, since they are both “choose” tasks, which benefit from the exchange of views occurring in an electronic conversation. Cognitive conflict and mixed-motive tasks both deal with negotiation among group members. We can speculate that an EDS-type tool would probably be more effective in these situations than would brainstorming software, but empirical research is called for to test such a premise. A third type of tool may be best for such tasks. The last two task types, as described by McGrath, tend to be physical in nature, so it is difficult to see how either EBS or EDS (or EMS for that matter) would benefit groups engaged in such activities. Which tools best fit which tasks is, of course, an empirical question, for future study.

It is important to note that we kept group size constant in our study, at the relatively small size of 6. While both laboratory and field research have demonstrated that the University of Arizona's EBS tool works very well at supporting idea generation for large groups (of size 18 and over), it is doubtful whether EDS as configured here could work well for groups larger than size 10 [27]. EDS, with one file per group, would soon overwhelm the members of medium-sized groups with too much information. The larger the group, the more comments they would produce and the faster the comments would come. Keeping up with all of the comments would become difficult, and it follows that performance would suffer.

EBS can facilitate idea generation for a wide variety of group sizes, but EDS can provide electronic conversations primarily for small groups. There is a need, then, for a tool that facilitates an electronic conversation for large groups. One method may be using an EDS-type tool, with only one or two files for the group, that incorporates some type of turn taking. Users may have to queue up to contribute to the conversation. Some of the spontaneity of the conversation would be lost and some process loss would be introduced $[25]$ , but the resulting process would be superior to a verbal conversation for a large group. In addition, an electronic conversation for large groups could be useful in distributed EMS sessions, where users would work from the computers on their office or home desks. Large numbers of users could contribute to and follow the conversation taking place among large numbers of their peers dispersed geographically. They could each be privy to the entire process, in near real-time.

## 9. Conclusions

THE CURRENT STUDY WAS CONDUCTED TO INVESTIGATE THE NOTION of fit between EMS software tools and group tasks, and the resulting effects on group performance. The research question resulted from the findings of our previous EMS experiment [13], in which we found no differences in decision quality between groups that had EMS support and those that did not, even though the EMS literature demonstrates the ability of EMS to lead to better performance. We thought one reason for our earlier findings might be that the EMS software used did not match the task subjects were asked to perform.

Our findings supported our expectations regarding the match between software and task: EMS software can be bent to do what users want without detrimental effects, but software appropriate to the task helps facilitate higher quality solutions than inappropriate software. For the type of combined task used in this study, with small groups, outcomes depended on the software methods used.

The implications of our findings for EMS designers seem clear: design EMS with several different types of tools, each of which is designed for a particular class or type of task. Only one or two tools will be insufficient to meet the needs of all groups using the facility and for all of the tasks they will address. Designers should take both task type and group size into consideration. In general, groups will be able to use a given EMS tool for the task at hand, but they will not be able to optimize their performance unless the tools they use match the tasks they are working on. Such an approach has been taken in the design of the University of Arizona GroupSystems' software toolkit, and based on our findings, this seems like a productive approach.
