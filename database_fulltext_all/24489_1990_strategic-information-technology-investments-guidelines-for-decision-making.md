# Strategic Information Technology Investments: Guidelines for Decision Making\*

ERIC K. CLEMONS and BRUCE W. WEBER

BRUCE W. WEBER is completing his Ph.D. dissertation at the Decision Sciences Department of the Wharton School, University of Pennsylvania. In addition, he is a Research Fellow of the Reginald H. Jones Center's Project in Information Systems, Telecommunications, and Business Strategy. His education includes an A.B. in Applied Mathematics from Harvard University, and an M.S. in Decision Sciences from the Wharton School.

ABSTRACT: As the strategic importance of information technology (IT) has increased, the decision of where and when to allocate resources to IT programs has become more risky and more difficult. Executives are tempted by the opportunities for strategic impact, but struggle with the massive expenditures and uncertainties involved. Evaluating the opportunity afforded by a system and judging its strategic impact in advance have proven difficult, and even when analyses are performed well, they are frequently done on an ad hoc basis. IT can confer advantage under appropriate conditions, and equally important, even when it fails to confer advantage, it may still prove crucial. Both concepts—competitive advantage and strategic necessity—confound traditional financial analysis. We offer seven principles on which to base an evaluation of a strategic IT venture. Although we have not performed a statistically validating study, these principles are expressed as guidelines we believe to be true, based on experience. The guidelines range from modeling the investment decision, through managing risk, to preparing for unanticipated upside and downside implications.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: information systems for strategic advantage, investment in information systems, risk assessment for information system projects.

## 1. Introduction

## 1.1. The IT Gamble

New technologies and technology-driven opportunities are being developed and presented to managers at an ever-accelerating rate. Chances to join networks, forge electronic linkages with customers and suppliers, and develop and implement new systems architectures require careful consideration and analysis. The stakes and the possible returns are enormous.

In September 1988, AMR Corporation (parent company of American Airlines) announced that it would consider bids for selling off its highly regarded Sabre travel reservation system, in use in over 14,000 travel agencies. Wall Street put a value on the system of \$1.5 billion. At the time, AMR's market value was \$2.9 billion, indicating Sabre's value in the 12 years since its introduction had grown to exceed AMR's core airline business. $^{1}$ And yet when American was first considering the \$40 million start-up cost of the system, the decision to develop was not easy to justify [40]. AMR's chief competitor, United Airlines, had decided against wider distribution of its own travel agent reservation system, which was in use on a small scale. A full roll-out of United's Apollo system came several months after Sabre's introduction. The market share advantage gained by Sabre has continued, and its share is now 40 percent, compared to Apollo's 29 percent [37, 43].

The downside can be substantial as well. Chemical Bank and a Chemical and AT&T joint venture abandoned their disappointing electronic home-banking product, Pronto, in December 1988, and were estimated to have written off \$70 million in assets associated with the system [21]. Despite aggressive promotion and technological excellence, Pronto attracted only 10,000 subscribers in six years, which was far short of expectations and well below the level necessary to break even.

Strategic systems initiatives such as these carry immense risk, yet may offer commensurate reward. They are difficult to undertake, and the decision to develop is rarely obvious in advance. Sabre's ultimate value was not readily apparent in the mid 1970s and the investment decision was neither obvious nor easy. Difficulty in justifying an investment for which the value now seems indisputable should not be surprising. User adoption, future benefits, and competitive impact are difficult to forecast. And the system itself may so radically alter the industry that prior assumptions are invalidated. $^{2}$ We have found that strategic IT programs often lack formal economic justification. We disagree, however, that an act of faith is necessary to justify strategic technology investments. $^{3}$ Principles exist, and many of the novel techniques that have been used to justify some of today's highly visible and successful systems are rational and can be applied to other IT investment decisions.

## 1.2. Plan for the Paper

This paper describes the IT investment decision problem, and proposes principles for evaluating and selecting IT opportunities in which to invest. Our guidelines are based on empirical studies, and on theoretical work on the valuation and timing of risky investments. We also consider the role of a company's unique resources in generating competitive advantage, and the role of strategic necessity in guiding IT investment decisions [10].

The second section of this paper will describe the general problem of making technology investment decisions. Companies are no longer able to justify investments in advanced technology solely on the basis of cost savings and tangible returns on investment. At the same time, these projects are considered essential for firms to maintain their competitive position. The third section examines the difficulties specific to strategic IT decisions that executives face. In the fourth section, we detail intangible benefits of IT that should be considered when making the investment decision. Often these benefits appear serendipitously midway into the project. In the fifth section, lessons from strategic IT successes are developed. In our studies, nontraditional justification methods have often proven more appropriate for making strategic IT commitments. The sixth section proposes seven principles for guiding the evaluation of an IT investment. These range from applying analysis tools, through identifying upside and downside implications, to recognizing multiple forms of risk that are inherent in a strategic IT venture. The final section presents ongoing research into active management of the risks of large-scale IT developments.

## 2. The Technology Investment Decision Problem

COMPANIES CONCEDE THAT THEY CAN NO LONGER JUSTIFY leading-edge automation projects in their factories, the development of large-scale systems architectures, or communications networks for their organizations, solely on the basis of return on investment. The direct-cost savings and higher production rates that once paid back IT investments in a short time are no longer sufficient to offset the high price of many of today's technology projects. Apprehensively, managers are using broad strategic considerations, such as enhanced quality, better customer service, and keeping up with the competition, to justify technology investments. Executives are considering alternative justification methods, and questioning the use of discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis. Unfortunately, generally accepted principles for making IT investment decisions are not yet available.

In this section we review evidence that managers struggle to evaluate technology investments. We review the strategic systems literature, and approaches to justifying strategic IT initiatives. Work in the early years of data processing management by Emery [18] identified the critical role of intangible benefits in justifying systems investments at a time when the applications were largely intended to reduce costs and to automate paperwork and routine tasks. Emery noted that “the difference between a tangible and an intangible lies in the difficulty of estimating monetary value.” He proposed methods for calculating the value of intangibles, such as the reduction in stockouts from a new inventory system. Subsequent research on measuring the intangible benefits of strategic systems has not kept up, and shortcomings still exist. The impassioned support of a “champion” [3], or convincing top management of less visible benefits of strategic systems [31], may not be suitable for some of today’s board-level decisions on \$100 million IT infrastructure investments. $^{4}$

## 2.1. Evidence

Recent studies have shown that executives are troubled by their firms' technology investment decisions. A 1988 survey of technology managers in UK banks found that 80 percent of the respondents feel technology has fallen short of their expectations, and more than half confirm that systems investments are not generating the anticipated improvements in productivity [48]. IT applications have grown beyond supporting efficiency improvements. In seeking improved effectiveness and strategic goals, performance and return on an IT investment can no longer be quantified so easily. Instead, executives struggle to weigh intangible benefits and strategic impact against the often imposing hardware and development costs.

In a 1986 survey of how technology investments are justified in 67 Fortune 500 companies, 77.0 percent of respondent firms felt they will soon be making additional IT investments. However, 89.5 percent have great difficulty quantifying the intangible benefits of proposed systems, and another 61.2 percent find that the uncertainty of benefits makes justification methods hard to apply [2]. Clearly, executives are confronting challenging IT investment decisions, yet few appear to be well served by existing investment justification models.

## 2.2. Discounted Cash Flow Analysis

A broad debate has unfolded on the appropriate use of Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) methods for directing investment spending. $^{5}$ Managers recognize that many of today's large-scale technology investments—flexible manufacturing systems and integrated account processing systems in banks—are not easily justified on a cost-reduction basis [25, 39]. As we discuss in subsequent sections, this is because the costs of not making the investment are frequently ignored. Critics of DCF methods feel that an overreliance on discounting techniques has inhibited capital investment in American businesses, reduced research budgets, and harmed the competitiveness of U.S. industry [23].

Discounting's defenders reply that DCF analysis is based on sound theoretical principles [33]. Rather than biasing against long-term investments, they contend that DCF is an objective framework for comparing the relative merits of investments with different cash flows [6].

We feel that the evaluation of strategic IT programs is fraught with complexity that is not well handled by DCF analysis. The debate over the use of DCF analysis for technology investments obscures the current misunderstanding of how IT can provide strategic value. Rather than continuing the debate, our guidelines are techniques for augmenting—not replacing—formal financial analyses.

## 2.3. The Vanishing Status Quo

The trap of the vanishing status quo is real and prevalent. As management often neglects in its analysis of IT opportunities, the alternative to investment and development is rarely a continuation of existing industry conditions. Technological innovations have continually reshaped industries since the Industrial Revolution began in Britain in the 18th century [27]. IT has increased the pace of change. Centuries-old traditions of floor trading in London's stock market were abandoned a few days after the Big Bang market reforms took effect in 1986 [14]. Activity moved to a screen-dealing system that was better equipped to handle the demands of modern securities trading.

Executives making IT investment decisions must acknowledge the near certainty that technology-induced change will alter the structure of their industry. Continuation of the status quo, as the alternative to a proposed IT program, is a glaringly naive assumption. Barwise et al. [1] identify misstating the base case—what will happen if the project is not carried out—as a primary flaw in most companies' investment decision making. They recommend spending more time considering the likely outcome of not making an investment; the company's position is likely to deteriorate, rivals will act, and market share and margins of firms that fail to respond will fall.

## 2.4. Strategic Systems

The strategic systems literature has focused on innovation champions and winning the support of the CEO as methods for gaining acceptance of hard-to-justify IT projects. Identifying opportunities and threats, and analyzing the financial consequences of competitive advantage and strategic necessity, have received less attention than they deserve. IT affects more aspects of company's operations and has become a critical enabler of many business strategies. For instance, IT has:

\- formed distribution channels, reaching out to the organization's customers; McKesson Economost, AMR's Sabre, and BZW TRADE are examples [12, 14];

\- enabled tight linkages with suppliers; GM and Hewlett-Packard have extensive EDI programs with vendors;

\- spawned powerful cooperative partnerships; examples include MAC, a shared network of ATMs managed by Philadelphia National Bank [15], and the “virtual” merger of SAS and Texas Air by linking their reservation systems and combining their flight codes.

In spite of the attention that several of these successful IT initiatives have received, managers have trouble evaluating the initial opportunities to use technology strategically, and have difficulty making investment decisions when these opportunities arise. The CFO of a Fortune 500 company said, “in today’s environment we don’t think the numbers really tell the story—many investments are required for survival or to stay ahead of the competition” [39]. Clearly, the decisions concerning where and when to allocate resources to technology programs have become far more risky and difficult.

## 2.5. Evaluating Strategic IT

Our approach to developing IT investment principles is to review the determinants of a strategic system's value, and use them as a basis for making investment decisions. Few companies that have spent heavily to create a competitive advantage through IT have been rewarded. However, those firms that fail to respond to competitive requirements, and assume the continuation of a comfortable status quo, consistently find themselves in deteriorating positions.

## 3. Strategic IT Investment Analysis: Downside Risks

UNFAMILIAR RISKS AND DIFFICULTIES CONFRONT EXECUTIVES deciding on strategic IT programs. Many firms pursue competitive advantage, but few systems actually provide it. Still, the benefits that result from strategic implementations are real, even if often unquantifiable and even if omitted in the initial financial analysis. A number of hard-to-quantify parameters have a strong bearing on a system's strategic impact, but these factors are often overlooked in the investment analysis. Our studies indicate executives must consider the following factors in the evaluation of strategic IT programs.

## 3.1. Replicability and Competitor Response

In general, IT capabilities are easily copied, and the benefits of an innovation (e.g., lower production costs, enhanced customer service) are not held exclusively by the innovating firm. $^{6}$ The technology itself is rarely a source of advantage. Instead, IT can enable other resources controlled by the firm to be exploited for advantage [9]. Lacking patent protection, genuine first-mover benefits, or a truly sustainable edge, managers usually are not justified in forecasting competitive benefits from a system. The ability to duplicate IT capabilities readily means that any advantage conferred will most likely erode. In addition, since opportunities to apply technology are usually open to all competitors, rejecting a technology program leaves the firm vulnerable to other players.

## 3.2. Misapplication of Financial Models

In practice, many firms misapply discounted cash flow models to IT investments. Surveys of current capital investment practice indicate that [23, 26]:

\- Benefits that are not easily measurable tend to be ignored and hence set to zero. The advantages of better quality, greater flexibility, lower throughput times, and experience with new technology are additional, but rarely quantified IT benefits.

\- Financial analyses focus too narrowly on labor and materials savings; i.e., cost displacement is emphasized over new products and enhancements to existing offerings.

\- To compensate for high perceived risk, rate-of-return hurdles are set at arbitrarily high levels. True cost of capital is far lower than that generally applied in deciding on new investment projects.

\- High hurdle rates set in order to compensate for risk have no theoretical justification and have a discouraging effect on technology investment.

Poor application of DCF techniques to risky, long-term, or strategic programs is capable of misleading managers, and misguiding firms in their use of IT innovations.

## 3.3. Industry Restructuring and Environmental Hazard

Because of IT's ability to alter the structure of an industry, environmental conditions (market size, margins, etc.) can change radically with the introduction of new systems, thus invalidating even well-constructed financial models. By creating new communication links and enabling coordination of businesses, IT brings about fundamental economic change. For instance:

\- Big Bang, the sweeping deregulation and restructuring of the London stock market in 1986, was accomplished in large part by the introduction of a screen-based dealing system. The improved market efficiency, however, has had disastrous effects on member firms' profitability, causing losses that several years earlier would have wiped out the capital of most firms [14].

\- The Chicago Mercantile Exchange, the Chicago Board of Trade, and Reuters are progressing in a joint venture, Globex, to facilitate computer-assisted, off-hours trading in futures contracts [7]. Available worldwide on 173,000 Reuters terminals, Globex could radically alter the markets' liquidity, and the role of floor brokers and other intermediaries that have had a long-standing presence in Chicago's derivative markets.

Anticipating the environment at the time of the investment decision is difficult and perilous. While predicting the future is always subject to error, technological advances make even the most expert forecast unreliable.

## 3.4. Long Lead Times

Information technology investments have long lead times. Before a full roll-out, an IT project typically passes through many phases of development, which include requirements analysis, coding, testing, modification, training, and limited implementation with close monitoring. Fourth-generation languages and CASE tools can shorten the timetable, but in most cases a strategic IT program will have a substantial lag between acceptance of the investment proposal, and the eventual time when benefits can be gauged. This delay introduces added uncertainty into assumptions made about market conditions and the actions of competitors.

In general, the future environment is highly uncertain at the time of the investment decision, and simply bounding the range of future cash flows may be extremely difficult, even for experts. To have projected Sabre's value correctly would have required accurate predictions of the airline industry's growth under deregulation, the adoption rate of travel agents, and American's competitors' response to paying a booking fee to a competitor. Even extensive scenario evaluation would not have yielded the full range of future environments to be considered in making the strategic commitment to Sabre.

## 3.5. Organizational Barriers

Several organizational barriers handicap management in properly evaluating strategic IT ventures. The size of the commitments is intimidating, and often requires board-level justification. NatWest Bank in the UK recently committed itself to the first part of a £500 million program to develop a new IT infrastructure for the bank and its 3,500 branches [8]. Presenting the decision to the bank's executive committee forced senior IT managers to justify a massive technology program to a group unfamiliar with many of the risks of large-scale strategic IT developments.

As the costs and the number of risks and environmental uncertainties grow, IT decisions will not get safer or easier. As a result, paralysis can occur. Managements may have the misplaced belief that a technological panacea lies around the corner, obviating their need to act now. Promising new technologies that may threaten a company's existing products are often neglected. Also, it is simply difficult to deviate from existing practice. There is an observed incapacity of incumbents and leading firms to respond to challenges of start-up firms that apply new technologies and innovations that represent generational change rather than incremental change [24].

## 4. Strategic IT Investment Analysis: Upside Opportunities

SEVERAL BENEFITS OF IT ARE OFTEN OVERLOOKED or not fully valued in traditional investment decision models. All too often, to maintain precision, financial analyses assign zero for the value of intangible benefits. Our studies indicate that less visible factors can significantly enhance the realized value of IT projects, and may prove to be the principal value realized from the investment.

## 4.1. Divisibility and Expandability

Systems are usually highly leverageable. Once developed, systems can be expanded and implemented in other parts of the organization with little incremental cost. There are few technical restrictions (and low marginal costs) for sending more bits to travel agents or hooking up another customer into an order entry system.

Systems can be designed to have flexible and readily increased capacity. A customer database developed by the sales department can be used in new product development, and summaries could be available for executive-level decision support systems. The output of a production planning system can be fed to an inventory and supplier ordering system to ensure that components are on hand at the beginning of a manufacturing run. The result is lower ordering costs and fewer stockouts.

It has the capability to enhance a company's offerings and their convenience to its customers by expanding geographical reach or extending the hours of business. These enhancements can reach new segments of demand, and enable the business to grow in ways unforeseeable at the time of the investment decision.

## 4.2. Marketing In-House Systems

IT cost centers can become profitable ventures for firms that commercialize the systems they create. One writer points out that, “as IT takes up an increasing proportion of budgets, the attraction of turning parts of that investment into a revenue stream—of turning programs into products—is growing too” [34]. A number of systems that were developed as in-house projects and received a favorable internal response have been offered as a product, or on a licensing basis, often resulting in generous returns on the incremental selling expense.

\- First Boston has spun off a separate company, and is now licensing its proprietary systems development technologies—CASE tools—and its advanced global trading and clearance systems to competitors [20].

\- British Telecom sold its Customer Service System (CSS) to Singapore Telecom for £13 million, fractionally offsetting its £500 million development cost [34].

\- Mellon Bank developed sophisticated, large-capacity transaction and account processing systems in the early 1970s. Mellon became a leading provider of processing and other correspondent banking services to small banks [42].

\- SNCF, France's national rail body, recently purchased a license to use Sabre software from American Airlines for \$160 million [46].

The process of turning an in-house system into a marketable product can be difficult. However, the possibility of packaging and selling internally developed IT services and applications creates an opportunity for additional value from an IT investment. Many IT managers now recognize that any advantage from a system is generally short-lived, so commercializing a system is unlikely to result in the loss of competitive opportunities.

## 4.3. Timing Value

Timing value is often overlooked when evaluating IT investments. Technology offers management a choice of when to adopt an innovation. The ability to choose when to implement a system is valuable, and there are several approaches to the optimal timing of a technology implementation. Lee [32], for instance, modeled the returns that an investment project generates as a stochastic process, and analyzed the value of the timing option—the ability to defer the project—contained in many technology investments. Using options valuation, he derived the optimal deferment policy and calculated a trigger-point value, the value of the project, which, when reached, should trigger its implementation.

## 4.4. Flexibility and Option Value

Although difficult to incorporate in traditional financial frameworks, quicker response to customer orders, greater customer satisfaction, and enhanced service quality are aspects of IT programs that affect market share and growth, and thus require explicit consideration in making an investment decision. Fine and Freund [19] note that there is scarce “knowledge and experience in evaluating investments that enhance flexibility.” They derive the added economic value provided by flexible production capacity when demand is stochastic, and note that this added value is rarely considered in investment decisions. Harrigan points out that flexibility—by enabling firms to overcome exit barriers in declining industries and unprofitable market segments—has strategic value [22].

## 5. Lessons from IT Successes

## 5.1. IT Investment Decision Cases

In spite of the obstacles to evaluating IT programs accurately, and the resulting difficulty in making sound IT investment decisions, it can be done. We examined five strategic systems investments made with sound, rational methods [17]. The price of these investments ranged from \$2 million to well over \$100 million. The companies justified strategic technology investments by viewing IT as a source of change, and as a tool for strategic positioning; i.e., several of the systems investments formed a platform for reaching a number of strategic goals. Measuring IT ventures solely on the basis of fallible estimates of future cash flows, omitting other benefits, was recognized to be misleading. The analyses these firms conducted prior to the launch of their IT ventures made use of traditional capital investment methodologies, but return on investment projections were not the primary criteria. They used evaluation methods that are rational, but not quantitative.

## 5.2. Strategy and Systems Decisions

As technology increases its strategic role, successful firms are making the evaluation of IT opportunities look more like the company's strategic planning process. Managers base strategic planning on the application of a number of different models and paradigms. Lederer and Sethi [30] report that “organizations generally apply one of a number of methodologies in order to perform strategic IS planning,” and may “select features of the alternative methodologies and in effect combine them to arrive at decisions.” Brealey and Myers [6] refer to strategic planning as “capital budgeting on a grand scale.” The “grandness” is accomplished by taking explicit consideration of factors and of strategic risks and benefits not included in a standard NPV analysis.

## 5.3. Recognizing the Components of Risk

Risks in IT investments may come in forms with which management is unfamiliar. Successful companies recognize these alternative forms of risk, and take steps to hedge these risks or mitigate the impact of an IT project's failure. The first companies to switch over to automated payroll processing faced technical risk—there was uncertainty whether the technology was in fact capable of supporting the payroll processing needs. On the other hand, the development of electronic securities markets today presents entirely different types of risk. Environmental hazards arise; the electronically mediated market can so radically change the environment and business practices that forecasts are grossly unreliable estimates of what the response and the future environment will be. Given the unfamiliar risks, and the likelihood of error in ex ante financial valuations of IT projects, alternative principles for making IT investments are necessary.

## 6. Guidelines

WE OFFER BELOW SEVERAL GUIDELINES, WHICH WE BELIEVE to be useful in structuring the evaluation of strategic investments in information technology. We have not sought to validate them in a statistically significant sense. We have, however, observed them to be useful in numerous case studies, and in real implementations where managers had to make decisions and millions of dollars had to be committed. Inquiry through future case research can be used to support or undermine these hypotheses.

G1: Investment decisions can be made on a rational analytical basis, even when the numbers required for discounted cash flow analyses cannot be obtained.

Using discounted cash flow analyses to rank alternatives generally requires that reasonably good estimates be available for all relevant cost and income streams. However, even when true investment values are not obtainable, a ranking may still be possible. As noted, estimating future cash flows resulting from a strategic IT investment is complex and subject to significant error. An alternative is to perform decision-tree analysis. Identifying branches that characterize scenarios—e.g., rapid or slow customer adoption, and limited or widespread competitor implementations—is often easier than valuing future cash flows. A decision tree may look as shown in Figure 1.

As can be seen in Figure 1, the threat of scenario C, in which customers adopt quickly, competitors act, and your firm does not invest, may be sufficiently compelling to justify investing. When properly structured, decision-tree analysis can frequently establish a ranking of outcome scenarios even when precise valuations are not available. It also surfaces assumptions and lays out the arguments for the viability of an IT program. In some cases the eventual realizations of individual parameters may be irrelevant to the decision. The ranking of outcome scenarios may not be affected within wide ranges of parameter values, and some choices may turn out to be dominated.

G2: Thresholds established by sensitivity analysis can be used as trigger points for fine-tuning a project once it is initiated.

Sensitivity analysis is simply a procedure for calculating the consequences of mis-estimating parameters in a decision model. It entails using alternative estimates and examining their implications. As Leamer [29] points out, a model's “conclusions are judged to be sturdy only if the neighborhood of assumptions is wide enough to be credible, and the corresponding interval of inferences is narrow enough to be useful.” In making investment decisions, sensitivity analysis is used to:

\- Determine input assumptions required for the viability of an investment

\- Establish control ranges for changes in input parameters and constants over time for which the present investment decision remains optimal

An IT program, like any significant investment, should be subjected to sensitivity analysis. An investment that relies on a particular adoption rate by users or a certain completion date in order to be viable should be tested for its robustness to mis-estimation.

Furthermore, IT developments that follow a sequential plan allow systems efforts to be shut down early at substantial savings if prospects no longer appear favorable, or alternatively scaled up if conditions improve. While an IT project may be worth undertaking on the basis of present information, if a parameter value falls below a threshold indicated by sensitivity analysis, it signals the project no longer has a positive value and abandoning it ought to be considered.

In addition to testing for the attractiveness of a project when one input parameter is varied at a time, it is also valuable to consider scenarios, different but consistent combinations of variables. One reason for creating plausible scenarios is that inputs themselves may not be independent. For instance, the cost of providing third-party processing may be related to the size of the market. If demand rises it may be possible to charge less and thus attract more business. Also critical to assess in scenario analysis are possible unspecified variables, or unknown unknowns.

## G3: Advantage results from unique assets and resources of the implementing firm.

The underpinnings of this hypothesis are fundamental economic postulates. If technology increases margins, we expect entrants and copying by existing players. As Teece [45] has demonstrated, when there are limited barriers to copying—a “weak appropriability regime”—advantages will be competed away. Unless patents or trade secrets offer protection, the advantages from most IT capabilities are likely to have weak appropriability.

IT will likely have an asymmetric impact on different players. IT innovations will make some assets singularly more valuable. Since firms in the same industry often operate with different portfolios of assets, an IT capability, even when universally implemented, can take two firms with equivalent profitability and radically affect their future margins and position in the industry [9]. As a result, those players that develop or acquire the requisite cospecialized assets before IT hikes their value will be advantaged. $^{7}$ With access to cospecialized assets, a firm may be able to derive advantage that is not readily available to its competitors who lack these assets. Two examples are:

\- In the drug distribution business, market share was the cospecialized asset whose value was greatly enhanced by the automation of the ordering and distribution functions. Systems such as McKesson's Economost required economies of scale to make their development and operation economically viable [12].

\- In third-party processing, Provident National Bank had developed resources in-house that enabled them to win a large contract to handle Shearson's processing for its response to Merrill Lynch's Cash Management Account. In subsequent contracts, Provident enjoyed economies of scale and experience that drove their costs even lower [13].

Thus, the value of an IT innovation will in part be determined by a firm's ability to exploit it.

If a fundamental impact of an IT innovation is to raise the value of a set of cospecialized assets, then one of the key decision criteria in considering a strategic IT venture will be which of these assets to buy and when to buy them.

G4: Several types of risk exist and must be recognized early in the evaluation of an IT development.

A number of forms of risks arise in undertaking strategic IT programs. Companies' IT investments have several dimensions of risk. As IT projects have become bigger and more complex, there are more ways they can fail. These risks need to be recognized, and in some cases, the risks can be hedged, through out-sourcing and joint development, or by investing sequentially and scaling the project up or down as uncertainties become resolved. Components of IT risk include:

Technical risk.- The systems and the envisioned capabilities are not feasible with the current technology.

Project risk.- The IT capability that a firm seeks to develop is too large or too complex, or overwhelms the staff's technical skills. It may be possible to bring in outside help, such as consultants or independent software developers, to reduce this risk.

Functionality risk.- The firm may get the system design or implementation right—that is, according to specification—but still fail to realize the anticipated benefits.

\- The design is wrong and systems analysis failed to assess the user needs accurately.

\- The target moved in an unexpected way. A system has been built for what was originally wanted but the world and the business environment have changed; the system as designed is no longer appropriate.

Internal political risk.- An IT investment can be undermined by vested interests in the innovating organization. Reacting to a perceived threat to their positions in the current environment, staff or divisions of the company may not cooperate in the development or may be slow to adopt an implementation.

External environmental risk.- The source of the risk is unanticipated response from competitors or regulatory bodies. Responses from competitors include imitation and substitute products that are intended to prevent an advantage from developing. In addition, customers may bypass, or threaten to develop their own system.

Systemic risk.- An extreme case of environmental risk, the IT program may so dramatically shift the environment that the advantages expected from it vanish. An IT project may precipitate a large-scale discontinuity in an industry, and so radically alter the industry and change prevailing economics that prior assumptions about market share, growth, and profitability are rendered obsolete.

G5: Technology investments may have option and timing value, and unexpected upside benefits.

Options theory can improve the evaluation of strategic IT investments. IT developments give firms valuable options and provide a platform for enhancing the bundle of assets and resources that make up the firm. For instance:

Similar to a call option, IT ventures frequently have significant upside potential. Several authors have pointed out that a technology investment can result in a residual value that is similar to a financial option that ends up “in the money” [4, 36]. For instance, an R&D investment may position the firm to move quickly into a new technology at a later time, or to respond to needs and opportunities created by a changing regulatory or business environment. Thus, a bank with an outdated and inflexible systems architecture might consider investment in technology to increase flexibility, and thus to facilitate responding to opportunities created by regulatory change, and to increased threats posed by new competitors. It might consider this investment to represent buying an option on the future profitability of the firm; in the extreme, this investment might even represent buying an option on the firm’s survival. Thus, these investments might well be undertaken even when no specific requirements or threats are perceived.

Firms can cobble together option-like prototypes. The initial versions of Merrill's Cash Management Account and McKesson's Economost were initially rolled out as prototype versions [12, 13]. When market response permitted more precise and accurate estimation of their impact, these prototypes were replaced by full implementations. Leasing or subcontracting to gain an IT capability is a second alternative analogous to a call option. If demand and benefits of the leased IT fail to justify it (similar to an option expiring "out of the money"), the project is cut at minimal loss. In the early 1980s, Manufacturers Hanover built GEONET, a \$300 million international X.25-based telecommunications network [35]. Actual volumes reached only 50 percent of the estimates, well below capacity, and well below the level needed for recovery of the system's costs. And yet, since virtually all of the technology was acquired from a third-party provider of packet-switching services, it is likely that initial capacity could have been leased. Necessary expertise would have been acquired, and the bank could have begun conversion of existing applications as well as the development of new applications to exploit packet-switching technology. This, too, is equivalent to acquiring a call option; if demand had materialized, the bank would have been in a position to respond rapidly with its own network.

Other sources of call option value from an IT program derive from the ability to scale up or down an IT capability in response to demand, and the ability to achieve scope advantages through a system that is capable of extension beyond its originally intended application. For instance, a bank that develops a corporate account management system may later use it to support trust department operations. In addition, timing options are often available that can improve the value of an IT program. Brealey and Myers point out “the fact that a project has a positive NPV does not mean it is best undertaken now” [6, p. 95]. Waiting may have benefits and may result in a greater project value.

G6: Downside risk exists in rejected IT programs, which then may become strategic necessities through another firm's initiative.

Since effective technology is generally widely adopted, firms rejecting IT programs because they are satisfied with the status quo should assess the position of ending up a follower in that technology. If a firm decides not to initiate the development of a system, some competitors most likely will. Also, the unit of analysis for analyzing an IT program needs to be chosen carefully. If development of an IT capability appears uneconomic, not developing while the rest of the industry does may be even less attractive.

Managers deciding on a strategic IT program need to decide on a clear metric for measurement of its value. The unit of analysis may not be clearly defined when an investment is intended to have a strategic impact. The desired strategic objectives could include increased market share, lower costs, new geographic reach, and/or business growth. The alternative to investment is unclear and hard to know in advance. It might be lower margins, or a necessary “catch-up” investment later. As a result, the decision to invest in a strategic system needs to be compared not to the status quo, but to the future environment, assuming competitors develop it and users adopt it. Falling margins and vanishing market share are usually the true alternative to a strategic IT investment. The negative NPV of an IT program may be less negative than that of not doing it.

G7: Cooperation may be the dominant investment alternative under conditions of strategic necessity.

Cooperation enables smaller and more specialized firms to counter scale weaknesses that they may face in developing and implementing strategic systems. If a strategic system relies on high volumes to be economic, the smaller players could be better off by initiating joint development efforts and providing a shared system. After Citibank (the most powerful player in the New York retail banking market) rolled out its ATM network in the late 1970s, smaller institutions banded together and formed the New

York Cash Exchange to offer equivalent ATM service on a cooperative basis. Other motivations for cooperating can be to:

Enhance scale advantages.- Had Delta been allowed to merge its travel agent reservation system with Sabre, the combined system would have controlled close to half of the market for travel agent reservation systems. Maintenance costs, marketing efforts, and the expense of any further enhancements would be spread over a wider user base.

Leverage existing assets.- TRADE, BZW's system for stockbrokers to enter customers' buy and sell orders, has been enthusiastically adopted by brokers [16]. In large part, the favorable reception came because the securities house had strong relations in place with the brokers, and the system did not try to bypass them, but was intended to enhance a broker's service to his or her customers.

Make up for resource deficiencies.- SAS and Texas Air, two airlines with strong regional franchises in Europe and the United States, respectively, decided to share flight codes and link their reservation systems. In the hub-and-spoke operations made popular by U.S. carriers since deregulation, there is a certain minimum size or minimum number of spokes needed to operate effectively. The virtual merger increases the number of spokes each airline has available to it. In essence, SAS and Texas Air will now serve as a feeder system for each other, enabling them to compete more effectively with larger carriers that cover both regions.

## 7. Ongoing Research: Managing the Components of IT Risks

## 7.1. Potential for Active Management of IT Risks

There are different components of risk associated with strategic information systems projects, and different projects have different risk profiles. Additionally, these different components of risk have different attributes, and our investigations show they can be actively managed. Steps can be taken to mitigate the impact of failure, or to reduce the loss associated with failure. For example, when dealing with uncertain technology, one can explore alternatives, diversify the risk, and reduce the likelihood of significant loss if one technological route proves infeasible. Alternatively, in some instances managers can secure necessary services from a reputable third-party provider on a fixed-cost basis; if development proves to be more expensive than anticipated, the loss is borne outside of the organization. In addition, research by Sherer and Clemons [44] developed an estimation procedure for the economic impact of software failures, one aspect of technical and project risk. Based on expected failure costs, resources can be appropriately allocated to the testing and review of critical program modules.

The components of risk we find most pervasive are project risk and functionality risk, and often they can be traded off against each other; actions taken to reduce one will often increase the other. This is most evident in strategic architecture projects, investments made to replace strategic IT infrastructure to cope with environmental change, especially after deregulation. Implementation through a centralized systems development group will frequently allow use of experienced and expert technical personnel, and can reduce project risk. Unfortunately, this can greatly increase functionality risk. In the unknown future environment associated with regulatory changes, future systems requirements are often uncertain, and the end users are in a far better position to evaluate and predict future needs than are systems personnel. More compelling, however, is the difference in incentives. The users will be unable to perform their jobs in the future if the systems requirements are not obtained correctly, or if the necessary flexibility is not incorporated to deal with uncertainty. However, meeting their needs can add substantially to the cost and the development time, and the systems group is judged by cost and adherence to schedule; thus their incentives make project risk more important to them than functionality risk. On one occasion, we heard the argument that, since the systems group almost invariably was able to deliver to the user exactly what had been originally promised, and at the scheduled time, there was no need to allow any other group to participate in systems development. While meeting deadlines is valuable, such a record needs to be measured in relation to users' needs that may go unmet by adhering to an inflexible deadline.

Actions are available to reduce project risk and functionality risk. User-sponsored development efforts can use outside experts, from consulting firms or from the firm's central systems group, to reduce the risk associated with complex aspects of the project. For example, expertise needed to manage the phased conversion from files to a shared database management system environment can be obtained on a contracting basis. Or, if development is through a central data processing organization, management can take steps to assure that the incentives of the systems group lead them to work closely with users to determine requirements and thus assure that the flexibility necessary to meet future needs is provided.

## 7.2. An Investment Analysis Mosaic

Investing in strategic IT programs is a high-stakes decision that carries enormous risk and large potential rewards. We have addressed the difficulties that management faces when making these investment decisions. How can the strategic impact of an IT opportunity be judged in advance? What factors distinguish an IT program likely to generate competitive advantage? We feel that the success of a large-scale IT introduction depends on the company's existing strategic plan, and the assets and resources of the firm, which any IT venture should seek to leverage [9].

Given the degree of uncertainty in development and implementation, and in the eventual environmental response, precise valuation methods such as DCF are frequently inadequate. In light of the vast sums of money and high levels of corporate vulnerability associated with strategic IT programs, we sense that many of the existing methods are biased dangerously toward conservatism and risk aversion. We have presented several procedures for evaluating IT investment programs, and have offered guidelines for making decisions to pursue strategic programs in IT. The credibility of these principles will be established through further experience, and ultimately through more formal empirical study. Due to the size of the commitments and the period of
