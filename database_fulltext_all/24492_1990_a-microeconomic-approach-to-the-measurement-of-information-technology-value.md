# A Microeconomic Approach to the Measurement of Information Technology Value\*

PAUL ALPAR and MOSHE KIM

PAUL ALPAR is Assistant Professor in the Department of Information and Decision Sciences at the University of Illinois at Chicago. He received his doctorate degree from Goethe University in Frankfurt am Main, West Germany. In addition to work on the measurement of information technology value, his current research interests are in the use of information technology in small and entrepreneurial businesses and in business applications of expert systems. He has published in Annals of Operations Research, Information and Management, Interfaces, International Journal of Research in Marketing, and other European and international journals.

MOSHE KIM is Associate Professor in the Department of Economics at the University of Haifa, Israel. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Toronto, Canada. His research interests are focused on various aspects of bank production. He has published in journals such as Journal of Money, Credit, and Banking, Journal of Monetary Economics, and Journal of Business and Economic Statistics.

ABSTRACT: The problem of measuring the impact of information technology on economic performance at the business unit or enterprise level is receiving increased attention. We develop a methodology based on the microeconomic theory of production and apply the model to data from the banking industry. In addition, we compare our methodology to approaches based on key ratios of information technology deployment. Both procedures are applied to the same set of data. The results show that reasoning about information technology value based on key ratios may be misleading, especially when the figures are only calculated for a cross-section of data.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: Measurement of information technology value, production in commercial and retail banking, translog cost function, key ratios of firm performance and information technology deployment

## Introduction

THE EFFECTIVENESS OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS has been measured by many researchers by surrogate measures such as user satisfaction or extent of use. In addition to problems with the accuracy of the proposed measures, they could never be tied to the overall economic performance of a firm. Our research, in contrast to that work, is part of the effort to develop methodologies that directly measure the economic value of information technology (IT) to the firm. A recent review of research in the area of measurement of IT value has shown that efforts to quantify the value of IT are still scarce [15]. However, when comparing this overview with an earlier one [10], it becomes obvious that the field is growing. In addition, respected consulting companies increasingly address the issue in their studies, publications, and consulting practice [6]. The difficulty of making the IT investment decision is well documented [8]. The growth of interest in measuring IT value has led to a variety of approaches and to studies of various industries, but to no conclusive results, as pointed out by Kauffman and Weill [15]. Since the studies were conducted with different data sets, most of which are not generally available, it is difficult to compare the merit of different analytic approaches. This paper compares two approaches by applying them to the same data set from the banking industry.

The studies found in the literature also differ with respect to the time horizon of the data. Some use a single cross-section, some work with a panel of cross-sections, while a few use a longitudinal panel. The data used in this paper stem from a panel of cross-sections, but a longitudinal panel is also created by selecting only those respondents who participated in all surveys.

The main goal of this paper is to develop a methodology that allows measuring of the economic impact of IT on the production of bank services. This is done in the next section. Following that is the description of the data set to which our model is applied. Then, the key ratios approach is presented and adapted to banking for comparison purposes. The comparative statistical results and their discussion end the paper.

## The Cost Function Approach

THIS APPROACH IS BASED ON ESTABLISHED MICROECONOMIC THEORY. The starting point of the analysis is the desire to capture the underlying production state of the firm or industry and, in our case, also the economic impact of IT.

We represent a multi-output production by the following product transformation function

$$
\mathbf {Y} = F (\mathbf {X})\tag{1}
$$

where Y is the vector of outputs and X is the vector of inputs. It has been shown [19] that a proper representation of bank production should include multiple outputs rather than some aggregate of such.

Given that the specific firm behaves as a cost minimizer for every transformation function (1), there exists a cost function [11]

$$
C = H \left(Y _ {1}, \dots , Y _ {m}, W _ {1}, \dots , W _ {n}\right),\tag{2}
$$

which solves:

$$
\min _ {X _ {i}} \left[ \Sigma_ {i} W _ {i} X _ {i}: F \left(Y _ {1}, \dots , Y _ {m}, X _ {1}, \dots , X _ {n}\right) \leq 0 \right]\tag{3}
$$

where $W_{i}$ are the unit prices corresponding to the quantities $X_{i}$ in (1). Price-taking behavior with respect to inputs is a sufficient condition for equation (2) to uncover the true production state [18].

Conventionally the state of technology has been incorporated in the estimation of production functions by appending a variable, say T, to equation (2), which is supposed to reflect an index of technology. This leads to the following representation of the cost function

$$
C = G \left(Y _ {1}, \dots , Y _ {m}, W _ {1}, \dots , W _ {n}, T\right)\tag{4}
$$

However, the drawback of such a representation is that a time trend or a time dummy variable is often used for such purposes. This is an undesirable practice, since such a variable may capture things other than technological change that occurred over time. It therefore may not represent the pure effect of IT on production and costs.

In the proposed model, we specify an index of IT, denoted by V, which is a function of IT attributes to be elaborated on below. This approach has been previously used in other studies of production [24]. Since we analyze the banking industry, we also allow the cost function to be affected by the organizational nature of the specific banks by appending the variable B, which represents the number of banking offices, as is customary in the banking literature (e.g., [4]). Thus, the cost function we actually estimate is

$$
C = g (\mathbf {W}, \mathbf {Y}, V, B)\tag{5}
$$

where W, Y, V, and B represent a vector of input prices, a vector of outputs, an index of IT, and the number of branches, respectively.

In order to estimate equation (5), one needs to assure a certain functional form. We have chosen the translog functional form, which is a Taylor series approximation to any arbitrary cost function in which the highest terms are quadratic (of the second order). It is flexible in that it does not impose any restrictions on the underlying production function with respect to demand elasticities, elasticities of substitution, returns to scale, and other production characteristics [7]. Restrictive functional forms, such as the Cobb–Douglas, would not allow for such flexibility in that U-shaped average cost curves could not be derived even if the data warrant it. Specifically, the translog cost function is specified as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln C = \alpha_ {0} + \Sigma_ {t} \sigma_ {t} + \Sigma_ {f} \mu_ {f} + \Sigma_ {i} \alpha_ {i} \ln Y _ {i} + \Sigma_ {j} \beta_ {j} \ln W _ {j} + \tau_ {B} B \\ + \delta_ {V} V + 1 / 2 [ \Sigma_ {i} \Sigma_ {s} A _ {i s} \ln Y _ {i} \ln Y _ {s} + \Sigma_ {j} \Sigma_ {k} A _ {j k} \ln W _ {j} \ln W _ {k} + A _ {B B} (\ln B) ^ {2} \\ + A _ {V V} V ^ {2} ] + \Sigma_ {i} \Sigma_ {j} A _ {i j} \ln Y _ {i} \ln W _ {j} + \Sigma_ {i} A _ {i B} \ln Y _ {i} \ln B + \Sigma_ {j} A _ {j B} \ln W _ {j} \ln B \\ + A _ {B V} \ln B V + \Sigma_ {i} A _ {i V} \ln Y _ {i} V + \Sigma_ {j} A _ {j V} \ln W _ {j} V \end{array}\tag{6}
$$

where $\sigma_{t}$ and $\mu_{f}$ are time- and firm-specific (binary) effects to account for the “between” and “within” properties of a panel data set as suggested in the literature [23]. This is to preclude biases in the coefficients of the included explanatory variables that might arise from the exclusion of variables that are constant over time for a given firm. The $\alpha_{i},\beta_{j},\tau_{B},\delta_{V}$ , and the various $A$ 's are parameters to be estimated. They are used to calculate the various above-mentioned elasticities.

Applying Shephard's lemma $(\partial C_j / \partial W_j = X_j)$ to (6) yields a set of input demand equations in share form:

$$
\frac {\partial \ln C}{\partial \ln W _ {j}} = S _ {j} = \beta_ {j} + \Sigma_ {k} A _ {j k} \ln W _ {k} + \Sigma_ {i} A _ {i j} \ln Y _ {i} + A _ {j B} \ln B + A _ {j V} V\tag{7}
$$

where $S_{j}$ is the cost share of the $j$ th input.

Equations (6) and (7) are estimated jointly and therefore comprise the “cost system.” The IT index V, which entered both the cost function and the share equations, is in fact a function of the various IT attributes. Thus, the IT index is specified as a translog function of its attributes $\Gamma_{i}$ as,

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ln \varphi_ {V} \equiv V = B _ {0} + \Sigma_ {i} B _ {i} \ln \Gamma_ {i} \\ + 1 / 2 \Sigma_ {i} \Sigma_ {j} B _ {i j} \ln \Gamma_ {i} \ln \Gamma_ {j} \end{array}\tag{8}
$$

for the $i, j = 1, \ldots, k$ attributes. The $B_{0}, B_{i}$ , and $B_{ij}$ are parameters to be estimated. The $\Gamma_{i}$ are chosen in such a way that they are representative of IT use in banks. Specific banking technologies, such as ATM machines and computerized points-of-sale (POS), are of special interest in this context (see [2, 17]).

The index number formula (8) is estimated jointly with the cost and share equations system (6) and (7) by replacing the V terms in (6) and (7) by the right side of equation (8). In order to assure that certain economic properties of cost functions hold, e.g., that the cost shares in equations (7) sum up to unity, we impose restrictions on the parameters. These restrictions and further details on the estimation procedure can be found in [1].

Embedding (8) in (6) and (7) and imposing the proper parametric restrictions leads to the estimation of the underlying production, which is affected by the index of IT. The point to be stressed is the fact that the index of IT is not assumed separable from input prices, outputs, and other variables in the cost function, since its parameters $B_{i}$ and $B_{ij}$ are estimated jointly with all the cost function parameters. This way we allow for the IT index to affect the relative demand for the various factors of production. Moreover, the estimation of the system of equations consisting of (6) and (7) with (8) embedded in them enables us to measure the effect of IT on costs. The cost elasticity of IT is measured by

$$
\frac {\partial \ln C}{\partial \ln \Phi_ {V}} = \delta_ {V} + A _ {V V} V + \Sigma_ {j} A _ {j V} \ln W _ {j} + A _ {B V} \ln B + \Sigma_ {i} A _ {i V} \ln Y _ {i}\tag{9}
$$

Thus, the above specification points to the fact that the cost elasticity of IT is a function not just of the IT index V but also a function of input prices (via the $A_{jV}$ parameters), number of branches (via the $A_{BV}$ parameters) and the various outputs (via the effect of the $A_{iV}$ parameters). As a result, the effect of IT on the production (cost) technology is allowed to express itself in a non-homothetic manner if the data warrant it. This means: different levels of the $\Gamma$ attributes (the $\Gamma$ 's) may give rise to different factor proportions, and hence different costs.

Moreover, we can use the above methodology to investigate whether IT has been factor biased, that is, whether IT has been factor saving $(A_{jV} < 0)$ or factor using $(A_{jV} > 0)$ in the sense of whether IT has altered equilibrium factor shares holding W, B, and Y constant. In addition, one can investigate the effects IT has exerted on the various outputs, i.e., whether quantity demanded has changed due to the IT. The production or cost function approach has been considered the ideal way of linking IT to the business value it creates [16].

## The Data Set

THE DATA USED IN THIS RESEARCH WERE COLLECTED by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York in a program called Functional Cost Analysis. Within this program, banks voluntarily report detailed figures about items on their balance sheet, about the operating income, about interest and noninterest expenses, about the allocation of expenses to the different bank functions (e.g., demand deposits or installment loans), and about other miscellaneous items. In return, each participating bank receives a comprehensive report that contains average figures and additional ratios. In this report, banks are stratified in three groups according to their deposits. There are some doubts in the industry about the precision of the expense allocation to the different bank functions. This is of no concern in this research, because no use is made of these allocations. Clearly, the participating banks have a self-interest in correct reporting of figures. There are also a number of plausibility checks that help to maintain the consistency of data. Overall the quality of data, especially the nonallocated ones, is considered good and the data have been used in a number of studies of production characteristics in banking (e.g., [5, 21]).

For this research, data from years 1979 through 1986 were available. The sample size ranged from 624 to 759 banks, which represents about 4 percent of all banks in the United States. Among the responding banks, 175 participated in all years. The identity of banks was not disclosed (name and exact geographic location were missing), but each bank was distinguished by an identification number bearing no meaning. One caveat is in place: the majority of participating banks were small. Thus, the results reported below mainly apply to smaller banks. More specifically, in 1986 the median assets volume was \$103.4 million, ranging from \$7.7 million to \$27.0 billion. Except for the underrepresentation of the largest banks, the sampled banks are considered to be representative of the population of commercial banks [25].

## The First Key Ratios Approach

Bender [3] analyzed the impact of IT on the performance of life insurance companies. He computed the correlation between the ratio of information processing expenses to total operating expenses (IPER) and the ratio of total operating expenses to premium income using data of 132 insurance companies for the year 1983. The correlation coefficient was -0.27 (significant at the 5 percent level). In a following step, Bender grouped the companies by their IPER in 5 percent steps and displayed them against their ratio of total operating expenses to premium income. The group whose IPER fell between 20 and 25 percent had the smallest average ratio of total operating expenses to premium income (18.62 percent). On that basis he concluded that the optimal investment in IT might be at that ratio level. This approach will be later referred to as KEY1 in this paper.

## The Second Key Ratios Approach

Cron and Sobol [9] studied the impact of computer utilization on the performance of 138 medical wholesalers. With sales ranging from \$1 to \$10 million, these companies can be characterized as small businesses. Cron and Sobol defined four levels of computer usage based on the reported number of computer applications in use: no computer use, light computer use, medium computer use, and heavy computer use. The companies were also grouped into quartiles using four different profitability measures. Then, two-way frequencies were calculated for a pair of measures of computer utilization and of profitability. The analysis was conducted for the year 1979 only. One of the main conclusions of the analysis was that firms with heavy computer usage are either very strong or very weak performers. Light computer use was associated with poor or average performance. This approach will be later referred to as KEY2 in this paper.

## Adaptations of the Ratios Approaches to Banking

KEY1

Bender's approach [3] needed to be adapted somewhat to our data, because banking differs from the insurance business. The ratio of profit to equity was chosen to represent the firm performance. This ratio is called return on equity (ROE) and is considered the best performance criterion for banks (e.g., [27, 24]). Since there are several possibilities to define ROE [25], the exact definition of the measure used here follows:

$$
\mathrm{ROE} = \frac {\text { operating   income } - \text { operating   expenses }}{\text { equity   capital }}
$$

Operating income includes investment interest and dividends, interest from all types of loans, all loan and deposit service charges, trust fees, safe deposit fees, charges for data services for customers, and income from nonbanking functions. Operating expenses include all interest and noninterest expenses. The resulting profits used here are pretax profits. The use of after-tax profits is more common, but tax figures were not available. In this case, operating profits are probably a better measure anyway, since the use of IT has relatively little direct impact on taxes. Equity capital includes common stock, surplus, undistributed profits, and contingent reserves. Income and expenses in the numerator are flow variables. Therefore, the equity items in the denominator represent averages for the year as suggested in the literature. The “input” measure did not have to be changed; it remained the ratio of IT expenses to total operating expenses (IPER). The ROE and the IPER of the banks surveyed are given in Table 1 to give an idea of their economic performance and their expenses for IT in the time frame of the survey.

The table shows that the average economic performance of banks as measured by the ROE has been going up and down. This is still true when the medians are observed, which are not influenced by outliers. The variation in performance among banks is even more dramatic as the large standard deviations in years 1982, 1985, and 1986 show. The IT expenses as a percentage of total operating expenses have also been fluctuating, though independently of the ROE (as will be exactly shown below). However, the variation in the IPER among banks was much smaller than the variation in the ROE. It should be noted that the median ratio of IT expenses to total noninterest expenses was about three times the value of the corresponding IPER.

## KEY2

Cron and Sobol's approach [9] was adapted to our data in the following way. Return on equity was again used as the firm performance measure, and banks were classified into quartiles using this measure. The number of computerized applications was kept as the measure of IT utilization. Respondents of the survey could check whether a specific banking function was processed using a computer. The menu contained 17 functions, such as regular checking, real estate mortgage loans, and trust (see appendix for the full list). Since the level “no computer use” barely occurred, only three levels of (increasing) computer utilization were defined: light, medium, and heavy usage. Following Cron and Sobol, the limits were set so that each class had approximately the same number of members. The level of computerization has been growing over the years. Therefore, these limits had to be adjusted from year to year to maintain approximately the same frequency in each class. Table 2 shows the allocation of banks to three classes of computer usage based on the number of computerized applications.

The main problem of the ratios approach is that it disregards that economic performance (here measured by ROE) may change for many other reasons than IT. The approach does not take major factors of production and their relationship into account. Further, our approach accounts for differences in importance of different lines of business to various banks as all production outputs enter the calculations with their exact volumes. The key ratios approach completely disregards the structure of the output. Finally, characterizing companies as light, medium, or heavy users of IT only on the basis of the number of computerized applications does not seem to be an accurate approach, as it does not measure the real extent of computer usage.

## Cost Function Approach

Before reporting results obtained with the cost function approach, we need to report how we determined the specific input and output variables, IT characteristics, and input prices. The choice of inputs and outputs of bank production is not trivial. Clearly, labor and capital are production factors. However, a heated discussion exists in the banking literature with respect to the treatment of deposits. Here, the procedure suggested by Hancock [13] has been followed in making a decision as to whether certain deposits should be considered an input or an output. This procedure hinges upon the idea that whatever brings net revenues to the bank should be considered output and whatever brings about net cost should be considered an input. Information systems (IS) expenses were treated as a separate input factor. They were represented by the sum of total expenses for data services, total software expenses, and total off-premise computer expenses. We experimented with different attributes for the IT index. The final model contained the following variables:

output: installment loans, real estate mortgage loans, commercial and other loans, and demand deposits

input: time deposits, labor, capital, and IS expenses

IT index: number of ATMs and number of computerized bank functions

All input and output variables are measured in terms of dollars. However, the estimated equation is independent of the unit of measurement. This is because the translog function, as a Taylor series approximation around some arbitrary point, is calculated here (as is conventionally done) around the sample mean. Thus, all the above variables are measured in natural logarithms of the variable deflating them by sample means.

Note that in this context it is not important how a bank organizes the operations related to the two IT characteristics. Whether the ATMs are operated by the bank itself or a service firm, whether a computerized bank function is run on an on- or off-premise computer, the bank has to pay for these services somehow. If one were to study the organization of the information services function in banks, for example, such questions would be very important.

The input prices were determined as follows. The price of labor was calculated by dividing total salaries of all employees (including fringe benefits) by the total personnel. The price of capital was determined by dividing yearly expenses for occupancy and equipment by the book value of buildings and equipment. We note that, as practiced by all other researchers who estimated bank cost functions, the book value of building and equipment has been used as a proxy for the quantity of capital. In the absence of a better measure, we adopt this practice. The price for IS expenses was determined by dividing total IS expenses (as described above) by the average weekly CPU hours. The price for time deposits was obtained by dividing total time deposits interest expenses by the value of all time deposits. Obviously, these measures are just the best available proxies for some “real” prices. The described price calculations are based on the various previous studies of bank production $[4, 5, 12, 13, 20, 21]$ .

Our estimation shows that the cost elasticity of IT, measured by $\partial \ln C/\partial \ln \phi$ , is -0.1987 (with an asymptotic -statistic of -2.7 and an $R^{2}$ for the system of equations of 0.86), indicating that IT has been cost reducing. In other words, a 10 percent increase in IT has been associated with a decrease in total costs of about 1.9 percent. Heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation are not present. $^{1}$ The effects of IT on the various inputs and outputs are presented in Table 5. A positive sign of a coefficient for an output indicates that an increase in IT has been associated with an increase in that output. A positive sign of a coefficient for an input factor indicates that IT was factor using. In other words, with all else held constant, an increase in IT has been associated with an increase in that factor's cost share. Negative signs of coefficients are interpreted accordingly.

Thus, IT has contributed to the reduction of the amount held by individuals in demand deposit accounts and to the increase of time deposits. This is perhaps due to the fact that IT enables depositors to move their balances in a rapid manner from no- or low-interest-bearing accounts into higher-bearing (time) deposit accounts. IT supported an increase in other loans and a decrease in installment loans. This latter result is not intuitive and needs further exploration. The impact of IT on capital and labor was as expected: IT was capital using but labor saving.

## Discussion

THE FOLLOWING DISCUSSION IS CONCENTRATED ON THE ANALYSIS of the above approaches as they have been applied to the common data set. The approach KEY1 produces results that may lead to different conclusions depending on the year of the cross-sectional data. There is no basis to assume that the results of one year could be generalized for a longer time period. Especially in the case of large and strategic IT investments, one would expect that benefits accrue over a longer time period. Observing the correlation coefficients for the panel of cross-sections would lead to the conclusion that no conclusion about the association of the two ratios can be made. With a panel of cross-sections, one can observe some long-term effects of IT investments. However, the results need to be interpreted with great caution, because of the possible significant turnover of respondents in the panel. In the case of bank data, only 10 percent of the banks participating in the program in the period from 1979 through 1986 did so in all years.

Harris and Katz [14] have extended Bender's analysis of insurance companies. They concentrated on technology leaders, and observed the investments in IT and the overall performance of these firms over a period of three years. Their use of key ratios gives an interesting insight into the use of IT in insurance companies. The authors did not attempt to define an optimal IT investment level, but they did make some conclusions about IT impact on overall performance. Again, such conclusions must not be drawn when major factors of production (labor and capital) are not considered. On the one hand, any correlation between IT investments and firm performance may be spurious. On the other hand, a lack of a correlation does not prove that IT has no impact on performance.

The application of KEY2 leads to inconclusive results. While this result is not surprising, based on results obtained by Cron and Sobol, the confusion that the approach generates is even bigger now. The reason is that the analysis has been extended over eight years (rather than just one). If the differences in class associations are significant at all, one can obtain completely different results, depending on the survey year. It does not seem that the number of computer applications alone can explain the overall firm performance. While the number of computer applications in banks has been continuously rising over the years, this was not the case with ROE. In other words, adoption of IT does not guarantee performance gains, and certainly does not guarantee competitive advantage. Simple association measures between an IT characteristic and firm performance do not capture pressures from competition on the firm's cost and profit structure.

Using the results from the cost function approach as a benchmark, it appears that none of the ratios approaches could identify the “true” relationship between IT deployment and enterprise level performance. The latter approaches are also not capable of answering important economic questions about the relationships between

IT and production factors, or other characteristics of a given production setup. As shown above, our approach makes it possible to measure how the demand for input factors has changed with the use of IT. Since we estimate the impact of IT together with all major input factors and production characteristics, we can observe exactly the cost and output changes associated with changes in IT.

A drawback of our approach might be its complexity and the difficulties in determining the appropriate inputs, outputs, prices, and IT index attributes. It should also be remembered that, since the translog function was approximated around the sample mean, extrapolation of the cost function must be exercised with caution for data points that are far from the mean.

In the next steps, we intend to study other interesting aspects of the role of IT in bank production based on our specification of the cost function. Specifically, we want to analyze the interaction between IT and economies of density (i.e., influences of bank branching on production costs [20]) and economies of scope (i.e., cost consequences of joint production of bank services [12]).

The above argumentation had the purpose to explain and demonstrate why the analyses of association between key ratios or simple characteristics of IT use on one side and key ratios of enterprise performance on the other side are not appropriate approaches to the measurement of IT impact. While both KEY1 and KEY2 could be refined, they do not seem capable of capturing enough of the complexity of production processes of goods or services. This does not mean that the observation of key ratios is not meaningful. They are excellent indicators of the state of a firm and an industry. The papers from which the key ratios approaches were borrowed were valuable contributions to the area of IT value measurement, as they were among the first to address the issue. Now, the time for more rigorous approaches has come (such as those applied by Loveman [22], Banker and Kauffman [2], and the one proposed here). Their application may be still hindered by a lack of data. However, if the value of the rigorous approaches can be demonstrated to firms, they will be more willing to collect the necessary data and possibly report them to trade associations, regulatory bodies, or other agencies. These agencies can provide the necessary confidentiality, while making the data available to researchers who can provide firms with appropriate models for the measurement of IT value.
