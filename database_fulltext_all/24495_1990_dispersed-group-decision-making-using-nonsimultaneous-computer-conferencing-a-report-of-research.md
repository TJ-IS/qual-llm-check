# Dispersed Group Decision Making Using Nonsimultaneous Computer Conferencing: A Report of Research

JILL Y. SMITH and MICHAEL T. VANECEK

JILL Y. SMITH is Assistant Professor of Management Information Systems at the College of Business Administration, University of Denver. She received her Ph.D. in Business Computer Information Systems from the University of North Texas. She is also a research associate in UNT's Information Systems Research Center. Her research interests include the communication components of group decision support and IS planning. She has published in Journal of Systems Management, Information & Management, and Journal of Behaviour and Information Technology. In addition, she consults with medical doctors in planning for information systems.

MICHAEL T. VANECEK is Regents Professor of Business Computer Information Systems at the University of North Texas. He was the founding director of the College of Business Administration's Information Systems Research Center. He received his Ph.D., with a major in Accounting and Information Systems, from the University of Texas at Austin. He also has an M.B.A. from the University of Houston as well as B.S.E.E. and M.E.E. degrees from North Carolina State University. Dr. Vanecek's research interests include human factors and behavioral issues, information systems technology impact, telecommunications and distributed systems, and methodological issues in information systems research. He has published articles in MIS Quarterly, Information & Management, Journal of Systems Management, Journal of Accountancy, and Journal of Accounting, Auditing, and Finance.

ABSTRACT: A quasi-experiment was conducted in the field to compare the decision-making process for groups using a nonsimultaneous computer conferencing (NCC) system with similar groups using face-to-face (FTF) communication. Groups of five participants performed group decision making using a general knowledge task. Group members searched for the solution by sharing information and generating alternatives. The participants were professional workers from several different organizations.

The results of the study suggest that groups using the NCC system were significantly less effective than FTF groups in solving the problem. NCCs shared less information, were less comprehensive in considering all aspects of the decision-making task, and made less progress toward the perceived decision goal.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: Group decision support systems, computer conferencing, nonsimultaneous computer conferencing, teleconferencing, task-oriented decision making, interpersonal communication.

## Introduction

MANY RESEARCHERS HAVE SUGGESTED THAT PROFOUND and perplexing changes occur with the use of group decision support systems (GDSS) [7, 8, 14, 17, 20, 21, 28]. However, most GDSS research focuses on group decision making within an environment that supports simultaneous and concurrent communications. For example, the participants meet in a common room where they use both electronic and face-to-face activities as aids to their decision making. Part or all of these activities may use some form of simultaneous computer conference (SCC). In contrast, our study examines a related but different situation. We studied group decision making using a nonsimultaneous computer conference (NCC) by participants who were geographically dispersed.

Our paper first outlines the research problem and dual purposes for conducting this quasi-experimental field study. The ensuing section explains the theoretical research framework that defines and anchors the experimental variables to previous communication research. Past GDSS research can be divided into three themes: (1) studies comparing GDSS decision rooms to local area decision nets, (2) studies examining SCC, and (3) studies focusing on NCC. The next section presents the research hypotheses, followed by a detailed methodology, data analysis, and discussion of results. The paper includes some suggested research extensions prior to its conclusions.

## The Research Problem

THE RESEARCH PROBLEM IS THAT RESEARCHERS DO NOT KNOW what effect using an NCC may have on people's ability to perform high technical/low social decision-making tasks [33]. Some have suggested that an NCC is appropriate for situations involving geographically dispersed decision makers [4, 6]. However, little is known about the effects of computer-mediated communications on dispersed group decision making.

Evidence about the quality of dispersed group decisions using NCC technology compared to using face-to-face (FTF) communications is sparse. For example, Kiesler [23] suggests computer-mediated communication provides a potential “technological fix” as one of its intended effects. Thus, NCC presents potential savings in reduced travel, in less time away from the job, in reduced “telephone tag,” and in freedom from the limitations of geographic time differences. Missing from the research, however, are the implications of Kiesler’s “third-order” effects. We do not clearly understand what happens to communication effectiveness when social context cues are absent [23]. These cues include tone of voice, nonverbal communication, status symbols, FTF turn-taking, and politeness norms.

## Purpose

THE PRIMARY PURPOSE OF OUR STUDY WAS TO COMPARE the effect of NCC on dispersed group decision making relative to an FTF process. The study measured several post-communication variables while controlling task type and information available. The variables measured included the amount of shared information, comprehensiveness, progress toward goal, and freedom to participate. One measure—deviation from the correct answer—provided the outcome result variable.

A second purpose of this research was to replicate and extend a previous study $[34, 35]$ . Smith and Vanecek $[34]$ reported that FTF groups (1) shared more information; (2) derived more correct reasons for not selecting wrong alternatives (significance); (3) considered more important case attributes (comprehensiveness); (4) perceived more progress toward the goal; and (5) perceived more opportunity to participate when they wished. Further, communication quality (deviation from the correct answer) was greater in the FTF condition. The results of the previous study cast doubt on the utility of using an NCC for task-oriented decision making.

The current extension and replication, however, has several design improvements. The first study used a simulated conferencing system, student subjects, a two-member group, and a two-hour time frame. In contrast, the current study used a well-established computer conferencing system (the EIES computer conferencing system based at the New Jersey Institute of Technology). The subjects were business professionals in the field. Also, most of the NCC subjects had some experience using computer conferencing technology. A two-week EIES NCC time period increased the nonsimultaneous nature of the NCC activities. Finally, the groups were a typical size (five members).

## Theoretical Research Framework

THE COMMUNICATION VARIABLES SELECTED FOR STUDY stem from a Temporal Framework of Task-Oriented Interpersonal Communication, portrayed in Figure 1, developed by Smith and Vanecek [34, 35]. The framework is particularly amenable to studying alternative forms of communication activities. The framework incorporates research on IS and end-user communications done by DeBrabander and Thiers [3] with that of interpersonal communications done by Rogers and Kincaid [32], Leathers [26], and Heslin and Dunphy [16]. The following sections briefly summarize the framework with emphasis on current study variables.

## Antecedent-Communication Variables

Antecedent-communication variables are those that exist prior to a communication interaction. Human attributes are comprehensive, encompassing personality traits and demographic characteristics. Personality traits (e.g., cognitive problem-solving style, and internal–external control) and demographic characteristics (e.g., age, profession, and education) have profound cumulative impacts on ensuing communications.

Relative power position, semantic gap (i.e., professional differences in vocabularies), and third-party intervention (i.e., a change agent) also exist prior to (and will affect) information-sharing communication [3, 6, 27]. So too will the controlled variables in this study—task type and information available.

The task in our study was intellective and required a technical exchange of information. In comparison, socioemotional exchanges include activities such as understanding, giving aid, or, in an opposite vein, critically evaluating another's contribution. Since task type is a pivotal variable in the GDSS literature [7], a later section relates our task to the spectrum of possible GDSS task types.

Our other antecedent-communications variable is information available. The amount of information shared may be less than the information available during a communication process. Individuals may be unwilling to share information they have, or not perceive it to be important, and thus not share it. By controlling the information available, researchers may then measure the amount of information shared (a variable under post-communication).

## Communication-Mode Variables

Besides the FTF communications channel, the other modes are primarily remote communication activities. The primary difference in remote modes of communication is whether participants need be physically connected at the same time. With the telephone, and in most teleconferencing situations, the communications activity is simultaneous. However, a computer conference may be either simultaneous or non-simultaneous. Our study used nonsimultaneous communication.

## Post-Communication Variables

These variables provide efficiency indicators of the communication interaction. The amount of information shared is a measure of the meaningful data interchange among participants.

Other post-communication variables include Leathers' [26] productivity dimensions of small group communications. These include effectiveness, feasibility, creativity, significance, and comprehensiveness. Comprehensiveness, a variable in this study, is the degree to which the decision or solution reflects a response to all dimensions of the problem.

Our study also considered two of Heslin and Dunphy's [16] satisfaction (or dissatisfaction) indicators: perception of progress toward a goal and perceived freedom to participate. Heslin and Dunphy culled these two factors along with status agreement from a literature search of 450 studies. These factors represent three ubiquitous dimensions to group-member satisfaction. The perceptions pertain more to the communication process than to the decision. Perception of progress toward a goal and perceived freedom to participate are defined as:

Perception of progress toward a goal.- the group member's perception of the value of the problem-solving session in the progress made toward stated goals and objectives. Satisfaction depends more on perceived than actual progress.

Perceived freedom to participate.- the salient relationship between participation and satisfaction. Specifically, it is each group member's perception of freedom to participate and not the degree of participation [35, p. 126].

Freedom to participate differs from measuring discrete participation events. The latter measure has been the dominant pattern in prior GDSS research $[18, 33, 37]$ . Perceived freedom to participate may be a better indicator of satisfaction for the group experience than a measure of the actual amount of participation per participant. In a given decision-making situation, it may not be desirable that all members participate equally. Some may be more knowledgeable or have a greater psychological investment in the decision.

## Outcome-Result Variables

The outcome-result variable is communication quality that is a measure of effectiveness and has two components. The component pertinent to this study was deviation from the correct answer. The other component, convergence, means the degree of mutual agreement and understanding present as a result of the communication interaction [32].

To summarize, in relation to the theoretical framework, the post-communication variables include the amount and comprehensiveness of shared information, progress toward goal, and freedom to participate. The outcome-result variable was one component of communication quality—deviation from the correct answer. The quasi-experiment controlled the task type and information available, both of which are antecedent communication variables. This framework, as the next section illustrates, is consistent with the existing stream of GDSS research models.

## Prior Research

SEVERAL RESEARCHERS ARE STUDYING GDSS processes and structures. Unfortunately, the domain of GDSS research is quite large and variable. As a result, most research has used a micro model to examine a subcomponent of the overall GDSS taxonomy [4]. This section briefly examines two GDSS research taxonomies and an existing stream of GDSS pertinent to this study.

## GDSS Contingency Model

DeSanctis and Gallupe [7, 8] advocate an elemental, iterative approach to GDSS research as a means to generate cumulative knowledge. They suggest a simple-to-complex progression of research designs to gain insights on needed features and impacts of GDSS.

The progression of research designs suggested by DeSanctis and Gallupe includes three different levels. Each higher level represents an increasing degree of technical sophistication and a dramatic intervention into group exchange activities. At level 1, the emphasis of the technology is on the facets of the GDSS as a communications medium. Level 2 adds automated planning tools [23]. The progression of the continuum to level 3 adds more sophisticated technology. For example, level 3 would include expert systems used to structure and filter communication patterns (e.g., an automated parliamentary process). The NCC used in our study is at level 1 of the DeSanctis and Gallupe framework. The task type is intellective. Member proximity is dispersed and group size is small (five members).

## EMS Taxonomy of Environments

Dennis et al. [4] synthesized an electronic meeting systems (EMS) taxonomy from the work of several researchers. Their model also consists of three dimensions: group size, group proximity, and time dispersion. Their model divides groups into small (ten or fewer) and large. Group proximity is an indication of geographical dispersion. Time dispersion classifies the currency of GDSS activities. Their definition of an EMS illustrates how these dimensions are related:

... an information technology-based environment that supports group meetings, which may be distributed geographically and temporally. The IT environment includes, but is not limited to, distributed facilities, computer hardware and software, audio and video technology, procedures, methodologies, facilitation, and applicable group data. Group tasks include, but are not limited to, communication, planning, idea generation, problem solving, issue discussion, negotiation, conflict resolution, systems analysis and design, and collaborative group activities such as documentation preparation and sharing. [4]

Dennis et al. do not explicitly address the issue of nonsimultaneous group work. Instead, they concentrate on defining the EMS environments as they relate to the simultaneous work groups. However, they suggest that, although little research has been done in the area of nonsimultaneous settings, the potential does exist for the creative application of the EMS technology in that area.

## Comparability of Prior Findings

Several researchers are examining related but quite different GDSS issues. As a result, it is often difficult to make comparisons across studies. For example, Dennis, Nunamaker, and Vogel [5] earmarked two related but very different research streams: (1) local area decision nets (LADN) versus GDSS decision rooms, and (2) LADN versus FTF conferences with no computer support.

One caveat that exists when examining GDSS research is the question, “How well is the research related?” Others in the GDSS research domain have asked similar methodological questions $[11, 13]$ . Comparison among studies is difficult for at least the following reasons.

1. Variables may be labeled similarly, but if measured differently, findings are not comparable. For example, the definition for participation by Siegel et al. [33] was similar to Hiltz et al. [18], but differed from Smith and Vanecek [34].

2. Cross-study interpretation of results demands consistent task types. Most of the related studies have used intellective, high technical, and low social tasks.

3. Research using an NCC mode for decision-making tasks is sparse. Much of the experimental research compares simultaneous computer conferencing (SCC) activities to FTF meetings having no computer support. An NCC mode is different from an SCC mode. Hence, the generalization of SCC research results into NCC environments can be very misleading.

4. A LADN may consist of just the communication components included in a computer conference. However, it may combine computer-mediated communication with more advanced GDSS technological features. These features include organization of alternatives, voting, and ranking techniques [12]. This additional communication structure may also influence the research results and lead to comparability difficulties.

## Research Hypotheses

THE FIRST HYPOTHESIS POSITS A SIGNIFICANT DIFFERENCE in the communication modes when measured by the combination of five dependent variables interacting with the independent variable, communication mode. Additional hypotheses predict that for each dependent variable studied, significance will favor FTF communication modes. The posited significance of these substantive hypotheses is consistent with our theoretical analysis and the reported results of previous research. The hypotheses are:

H1: There will be a significant difference among communication modes as measured by a combination of the (1) amount and (2) comprehensiveness of the shared information, (3) perception of progress toward goal, (4) perception of freedom to participate, and (5) deviation from the correct answer.

H2: There will be significantly more shared information in the FTF communication mode than in the NCC mode.

H3: There will be significantly greater comprehensiveness in discussing important task attributes in the FTF mode compared to the NCC mode.

H4: There will be significantly greater perception of progress toward a goal for an FTF conference compared to an NCC.

H5: There will be significantly greater perception of freedom to participate for an FTF conference compared to an NCC.

H6: There will be significantly less deviation from the correct answer for an FTF conference compared to an NCC.

## Methodology

THIS STUDY CONSISTED OF A QUASI-EXPERIMENT IN THE FIELD to compare the group decision-making performance between experimental and control groups. The sample consisted of seven NCC experimental groups and ten FTF control groups. The seven NCC experimental groups consisted of five geographically dispersed subjects. The difference in the number of groups resulted from operational difficulties in managing geographically dispersed NCC groups. However, an examination of the data did not reveal any evidence that the difference in the number of groups injected any material bias into the results.

Each NCC experimental session lasted for two weeks. The experimental period selected was somewhat arbitrary, since we did not have any comparable previous experimental results to use as a guide. However, we did confer with EIES staff before deciding on the two-week period. Although subjective, we believed that two weeks was sufficient to solve the case. We thought that any longer time would result in the participants losing interest in the case and not finishing.

The ten FTF control groups consisted of five subjects who convened for approximately 45 minutes to one hour to discuss their case verbally. All groups (NCC and FTF) had to make a group decision by the end of their session(s).

## Subjects

Subjects for four of the five-member NCC groups were volunteer members of EIES located in all parts of country. Dallas-based employees of ARCO, GTE, and Burlington Northern Railroad made up the remaining three groups. Eight of the FTF groups came from the membership of the Association for Systems Management (ASM). Professional members of the Administrative Management Society (AMS) volunteered for one FTF group and Zales' corporate headquarters employees made up the remaining FTF group.

Random assignment of subjects to experimental and control groups was not possible. The groups were either dispersed geographically (NCC) or came together for a single meeting (FTF). This operational requirement made it impractical, especially with very busy volunteer participants drawn from organizations and businesses in the field, to use random assignment. However, the general characteristics of the experimental and control groups were comparable. As a result, the research design of this study is quasi-experimental.

## NCC—Dispersed Proximity

Participants using an NCC as a communications medium for decision making do not have to be signed on at the same time. As a result, message flow will be nonsimultaneous and sequential. Also, the participants may be dispersed geographically.

Two fundamental forms of nonsimultaneous, computer-mediated communication exist: electronic mail and computer conferencing. With electronic mail, communications consist of discrete messages sequentially spaced in time. The NCC differs from electronic mail in that messages are not discrete, but rather form a shared text of sequentially attached conference comments. Each participant peruses the sequential transcripts of comments made by all group members. The participant is free (while logged on to the computer conference) to add to this growing list of comments at any time. Further, a group moderator may monitor the conference and act as a decision-making facilitator. However, in this study, the moderator acted only to facilitate system use.

Participants may also send private electronic mail messages to each other outside of the conference file. Thus, when group members log on, they have the ability to communicate privately with other members. Additionally, they can peruse and add to a complete transcript of the ongoing conference. In this instance, the NCC acts as a communication component of a GDSS for geographically dispersed decision makers $[9, 34]$ .

## Task Type—Intellective

The characteristics of the intellective task type used in this study include shared data gathering, data interpretation, alternative generation, and alternative-assessment activities. It is a “high technical/low social” activity. Herold [15] describes high technical/low social tasks as:

technically complex. . . (in terms of searching for an optimum solution) that may require technical skills, use of complex methods, thorough knowledge of various environmental contingencies, etc. But once a response has been determined, it is accepted on its technical merits or soundness of approach, with social processes playing a minor role. [15, p. 317]

The reason for selecting a high technical/low social task was that the literature suggests that computer-mediated communication may be appropriate for doing such a task. Daft, Lengel, and Trevino [2] and Korzenny and Bower [25] believe that communication-medium richness must match the degree of ambiguity or subjectiveness of the task. Richness is the degree of feedback, multiple verbal/nonverbal cues, language variety, and personal focus available with the communication medium.

FTF communication may be a better medium for tasks demanding more communication richness. The richness of FTF may enable mutual understanding and agreement between group members. Empirical studies suggest that computer-mediated communication lacks important social context cues necessary for higher levels of social demands $[23, 24, 33, 36]$ . On the other hand, some indication exists that NCC may be appropriate for exchange of highly technical information and routine decision making $[31]$ .

The task was a murder mystery adapted from the original published in Pfeiffer and Jones [30]. Succinctly, there are seven murder suspects. The group must select one suspect as the murderer, based on the information they shared during the conference. Each participant had a different case version. The information consists of two unique clues embedded in each case version. Other than the unique clues, the cases were identical. To solve the case correctly, participants had to uncover and share their clues.

Admittedly, a murder mystery is not a typical business-oriented subject. However, we selected this case for four reasons. First, the case required the requisite cognitive processes for an intellective, high technical/low social task. Also, the mystery required enough information exchange to discover who committed the crime. It was technically complex in that it required searching for an optimum solution among the many different clues. Participants had to use abstract and objective reasoning. They also had to have an understanding of the relevance of the available information (clues). They had to evaluate several different contingencies. Conversely, the search for the solution was a low social task activity.

A second reason for using this task was to provide a general knowledge case that a diverse subject population could solve. The subjects represented a wide range of professional interests. The murder mystery required only general knowledge as opposed to expert knowledge of a particular functional area. Hence, this avoided biases due to differing levels of expertise in a specific knowledge area.

Third, the mystery facilitated variable measurement, because one correct answer existed. A definite reason existed, based on the shared information, to reject each competing alternative. Further, pertinent case attributes (e.g., blood type, age) were predetermined, thus providing a measure for comprehensiveness.

Fourth, an acknowledged purpose of this study was to replicate and extend with improved design features our previous study [34]. Consequently, we wished to keep the task constant across the two experiments.

## Data Collection and Analysis

Primary data collection consisted of an analysis of NCC hardcopy transcripts and FTF recorded audio cassettes. The administration of the semantic differential scales provided the attitudinal measures. Statistical analyses of significance consisted of ANOVA and MANOVA tests using SPSS-X.

Table 4 gives the descriptive statistics for each variable by communication mode. Empirically, each variable fared better in the FTF mode, both in the mean score and in the standard deviation.

The investigators used MANOVA to test the statistical significance of the mean differences shown in Table 4. This test is appropriate because of the known correlation among the dependent variables. MANOVA requires a larger mean difference for significance. This reduces the probability of the alpha error—that the null hypothesis is rejected when it is, in fact, true. Table 5 displays the multivariate results and Table 6 displays the univariate F-tests for each dependent variable.

The MANOVA test was significant at p < 0.031. Thus, the results support rejecting a statistical hypothesis of no significant difference based on Hypothesis 1. A significant difference does exist between communication modes measured by a combination of the (1) amount and (2) comprehensiveness of shared information, (3) perception of progress toward goal, (4) perception of freedom to participate, and (5) deviation from the correct answer. The results of the univariate F-tests (Table 6) indicate which variables were responsible for the MANOVA difference.

As seen in Table 6, three dependent variables are significant at the p < 0.05 level. They are: amount of shared information (p < 0.001), comprehensiveness (p < 0.033), and progress toward goal (p < 0.000). Therefore, hypotheses 2, 3, and 4 are not rejected, and hypotheses 5 and 6 are rejected at the 0.05 level. From the greater mean scores displayed in Table 4, we conclude more shared information, more comprehensive analysis of case attributes, and more perception of progress toward the goal occurred when groups used the FTF mode for decision making. However, contrary to the previous study [34], the mean differences between the two communication modes are not significant for perception of freedom to participate nor deviation from the correct answer. ## Discussion of Results

IN TERMS OF THE THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK, the observation that NCC groups were less comprehensive is one indication that an NCC may be less productive than FTF meetings [26]. Again, comprehensiveness is an indicator of how many of the pertinent case attributes the participants discussed. To reiterate, the case attributes in this case were blood type, age, weight, height, location at 7:00 p.m., and location at 10:00 p.m. Each of these attributes, connected with a shared clue, presented a reason not to arrest one of the six nonguilty suspects. Since NCC groups shared less information (in the form of clues), it is reasonable that the comprehensiveness score was depressed in the NCC condition.

Progress toward the goal is one factor in group satisfaction with their communication activities [16]. That NCC groups perceived less progress toward the goal indicates less satisfaction and may partially explain other findings that dispersed groups are less satisfied with computer-mediated communication [12, 22].

The transcript analysis suggests that perceived progress toward the goal may be exasperated by NCC. Many participants appeared frustrated by the time lag between asking a question and receiving a reply. Sometimes a reply never occurred, sometimes the reply came after several intervening comments. The latter event tended to dilute the impact of the reply.

The lack of real-time communication strapped progress and caused potential frustration for some. For example, one individual initially summarized his data rapidly, exchanging his two unique clues—one suspect in police custody, and blood types A and B found at the murder scene. Then he requested the blood type for one suspect, which was missing from his data. Four days later an answer came.

Several “flip sides” to often-cited NCC advantages surface in these transcripts. Frustration from the lack of immediate feedback is the flip side to the NCC advantage that people need not be present at the same time. Certainly such frustrations might contribute to a perceived lack of progress toward a goal. As one person lamented:

... as time passes, the information seems to go a bit stale. I'm losing track of what we've been considering and find myself having to invest more time in reviewing what has been said so far. I would like to bring this to a closure. I'm feeling frustrated and impatient.

Another advantage to NCC is that people may choose when they wish to participate. However, the corresponding flip side observed in the NCC transcripts is that participants have to be more self-regulating. This is particularly true if participation is by choice (as in this experiment). Conferees often had to be urged to log on and contribute to the discussion. While this encouragement could be perceived as a study limitation, we believe that it may reflect a tendency to procrastinate in the absence of FTF pressure.

This may be a characteristic of NCC that future designers and researchers should consider.

When people log on to an NCC, they must spend a certain amount of time reorienting themselves to what is happening. This is not a new phenomenon. Reorientation is necessary whenever one studies a text, sets it down to do something else, and then renews the study activity. However, this phenomenon is another flip side to the NCC advantage of having a written transcript to study.

There is yet another flip side to a suggested NCC advantage. The advantage is that the nonsimultaneous aspect will give one more time for reflective thought before responding. While this is true, we also noted that too much time seemed to preclude a targeted response. There was a tendency to get sidetracked easily; analysis paralysis was often apparent. For example, consider the following transcript comments:

Make sure they get full Miranda rights! Anybody that loses evidence for Miranda failure is suspended!

Arrest them all. Fingerprint to secure identity of each. Arrange a line-up that will stand up in court. Emergency referral to Crime lab for hair analysis....

... they probably are on federal or state parole with substantial regulations on their behavior. We need to contact the parole departments to see if they have current violations that will enhance the length of the period we can hold the suspects.

Lack of immediate feedback is inherent in an NCC. In addition to reducing perceptions of progress toward goal, the lack of immediate feedback may also explain why the NCC produced less shared information and a less comprehensive discussion of case attributes. The NCC in this study was less effective than the FTF conference in stimulating new avenues of thought, new opportunities for consensus.

Unless someone took the initiative to integrate ideas, we observed the “multi-headed animal syndrome” operating. Doyle and Straus [10] coined this term to describe a meeting where participants maintain their own “world views,” are supported by their own data, and perceive others as not cooperating. Perhaps researchers could devise human and/or technological systems to counteract this tendency. However, an integrative social structure did not operate as naturally during the NCC process as compared to the richer FTF communications process.

## Research Extensions

FUTURE RESEARCH SHOULD EXTEND THE TWOFOLD EXPERIMENTAL limitations of our study. First, we had limited control over motivational factors. The participants were not solving an actual business-oriented decision-making problem. Due to operational necessity, we expected and observed that their motivation to complete our artificial task was lower than that associated with their job demands. The subjects participated on a volunteer basis. We were not able to offer any external incentives. Also, they were solving a task having very specific characteristics (a high technical and low social intellective task). Hence, one should not generalize the results to all types of business tasks. Even so, however, many business decision tasks do have high technical and low social characteristics. If the NCC were the only means of communication available, geographic separation a necessity, and the problem very real to the participants, then computer-mediated communication might have different results. Past research indicated that NCC might be suitable for routine decision making [31]. However, decision making under crisis conditions may be more suited to NCC than the more routine decision-making events simulated in this study.

Second, the NCC was simply a sequential succession of participant statements. EIES tracks the individual's place in the conference and provides search facilities. However, more advanced technology may enhance people's ability to keep track of what is happening—at least enable them to better organize their thoughts. For example, one might want to follow several threads of thought through the message database. We would like to see hypertext features incorporated with an NCC in the future.

Another potential research extension is to study how an NCC may contribute to a GDSS in other ways than as a decision-making communication channel. In other instances, NCC has proved to be effective and satisfactory for informing, checking, probing, updating, and coordinating activities. An NCC may be well suited to planning a GDSS or to integrating communication between FTF GDSS sessions.

## Conclusion

In comparison with FTF group decision making, the results suggest that a nonsimultaneous, and thus sequential, computer conference may not be an effective communication medium for a GDSS. For the quasi-experimental design used in the study, a nonsimultaneous computer conference (NCC), in comparison with a face-to-face (FTF) process, was found to reduce the sharing of information, hinder a complete discussion of the case attributes, and decrease the perception of progress toward the goal. This is particularly true if the NCC lasts too long or if participation is by choice. Further research is necessary to discover what machine and human design features might enhance an NCC. Researchers also should examine how an NCC might augment planning and interim FTF GDSS activities. More advanced technology—a higher-level NCC GDSS that supports a better organization of thoughts within the message database—might give different results.
