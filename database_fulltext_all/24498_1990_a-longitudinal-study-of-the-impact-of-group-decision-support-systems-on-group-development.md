# A Longitudinal Study of the Impact of Group Decision Support Systems on Group Development

LAKU CHIDAMBARAM, ROBERT P. BOSTROM, and BAYARD E. WYNNE

ABSTRACT: This paper reports on a study aimed at integrating an important but neglected behavioral issue—group development—into group decision support systems (GDSS) research. Group development is based on the fact that most groups have a past and a future, and this affects group outcomes. However, most GDSS studies have investigated only a single group session. This study examined the behavior of groups using a GDSS over multiple sessions.

The study reported in this paper specifically examined the following two questions: (1) does computer support affect the development of decision-making groups? and (2) do the patterns of development differ over time between computer-supported and manual groups? These two questions were studied using a two-factorial repeated measures research design. Results showed significant differences in development patterns between computer-supported groups and manual groups in terms of conflict management and degree of cohesiveness. After adapting to GDSS, computer-supported groups displayed more productive conflict management and higher group cohesiveness than manual groups.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: group decision support systems, group behavior, group development.

## 1. Introduction

THE IMPORTANCE OF GROUP DECISION MAKING and the drawbacks of face-to-face meetings have prompted academicians and practitioners to explore new techniques and technologies to help teams develop into effective decision-making groups. Recently, a new technology called group decision support systems (GDSS), aimed at improving team functioning, has gained prominence. Yet, the important behavioral issue of how groups form and grow over time has been largely ignored in the GDSS literature. Most GDSS studies [e.g., 11, 34] have examined the effect of computer support during the first (and in fact the only) meeting of ad hoc groups. GDSS researchers [39, 41] and group dynamics researchers [4, 21] have pointed out the problems of using zero-history groups to study group behavior. The primary focus of this paper is to examine the impact of GDSS on group development.

This paper reports on a study that specifically examined the following two questions: (1) does computer support affect the development of decision-making groups, and (2) are the patterns of development different between computer-supported and manual groups (in terms of group cohesiveness and the group's ability to manage conflict)?

Section 2 develops the theoretical foundation for the study and reviews the relevant group development and GDSS literature; section 3 describes the research framework and methodology used. Section 4 presents the results of the statistical analysis, and section 5 discusses these results. Section 6 outlines some of the limitations and assumptions of this study, and section 7 presents a brief summary and conclusion.

## 2. Theoretical Foundations, Prior Research, and Hypothesis

AN IMPORTANT ISSUE WHEN STUDYING GROUP DEVELOPMENT is what constitutes a mature, well-developed group. This question has not been examined critically either in the group behavior literature or in the GDSS literature. Based on a review of the group development literature $[2, 3, 7, 10, 19]$ , it appears that a well-developed group has the following characteristics:

1. It is a well-knit, cohesive unit, with members working toward the same common goal and not at cross-purposes.

2. Members learn to handle major conflicts productively, while maintaining differences of opinion.

3. The group is capable of productive work; the members are creative, efficient, and effective.

4. Groupthink, group myopia, and other disruptive forces are at their lowest in well-developed groups.

This study focused on the impact of GDSS technology on issues (1) and (2): the degree of cohesiveness and the ability to manage conflict. A review of the literature on group cohesion and conflict management indicates a reciprocal causal linkage between these two variables. Thus, cohesive groups are more likely to handle conflict constructively [28], and groups that manage conflict productively are more likely to be cohesive in the long run [33, 39].

Both productive conflict management and group cohesiveness have been linked to a number of positive outcomes (including factors 3 and 4 above), whereas the lack of these variables has been tied to negative outcomes. For example, positive consequences of productive conflict are a heightened awareness of problems; a proclivity to change; enhanced motivation, morale, and cohesion; better decisions; greater creativity; and group maturation [37]. Some consequences of destructive conflict are stress, misallocation of resources, and diminished performance [16]. Cohesive groups tend to work harder to achieve group goals [1, 17], communicate more evenly [18], exert stronger pressures on members to conform to group norms [6], and have higher job satisfaction than noncohesive groups [17].

Chidambaram and Bostrom [7] reviewed group development studies and found no consensus on the type of developmental pattern that groups undergo. The research suggests several contingency factors, such as task characteristics, member abilities, technological support, and organizational environment, affect group development. The common theme in these studies is that groups follow some pattern of development unique to their particular settings. Therefore, this study did not test for a specific development pattern. Rather, the intent was to see whether computer-supported groups and unsupported groups follow the same developmental pattern in a particular setting.

Adaptive Structuration Theory (AST), as described by Poole and DeSanctis [25, 26], provides insight into how groups develop in a given situation, especially when technology is introduced. AST holds that a primary goal of group action is adaptation to the situation. Exogenous variables, such as task and technology, form the context in which a group develops. Group outcomes are not directly affected by exogenous variables (e.g., technology), but rather by how these variables are appropriated and used by the group. AST focuses on group interaction processes as the key to what "structures" are appropriated and reproduced from the group's context, and how the structures are adaptively applied to meet the group's outcomes.

Thus, in investigating the effects of GDSS, the important element is the structures in the group promoted by the technology and not the features of the technology per se. Poole and DeSanctis [26, pp. 9–10] define “structures” as the “rules and resources which actors use to generate and sustain [the group entity].” They further distinguish two aspects of technological structures: the spirit, “the general goals and attitudes the technology aims to promote” (ibid., p. 10) (such as promoting diversity, consensus, and/or democracy); and the structural features, specific rules (e.g., anonymity) or resources (e.g., specific GDSS tools) that operate in the group. The spirit is a larger frame of reference that integrates a set of rules and resources.

A GDSS provides a set of structures in the form of linked software tools, as well as global structures such as anonymity, simultaneity, process structuring support, electronic recording and display, and enhanced information processing capabilities [5].

The GDSS makes these structures and their benefits available for appropriation (or not) by the group. It is our general hypothesis that GDSS structures facilitate the appropriation and reproduction of structural features that lead to the development of mature groups.

Specifically, in this study we examine the following hypotheses:

H1: The group development profile representing the ability to manage conflict for GDSS groups will be different from that of manual groups.

H2: The group development profile representing the degree of cohesiveness for GDSS groups will be different from that of manual groups.

Based on a review of the literature, we developed a list of structural features of groups that are associated with productive conflict management and group cohesion (see Table 1). For example, simultaneous entry and anonymity support active and equal member participation, while anonymity and electronic display (esp. public screen) promote members being critical of ideas, not persons. A notation of “yes” in column 3 or 4 indicates support in the literature for our claims. Question marks indicate that there is limited or no support. The double arrow between cohesion and productive conflict management symbolizes their reciprocal causal relationship.

However, GDSS features may be misused and have a negative impact on group development. For example, anonymity may result in overly negative expression of emotions (reverse of feature 7 in Table 1), thereby resulting in negative conflict. Another example is that electronic voting could be used to cut off a discussion of alternatives instead of facilitating their exploration (feature 3). We expected these negative appropriations to be minimized by strictly enforcing a structured environment and by studying the groups over time.

The GDSS literature has generally ignored the issue of group development. No GDSS study has investigated group cohesiveness, and only one has examined conflict management $[27]$ . This is an interesting issue, given that a number of studies have found that GDSS increases the amount of conflict experienced by groups $[11]$ . A probable reason for this lack of attention on group development is the almost exclusive use of single-session experiments or field studies to investigate GDSS effects. AST and other group development models, and our own experience with facilitating “real world” groups $[5]$ , clearly document the need for studying groups over multiple sessions.

The Poole et al. study [27] examined conflict behavior in computer-supported groups compared to unsupported (manual and baseline) groups. However, they focused on behavior in a single session. They found that GDSS groups did not manage conflict as well as manual groups, and this was reflected in their meeting behavior and outcome measures (e.g., positional change). There was some evidence that the technology got in the way of productive conflict management. Recent research by Sambamurthy [30] further highlights this point. His research demonstrated that comfort with a GDSS was critical to a group's successful appropriation of its use.

Citing the AST perspective, the Poole et al. study [27] concluded that GDSSs will not always result in less effective conflict management. Our study created an entirely different context to study conflict behavior—different tasks, different systems, multiple sessions, etc. Our hypotheses, as presented above, were that over time GDSS groups would appropriate structures that promoted better conflict management.

## 3. Research Design and Methodology

THE RESEARCH DESIGN WAS A TWO-FACTORIAL REPEATED MEASURES DESIGN. The computer-support variable had two levels—GDSS and no GDSS (manual). Each of these two treatment conditions was given four different tasks—one per session—at intervals of one week.

Figure 1 provides an overview of the conceptual and operational aspects of the key variables in this study. Each of these variables is discussed below.

## 3.1. Independent Variables

## 3.1.1. Group History/Time

Initially subjects were randomly assigned to groups, and groups were randomly assigned to one of two treatment conditions. After the initial assignment, members remained in the same group, and groups in the same experimental condition, until the end of the study. Groups met once a week for four consecutive weeks. They did not have any opportunity (other than this study) to work together as a group. Hence, the number of times a group met to work on a problem was manipulated.

## 3.1.2. Computer Support

Access to computer support was manipulated. Two experimental treatments, GDSS and manual, were employed. GDSS-aided groups had computer support in generating alternatives, evaluating them, and choosing a solution. Manual groups did not have computer support.

The University of Arizona's GROUPSYSTEMS software was used for all GDSS groups, and flip charts were available for all manual groups. The task structure/agenda was implemented using three separate GROUPSYSTEMS tools to generate (Electronic BrainStorming), review (Issue Analysis), and evaluate (Voting) decision alternatives (see [9] for tool descriptions). All GDSS groups used the same sequence of tools. Manual groups, following the same agenda, used flip charts for recording and reviewing alternatives and for voting. All GDSS sessions took place in the Institute for the Study of Developmental Disabilities (ISDD) Collaborative Work Support Lab at Indiana University. This lab is an electronic meeting facility, comprised of ten microcomputers with color displays seated on a U-shaped table, connected to a rear-mounted public screen and linked by a Novell LAN.

## 3.2. Dependent Variables

## 3.2.1. Ability to Manage Conflict

Conflict can have either a positive effect or a negative effect on a group, depending on how it is handled. In this study, a group's ability to manage conflict was measured, not the existence of conflict. A postsession questionnaire was used to obtain data about the demonstrated ability of the group to manage conflict. The questions were developed based on a review of team development instruments used in practice and a review of the literature on group conflict, and were validated in a pilot study.

## 3.2.2. Degree of Cohesiveness

The degree of cohesiveness indicates how close members feel to each other and their attraction to the group. Seashore's Index of Group Cohesiveness [31] is a widely used instrument for measuring group cohesiveness. However, the instrument is more applicable to actual work teams in firms than to student groups. Hence, the wording of the instrument was changed in this study to relate more to student groups. The scales were also anchored using a five-point Likert-type scale rather than the four-point scale used in the original instrument. The modified instrument was pretested and found to be reliable and simple to administer, and to take very little time for subjects to complete (see [8] for more details).

## 3.3. Controlled Variables

## 3.3.1. Task

The four tasks were business case analyses that involved strategic decision making by the board of directors of a hypothetical international wine maker. Tasks were tested for equivalence, flexibility, understanding, complexity, and need for specialized domain knowledge by a panel of expert judges (see $[8]$ for details). They were decision-making tasks with no right answer on McGrath's $[20]$ task circumplex. A single consensus decision was required to induce group members to make compromises and trade-offs. The tasks involved similar multistage processes (see Table 2) and a set agenda structure was used for both manual and computer-supported groups. The study's four tasks were created to be of moderately high complexity, because research has shown that GDSSs do not help groups in the execution of simple tasks $[11]$ . Each group performed a different task in each session. A pilot study utilizing 60 subjects was carried out to validate the tasks and the experimental procedures.

The repeated measures design offers a greater degree of precision than completely randomized full factorial designs. It also requires fewer subjects to achieve the same amount of statistical power of other designs. However, this design may suffer from order or learning effect. The order or learning effect refers to the improvement of subject scores merely due to the repetition and ordering of tasks. A solution suggested by many researchers, including Myers [24], is to randomize the order of treatments. Hence, the four tasks developed for this study were randomly assigned to all participating groups with computer support. The same task order was matched for groups without computer support.

## 3.3.2. Group Size

Group size—a variable of interest in other studies $[39, 41]$ —was held constant in this study. Groups of five members were used. The choice of number of members was based on a review of the group size literature $[14, 15, 35, 36]$ , which suggests that five is the “optimal” size.

## 3.3.3. Individual Differences

Differences in cognitive abilities and psychological traits of individuals may account for differences in group behavior and performance [20]. Random assignment of subjects to groups and groups to treatments was undertaken to control the effect of this variable on group outcomes.

One hundred forty student subjects from five sections of an undergraduate management class participated in the experiment. Most of the students were juniors and seniors with a major in business and at least a few months of part-time work experience. Students had the option of writing a term paper or participating in an experiment to fulfill a course requirement. At the end of the study, the group with the highest quality decisions, in each treatment condition, was given a prize of \$50.

## 3.3.4. Physical Environment

The physical proximity of members, seating arrangements, lighting, heating, and other physical considerations may also affect group performance $[11, 20, 32]$ . The physical characteristics of the environment were the same for all groups. The only difference between the groups was the availability of computer support for GDSS groups and no computer support for manual groups.

## 3.3.5. Time Taken

The maximum time available to make decisions was the same for both treatments. Every activity was allotted a specific time limit. Hence, the maximum termination time for each activity, during each session, was controlled. If a group finished a particular activity before its scheduled time limit, it could proceed to the next activity immediately. The facilitator(s) ensured that the specified time limits were met by all groups. The maximum time for each meeting was 90 minutes.

## 3.3.6. Facilitation Factors

Two facilitators—one male and one female—were used for the entire study. While an attempt was made to assign the two facilitators equally to both treatment conditions, logistical difficulties prevented such assignment. However, an ex post analysis of subject responses to questions concerning facilitation effects revealed that individual facilitators had no significant impact on the outcome of the meeting for either experimental condition.

## 4. Results of Statistical Analyses

THIS SECTION PRESENTS THE RESULTS OF THE STATISTICAL ANALYSES carried out to test the hypotheses. An alpha level of 0.05 was used to test for statistical significance.

The total sample size included in the analysis consisted of 28 groups of five members each. Each group met four times during the course of the study. A presession survey required subjects to provide some background information relevant to this study (e.g., whether the subject liked to work in groups). Post-hoc tests showed no significant differences between the subjects assigned to the two treatment conditions based on this background information.

## 4.1. Measures

The ability to manage conflict was measured using a postsession questionnaire. The two questions pertaining to conflict management had a seven-point Likert-type scale that subjects used to record their perceptions. The conflict management score for a group was the average score of all members in that group. The conflict management score for an individual member was the sum of the scores on both questions. Hence, the total conflict management score for a member took into consideration the member's perception of how openly the group dealt with conflict and how effectively the group handled it. The reliability (Cronbach's alpha) of the two questions in measuring conflict management was calculated at 0.75.

The degree of cohesiveness was measured using a modified form of Seashore's Index of Group Cohesiveness [31]. Each member recorded his/her perceptions after each meeting. Each response was worth a maximum score of 5 points and a minimum score of 1 point. A simple additive formula provided the cohesiveness index for each group member. The possible range of scores was from 5 to 25. All individual member scores were totaled and averaged for each group. The reliability for the modified instrument was calculated as 0.89.

## 4.2. Mean Analyses

T-tests were used to test differences between the pooled means of groups in the two treatment conditions (see totals in Tables 3 and 4). The results indicated there was no significant difference between the GDSS groups and manual groups in terms of conflict management ( $\alpha = 0.35$ ) or degree of cohesiveness ( $\alpha = 0.45$ ). However, these results only show there was no difference between the four session averages; it does not reveal what occurred during each session or differences in development profiles over the four sessions.

## 4.3. Multivariate Analyses

Development profiles (hypotheses H1 and H2) were tested using multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) for repeated measures designs. The two appropriate test statistics for these analyses are Hotelling's $T$ -squared and Pillai's $V$ statistic; both have an $F$ distribution. These two statistics were calculated in this study and are provided along with other relevant information.

The model was first tested for two major assumptions made in the multivariate analysis of variance: (1) equality of dispersion matrices, and (2) multinormal distribution of dependent variables. The first assumption was tested by calculating Box's M and verifying its significance. The second assumption was tested by generating a plot of the dependent variables and verifying the graph visually. Both assumptions were found to hold true in testing hypotheses H1 and H2.

After the two assumptions underlying the MANOVA model were met, the profile analysis to test for differences between the ability to manage conflict among manual groups and GDSS groups was carried out. Pillai's statistic of 0.34 and Hotelling's $T$ -squared of 0.51 both have an $F$ -value of 3.57, which was significant ( $\alpha = 0.031$ ). Hence, the ability to manage conflict among manual groups was significantly different from the ability to manage conflict among GDSS groups across the four sessions (supporting hypothesis H1). Figure 2 provides a visual representation of the profiles of the two groups. A post-hoc analysis was conducted to identify differences in the ability to manage conflict between the two groups during each of the four sessions. The results during the fourth session were exactly the opposite of the results during the first session. Looking at the results in Table 3, it is evident that the ability to manage conflict for the two groups started to converge during the first half of the study and thereafter began to diverge. ## 5. Discussion of Results

THE TWO DEPENDENT VARIABLES OF INTEREST IN THIS STUDY were the ability to manage conflict and the degree of cohesiveness. The results indicate that both of these variables were initially higher for manual groups. This pattern, however, reversed itself in sessions three and four, when GDSS groups scored significantly higher than their manual counterparts. The discussion below explores some reasons for this pattern.

## 5.1. Adaptation

Comments made by subjects in postexperimental debriefings indicated the major explanation for the GDSS development profiles was that GDSS groups needed time to appropriate the technology effectively, i.e., to become comfortable with the technology. In GDSS groups, the introduction of the technology was an alien element. None of the subjects had used a GDSS before, hence they were apprehensive about using it for group communication. Moreover, some subjects said that initially it was difficult to curb their natural tendency to talk during idea generation. An insightful comment was made by one of the subjects in a GDSS group: "The system prevents interaction, until you learn to adapt." This explanation coincides with the general Theory of Structural Adaptation, and the specific findings of Poole et al. [27] and Sambamurthy [30] as to the importance of the group's level of comfort with the technology. These findings highlight the importance of training and experience with the technology in both practice and research settings.

The subjects' comments and facilitators' observations also indicated that a key reason for differences in GDSS and manual group development profiles was the formation of cliques and subgroups. Putnam [29] argues that coalition formation is an integral part of group development. As groups meet more frequently, the coalitions are strengthened and conflict increases. It also becomes more difficult to manage conflict, because coalitions tend to wrench groups apart, making it difficult to develop group cohesion. In manual groups, it appeared that coalition formation was faster than in GDSS groups, because it was easier to identify other members' attitudes and personalities. In GDSS groups, the process of coalition formation was more difficult, because most of the discussion was task oriented and the role of personalities was kept to a minimum. Also, the anonymity of input reduced the emergence of strong, dominant personalities early in the development of the group. Hence, as the experiment progressed, the coalitions in manual groups seemed to grow stronger, making conflict management and cohesion building more difficult.

## 5.2. Midpoints and Time

An additional explanation of the results deals with the role of time in the developmental patterns of task groups. Recent research $[12, 22]$ raises some very interesting questions about the use of time. Gersick $[12]$ , for example, found that each of a number of natural groups working with a specific preset task and a preset time limit made drastic and dramatic changes at almost exactly the middle of the group's lifespan, even though the different groups had quite different lifespans. There is little understanding of what causes this shift in behavior at the halfway point. The change appears to be triggered by a realization that time is growing short (why at midpoint rather than one-third, etc., is not known). Our student groups were aware of their preset time limit (4 sessions, 90 minutes each) and the general nature of the tasks they were to accomplish. Although their changes were not dramatic, they followed this midpoint shift pattern.

The role of time within and among GDSS sessions is an unexplored area worthy of attention. McGrath's [21] proposed time-based theory of group functioning and the material in his recent book [22] are good starting points. The emerging efforts to develop a social psychology of time need to be integrated into Adaptive Structuration Theory (AST). AST provides a framework to study the complex and time-related aspect of interactions in an Input—Process—Output model in which group processes are viewed as changing over time [25].

Based on the qualitative data from debriefing sessions, a number of key structural features that contributed to the GDSS groups' higher maturity level (i.e., more effective conflict management and higher group cohesion) in later sessions were identified. The features in Table 1 that appeared to be most prominent were: (1) active and equal member participation; (2) criticism of ideas, not persons; (3) exploration of alternatives; (4) use of structured procedures; and (5) task focus. The qualitative data indicate that the GDSS structures did facilitate (once groups were comfortable with technology) the appropriation and reproduction of those structural features that lead to the development of mature groups. Further research is needed to study these appropriation processes more directly and in greater depth.

## 5.3. Process Management

The generation and discussion of ideas were unevenly distributed in manual groups. Ideas generated by dominant individuals were discussed the most, whereas ideas generated by others did not receive as much attention. Although such observations were more difficult to make in GDSS groups because of anonymous input, it appeared that the GDSS created a context of active and equal participation.

Putnam [29], in her review of group conflict, states that one of the key issues in effective conflict management is related to handling procedural details, such as what ideas to discuss first and when to follow an agenda. Both manual and GDSS groups had a strict agenda that was enforced by the facilitator. However, the GDSS offered added procedural support, especially during the discussion phase of the experiment. For instance, GDSS groups would almost invariably start their discussions with the first issue on the screen and continue sequentially until they reached the last issue. Rarely, if ever, did the discussion start at the middle of the list. The fact that only a subset of the ideas (maximum of 20) was visible on the screen at a time, and the fact that the screen “scrolled” from top to bottom, caused groups to discuss ideas sequentially. Moreover, the facilitator would “open” the entire idea on-screen (as only the first line of each idea was visible) to facilitate discussion. This would keep all the attention of the group focused on the idea being discussed.

On the other hand, in manual groups the discussion would frequently begin with the dominant members speaking positively about their own ideas. This tended to inhibit “sequential” discussion of ideas in manual groups. Hence, the conversation would often take a rambling and tangential route—one in which important issues would be “lost in the shuffle.” Hence, the degree of procedural support offered by the system was lacking in manual groups. According to Putnam [29], absence of procedural protocols could lead to ineffective conflict management.

## 5.4. Global System Attributes

Another factor that contributed to group development in GDSS groups is the anonymity offered by the system. It enabled members of GDSS groups to focus on an idea, independent of who generated it. It also appeared that members of GDSS groups were more likely to think of an idea as “our” idea or the “group’s” idea, especially after several ideas had been merged together. However, in manual groups, the lack of anonymity prevented members from freely discussing and merging ideas. There was often a sense of “my” idea versus “his” or “her” idea, and this resulted in lack of consensus and ultimately in ineffective group development.

The role of the public screen in providing procedural support and creating a group identity (i.e., cohesion) was important. In GDSS groups, all ideas appeared on the public screen and were the focus of the entire group. Hence, during a discussion all members would turn to the public screen and discuss the ideas displayed on screen. The public screen became the place where “our” or the “group’s” ideas were displayed, manipulated, and discussed. Rarely did members look at each other while discussing ideas; this helped keep discussions objective and task-related. Members were more objective, because entering ideas into the GDSS and viewing them on the public screen allowed them to disassociate themselves from their ideas. This type of dynamic tended to reduce negative emotional responses and enhance constructive emotional participation. More research focusing on the role of the public screen and this disassociation process in GDSS settings is needed.

The postexperimental debriefings also indicated that the anonymous voting process helped GDSS groups manage development better. In GDSS groups, members could rank order the alternatives anonymously without intimidation by other group members. In manual groups, voting was not anonymous. Many subjects in manual groups said they were rather tense when it was time to vote, because they were concerned about offending other members of their group. This tension seemed to increase among manual groups as the study progressed.

The above discussion suggests a number of factors that might explain the differences in development profiles between GDSS and manual groups. Since this was one of the first longitudinal studies using GDSS in a lab setting, we felt the focus on outcomes using a simple Input-Output model was appropriate. Future studies need to adopt an Input-Process-Output model and focus on processes that lead to particular outcomes. These studies can provide added insight into group development and how a GDSS can facilitate this process. Interaction analysis and process tracing will be important methodological tools in future studies [41]. The Poole et al. [27] study provides a framework for interaction analyses of conflict-managing behavior in GDSS environments.

## 6. Limitations and Assumptions

A KEY LIMITATION OF THE STUDY IS A SIDE-EFFECT OF THE RESEARCH DESIGN USED. Experimental control is more difficult to maintain in studies using repeated measures designs, as subject interactions outside the laboratory setting cannot be monitored [24].

However, student subjects were asked not to discuss their laboratory experiences with other subjects. Moreover, subjects had no other opportunity, except within this study, to interact as a decision-making group. Also, at the end of every session the facilitator reminded the group not to discuss the study with members of other groups, as this would jeopardize their chances of winning the \$50 prize money. Analyses of responses from the postsession questionnaire revealed that very few subjects had discussed the study with members from other groups. Hence, the issue of group development was studied without significant contamination by outside influences.

A major assumption of the study was that group development could be studied in four sessions. However, “real world” groups may take longer to develop. But the literature on group development suggests that in lab situations groups can be made to develop faster and their development studied in fewer meetings. This was done in this study by soliciting subjects early in the semester, so they had little or no opportunity to work together as a group, except for this experiment. Moreover, the frame of the experiment was explained to them at the start of the study. Hence, subjects were told specifically that this was a four-session study at the start of the experiment, and this fact was reinforced every time the groups met. It would be interesting for future research to study groups over even more sessions and/or to provide better training at the beginning in order to examine the impacts on the group’s development profiles.

Despite some of the limitations and assumptions described above, this study was the first to examine the issue of group development in computer-supported groups in a controlled lab setting. The assumptions made in the study were, for the most part, logical and supported by theoretical or empirical evidence. Some limitations of this study are inherent in all laboratory studies using repeated measures designs, and are not unique to this particular one. However, caution should be exercised in generalizing the results from this study to other populations and environments. The results may not be true for other kinds of groups (e.g., unlimited duration, larger groups, non-face-to-face groups, and nonhomogeneous groups). Moreover, the results may be dependent to a large extent on the type of tasks and technology used. These are issues that need further empirical investigation.

## 7. Summary and Conclusions

THIS STUDY COMPARED THE BEHAVIOR OF GDSS AND MANUAL GROUPS over four sessions in a controlled lab setting. In general, groups with computer support and those without computer support exhibited different patterns of development over time. Post hoc analyses also provided added insight into what happened during each session. The ability to manage conflict and the degree of cohesiveness were both higher for manual groups during the first session. However, this was not true for the entire duration of the study. As the experiment progressed, GDSS groups became more cohesive and managed conflict better than manual groups.

Group behavior changed over time for GDSS and manual groups. These findings are important for GDSS researchers, because they suggest that the first-time behavior or performance of groups does not tell the entire story. In order to have a more complete understanding of the impact of GDSS on group behavior, longitudinal studies such as this one should be undertaken in the future.
