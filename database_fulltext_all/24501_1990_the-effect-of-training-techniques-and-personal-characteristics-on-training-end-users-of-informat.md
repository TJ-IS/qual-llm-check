# The Effect of Training Techniques and Personal Characteristics on Training End Users of Information Systems

DONALD L. DAVIS and DONNA F. DAVIS

DONALD L. DAVIS is Professor of Computer Information Systems at Southwest Missouri State University. He received his Ph.D. in Business Administration from the University of Florida. His current research interests are in user-system interfaces in DSS and expert systems.

DONNA F. DAVIS is Assistant Professor of Computer Information Systems at Southwest Missouri State University. She received her Ph.D. in Business Administration from the University of Mississippi. She is pursuing research in problem recognition and classification in decision making for DSS.

ABSTRACT: End-user training is considered to be one of the top ten issues for management information systems management in the 1980s. Central to addressing end-user training is the appropriate training method for use to secure the most efficient and effective results. This study explored, by experimental means, the two extremes of training techniques: the lecture method with a trainer, and the self-study method. Also, the study examined the effect of end-user personal characteristics on end-user learning performance in the training environments. The results of the study provided some guidelines for organizations pursuing end-user training, and suggestions for additional study.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: end-user training, human information processing, end-user characteristics, end-user computing.

THE IMPORTANCE OF END-USER COMPUTING cannot be overemphasized, as is illustrated by the following statement from a research colloquium involving academic and industry leaders in information systems on the challenges for information systems research: “. . . there was general agreement that the next stage in IS is end user systems” [25, p. 249]. Further evidence is provided by the forecast that end-user computing will consume up to 75 percent of the corporate computer budget by 1990 [2]. This projection, when coupled with the fact that “user education” was determined to be the sixth most important issue of the key information system issues for the 1980s [11], and held that same position in a recent update of that study [3], places a high priority on end-user training.

A recent study on information centers by Carr [7] found that more competent or literate users provide improved productivity, better information, and an enhanced view of data processing. In a field study on personal computing trends and problems, Guimaraes and Ramanujam [18] found that the most crucial problem facing the 400 firms examined in the study was lack of user education.

With the increasing need for company employees to become more computer literate, there is a concurrent need to explore how best to prepare them to assume their end-user roles. Naturally, the management of organizations expects the most efficient and effective techniques to be used in training their employees for this increasingly important role.

Many techniques are available for end-user training, e.g., a lecture-type format or a self-directed method. Several techniques are discussed by Sprague and Carlson [34]. The various techniques are only variations of the two extremes—formal lecture and self-study. Those two extremes are the ones most commonly used in industry end-user training [16]. However, there may be characteristics of the end users that could call for one or the other methods of instruction, e.g., how the end user processes information, sex, level of education, age, and previous computer experience and/or training.

Most learning theories cite individual and group differences as either having mediating or direct effects on learning $[12, 13, 15]$ . This study examines those differences that have been both cited in the learning theories and included in other MIS papers as having some effect on other variables of interest in MIS, such as quality, performance, or satisfaction. Thus, the independent variables in the study include age, previous training, education, prior experience, and other personal and psychological characteristics that have been suggested to affect the use of a corporate information system by employees $[9, 14, 32, 33]$ . The appropriate training method may depend on evaluation of one or more employee characteristics to select the most efficient and effective training technique, or a mix of those techniques.

A recent analysis of the literature concerned with end-user education led Cheney, Mann, and Amoroso [8] to call for additional research into the “most effective forms” for end-user training. In search for answers to some of the questions raised by the previous discussion, an experimental study was conducted to explore the problem of selecting the most effective techniques for training an IS end user for certain job-related tasks.

## The Study

END-USER COMPUTING HAS BEEN DESCRIBED AS “... the direct, hands-on use of computers by end users—not indirect use through systems professionals on the data processing staff” [35, p. 285]. One categorization [30] defines six types of end users who need varying levels of IS knowledge of both software and hardware. The software used may range from simple menu-driven access or report generation, to command and procedural-level languages. Within these broad categories, end users perform many IS job-related roles. Even though they are professionals (e.g., accountants), during their careers many write programs, develop small systems, and control databases. Therefore, they may assume the roles of analyst, programmer, database administrator, data entry personnel, and/or operator and manager of a particular system. In these roles they will use some or all of the IS skills, including concepts and programming. Generally, end users acquire those skills through formal instruction (using a trainer) or through self-study [17].

The authors designed an experiment to explore both of those training avenues for answers to several questions concerning end-user learning, including examining which is the most effective of the two techniques.

Some other questions organizations may have concerning end-user learning are:

1. Is age a factor in an employee's ability to learn to be an effective end user?

2. Does an employee's level of formal education affect his/her ability to learn the skills required of an effective end user?

3. Does the gender of an employee affect his/her ability to learn to be an effective end user?

4. Does the manner in which an employee processes information affect his/her ability to learn to be an effective end user?

5. Should the age, level of formal education, gender, or way in which an employee processes information affect the choice of the appropriate training technique (lecture or self-directed) for the employee?

6. Should the fact that an employee has had previous training or previous computer experience affect the choice of the appropriate training technique (lecture versus self-directed) for that employee?

This study compared the formal lecture method with a self-directed method of acquiring knowledge of both a programming language and concepts of computerized information systems, as outlined in Table 1. Also, the training techniques were examined to determine if they had different levels of effectiveness for subjects with different human information processing (HIP) styles, and for other subject characteristics (i.e., sex, age, previous experience, and previous training). The lecture method consisted of a daily one-hour lecture conducted by an experienced, highly rated instructor (determined by both the previous two-year evaluations of all instructors by students and by peer evaluation).

In order to provide the same information content to both groups of subjects (lecture and self-directed), a written transcript of the daily lecture was made available to the self-directed subjects for examination at their convenience. All study participants had a schedule of study topics, readings, and a textbook. Beyond those provisions and the availability of assistance in the microcomputer laboratory on request, the subjects in the self-directed group provided their own direction for learning the material. The technique was not actually self-paced, since all 91 participants in both the self-directed and lecture groups were required to participate in regularly scheduled exams and to submit programming assignments. However, the method involved self-study by the subjects, i.e., no classroom (trainer) instruction was given.

The microcomputer facilities were available to all subjects. The assistants in the laboratory acted as consultants to the subjects, providing information on the use of the computer hardware, and help with syntax errors. No aid was provided on logical errors.

The requirements for subjects involved in both methods were the same: two scheduled exams and a final exam, in addition to five BASIC language programming assignments. Subjects' evaluations were based on the exams and assignments.

## Subjects

Participants in the study were students in a first-level IS course at the college of business of a large university. Of the 91 subjects who participated, 59 percent were female and 41 percent male. The subjects ranged in age from 18 to 39 years, with a mean of 26 years. Their educational level ranged from 12 to 18 years of formal education, with a mean of 14 years.

Previous experience using computers in an organization was reported by 23.7 percent of the subjects, ranging from 1 month to 36 months experience, with a mean of 11 months. In contrast to actual experience with an information system, 22.6 percent of the subjects had previous instruction in computerized information systems, ranging from 2 to 24 months, with a mean of 7 months.

## Learning Theory

## Learning can be described as:

... a more or less permanent change in behavior which is not attributable to maturational variables or to factors producing relatively temporary or cyclical effects (such as drugs or fatigue). [5]

According to Carlson [5], all learning theories can be classified into three categories: associationistic, functionalistic, and cognitive. Associationistic learning theories stress the connections formed among environmental events and the assumed resulting behavior. These theories view learning as the process of the formation of those associations, while functionalistic learning theories emphasize the functions that the behavior serves. The stimuli initiating the action and the resulting behavior are not considered to be as important as the outcomes or consequences of the behavior. Both associationistic and functionalistic learning theories consider the appropriate variable for the study of learning to be the exhibited behavior rather than the internal processes of the individual involved in learning. In these theories the internal mental process is viewed as a black box, while the cognitive class of theories places the major emphasis on internal processes—perception, thinking, planning, and decision making—rather than on the external environment and overt responses.

The most prominent theory under study now in the cognitive area of learning is human information processing (HIP) [1, 22]. HIP is generally considered to consist of various internal subsystems. The number of subsystems and their process depends on the specific model, but the basic model (Figure 1) involves sensory storage registers for input, short-term memory, and long-term memory. The sensory registers are characterized by a very rapid loss of information. These registers feed into a short-term memory subsystem that can hold a small amount of information only if an active effort is made to retain its contents. The short-term memory in turn feeds into a long-term memory with a huge storage capacity, where information need not be actively maintained. Learning takes place by appending new information to networks of previously learned material in long-term memory [1]. Learning can then be considered as a byproduct of HIP [16].

Human information processing is based on the proposition that behavior is determined by the internal flow of information. Since that internal flow is not directly observable, surrogate methods are used to determine the types of flow. One of those methods is HIP style.

It should be noted that there is a large amount of research involving HIP and IS [32, 38]. However, the focus of that body of research is decision-making performance and/or the design of information systems. The focus of this study relative to HIP is on learning behavior, and its effect in different training environments.

## Human Information Processing (HIP) Style

HIP style is a term used to refer to “an individual’s way of performing perceptual and intellectual activities” [37, p. 90]. That is, the process consists of two activities, perception and evaluation. Perception involves the input, filtration, and condensation of cues from the environment, while evaluation involves analyzing and reaching a conclusion based on what is perceived. In particular, an individual’s HIP style may describe how an individual learns, i.e., inputs, assimilates, and stores information.

There are several instruments available to measure individual HIP style. A description and critique of most of those instruments can be found in Robey and Taggart [31]. The instrument used to measure HIP style in this study was the Myers–Briggs Type Indicator [26]. The indicator follows the psychology of types developed by Jung [21] and is self-administered. Based on the scoring of the instrument, an individual is assigned to one of two preferred modes for perceiving and one of two preferred modes for evaluating what has been perceived. The two modes for perceiving are sensing and intuition. Sensing is perceiving by means of the five senses. Intuition is an “indirect perception by way of the unconscious, accompanied by ideas or associations which the unconscious tacks on to perception from the outside.”

The two modes for evaluating are thinking and feeling. Thinking is a logical process, impersonal and rational in nature. Feeling, on the other hand, is a process of appreciation and is subjective in nature. The feeling type is characterized by evaluations in the good/bad, pleasant/unpleasant vein.

The modes are mutually exclusive, and the two modes for perception are independent of the two modes for evaluation. Thus, the four modes can be partitioned into four HIP styles:

1. Sensing-Thinking (ST)

2. Sensing-Feeling (SF)

3. Intuition-Thinking (NT)

4. Intuition-Feeling (NF)

The MBTI has been used in many areas of research, including MIS [10, 19, 20, 32]. Also, it has been subjected to extensive validity testing and found adequate for use in this area [6]. Subjects in this study were classified by the MBTI and randomly assigned to one of the two treatment groups (lecture or self-directed).

## Hypotheses

In order to explore the questions mentioned above, the following hypotheses were evaluated in the study:

H1: There will be no significant difference in subjects' performance due to training method, i.e., lecture or self-directed.

H2: There will be no significant difference in subjects' performance due to age.

H3: There will be no significant difference in subjects' performance due to formal educational level.

H4: There will be no significant difference in subjects' performance due to HIP style.

H5: There will be no significant difference in subjects' performance due to gender.

H6: There will be no significant difference in subjects' performance between training techniques due to subjects' age level.

H7: There will be no significant difference in subjects' performance between training techniques due to subjects' educational level.

H8: There will be no significant difference in subjects' performance between training techniques due to subjects' HIP style.

H9: There will be no significant difference in subjects' performance between training techniques due to subjects' gender.

H10: There will be no significant difference in subjects' performance between training techniques due to subjects' prior experience.

H11: There will be no significant difference in subjects' performance between training techniques due to subjects' previous training.

## Experimental Design

A $2^{3}$ factorial version of the “pretest–post-test control group” experimental design [4, p. 13; 23, p. 335] was used to conduct the experiment. One of the advantages of the design is that it provides the ability to control for nontreatment effects. In this study, it was necessary to control for a subject’s previous learning of the experimental information systems content (23.7 percent of the subjects had previous computer experience and 22.6 percent had previous computer instruction). Therefore, a pretest was administered at the beginning of the experiment, and was used as a control for the subjects’ prior learning and experience. In addition, the design controls for sources of internal invalidity including history, maturation, testing, instrumentation, regression, selection, mortality, interaction of selection and maturation, as well as the interaction of the other sources of invalidity [4, pp. 13–16]. The recommended procedure [4, p. 23; 23, p. 338], analysis of covariance [28, 29], was then used to partition out the effects of the subjects’ prior learning on their performance in the experiment and to analyze the effects of the experimental variables.

Independent variables in the study consisted of training technique at two levels (i.e., lecture and self-directed), perception at two levels (i.e., sensing and intuition), and evaluation at two levels (i.e., thinking and feeling). Data were also collected for unmanipulated independent variables (sex, age, education). Prior computer experience and prior computer training were treated as binary variables, i.e., subjects were coded as either having prior experience or training, or not (Table 2).

Following the definitions established by Sprague and McNurlin [35] and Nelson and Cheney [27], education and/or training were used in the study to refer to efforts to transfer information system knowledge. Education deals with concepts and understanding of IS knowledge, while training is primarily concerned with skills [35]. Thus, the dependent variable in the study was learning performance.

Learning performance had two measures. In attempting to answer the questions relative to the overall effectiveness of the two training techniques, it was necessary to control for the subjects' previous training and experience. Thus, the measure of learning performance used for hypotheses 1 through 9 was the overall percent change in performance from the pretest. However, to answer the question as to which is the most effective training technique for those who have had prior experience or training and for those who have not, the cumulative score obtained by the subjects was used as a measure of performance for hypotheses 10 and 11.

Analysis of covariance with the pretest as the covariate along with analysis of variance, and Duncan's Multiple Range Test, were used to analyze the results [28].

## Conduct of the Experiment

Prior to the beginning of the experiment, the MBTI and a pretest covering both IS concepts and BASIC language programming were administered to all subjects. Also, a questionnaire concerning demographic data was completed by each subject.

## Education

Education was measured at five levels. The levels were based on the number of years of formal education completed by the subjects. Consequently, level 1 was comprised of those subjects who had completed 12 years of formal education; level 2 of subjects with 13 years; level 3 of those with 14 years; level 4 of those subjects with 15 years.

Finally, level 5 consisted of those subjects who had completed more than 15 years of formal education.

## Age

Subjects were classified by age at three levels. Age level 1 consisted of those less than 20 years of age; age level 2 comprised those subjects at least 20 years old but less than 25 years old. The last age level, level 3, consisted of those subjects 25 and older.

## HIP Style

Using the HIP styles (i.e., ST, SF, NT, and NF) as determined by the MBTI, subjects were randomly assigned within HIP style to one of the lecture or self-directed training method treatment cells.

## Results

RESULTS OF THE EXPERIMENT WERE ANALYZED by the use of ANCOVA, one-way analysis of variance, Multiple Comparison (Duncan's Multiple Range Test—DMRT). The statistics are shown in Tables 3 through 5.

## Adequacy of the Experimental Model

A description of the experiment is shown in Figure 2. The $R^{2}$ of 0.88 obtained for the experimental model for overall learning performance, Table 3, indicates that the model does adequately represent the experimental situation by explaining 88 percent of the variance for overall learning performance. The experimental model for concepts learning performance also adequately represents the experimental situation with an $R^{2}$ of 0.71 (Table 4). However, the experimental model for programming language learning is less representative of the experimental situation, providing an $R^{2}$ of 0.47 (Table 5). This suggests that there are variables other than those in the model with more influence on the learning of at least the BASIC programming language and possibly other higher-level languages as well.

## Effect of the Independent Variables

Analysis of covariance (Table 3) shows that age, training method, and HIP type all had a significant effect on the subjects' overall learning performance. ANCOVA analysis also indicated that education and HIP style were the only variables with significant effects on concepts learning performance (Table 4) and on language learning performance (Table 5).

## Training Method

As shown in Table 3, overall performance was affected by the training method (F = 3.69, p < 0.05), and hypothesis H1 is rejected. Based on improvement from pretest scores, subjects in the lecture group outperformed those in the self-directed group by more than 24 percent in combined measurement of information concepts and language performance. It is interesting to note that, when learning performance was measured on concepts alone, the self-directed subjects outperformed the lecture subjects by over 19 percent (Table 4). However, the difference was not significant at acceptable levels. When learning performance was measured on language alone, the situation was reversed and those in the lecture group outperformed those in the self-directed group (Table 5), but the difference was again not significant.

## Educational Level

Hypothesis H3, concerning the effect of educational level, was rejected for concepts performance and for program language learning performance, but not for overall learning performance. Analysis indicated a significant effect for educational level on concepts learning performance (F = 7.56, p < 0.00; Table 4), and language learning performance (F = 2.29, p < 0.06; Table 5).

Subjects who had completed more than 15 years of formal education exhibited the highest overall performance (Table 3). They outperformed the group that had completed 12 years of education by 28 percent, the group with 13 years by 71 percent, the group with 14 years by 85 percent, and the group with 15 years of education by 102 percent. However, the effect was not significant at an acceptable level.

The analysis of the effect of education on subjects' performance in IS concepts provides a different ranking between subjects with 12 years of education and subjects with over 15 years of education. The group with 12 years of formal education had the highest percent change from the pretest, but the difference between that group and those with more than 15 years of education was not significant. However, the differences between those two groups and the other three groups were significant (Table 4), $p < 0.05$ . The rankings for the other three groups were the same, with subjects who had completed 15 years of school showing the poorest performance.

The high performance by those subjects with over 15 years of formal education was not surprising, but the relatively high performance by the subjects with only a high school education was somewhat of a surprise. One explanation might be that, since the “high school education” subjects knew that they were in a group composed of a large number of individuals with more education, they were motivated to overachieve.

The rankings for language performance learning (Table 5) based on education were substantially different from the other educational rankings. Although subjects with 12 years of formal education and more than 15 years of formal education were ranked 1 and 2, respectively, subjects with 14 years of education moved into third place and subjects with 13 years of education moved into fourth place. The group of subjects with 15 years of formal education maintained their last-place performance ranking. The differences were significant only between subjects with 12 years of formal education and the other educational groups.

It is interesting to note that the differences between the 12-years-of-education subjects and the two lowest performing groups were large. The 12-years-of-education group outperformed the 13-years-of-education group by 265 percent, and the 14-years-of-education group by over 518 percent, and the differences were significant at p < 0.05 (Table 5).

## HIP Style

Analysis showed a significant effect by HIP style (F = 0.02). Therefore, hypothesis H4 was rejected. Further examination by multiple comparisons via Duncan's Multiple Range Test (DMRT) provided evidence of significant differences between sensing–feeling and intuitive–thinking styles, and between intuitive–feeling and intuitive–thinking styles when performance was measured on overall improvement from the pretest (p < 0.05). Subjects with an intuitive–thinking style outperformed those with a sensing–feeling style by 36 percent, and those with an intuitive–feeling style by 38 percent on combined concepts and language topics.

The HIP style effect on concepts learning performance was significant at $F = 2.33$ ( $p < 0.08$ ; Table 4). Multiple comparisons indicated that subjects with an intuitive-thinking style outperformed those with a sensing-feeling style by 36 percent and those with an intuitive-feeling style by 37 percent. The differences were significant at the 0.05 level.

There was also a significant effect on program language learning performance. Intuitive-thinking styles outperformed intuitive-feeling styles by 252 percent, $p < 0.05$ (Table 5).

## Sex

Hypothesis H5, concerning the effect of the subject's gender on learning performance, was not rejected based on the experimental results. Tables 3, 4, and 5 provide the statistics on the effects of gender on learning performance. The differences in performance were very small and were not significant.

Effects of Age, Education, HIP Style, and Sex on Training Technique

Hypotheses H6 through H9 are concerned with the differences in learning performance between the two training techniques due to age, education, HIP style, and gender. Analysis of covariance demonstrated no significant results for the influence of age, education, or gender on subjects' learning performance between the two training techniques. Therefore, hypotheses H6, H7, and H9 were not rejected. However, the difference in overall learning performance between training techniques by HIP style was significant (F = 2.15, p < 0.04), and hypothesis H8 was rejected. There was a significant difference between intuitive–feeling subjects in training techniques. Intuitive–feeling subjects in the lecture group outperformed those in the self-directed group by 58 percent (p < 0.07). Other differences between training techniques due to HIP style were not significant.

## Prior Experience

In testing for the effects of prior experience, the cumulative score was used rather than the percent change between the pretest and the post-test. For neither experienced subjects nor nonexperienced subjects was there a significant difference in performance (cumulative) between the self-directed and the lecture groups. Therefore, hypothesis H10 was not rejected.

## Prior Information Systems Training

As was the case with prior experience, the scores used in testing for the effects of prior training were the cumulative scores. There were no significant differences in learning performance (cumulative) between the two training techniques for subjects with prior IS training, nor for those with no prior systems training. Therefore, hypothesis H11 was not rejected.

## Conclusions

THE STUDY PROVIDES SOME TENTATIVE ANSWERS to the questions posed earlier in the paper concerning end-user training. Also, the study provides some interesting implications for organizations involved in end-user training now, or for those planning to be involved in the future. Several questions were posed earlier in this paper concerning the training techniques, and the personal and psychological characteristics that have been suggested to have an effect on end-user learning effectiveness. In the following text, the results of the study are used to answer those questions.

A clear statement from the results of the study concerns the appropriate training method. The use of the lecture approach (obviously with a skilled trainer, as was the case in this study) is the better training method when compared with the self-study method. The very large margin of subjects' performance in the lecture group over those in the self-directed group when measured on improvement from pretest performance provided evidence for this conclusion. This finding substantiates the stated but unsupported conclusion by Tucker [36]. Certainly, cost analysis would be appropriate prior to implementing either approach. Based on effectiveness, the lecture method appears to be the appropriate choice.

The answer to study question 1, relative to age, is that age is not a factor in an employee's ability to learn to be an effective end user. Age (at least for the ages represented in the study) presented no barrier to end-user knowledge attainment. The oldest group, 25 to 39 years old, performed best. The results of this study imply that age should not be a factor when considering end-user training.

The answer to question 2, concerning an employee's level of formal education affecting his/her ability to acquire the skills required of an end user, is yes. However, implications for end-user training based on the effects of the end user's years of formal education are focused on the group of subjects with the least amount of education (12 years of formal education), and the group with the most education (over 15 years). Although those with the most education outperformed those with only 12 years, the very credible performance of the least educated group argues for their consideration in end-user training. The message appears to be that formal education counts, but the lack of a college education doesn't prevent one from developing the skills to become a knowledgeable end user.

Does the manner in which an employee processes information affect his/her learning to be an effective end user? The answer to this question, posed earlier, is yes. Results of the study show that intuitive-thinking subjects had a significantly higher learning performance than intuitive-feeling subjects and sensing-feeling subjects. This finding suggests that, regardless of the training method used by the organization, some form of assistance will be required for end users with feeling styles. Additional trainer involvement, perhaps a tutor, will be required to bring end users with those styles up to the performance level of those with thinking styles.

Question 5, posed earlier, was concerned with whether or not the age, level of formal education, gender, or way in which an employee processes information affects the choice of the appropriate training technique (lecture or self-directed) for the employee. Study results imply that the answer is no for age, education, and gender. This suggests that an organization designing a training program could ignore the characteristics of age, education, and gender of the participating end users. However, the answer to question 5 is yes concerning HIP style. It appears that the intuitive–feeling end user will profit from the use of one training method over the other. Results indicate that intuitive–feeling end users will learn better in a lecture format than in a self-study format. This finding also reinforces the previously mentioned need for more trainer involvement with end users who have feeling styles.

Whether or not the fact that an employee has had previous training or previous computer experience should affect the choice of the appropriate training technique (lecture versus self-directed) for that employee was the concern of question 6. Results from the study indicate that the answer is no. It appears that prior training and experience are not factors in choosing either the lecture or self-study training for end users (for the content of the study). The improvement in performance by subjects with prior training or experience was greater in lecture than in self-directed, but the difference was not significant.

This study, as most others, has limitations. The major limitation is the use of end-user surrogates. One could argue, however, that end users in organizations would not be that different from their surrogates in the study, when compared on the basis of the characteristics used in the study. Another limitation is the narrow age ranges of
