# Identification of Key International Information Systems Issues in U.S.-Based Multinational Corporations

P. CANDACE DEANS, KIRK R. KARWAN,
MARTIN D. GOSLAR, DAVID A. RICKS, and BRIAN TOYNE

P. CANDACE DEANS is Assistant Professor of Business at Wake Forest University. She received her PhD degree in MIS with a minor in international business from the University of South Carolina in 1989. Her research interests center around issues relevant to the management of international information systems.

KIRK R. KARWAN is Associate Professor of Management Science at the University of South Carolina. He holds degrees from Johns Hopkins and Carnegie-Mellon. His current research interests focus on the management of service and international operations. He has published in a variety of journals on topics in MIS, operations management, and management science.

MARTIN D. GOSLAR is Assistant Professor of MIS in the Management Science Department at the University of South Carolina. He has published widely in the information systems literature, including articles in such journals as Decision Sciences, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Computer Information Systems, and Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science.

DAVID A. RICKS is Professor of International Business at the University of South Carolina. He is editor-in-chief of the Journal of International Business Studies and is on the editorial boards of eight other journals. He also is currently serving the Academy of Management as chairman of its International Management Division.

BRIAN TOYNE is Professor of International Business at the University of South Carolina. He has published widely in the international business literature, including articles in such journals as the Academy of Management Review, Industrial Marketing Management, Journal of Business Research, and Journal of International Business Studies. He is also author or coauthor of seven books.

ABSTRACT: The transfer and management of information systems (IS) technology present a significant challenge to multinational corporations (MNCs). Very few studies have investigated the managerial concerns and issues that confront IS executives in an international context. This paper presents the empirical results of a two-phase study designed to identify and prioritize important international information systems issues in U.S.-based MNCs. The findings are compared to those reported in the earlier domestic MIS issues studies. The study reported here (a) identifies a number of international IS issues unique to multinational corporations; (b) emphasizes that top-rated international IS concerns are a mixture of managerial and technical issues, much like the domestic issues studies of the 1980s; (c) concludes that multinational service and manufacturing firms perceive the importance of international IS issues differently; and (d) demonstrates, for manufacturing MNCs, that the level of international involvement does impact on how IS issues are perceived.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: key MIS issues, multinational corporation, international business, information systems management, information technology.

## 1. Introduction

THE LAST TWO DECADES HAVE WITNESSED an explosive growth in the number and size of U.S. and foreign multinational corporations (MNCs). The accompanying demands of managing in the international operating environment have enhanced the need for increased coordination and integration of all business activities [2]. To remain competitive, it is apparent that firms will increasingly look to information technology for solutions to managerial challenges and opportunities of worldwide operations [4].

In the MIS literature, several authors have argued that multinational corporations face unique problems associated with the international transfer and management of information systems technology (see, for example, [11, 33]). Despite this, relatively few studies to date have dealt with the information systems (IS) concerns of the MNC. Of those appearing in the literature, most are speculative in nature and based on extremely limited empirical data. At this point, there appears to be a significant need simply to broaden our understanding and to identify the problems with globally managing information resources.

This paper addresses that need by reporting the results of a study investigating concerns of IS executives who are currently managing information and transferring IS technology on an international scale. Specifically, we present the findings of a two-phase survey designed to identify and prioritize the key international issues currently confronting U.S.-based multinational corporations. The survey respondents were primarily chief corporate IS executives and represented a mix of large manufacturing and service firms with varying degrees of international involvement.

Since research in global IT management is so new, this study is necessarily exploratory. In particular, a set of research questions were formulated based on limited work regarding global systems that has been reported in the IS area and some basic tenets from the international business literature (see the background section below). These questions are the focus of this paper and include:

1. What are the issues associated with the international transfer and management of information systems technology?

2. What is the relative importance of these issues?

3. Does the importance of the issues vary for multinational manufacturing and service firms?

4. Does the importance of the issues vary depending on the firm's level of international involvement?

The paper will also compare the relative ratings of international IS concerns to the relative ratings of domestic IS concerns reported by other authors.

## 2. Background

SEVERAL PRIOR STUDIES HAVE IDENTIFIED key information systems issues [i.e., 1, 8, 13, 18, 19, 26], but in each case the issues were evaluated from a domestic (U.S.) perspective. In one survey of international business executives, Ricks and Czinkota [30] identified and ranked the importance of various international business problem areas, but their results were not specific to any functional area. In short, none of the earlier “issues studies” have focused on international IS management.

Studies that allude to international IS technology management concerns are central to the identification and justification of a list of issues to examine and evaluate. Although many of these have looked at specific concerns in some detail, the following works are particularly germane to this research. Two papers, both by Selig [33, 34], look at a broad set of issues associated with multinational IS management. Though focusing primarily on planning and strategy, Selig's studies highlight the differences between managing in domestic and in international environments. He emphasizes the increased complexity that arises from a need to deal with multiple languages, cultures, business philosophies, governments and regulations, non-uniform vendor support, variations in available technologies, and relative weaknesses in external and internal IS standards and protocols.

Cash, McFarlan, and McKenney [11] suggest many areas of possible significance and provide a comprehensive list of important multinational IS issues. In particular, they classify variables or issues from the perspective of (1) the IS environment, (2) the corporation, and (3) the countries in which the business operates. The authors indicate that international information technology (IT) management inevitably takes place in an environment where the relationships among these three factors are complex. They also stress that the success of the multinational IS function will probably require a long-term view on the part of the corporation, since the foreign assimilation of IT may be influenced as much (or more) by local conditions as by available technology.

The specific concerns of the IS function in an MNC will likely depend on the importance or dependence of foreign markets in the firm's overall strategy. It is clear that the orientation of a multinational business depends heavily on the degree and manner in which the corporation is “internationalized.” For example, multinational corporations may differ according to whether they (1) essentially engage only in exporting and importing, (2) produce some goods or services abroad but remain essentially domestically based, or (3) value their international operations as much or more than their domestic ones [24].

Although many factors may influence how IS executives prioritize issues, a number of existing studies in international business strongly suggest that (1) the type of business (i.e., service versus manufacturing) and (2) the level of international involvement will influence the firm's approach to managing its operations internationally. Whereas some authors have argued that many of the current theories of international management apply across a wide range of multinational enterprises [6, 14, 17], various studies have also shown that the differences between multinational manufacturing and service organizations require elaboration [6, 39]. In fact, one of the key differences between manufacturing and service firms is that services often obtain their competitive advantage through the capability to acquire, produce, store, monitor, and interpret information [14]. As a result, multinational services have been especially impacted by the nets of IT regulations established by countries trying to protect their own interests [17].

The separate treatment of firms according to their level of international involvement is also supported in the general international business literature and is exemplified in recent international business studies [e.g., 22, 23, 32]. Stobaugh [38], for example, provides empirical support for suggesting that the larger a company's overseas sales in proportion to total sales, the more control it will exert via information flows. Although IS issues research has not needed to control for this type of variable in domestic studies, level of international involvement would appear to be an important factor affecting multinational firms.

Factors other than type of business and level of international involvement and experience are also likely to influence the multinational corporation's IS objectives, policies, and orientation. The prioritization of IS issues may well depend on the corporate organizational structure, the way in which data-processing operations are organized, or the way in which the IS function is viewed by the MNC [11, 12, 21]. The current domestic IS literature, in particular, suggests that the perceived strategic impact of IS may be a very important moderating factor [33, 34, 42].

To summarize, although a number of other studies are likely to be underway, there is not currently a stream of existing literature in the international or global IS arena. To help define the research that is needed, a number of very basic questions related to current practice are warranted. This study explores one important set of these by (1) identifying and prioritizing the international IS issues confronting current IS executives, and (2) comparing how these priorities differ in multinational organizations according to certain factors.

## 3. Methodology

A TWO-PHASE METHODOLOGY SIMILAR TO THAT employed by Hartog and Herbert [18] was developed to test the four research questions listed in section 1. Phase I of the Hartog and Herbert study employed the development and administration of a research questionnaire. The authors used the Dickson et al. study [13] as a starting point for issue identification and asked respondents to rate the importance of the issues using a four-point scale. Phase II then involved detailed follow-up phone interviews with a selected sample of the respondents from phase I. The Delphi approach used in other issues studies [8, 13] was determined to be less viable for our study, since the respondents were executive-level MIS managers in multinational corporations and it would be difficult to obtain a response rate sufficient to evaluate statistically research questions 3 and 4 using an iterative survey technique. In both our study and that of Hartog and Herbert, phase II interviews were employed to provide support and clarification for the phase I findings. A more detailed comparison of the Delphi method and other survey techniques is provided in [8].

## 3.1. Issue Development

Hartog and Herbert's list of "domestic" issues was used as a starting point for developing the set of issues to be analyzed [18]. In particular, 14 of these were included because they had been cited in the MIS literature as having a significant international dimension. Drawing from Selig [33], Cash, McFarlan, and McKenney [11], other studies cited below, and the results of the pre-test described below, 16 other issues of significance to multinational IS managers were then added. An alphabetized list of all issues is provided in Table 1.

As commonly used in prior studies of this nature, “rationales” or descriptions were also included in the survey to clarify the intended meaning of each of the issues. Each rationale was stated neutrally so as not to bias the respondents’ assessments of the importance of any particular issue. Care was also taken to emphasize the international dimension of each issue. (Rationales are included in Table 1.) Respondents were asked to rate each issue on a seven-point scale, ranging from “not at all important” to “extremely important.” A seven-point scale was chosen in order that a neutral choice be available and to provide sufficient variability in the data.

## 3.2. Phase I

A mail questionnaire was developed that listed the issues and a number of other questions pertinent to multinational corporations (i.e., level of international involvement, international organization structure, international data-processing structure, number of foreign subsidiaries, and countries of operation). The first draft of the questionnaire was tested by ten IS executives representing five manufacturing firms and five service firms. At the same time, in-depth interviews were also conducted with two international IS consultants who are involved with extensive work in this area. Two issues, learning to conduct IS business in other countries and use of value-added network services, were added as a result of the pre-test. Issue statements, rationales, questions pertaining to the corporation, and the format of the survey were also modified and the final instrument prepared.

The questionnaire was mailed to the top IS executive (identified by name) at 588 of the largest U.S.-based corporations. The sample of 342 manufacturing and 246 service organizations was selected primarily from the Fortune Industrial and Service 500s. The Business Week list of the largest U.S.-based commercial banks was used to expand the number of commercial banks included. The World Directory of Multinational Enterprises published by Gale Research Company was used to verify that manufacturing firms included in the sample have at least one foreign subsidiary. After approximately four weeks, follow-up mailings were sent to all nonrespondents.

Of the responding organizations, 53 indicated that they had insufficient international involvement to be included in the study. ## 4. Results

THE BASIC RESULTS OF THE PHASE I SURVEY for all respondents are depicted in Table 5. The issues are listed from 1 to 32 in order of importance based on a mean score computed for each issue. A rank of 1 represents the issue with the highest computed mean score and a rank of 32 represents the issue with the lowest mean score.

Standard deviations are also provided as a means of identifying the degree of consensus among respondents on the individual issues.

Some caution should be taken in interpreting the reported “rankings.” The indicated order of the issues does not necessarily imply a statistically significant difference between the mean ratings for each issue. In that there are 32 means (issues) to compare simultaneously, it is virtually impossible for a multiple comparison of means test to detect significantly differing groups [20]. On the other hand, pairwise t-tests may be the simplest approach for multiple comparisons of means. Normality tests were conducted to substantiate the appropriateness of this technique. Results of pairwise t-tests between all pairs of issues then indicated three general categories of issues: numbers 1 through 8, 9 through 21, and 22 through 32. The first two categories have a focus toward both managerial and technological concerns of the firm. Issues 22 through 32, however, clearly focus on “country-specific” or international variables.

Multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) tests were then performed to identify statistically significant differences between groups of respondents. For this purpose, the 32 IS issues were treated as dependent variables and a number of categorical variables (firm type, level of international involvement, etc.) as independent variables. Analysis by firm type (the independent variable) revealed a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001 for Pillai, Hotelling, and Wilks tests) between multinational service and manufacturing firms in terms of their ratings of the importance of the issues. Similarly, analysis by level of international involvement indicates a significant difference (p < 0.01) in the overall responses of manufacturing firms with less than 25 percent international involvement and those with at least 25 percent (as measured by the ratio of foreign sales to total corporate sales). MANOVA tests for differences by firms with differing organizational structures, differing international data-processing operations, and other levels of international involvement were inconclusive at the p < 0.05 level.

To highlight firm differences, Table 6 summarizes issue rankings for service firms only, manufacturing firms only, and manufacturing firms split by international involvement. To further evaluate these differences in responses, univariate analysis of variance (ANOVA) was computed on each issue to determine which issues most account for the multivariate differences. In the case of service versus manufacturing firms, seven issues with statistically significant mean differences were identified (p < 0.05). For manufacturing firms split by 25 percent level of international involvement, three issues were identified. These results are shown in Table 7.

After controlling for firm type (manufacturing versus service), cell sizes were insufficient for further inferential analysis. Nonetheless, a number of descriptive statistics are noteworthy. Tables 8 and 9 categorize respondent organizations by type of international data-processing operations and type of international organization structure. Table 8 shows that, internationally, most service firms in our sample have centralized data-processing operations, whereas manufacturing organizations lean heavily toward decentralized operations. Differences for manufacturing firms split by level of international involvement are difficult to discern. Table 9 clearly shows that a majority of the service firms in our sample utilize an international division structure and that manufacturers tend toward geographic area and product area structures. Manufacturers with limited international involvement utilize international divisions. These results offer some support for the (arguable) contention that firms tend to start with an international division and move toward more complex organizational structures as international involvement increases [12]. Further work may be needed to substantiate these relationships.

A summary of the phase II interviews is provided in the following paragraphs, beginning with a brief discussion of the most widely discussed top-priority issues (Table 5).

Respondents indicated that educating senior personnel was the top-rated issue primarily due to the increased complexity of operating internationally and the need for senior management not to assume that what works in the United States will work in another country. Executives intimated that this was a high-priority issue, because senior management support is necessary to facilitate a better understanding of a number of the other international IS issues.

Data security and data utilization. These issues were of utmost importance to service firms and less so (though still very highly rated) for manufacturing firms. The explanations for this finding focused on differences between business activities of service and manufacturing firms. Data utilization, for example, is a key concern for service firms, because it is their business to process data and information, whereas manufacturers produce and sell a durable good. Services (and particularly financial ones) depend more directly on information about markets. This information is key to foreign exchange trading, arbitrage opportunities, and market trends. Data security was cited as important for protection against competition. This issue becomes particularly important to the IS function as communication becomes increasingly electronic. Dedicated communication channels are normally not economically feasible. As one bank executive stated, “Security for us is of utmost priority. ## 5. Discussion

A NUMBER OF IMPORTANT OBSERVATIONS can be made about the study results. The following discussion is arranged to correspond with the four research questions stated in the introduction.

## 5.1. The International Issues

First, phase I of the study included considerably more issues than earlier reported domestic IS issues studies. Although this is primarily the result of our selection process, respondents in both the pre-test and phase II felt that all of the issues have international significance for them. Several participants suggested that the long list reflects both the increased complexity of managing IT internationally and the uncertainty that executives currently face due to limited experience with international IS concerns.

Is executives who participated in phase II also strongly indicated that many of their current problems are truly unique to the international operating environment as opposed to being merely distance-related problems. In fact, the list of issues under consideration attests that many of them are country-specific variables unique to the political, legal, economic, cultural, and technological environments of the foreign countries in which the firm operates. (Table 10 notes these concerns in italic.) Interestingly, contrary to the sheer number of country-specific issues, most were rated near the bottom of the list. Executive interviews confirmed that these types of issues generally represented constraints or areas where little control is imposed by the corporate IS function. Other international issues rated more highly were viewed to be more directly controllable by the IS function.

As further evidence of the unique nature of international IS management, many firms participating in phase II had separate departments for international IS planning and others were considering a move in this direction. The executive interviews emphasized that more work is required to identify the reasons why various MNCs have elected to separate their domestic and international IS functions in a variety of ways.

## 5.2. The Top Issues: Comparison to Other Studies

Recognizing that differences in issue definition, methodology, and respondent coverage exist between this study and the “domestic” issues studies cited earlier, it is nevertheless useful to contrast similarities and differences in study results.

In their 1986 survey, Brancheau and Wetherbe [8] reported that managerial issues dominated the more technical ones (i.e., the top nine issues were identified by the authors as “management/enterprise” issues as opposed to “technological/application” issues). As depicted in Table 10, both managerial and technological issues in this study appeared in the top ten. Based on the executive interviews conducted in phase II, this finding was consistent with the view that companies are not as far along in the introduction and management of IS technology internationally as domestically. As stated by Cash, McFarlan, and McKenney [11], organizations manage the assimilation of technologies in phases and each phase requires a different management approach. In the initial phases, technological concerns dominate, whereas in later phases management control and technology transfer acquire significance. As U.S.-based multinationals become more involved in foreign markets, it is likely that the types of IS concerns will change in a similar manner. This assertion is borne out by a comparison of top-priority issues between manufacturing firms with under 25 percent foreign sales to total sales and those with 25 percent or greater (see Table 6).

In comparing our results to Hartog and Herbert [18], many of the top ten issues remained the same (e.g., educating senior personnel, data security, integration of technologies, end-user computing, and data utilization). Interestingly, their top three issues (MIS planning, aligning IS with the organization, and software development) were not in the top ten in our study. Some of these differences may be due to the degree to which certain concerns are domestically based and, therefore, are not as sensitive to foreign operations. Comparison with the top concerns reported by Brancheau and Wetherbe [8] is more difficult due to their definition of issues. The most noteworthy differences in the two studies are that data security, rated second here, was 18th in Brancheau and Wetherbe's study and their top rated-issue, IS strategic planning, was 18th in our study.

In fact, MIS or IS strategic planning was rated at or near the top in each of the recent domestic studies. In our study, this was the case only for manufacturing firms with 25 percent or over foreign sales to total sales. Thus, whereas IS planning may continue to be an important issue for a majority of domestic firms, the importance of international MIS planning currently varies widely from company to company. The high standard deviation reported for this issue in Table 5 supports this contention. The interviews in phase II revealed that this is due in part to the large variation in the way that IS planning is accomplished in multinational corporations. Many corporations are trying to resolve whether international IS planning is different and should thus be excluded from domestic planning.

In their study, Brancheau and Wetherbe [8] noted that integration of technologies had dropped in importance from earlier domestic studies. The explanation given was that much progress had been made in this area and that the concerns had become less important. This issue was top priority for all groups in the current international study and, in fact, was the top-rated issue for manufacturing firms with at least 25 percent foreign to total sales. One explanation may be the lag in the introduction of new technology and learning to manage this new technology in the new (international) realm. This result may also be due to recent introductions of new technologies that were not considered explicitly in the earlier studies. Also, from our phase II interviews, it appeared that the IS executives were increasingly interpreting integration of technologies as a managerial rather than technical issue.

The significance of this issue is no doubt also tied to the importance of many of the telecommunications issues mentioned earlier. Although businesses are quick to recognize the efficiencies of liberalized telecommunications policies, the PTTs in many nations view the move toward bypassing local telephone companies (e.g., satellite networks) as a loss of revenue. As Vignault [40] notes, “since the PTT is one of the largest employers of personnel in most countries, there is no rush to duplicate the U.S. model (of deregulation).” The combination of these problems, new innovations, and new telecommunications applications are likely to hold the attention of international IS executives for a number of years.

## 5.3. Multinational Service and Manufacturing Firms

One of the key statistical findings in this study is that ratings of issues do differ for U.S.-based service and manufacturing MNCs. A number of specific issues were rated differently (in a statistical sense), indicating that the problems currently facing the service firms in this survey were somewhat different from those facing the manufacturers. The results indicate that other variables may be contributing factors in this difference. For example, manufacturing MNCs tend to have more foreign direct investments than service MNCs, even though services may operate in many different countries. Foreign direct investment involves the direct participation in the management of foreign enterprises and, therefore, may influence the importance of certain issues. Also, in our sample, services tend toward centralized data processing whereas manufacturing firms tend toward decentralized operations. None of the earlier “domestic” IS issues studies indicate how precautions were taken to control for a firm’s industry or sector type. Although not statistically feasible for this study, it should be recognized that further industry breakdowns may be necessary to detect the salient differences. Our results indicate that researchers should look carefully at these differences, whenever possible, when evaluating the priorities and actions of the IS function.

## 5.4. International Experience and Involvement

It seems reasonable that a number of characteristics of the corporation's international business operations would also impact the relative importance of information systems issues. Firm variables such as international organization structure, size of the firm, number of foreign subsidiaries, multinationality (i.e., the number of countries in which a firm has subsidiaries), and location of foreign subsidiaries have proven to be key variables in other international business studies and may be important considerations for IS executives as well. In our survey, the level of international involvement (defined in this study as the ratio of foreign sales to total sales) had an effect on the ratings of IS issues. With our sample, this result was significant for manufacturing firms split at a foreign sales to total sales ratio of 25 percent. The result may also be applicable for multinational service firms, but the sample of service firms with substantial international involvement was too small to test such an assertion.

A related issue that requires examination beyond that reported here concerns international operations experience. Although involvement is, in some ways, a surrogate for experience, these two variables may need to be separated. Terpstra and Yu [39] suggest that the firm's international operations experience may prompt changes in the organizational structure as well as its information gathering and assessing systems in order to adapt to new challenges. In the MIS literature, Cash, McFarlan, and McKenney [11] emphasize that, as the firm's experience with the technology increases, management concerns and technology transfer acquire greater significance. There is no reason to believe that this perspective should not apply to international as well as domestic IS management.

## 5.5. Other Factors

In this study, other questions were included in phase I to measure a number of organizational characteristics that might be expected to influence the prioritization of IS issues. Even though further analysis using MANOVA was unable to detect differences among companies due to international organizational structure and type of international data-processing operations, detailed studies of these and other variables may also be warranted.

## 6. Implications for Future Research

TO OUR KNOWLEDGE, THIS STUDY REPRESENTS the first attempt comprehensively to identify and prioritize the issues and concerns for practicing international IS managers. This paper also presents, for the first time, empirical data that show that the international dimension is an important component of the MIS research domain. Extensive research has addressed the international dimension of other functional areas (i.e., accounting, marketing, finance), but no previous broad-based study exists in the MIS literature. Our empirical findings indicate that international IS management deserves special attention, both from practitioners who are currently confronting the issues and from academics who have largely ignored this area. This study is a first step in identifying the important issues and topics worthy of further research efforts and resources.

Specifically, a number of issues were identified in this study that comparable domestic firms would not encounter, including an array of country-specific concerns. The international dimension of each of the 32 IS issues identified through this research could be examined in greater depth. Furthermore, in that this study was limited to U.S.-based MNCs, such research efforts might also benefit from a broader base of multinational firms.

Some of the concerns of IS managers will clearly be more sensitive to the firm's foreign operations. The relative importance of the technical/managerial mix of issues contributes to our understanding of the current state of the art of IS internationally from the perspective of the U.S.-based MNC. The most recent domestic issues studies have shown that technological concerns have declined in importance over time while managerial issues currently dominate the top priority concerns. Our results also suggest that managerial concerns become more significant as experience with the technology on an international scale increases. Additional work is needed to evaluate these trends further.

The significant difference in responses for manufacturing and service firms suggests that this variable should be considered in future international and domestic IS studies. Although an intuitive observation, previous issues studies have not accounted for differences between manufacturing and service firms. As discussed earlier, our results suggest that other variables may be contributing factors to this difference (i.e., number of foreign direct investments, international data-processing operations, and the nature of the business activities of service versus manufacturing). Additional studies are necessary to determine precise relationships.

Our findings also indicate that the importance of specific issues depends on the firm's level of international involvement. Interview results implicate the significance of international experience both with the technology and with international business operations in general. To enhance our understanding, other important factors such as integration and coordination may need to be examined from the perspective of the firm's overall strategic posture. Manufacturers pursuing a global strategy need information systems that aid them in integrating and coordinating a set of production activities that must be rationalized across countries.

Manufacturers pursuing multi-domestic strategies may need information systems that aid them in the coordination of their marketing activities and the sharing of experiences. Thus, foreign sales and international experience need to be augmented in terms of the variables that will influence the ranking of priorities.

Most of the results reported in our study appear to be tied to the high levels of change and learning that are occurring as corporations increasingly use information technology to coordinate their multinational operations. Is executives will continue to be faced with an array of problems that range from dealing with technical telecommunications issues to understanding global corporate management. The dynamic nature of this study must be stressed, since the list of issues and ranks will likely vary over time. Data for this study were collected in late 1988 and analysis completed in 1989. Issues such as electronic data interchange and implications of Europe 1992, for example, may have taken on added significance since the time of data collection. More empirically based studies are needed to help track the changing concerns of the IS function, and to assess more fully the international dimensions of the various issues. Specifically, additional work is needed to develop a research model that more clearly categorizes these international IS issues pertinent to the dimensions of the foreign operating environment (i.e., political/legal, social/cultural, economic, and technological).
