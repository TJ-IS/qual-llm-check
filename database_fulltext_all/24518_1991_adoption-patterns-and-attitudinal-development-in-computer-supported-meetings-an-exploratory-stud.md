# Adoption Patterns and Attitudinal Development in Computer-Supported Meetings: An Exploratory Study with SAMM $^{1}$

ILZE ZIGURS, GERARDINE DESANCTIS, and JULIE BILLINGSLEY

ILZE ZIGURS is Assistant Professor in the School of Business Administration at the University of Colorado, Boulder. She holds a PhD in MIS from the University of Minnesota. Her primary research interests are in the use and impact of computer and communications technology for the support of collaborative work.

GERARDINE DESANCTIS is Associate Professor of MIS at the University of Minnesota. She received her PhD from Texas Tech University. Her research interests concern the design of systems to support managerial decision making and the effects of information systems use on individuals and groups within organizational settings.

JULIE BILLINGSLEY is Assistant Professor of Communication in the School of Communication, Information Studies, and Library Science at Rutgers University. Her research interests are in small-group decision and conflict interaction, structurational analyses of social processes, and group decision support systems.

ABSTRACT: To permit exploration of the development of attitudes in a group decision support system environment, eight groups of four and five persons each met in a computer-supported conference room over a period of two months. Each group addressed two strategic planning tasks, meeting for a total of eight two-hour sessions. The computer support provided was Software-Aided Meeting Management (SAMM), $^{2}$ a system designed to support common group needs such as problem identification, alternatives generation, and alternatives evaluation. Groups were permitted to use or not use SAMM, according to their own perceived needs. Members' attitudes toward the group decision process and their perceptions of meeting quality were assessed after each meeting. As part of an academic course in which they were enrolled, the groups were required to submit a detailed report of their plans for each task; the plans were evaluated by the course instructor. Results suggested two patterns of adoption of SAMM: (1) groups that accepted the technology and used it throughout their meetings, and (2) groups that discarded the technology and ceased to use it. This paper discusses attitudinal development in these two types of groups and explores the relationship between attitudes and the quality of group planning in the SAMM environment.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: group decision support systems, electronic meeting systems, decision support systems, group attitudes, group interaction process.

## Introduction

COMPUTER AND COMMUNICATIONS TECHNOLOGIES are being used in new ways to support the activities of groups engaged in decision making. Group decision support systems (GDSS) consist of specialized hardware, software, and procedures to support and enhance the process and outcomes of decision-making meetings [7, 23]. With increasing empirical evidence of GDSS effects on outcomes of face-to-face meetings, there is increasing interest in observing the adoption patterns of users over time. Few studies have observed computer-supported groups for more than one or two sessions, due to the high cost of collecting longitudinal data. Group members must be trained in GDSS use; the task type must be accounted for or controlled; and large numbers of groups must typically be studied to obtain adequate statistical power. These practical difficulties make repeated observations of GDSS groups a challenge.

The current study represents a first and modest effort to examine group development in a computer-supported face-to-face meeting environment, using the software-aided meeting management system (SAMM). The focus is on user attitudes toward group processes and outcomes. Attitude toward the technology per se is not a part of the study. While attitude toward a GDSS is an important consideration, it is often reflected in resistance or acceptance of the specific technology used. The question of interest here is whether groups resist or accept a specific GDSS and whether rejection or adoption is related to users' perceptions of satisfaction, quality, and so forth. The primary concern is with the evolution of user attitudes as they emerge through repeated meetings in a group decision room. A secondary concern is the relationship between user attitudes and group performance.

## Research Model and Motivation

## User Attitudes in GDSS Environments

Empirical GDSS research has generally concentrated on user performance, particularly on decision-making quality and consensus $[11, 20, 21]$ . While performance is important, user attitudes are also meaningful. The importance of users' perceptions of performance, satisfaction with the decision process, and subjective understanding of the relationship between system use and decision making are well recognized $[25, 35]$ . User attitudes are also recognized as critical to acceptance and adoption of the technology $[7, 37]$ .

Although the comparability of results from GDSS research is constrained by differences in implementations of the GDSS concept, certain prior work is relevant here. Several authors have speculated that computer-mediated communication may result in negative views of interpersonal relationships despite gains in communication efficiency or quality. Feelings of alienation, reduction in perceived leadership, and greater depersonalization are possible negative side effects of computer-based meeting technology $[7, 22, 37]$ . Boje and Murninghan $[2]$ and Siegel et al. $[34]$ found lower satisfaction in computer-supported meetings. Gallupe et al. $[11]$ found that groups using Decision Aid reported greater conflict and less agreement with the group's solution than freely interacting groups. Watson et al. [39] found that SAMM groups reported discussion to be less substantial and the problem-solving process less understandable than non-computer-supported groups. Hiltz et al. [17] found no differences in deindividuation between face-to-face and computer-mediated groups. Contradictory results include findings of greater confidence by computer-supported users [36] and greater satisfaction with process and outcomes in computer-supported groups than those supported by paper and pencil [10]. Applegate et al. [1] reported very positive attitudes toward the meeting process in a PLEXSYS setting when the meeting is properly conducted or managed by a facilitator, and Nunamaker et al. [27] found consistently high ratings on groups' satisfaction with process and perceived effectiveness.

Knowledge of effects on user attitudes and decisions to use or not use a GDSS is scarce. Most empirical studies examined user attitudes following a single meeting [e.g., 10, 11, 21, 24, 39]. Jarvenpaa et al. [20] is one exception, although their groups did not use the same system for all meetings. Attitudinal research suggests that attitudes frequently shift following initial exposure to a phenomenon, but eventually stabilize after repeated exposure [28].

## User Attitudes and Group Performance

FEW GDSS STUDIES HAVE EXPLORED THE RELATIONSHIP between user attitudes and performance, given the difficulty of assessing quality of judgments, reports, or other outcomes of group meetings. One exception is Gallupe et al. [11], who found a significant relationship between decision quality and perceived conflict in a problem-finding task. There is reason to anticipate an inverse relationship between group attitudes and group performance. Acceptance of a group's solution has been shown to have a low correlation with objective quality [18], although Steeb and Johnston [36] found a positive correlation. Turoff and Hiltz [37] argued that high satisfaction and high decision quality cannot be achieved simultaneously in computer-mediated meeting environments, due to the novelty of the technology and the possibility of greater perceived conflict and depersonalization. GDSS still remains novel to most organizational groups. Poole and DeSanctis [30] argue that a GDSS contains decision heuristics that set group members on unfamiliar ground. System use should be less novel for ongoing groups, but our knowledge of the practice variable with groups is still extremely limited.

## The Research Model

OUR RESEARCH MODEL (Figure 1) BUILDS ON PRIOR WORK IN GDSS, information systems (IS) implementation, and group behavior. The model is consistent with McGrath's [26] framework for group research, which identifies four variable classes that are key to understanding group process: characteristics of individuals, of the group qua group, of the task, and of the environment. Interaction processes both determine and are determined by these variables. Several GDSS studies have used this framework [11, 39, 40], and extensions to it have been proposed [4, 29].

Research in IS implementation emphasizes the importance of user attitudes in explaining system usage. Lucas [25] found attitudes and perceptions to be the variables most significantly related to implementation success. The implementation literature measures attitudes toward the IS being implemented, i.e., the technology as an available resource. The current study measures attitudes toward group process, i.e., the outcomes of the resources in use, which in the group literature are often treated as a critical outcome of group interaction [see 26]. Our model also accounts for the effects of repeated usage of the system.

In Figure 1, task demands are equivalent to McGrath's [26] "characteristics of the task." Available resources are both human and technological resources, including individuals, the GDSS, and the environment. The interaction process is the active use of available resources for accomplishment of task demands. As a group meets over time, its attitudes about a prior meeting influence how the next meeting is conducted.

User attitudes about group process are expected to relate to system usage. As groups use a system over time, they learn more about how that resource might support their activities. If a group perceives their decision process to be effective, they will continue to use the technological support and learn more about it. Both learning and group process are evolutionary, and the system might be viewed as more or less appropriate during different phases. The assumption is that groups view a GDSS as a resource and that GDSS usage is tantamount to participation in the group. A group's attitudes about the interaction process are expected to be tied to their perceptions of how well they are using available resources. If a group has positive attitudes about the process and perceives GDSS as useful, then usage should be high. Negative attitudes will result in low usage. The two mixed conditions are more difficult to predict and may depend on other factors.

## Variables and Measurement

TWO QUESTIONNAIRES FORMED THE BASIS OF attitude assessment. Although the use of self-report measures is generally regarded as inferior to direct observation of behavior, construct reliability and validity are usually higher for self-report instruments than for interpretive coding schemes, and the data are less costly to collect. For these reasons, our analysis of group process is based primarily on group members' self-report of attitudes, as supported by observation and interpretation. We acknowledge the importance of observational data in understanding causal relationships in the group setting and are actively using such methods in our research program [30, 31, 40].

Perceived quality of group meetings was assessed with an instrument developed by Gouran et al. [13]. (“Perceived quality” refers to the self-report scale, as opposed to “outcome quality” or simply “quality,” which is an objective measure determined by the course instructor.) Satisfaction with meeting outcomes was measured with an instrument developed by Green and Taber [15]. $^{3}$ Both instruments have been used in prior single-meeting GDSS [6, 10, 39, 40], and have demonstrated reliability and validity [13, 15]. The instruments measure the following factors:

1. Perceived Quality of Meeting. The higher the value, the more positively a member perceives the group discussion.

2. Group Behavior Assessment. The higher the value, the more positively a member evaluates acts performed by the group. Behavioral acts evaluated include task-oriented behavior, socioemotional behavior, and procedural behavior.

3. Personal Task Participation. The higher the value, the more group members perceive themselves as participating in the decision process.

4. Negative Socioemotional Behavior. The higher the value, the less a member perceives that negative socioemotional behavior was expressed during the meeting.

5. Solution Satisfaction. The higher the value, the more a member is satisfied with the group's final decision.

6. Decision Scheme Satisfaction. The higher the value, the more a member is satisfied with the processes by which decisions are reached.

7. Informal Leadership. The higher the value, the more a member feels that an informal leader emerged.

Group members' scores on the attitude scales were averaged to derive a group score. A meeting is assumed to be a group process and SAMM to be a group, not an individual, tool. One person's attitude or usage is significant insofar as that person communicates input with other group members; the combination of everyone's input contributes to group process. For ease of comparison, attitude scales scores were converted to an index ranging from 0 to 100. The greater the index value, the more positive a group's feelings.

As a general measure of task performance, the instructor of the course in which subjects were enrolled provided a rating on a scale of 0 (unacceptable) to 100 (perfect) of the group's final report on each task. This criterion was used to explore the relationship between task performance and user attitudes.

SAMM's system logs recorded the actual amount of system usage, which was then calculated as the average number of system entries per person per meeting. System entries included selection of menus as well as text or numerical input into SAMM. Selection of menus represented significant usage because a requested menu item could change the focus of group attention by changing displays on the public screen.

## Method

## Subjects, Setting, and Tasks

Subjects were 37 upper-division university students in their mid-twenties; 21 were male and 16 were female. The students were from two sections of a group communications class that included both management and communications majors. The research tasks were a significant part of the course requirement.

Eight groups of four and five persons each met for eight sessions each; each session lasted approximately two hours, for a total of sixteen hours of interaction in each group. The difference in group sizes was not expected to bias the results, since prior studies using SAMM had no size effect for small groups [39, 40]. $^{4}$ The two-day time interval between consecutive meetings was similar across groups, and the small variety in time intervals was not expected to impact the results. The groups were assigned two strategic planning tasks. One dealt with developing a plan for meeting a university's goals for excellence, and the other with the problem of declining enrollment in one department of a university. For each task, groups were told to identify stakeholders, determine criteria for making decisions, and specify action steps for implementation. Both tasks were designed to have a high degree of saliency for the subjects. During the first four meetings, half the groups worked on one task, while the remaining groups worked on the other. During the last four meetings, the task order was reversed.

SAMM WAS DEVELOPED AT THE UNIVERSITY OF MINNESOTA for a series of studies on the impacts of computer-supported meetings. SAMM is a Level 1 system [7] that provides features to facilitate information exchange and use among group members. SAMM, described in [5] and [8], has been used in several prior studies [6, 31, 39, 40] and is currently being used in six organizations, including universities, a non-profit foundation, a large government agency, and a major oil company.

SAMM incorporates a rational problem-solving agenda whose typical phases include awareness of the problem, analysis of the problem, suggestion of possible solutions, evaluation of solutions, and acceptance of the best solution $[9]$ . SAMM performs basic functions of recording, storing, and displaying problem definitions, criteria for evaluating solutions, alternative solutions, and a final group decision. Group members can enter relative weights for solution criteria, and the system will aggregate and display average group weightings. SAMM will also cumulate and display ratings, rankings, and votes associated with alternative solutions to a problem. A group scratch pad and messaging function are included. These features have been identified as appropriate for supporting the communication needs of groups $[7, 14, 19, 21]$ . The menu-driven system is easy to use and does not require a facilitator or technician.

A typical SAMM-supported group alternates between verbal discussion and system use. SAMM is used to generate ideas and to surface divergent opinions, followed by verbal discussion to explore differences, and concluding with SAMM to resolve differences. A group might begin by each person typing in individual ideas about alternative solutions to a problem. Alternatives are displayed anonymously on a public screen, and the group verbally discusses how and why their views differ. The group then uses SAMM to resolve differences through the voting, rating, and/or ranking features of SAMM.

## Procedure for the Study

THE GROUPS WERE GIVEN TRAINING and a warm-up task (The Foundation Task [39]) prior to their first regular meeting. SAMM was presented as an important resource to them—a resource that would be available in every meeting and which they were encouraged but not required to use. After each meeting, each group member completed the attitudinal instruments. All groups received the same training and were exposed to the same research procedures. There was no variable manipulation. The research design was a case study in the sense that there was no artificial manipulation of variables, and the setting (the decision room) in which the groups met was their regular meeting room in their organization (the university). This room was used throughout the semester for all their group meetings and thus was not an artificial setting for them.

Having the groups conduct all their meetings in the decision room allowed for control of the task, control of the meeting location, measurement of system use, and consistent timing in measuring attitudes. The intent was to observe and measure system use in a controlled setting, without intervention. The small number of groups was due purely to resource constraints and the high cost of data collection.

## Results

DATA ANALYSIS IS BASED ON SELF-REPORT INSTRUMENTS as supported by observation and interpretation. This study is one piece of a program of contrasting, comparing, replicating, and cataloging the interaction of human and technological systems in a GDSS environment [30].

## Attitudes across Groups

Figure 2 shows the index values for all factors, averaged across groups by meeting. Positive attitudes generally increased over meetings 1 through 4, dropped in meetings 5 and 6, and rose again in meetings 7 and 8. Meetings 1 through 4 were spent on the first task; meeting 5 marked the beginning of the second task, but the ordering of tasks varied for the eight groups. Therefore, the general drop in attitudes in meeting 6 cannot be attributed solely to the nature of the second task. The index for leadership had the lowest values, indicating that groups did not feel that a strong informal leader emerged, a finding consistent with [39]. A low leadership index is also consistent with prior findings that the relatively equal status among members of student groups hinders the emergence of a strong leader [3, 33]. The drop in attitudes around meetings 5 and 6 may be attributable to the pressure the groups were feeling from the workload of a new task. The need for a second “startup” on a new and complex situation probably served to dampen their enthusiasm. As the groups moved toward completion in meetings 7 and 8, their attitudes became more positive again.

Many phase theories focus on phases of reinforcement and celebration toward the end of a project $[3]$ , which accounts for raised attitudes in meetings 3 and 4, and 7 and 8. A conflict or divergent phase following initial orientation in a new project would parallel attitude drops in meeting 6.

## Attitudes among Groups

Figure 3 shows the average of the indices for each group by meeting. During the first three meetings, attitudes clustered within a minimal spread. With meeting 4, attitudes diverged and by the last meeting, the spread in attitudes was quite large, suggesting that differences in attitudes emerged over time, and adaptation to the GDSS environment varied across groups.

## Attitudes and Quality of Group Products

Table 2 shows the correlations between attitudinal factors and the instructor's ratings of groups' written reports. Perceived quality approached a significant correlation with quality at $p < 0.10$ ( $r = 0.54$ , $p = 0.08$ ). Task participation correlated significantly with quality at $p < 0.05$ ( $r = 0.63$ , $p = 0.04$ ), and negative socioemotional behavior approached significant correlation with quality at $p < 0.10$ ( $r = -0.56$ , $p = 0.08$ ). These results are consistent with what we might expect, since participation is generally thought to enhance outcome quality and negative behavior can be destructive to group process and outcomes.

## System Usage

The average number of system entries per group member tended to decline over time. However, as Table 3 shows, the variance across groups was quite high, with the standard deviation exceeding the mean in five out of eight meetings.

Based on observation during data collection, two types of groups were expected to emerge: discarders and adopters. Groups were rank-ordered based on total system usage over all eight meetings. The top four groups (with respect to usage) were compared with the bottom four groups as the initial break-point for adopters and discarders. One group was moved from the adopter to the discarder category because nearly 80 percent of its system usage occurred in the first two meetings and the group used the system very little after the second meeting. Thus the classification was based both on a natural break-point in total system usage and on consistency of usage across meetings. Discarders were groups whose usage was either relatively low from the beginning or quickly fell to a low level and stayed low. Adopters were groups whose system usage either started relatively high or grew to a relatively high level, and stayed relatively high throughout. $^{5}$ A matched-pairs t-test of mean usage of adopter versus discarder groups on a meeting-by-meeting basis showed a significant difference between the two categories (t = 4.27, p = 0.002). Figure 4 shows average system entries for the two types of groups. Adopters (groups D, G, and H) showed considerable variation in usage from meeting to meeting, but usage was often high and did not show a persistent decline toward rejection. Discarders (groups A, B, C, E, and F) showed a pattern of initial usage that decreased steadily to rejection.

## System Usage and Quality

A SPEARMAN CORRELATION BASED ON RANKS was used to analyze the relationship between system usage and quality. The Spearman correlation coefficient between average system entries and grade on written report was 0.0714, indicating no significant relationship between the two rankings. It should be noted that the measure of system usage did not differentiate between skilled versus unskilled usage. It may also be that the extent of use is not as significant in determining outcomes as the type of use, or how the groups use and interpret a GDSS [30]. Like many real problems, the tasks in this study had no right answer, and the evaluation of outcome quality was a subjective judgment. The criteria used for evaluation were appropriate to the situation, however, and internal consistency was maintained.

## System Usage and Attitudes

SYSTEM USAGE AND ATTITUDES WERE FIRST EXAMINED by categories. Figure 5 shows attitudes averaged across factors for adopters and discarders. Adopters had a higher variation in attitudes from meeting to meeting (standard deviation of attitude means across meetings = 9.4), whereas attitudes among discarders were generally more consistent across meetings (standard deviation of attitude means across meetings = 7.0).

The overall relationship between system usage and attitudes was examined by a series of correlations. The correlation between attitudes and system usage in all groups over all meetings was 0.175 (N = 64, p = 0.083). Although this correlation approaches significance, it only explains 3 percent of the variation in usage. Our research model argued that attitude outcomes in one meeting affect resource utilization in the next meeting, and system usage is one aspect of resource utilization. However, a lagged correlation (lagged by one meeting) showed no significant relationship between attitudes at time t with system usage at time $t + 1$ (r = 0.016, p = 0.45).

## Qualitative Description of System Usage

THE INTERACTION PROCESSES OF TWO GROUPS—adopter group F and discarder group A—serve as examples of how each type of group typically used SAMM, based on observation of videotapes of each group's eight meetings. Group F, as an example of adopter groups, was focused in its approach to the task and integrated verbal discussion and system use well. The group began meetings by discussing what should be accomplished. Group F referred often to written task instructions and the SAMM agenda and worked to accomplish its task through the agenda. For example, in their first meeting, Group F decided quickly to focus on identifying stakeholders and deciding issues of importance to stakeholder groups, using SAMM for idea generation and evaluation. Periods of verbal discussion were interspersed with system use, and members' body orientation moved back and forth repeatedly between facing individual screens, the group display screen, and other members across the table. (Individual computer screens were placed to one side of each person's chair at the table.) Displays on the public screen generated lively discussion and provided a focus and prompt for discussion.

Group A, a discarder group, exhibited a lack of focus and did not take advantage of either SAMM or the task instructions as a model for how to proceed. Group A spent considerable time in discussing the problem in its first meeting, but what they were really discussing was possible solutions. At the beginning of the second meeting, the group still had not agreed on goals. Group A referred to SAMM's agenda (one member repeatedly suggested defining the problem, which is the first agenda item), but did not take advantage of the agenda as a guide for process. The group did not use either the task instructions or the SAMM agenda to provide structure, and remained unfocused in their approach to the problem throughout the eight meetings.

## Discussion

## The Discarder Category

During the early meetings of all groups, overall attitudes were generally positive and at a level consistent with previous single-meeting studies [e.g., 39]. The separation of groups into categories of usage revealed different patterns of attitudes and usage over time and between categories. The one group that used the system very little from the beginning (Group A) began with less positive attitudes, and ended on a declining note, whereas attitudes in all other groups either stayed the same or rose from the seventh to the eighth and final meeting. At the end of each meeting, group members were asked to provide comments about the role that SAMM had played. After the first meeting, Group A members said they were too busy trying to get ideas about the task to use SAMM. But it is just this type of brainstorming that SAMM helps with, and other groups used SAMM well for this purpose. Later comments from Group A referred to the system as “serving no real purpose” and being merely an “enhanced typewriter or chalkboard.” Group A’s first impressions about SAMM and their failure to use it early on appeared to carry forward to subsequent meetings. Their inability to utilize a resource that was clearly available may have negatively affected attitudes about their meetings.

In the other four discarder groups, a “negative learning curve” seemed to be operating. The more group members learned about the system, the more they resisted using it. In a follow-up interview, two groups said they became worried about time constraints and stopped using the system because they “didn’t have time to be that organized.” When groups felt time pressure to finish their task, they resorted to more familiar ways of making decisions. Even though groups had 16 hours of interaction with technological support available, they resisted new techniques and processes.

Discarder groups also may have experienced frustration with trying to match the technology to specific subtasks or activities. Both tasks were characterized by multiple issues, connectedness of issues, and no correct answer. The tasks made strong demands on groups, and required skill in information processing and management of large amounts of data. The ideal approach to each task would have closely followed the rational problem-solving method that is well suited to SAMM. However, task demands seemed to have overwhelmed some groups, and these groups viewed SAMM as an additional information burden (i.e., having to learn how to incorporate the system into their group process) rather than as a resource for lessening that burden.

Frustrations also may have arisen from the system's technical characteristics. The version of SAMM used did not permit saving meeting information from one session to the next. Since this “group memory” could not be carried forward by the system, some groups did not develop the habit of referring to the system and depending on it. A system bug that appeared in early meetings caused at least one person to remark that the group should not use the system, because it was not “trustworthy.”

Group membership may have influenced system usage. The pros and cons of student subjects in experimental research are well documented $[12, 16]$ . In this case, the behavior of subjects in early meetings indicated that the tasks were very salient to them. But, like any organizational group, these groups experienced periods of lethargy and lessening of motivation. The poor results of the midterm exam became available just prior to meeting 6. Some groups showed a sudden drop in their attitudes for meeting 6, and the exam results may have been a partial cause. Student groups, like organizational groups, experience external frustrations and events that impact their attitudes within a meeting environment.

## The Adopter Category

Adopter groups experienced considerable fluctuation in usage and attitudes. The fluctuation in usage can be explained by several factors. As groups learn more about SAMM, they are likely to use it both less and more. Groups will use fewer keystrokes as they have less need to repeat incorrect actions. Groups will use more keystrokes as they become more comfortable with the system and experiment more. The rise and fall of usage within adopter groups might be explained by this learn/experiment/learn cycle.

One adopter group fluctuated in SAMM usage because it went back and forth between SAMM and an external system tool. Group G had a member who was familiar with the Unix operating system and knew how to use its text editor. In some meetings, this member became the focal point as others clustered around his terminal to put together written documentation for their report. This group appropriated resources external to SAMM as part of their computer-supported meeting. The one member developed into a secretary, or designated typist. Because only one member was typing, the measure of system usage was smaller than it would have been if all members had been typing. Yet the system input of the “secretary” represented all group members, since group inputs were funneled through the secretary. Where a secretary evolves, the secretary’s input might be more representative of the average system input of the group.

System use may also fluctuate as groups go through phases in subtasks. Most groups began with brainstorming, for which SAMM is particularly useful. In meetings 3 and 4, groups began to talk about implementation and scheduling issues, e.g., how the presentation would be done or what the outline of the written proposal would be. One would expect an upsurge in system use in meetings 5 and 6, as groups brainstorm on their second task, but system usage in meetings 5 and 6 never rose to the level of that in meetings 1 and 2. The tasks may have been related to some extent, in that both were set in the context of a university and the stakeholders and constraints in each task would be similar.

Adopter groups demonstrated that SAMM technology affects group behavior patterns over time. The wide variations in both usage and attitudes support this idea. In contrast, discarder groups were more alike in usage and attitudes, with a more linear relationship. Overall, a direct relationship from attitudes to usage was not found. Our research model provides a starting point for progressively more detailed specification of the factors that cause successful resource utilization within group interaction.

## Conclusions

THE RESULTS OF THIS STUDY REINFORCED our understanding of the fact that usage of a GDSS varies over time, and that conclusions about adoption or usage based on one meeting need to be viewed as tentative. Groups do not necessarily move directly from a novice to an expert state in GDSS use, but instead may follow a cycle of experimenting/learning/experimenting that takes them through progressively more complex

layers of a system. This study measured system usage regardless of intent; more complex measures could take into account the successive learning cycles of users.

A second finding is that different types of groups emerge over time. Two broad adoptive patterns were identified, but further research could isolate more complex patterns that would enhance our understanding of how a GDSS might be modified, or how users might be trained, to encourage more successful use. Separating types of groups could lead to more powerful ways of assessing system effectiveness. In addition, more process-dependent measures of quality could reveal outcome differences among types of users.

The factors that contributed to adoption or rejection of a GDSS included resistance and inadequate learning by users, perceived mismatch of the technology with the task, real and perceived system inadequacies, and exogenous events. The results of this study imply that the relationship between system usage and adoption (as revealed in user attitudes), as well as the relationship between system usage and performance, is complex due to the many ways in which groups can use or not use a GDSS to achieve their goals.

The findings also suggest implications for users and developers of group decision support systems. In this study, groups received initial training on the system and then were on their own. This “hands-off” approach may result in gradual decline in use. Continued intervention following initial introduction to the technology may be necessary, via periodic “retraining” or facilitator intervention. In addition, a Level 2 system [7, 32] may be necessary to realize the full benefits of a GDSS, rather than the generic Level 1 system used here.
