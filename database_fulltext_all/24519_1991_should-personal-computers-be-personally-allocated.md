# Should “Personal” Computers Be Personally Allocated?

JANIS L. GOGAN

JANIS L. GOGAN holds a master's degree from Harvard Graduate School of Education and MBA and DBA degrees from Harvard Business School. She is Assistant Professor of MIS at Boston University School of Management. Her research interests include organizational assimilation and use of emerging information technologies and cross-functional and interorganizational information systems planning, development, and use.

ABSTRACT: This paper reports on a field study examining the effects of different levels of PC availability on usage by auditors working in two large public accounting firms. Respondents' tasks and information-processing tools were held reasonably constant, permitting an examination of the effect of the independent variable, microcomputer availability.

Substantial support was found for the hypothesis that greater PC availability is associated with a greater amount of PC use, which was measured as self-reported hours of use per month and usage supporting 32 auditing tasks in three task categories. The study contributes to the theory of information source availability. The results suggest that future studies of end-user computing should account for the substantial effect of information-processing tools available.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: end-user computing, personal computing, user behavior, information-processing tool availability.

## Introduction

MICROCOMPUTERS HAVE RAPIDLY INFILTRATED business organizations in the last decade, presenting new management opportunities and risks. Because PCs and their users are highly dispersed, because these users are not IS professionals, and because end-user tools are continually evolving, it is necessary to develop new approaches for managing end-user computing. The management frameworks that have been applied to traditional data-processing activities—such as the design, development, and maintenance of large transaction-processing applications—do not address the different set of opportunities and problems associated with user-developed microcomputer applications.

Management prescriptions for the assimilation and management of microcomputers in organizations have focused on the need to balance acceleration and control strategies in order to optimize the benefits of personal computing while minimizing adverse effects $[1, 9, 10, 17, 22, 25, 28, 29, 34, 39]$ . Systematic empirical research into the determinants of usage behavior will provide further clarity into the efficacy of specific management choices.

One line of research examined the effects of alternative training approaches on users' attitudes and usage behavior [8, 30, 35, 44].

Another research stream examined the impact of end-user support services such as information centers [9, 12, 32, 46]. Bergeron and Berube [5] found that, while information centers increase user satisfaction, other types of support structures don't necessarily do so.

Another line of research examined factors associated with usage attitudes and behaviors. Rivard and Huff [42] found that perceived user friendliness of software tools, user attitudes toward user-developed applications, user computer experience, satisfaction with end-user support services, and several other factors were positively associated with user satisfaction. Lee [31] found that age, organizational position, and number of subordinates had significant negative correlations with hours of PC use, while prior computer experience and a number of college courses were positively correlated with use.

Moore [33] offered a model of the determinants of microcomputer adoption, based on Fishbein's Theory of Reasoned Action [21]. Similarly, Davis [19, 20] introduced a "technology acceptance model," an adaptation of Fishbein. Gogan [24, 27] proposed a broader research framework for examining end-user behavior (Figure 1). Gogan's model incorporates a larger set of influential variables and attempts to explain ongoing usage behavior in addition to acceptance/adoption.

Gogan's dynamic contingency framework postulates that an individual's work context, as perceived by the individual, has a strong influence on usage behavior. The work context is comprised of three usage-relevant elements: (1) tasks, which vary in the extent to which they are amenable to computerized support; (2) tool access, the extent to which information-processing tools are readily available to the user; and (3) perceived tool utility, that is, the user's perception that available tools are useful in supporting particular tasks.

In this framework, actual usage behavior is seen to be a function of a user's comprehension about available tools, and his/her motivation to use them. Comprehension and motivation are affected by social interactions occurring with people both in the immediate work context and outside organizational boundaries. Tool usage itself can also alter user motivation or comprehension (positively or negatively). This paper reports on a study examining one element in Gogan's framework—PC access (availability).

## Why PC Availability?

MANAGERS CHOOSE HOW MANY MICROCOMPUTERS to acquire, and who will have access to them. Policies concerning PC availability may have a strong impact on subsequent usage, yet to date no studies have investigated whether the quantity or variety of usage differs if personal computers are shared versus individually allocated.

Some surveys of office technologies usage suggest that end-user workstations need not be individually assigned, since they appear to be used only intermittently. Non-secretarial PC users (professionals, managers, executives) reportedly spend less than

A survey of the use of PCs by 20,000 sales representatives found that “only one-third of survey respondents who have put PCs in the hands of sale representatives have outfitted their entire sales force . . . many companies feel that not every salesman needs to have his own machine, that just as much can be gained by having representatives in a branch office share PCs” [45]. One study found that managerial and professional workers used their microcomputers less than 5 hours per week [13], while another reported an average of 9.5 hours of PC usage per week [31]. Pyburn [40] reported that 88 percent of users were willing to share a computer for their applications if it did not impair their performance. These findings suggest that “personal” computers can be shared, thereby reducing the investment without reducing usage.

An alternative view holds that PC availability will affect the quantity of usage. Researchers in organizational communications $[2, 23, 37]$ , library science $[38]$ , and management information systems $[36]$ investigated the availability of information sources. For example, Gerstberger and Allen $[23]$ found that “accessibility” of information was a stronger predictor of information usage than was the expected quality or amount of the information. Allen $[2]$ found that, as physical distance from a company library increased, technologists used it less, and similar findings were obtained in studies of public library use $[38]$ .

Culnan [18] investigated the use of online information retrieval and electronic mail systems by MBA students. Her results suggested that “once a user has mastered the command language, unimpeded access to a terminal and continuous availability of the system are essential if the system is to be perceived as accessible and is subsequently to be used.” Rice and Shook [41], who studied PROFS use by 148 employees of an aerospace corporation, found that closer physical proximity of a terminal $^{1}$ was associated with greater use of PROFS. They concluded:

The terminal should be very close to the user or, as the users made clear in their interviews, at least immediately visible to them. If users can at least see the terminal, they will use it when it becomes available. But if the distance is even moderate or if the terminal is not visible, they will not be as likely to spend the time and effort to take the chance that the terminal might not be available.... This is another example of the considerable effect of the uncertainty inherent in the value of information...in the face of real (even if small) costs involved in acquiring that information.

Based on an exploratory study, Gogan [26] proposed that:

\- perceived availability influences PC usage behavior more than would be expected given actual PC availability. A PC may have to be accessible close to 100 percent of the time if the intended users are to perceive it as available.

\- PC location and portability are important elements of perceived PC availability.

The above studies suggest that a systematic investigation of PC availability on usage behavior will inform both theory and management practice.

## Research Method

TWO STUDIES, DESCRIBED BELOW, examined the relationship between levels of microcomputer availability and usage. Two hypotheses were examined:

Hypothesis 1. Greater microcomputer availability is associated with a greater number of hours of microcomputer usage.

Hypothesis 2. Greater microcomputer availability is associated with a greater number of tasks supported by microcomputers.

An exploratory pilot study was followed by a field study that was designed to allow examination of usage by similar individuals working in comparable jobs and using similar hardware and software, but experiencing different levels of microcomputer availability.

Pilot Study. The pilot study was conducted to ascertain the suitability of the research population and to generate measures for assessing microcomputer availability and usage. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with both presently and formerly employed auditors in seven large public accounting firms, as summarized in Table 1.

Auditors were selected because:

\- the tasks they perform are highly comparable across audit engagements and firms, due to professional norms and regulatory constraints. This helps control for the effect of respondents' tasks.

\- their tasks are information intensive and include a range of activities that are amenable to computer-based support, including word processing, statistical sampling, database management, simple computations, multiple regression, and presentation graphics. Hence, sufficient theoretical variance exists for the usage variables.

The open-ended pilot interviews revealed that audit partners are concerned about their microcomputer allocation decisions. In assessing whether they had made the right selections of types, numbers, and allocation of PCs, most partners expressed worries about future obsolescence. Partners in different firms appeared to have comparable expectations about the efficiency gains that they hoped would be derived from PC uses.

The pilot study revealed that different offices within and across comparable accounting firms took different approaches to the acquisition and allocation of microcomputers, and they appeared to be achieving varied results. This variation enabled the researcher to utilize a “natural experiment” methodology. In a “natural” or “ex post facto” experiment, the research takes place after the experimental treatment has been applied $[7]$ . The “treatment” consisted of high versus low levels of PC availability during the year prior to data collection. The design also fit the definition of a “quasi experiment” $[11, 15, 16]$ , in that assignment to treatment groups was not truly random, but the factorial design helped to control for the effects of respondents’ training and other subject characteristics, the tasks they performed, and the hardware and software they were using.

A microcomputer usage questionnaire (see appendix) was developed, based on the pilot interview data and an examination of the audit literature. The questionnaire was pre-tested with four public accountants, revised, and subsequently tested with five pilot-study participants in three firms.

PC Availability Study. Seven audit practice offices in two large public accounting firms served as sites for the subsequent study. Sites that were visited in the pilot study did not participate in the subsequent research. Both participating firms had standardized on IBM-compatible machines. Both firms made available to their auditors similar word-processing, spreadsheet, and database management software. Each firm had developed its own audit support software, but the functionality was comparatively similar.

Four offices in Firm A (ranging in size from 44 to 79 professionals) and three offices in Firm B (ranging from 89 to 132 professionals) participated in the study. The regional offices, all serving major metropolitan areas, were selected to represent either high or low PC availability, which was determined as follows. A microcomputer availability questionnaire, filled out by the microcomputer coordinator in each office, $^{2}$ asked about the numbers and types of microcomputers available to audit professionals in each office. An “availability ratio” consisted of the ratio of microcomputers to professionals (the lower the ratio, the lower the tool availability), with correction factors $^{3}$ to account for machine portability, access to clients’ microcomputers, and microcomputers purchased by auditors for their own use. $^{4}$ The correction factors were derived from the pilot interview data. They were applied solely for the purpose of ascertaining whether the ranking of offices would change based on these adjustments (they did not), and to exclude from participation sites with “moderate” PC availability. The lack of previous studies of microcomputer availability argued for the use of dichotomous, extreme-valued variables, rather than a scaled variable. $^{5}$ Offices with adjusted availability ratios of greater than 0.35 and less than 0.65 did not participate in the study. Offices were then classified as having either “high” (0.65 or higher) or “low” (0.35 or lower) PC availability. Table 2 summarizes the availability data.

## Hypothesis Testing

PAPER AND PENCIL QUESTIONNAIRES (described below) were sent to all audit "seniors" and "managers" in each participating office. $^{6}$ Table 3 summarizes the response rates.

The first hypothesis examined the relationship between PC availability and hours of usage. Audit work is highly seasonal, and some interviewees in the pilot study commented that PC usage differed during the “busy season” versus the “off season.” Accordingly, auditors were asked to estimate their typical hours of PC usage both during their busy season and during less busy months. Hence:

Hypothesis 1a. Greater microcomputer availability is associated with a greater amount of self-reported hours per month on the micro in auditors' busiest season.

Hypothesis 1b. Greater microcomputer availability is associated with a greater amount of self-reported hours per month on the micro in auditors' least busy season.

Quantity of usage, although meaningful, does not fully describe the relevant differences among users. A second important dimension consists of the types of tasks supported by the microcomputers. Two individuals may log identical hours on a PC, yet one may use it to support just one category of tasks, while another may utilize it for many different types of tasks.

Audit engagements are conducted by teams of auditors, with a manager allocating the distribution of tasks. Accordingly, respondents were asked to report on the tasks supported by microcomputers by any member of the engagement team. Respondents reported on from one to three engagements. Each auditor was asked to report on the engagements for which he/she billed the most hours in the previous year. The usage questionnaire (appendix) contained eight items for each audit planning and engagement management tasks, and sixteen audit procedures. Items were derived from auditing textbooks that are widely used $[3, 43, 47]$ .

The pre-testing of the usage questionnaire with pilot participants suggested a bias toward underreporting task usage. Auditors keep detailed, legally accountable records of their engagement activities, in the form of “working papers,” which are mandated by professional standards. No participating firms allowed the researcher to examine working papers; however, during the questionnaire pre-testing, participants compared their questionnaire responses with the working paper records for the applicable engagements. All reported forgetting at least one microcomputer application on one or more engagements. However, there appeared to be no false positives in the pre-testing: no pilot participants indicated that PCs were used when the working papers did not indicate that they had been used. The pilot interviews gave the researcher no reason to believe that underreporting of usage would be different in offices with different PC availability levels. Analysis of the data concerning engagements on which multiple respondents reported (below) supports this assumption.

## Discussion of Study Findings and Limitations

THE RESULTS STRONGLY SUPPORT THE HYPOTHESIS that greater PC availability is associated with greater PC usage. Significant differences were obtained for self-reported hours of use, engagement management tasks supported by PCs, and audit procedures supported by PCs.

Use of PCs to support audit planning tasks was not significantly higher in the high-availability offices versus the low-availability offices. This result may reflect the low PC usage overall in this category, or it may be due to differing characteristics of the tasks. Audit planning consists of less structured activities than engagement management tasks and audit procedures. Further investigation may help reveal the relationship among PC availability, task characteristics, and PC usage.

The measures of the dependent variables were designed to be highly relevant to the research population under study. By selecting lists of audit tasks on which public accountants widely agree, and by distinguishing between auditors' busy and off seasons, the researcher ensured that the results would be meaningful and readily interpretable. This choice was made at the expense of generalizability to other populations, but as Yin [48] points out, customized measures do not preclude generalizing to an already established theory (of the determinants of information-processing tools usage behavior).

Confidence in the study findings will increase when future studies add to the body of evidence concerning the impact of tool availability. Replication studies with other groups of public accountants would strengthen the validity and reliability of the findings. The choice of a within-industry, within-function, within-and-across-firms design necessarily limits generalizability. However, because task and employee differences can be expected to lead to differences in information-processing tools usage, it would seem prudent to attempt to replicate the present study design in several other industries, using usage measures tailored to specific functions.

The analysis identified a statistically significant relationship between availability levels and reported usage, consistent with the hypotheses. However, these results do not necessarily indicate whether availability per se caused usage differences. Several rival explanations can be ruled out due to the within-industry factorial design. For example, it is reasonable to rule out task, training, hardware, or software differences, since these were held reasonably constant. However, it is possible that the true cause of usage differences is another variable that happens to co-vary with availability.

Rice and Shook [41] speculated that the true determinant of PROFS usage differences was users' uncertainty concerning terminal availability, rather than actual availability. The present results can be interpreted similarly. Note in Table 4 that, in the low-availability offices, auditors reported using PCs from 10 to 18 hours per month, on average. Auditors work at least 170 hours per month (4.25 weeks × 40 hours). The pilot study data suggest that this is a conservative figure, since many respondents reported working up to 80 hours per week during the busy season. Given 170 hours per month on the job, and less than 20 hours of PC usage, a ratio of one PC per three to five auditors would still result in considerable available machine-hours. Take the worst case: 170 hours divided by 5 auditors gives 34 available machine-hours per auditor. Usage of 18 hours per month yields 16 hours of slack availability per auditor. Hence, lower usage in low-availability offices may not be due directly to low actual availability of PCs. User uncertainty may be a key mediator. If a user is uncertain whether the PC will be available for each time when it is needed in order to complete a particular task, then, as was found with PROFS users [41], it may not seem worthwhile to begin the task on the computer. Possibly the threat to the user, however small, of not being able to complete a task is a large deterrent to use. As was reported elsewhere [26], the perception of a tool's availability may be more important than actual availability. Future studies should separately assess both actual and perceived availability.

By holding several influential variables (firm, tasks, hardware, software) reasonably constant, it was possible to attain a high degree of confidence in the relationship between PC availability and usage, although, as discussed above, it was not possible to rule out an intermediate intervening variable (perceived availability/uncertainty).

The study makes three contributions:

—First, the research contributed evidence to existing theory (on the availability of information sources), as well as evidence geared toward improving theory. By examining microcomputer availability and usage, the present study extends Culnan's [18] and Rice and Shook's [41] studies, which examined PROFS usage. The results suggest that future studies of end-user computing behavior should explicitly account for information-processing tools availability, since this variable appears to have a strong relationship with usage.

—Second, this study offers a somewhat richer set of usage measures than previous studies of personal computer uses. Early PC studies [4, 13] examined software categories in use (word processing, spreadsheets, etc.). Igbaria et al. [30] examined five dimensions of usage: duration, frequency, expertise, extent, and purpose. The present study focused on usage in task categories. The lack of significant differences for use of PCs in support of audit planning activities suggests a need for a close examination of task characteristics and end-user behavior. This lays the groundwork for future examinations of task-tool interactions. Cheney et al. [14] suggest that task structure, repetitiveness, and interdependence may be the critical variables. As end-user computing research moves beyond the exploratory stage, richer and more relevant measures of usage behavior will lead to more useful theory building.

—Third, the study offers preliminary data of practical interest to those managers charged with acquiring and allocating end-user information-processing tools in their organizations. If extensive usage is desired (a big “if,” since end-user tools are a means to an end, not an end per se), then the data suggest that tool availability needs to be much greater than would be expected based on a simple assessment of available machine-hours.

Although end-user tools are becoming increasingly ubiquitous in business organizations, managers continue to face an investment dilemma. The technology is evolving at a rapid pace. Today, fewer managers choose between “PC or no PC”; instead, they face the trade-off of investing in optimal numbers of today’s machines versus reserving funds for the more powerful workstations yet to be introduced. Similarly, managers assessing the costs of software site licenses versus purchase of individual users’ packages need a meaningful way to evaluate the trade-offs. Hence, questions associated with information-processing tools availability will remain with us for the foreseeable future.
