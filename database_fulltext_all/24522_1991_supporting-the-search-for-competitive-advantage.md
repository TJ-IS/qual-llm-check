ABSTRACT: Managers are beginning to realize that the search for opportunities to create competitive advantage should be included as one part of the overall strategic planning process. Since the search for competitive advantage is often undertaken by large groups of managers drawn from many departments and organizational levels within the firm, recent developments in information technology to support group work may be useful. In this paper, we examine the ability of this technology to support the search for competitive advantage. Building on prior research on competitive advantage and group processes, we propose a three-stage process to guide the search for competitive advantage, describe an information system to support this process, and examine the experiences of five groups from three firms that have used this system to support this process. In general, the experiences of these groups suggest that this technology can be useful in supporting the search for competitive advantage.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: competitive advantage, electronic meeting systems, group decision support systems, strategic management, strategic planning.

## 1. Introduction

“COMPETITIVE ADVANTAGE IS AT THE HEART of a firm’s performance in competitive markets. . . . Today the importance of competitive advantage could hardly be greater” [39, p. xv]. In recent years, articles have appeared in leading journals describing success stories of companies that have used innovative ways to create competitive advantage. In many cases, these successes have been by-products of a strategic or operational plan, rather than its objective. However, managers are now beginning to realize that the search for opportunities to create competitive advantage should be included as one part of the overall strategic planning process.

Many prior research contributions in this area have focused on the development of specific frameworks, techniques, and decision aids to assist in the competitive analysis of the firm [e.g., 22, 23, 25, 39]. Although the search for competitive advantage is often undertaken by large groups of managers drawn from many departments and organizational levels within the firm [e.g., 40], far fewer contributions have addressed the group process issues that need to be resolved in order for the search to be successful. Recent developments in information technology to support group work have had dramatic effects on the performance of large, diverse groups addressing complex organizational issues [e.g., 10, 33, 34, 35, 49], and this technology may be useful in the search for competitive advantage.

In this paper, we examine the ability of information technology to support the search for competitive advantage. In section 2, we consider the potential sources of competitive advantage and one process by which the search for competitive advantage can be undertaken. In section 3, we build on prior research and theory to describe one system to support this process that uses information technology to support groups. In section 4, we examine how this system has been used by five groups from three organizations. In section 5, we analyze these cases. We conclude with a brief discussion of the implications of using this technology to support the search for competitive advantage.

## 2. The Search for Competitive Advantage

A FIRM CAN OFTEN GAIN AN ADVANTAGE over its competition by recognizing and seizing opportunities in its environment or by promoting innovation within its own ranks. Innovation implies that new ways of doing something are adopted; such new “ways of doing” emerge from new ideas or new perspectives. With traditional goals of control, predictability, and error-free operations, many firms may not readily accept new ideas and perspectives. Thus, building and sustaining competitive advantage is an ongoing innovative cycle of searching for sources of competitive advantage and acting on them. Both are equally challenging. This paper focuses on the search, although some aspects of the search affect implementation.

## 2.1. Sources of Competitive Advantage

Innovation is more likely to occur in firms that have the motivation and the courage to scrutinize themselves, that encourage free thinking or that actively search outside their own boundaries for favorable influences. Market or demographic changes, unexpected successes or failures, and incongruities in a process, product, or simply perceptions about them represent potential sources of competitive advantage for the first firm to recognize and act on them $[13]$ . While gaining competitive advantage is important, sustaining the advantage is usually the primary objective—but this can prove difficult, since most new products, processes, and services can be quickly copied by competitors $[4, 15]$ .

Self-sustaining competitive advantage arises from the inability or unwillingness of competitors to copy an innovation $[3, 4, 14]$ . There are two generic sources of self-sustaining competitive advantage $[3, 4]$ . The first derives from innovations that exploit resources unique to a firm, often arising from vertical integration, geographic scope, diversification, information technology resources, or competence in individual production operations. For example, American Hospital Supply's wide range of products makes its electronic ordering system more attractive to its customers than the offerings of its competition, which are similar computer-based systems, yet more limited in their range of products $[3]$ . The second generic source of competitive advantage derives from innovations that capitalize on differences in strategy between firms. If a firm is pursuing a strategy of focusing on a specific market segment, it may be able to enhance the value of its products or services to its target market in a manner that will not be duplicated by less focused competitors. For example, Scandinavian Airlines (SAS), which is pursuing a strategy focusing on business travelers, offers electronic mail to customers in its aircraft and hotels; its competitors have been reluctant to follow $[3, 4]$ .

Identifying specific opportunities for innovation from within these generic sources of competitive advantage requires a rigorous examination of the firm itself: “Competitive advantage cannot be understood by looking at the firm as a whole. It stems from the many discrete activities a firm performs in designing, producing, marketing, delivering and supporting its product” [39, p. 33]. Although opportunities to create competitive advantage may exist in any one activity in the value chain, most span two or more interrelated activities [24, 31, 39]. The linkages between these activities may be direct or indirect. For example, making a sale requires the direct linkage of the marketing/sales activities to the outbound logistics activities: for the sale to take place, both must communicate. Indirect linkages may be less obvious to the individual actors in the value chain. For example, changes in manufacturing may dictate corresponding changes in service activities such as installation, training, and repair. Other means by which value chain activities are interrelated are vertical and horizontal relationships $[3, 39]$ . Vertical relationships are those between components in the same value chain, while horizontal relationships connect components in different value chains within a diversified firm. Using horizontal linkages to create competitive advantage builds on the concept of synergy, a concept that is no longer widely accepted because of the failure of many diversifications in the late 1970s $[39]$ . However, “the failure of synergy stemmed from the inability of companies to understand and implement it, not because of some basic flaw in the concept” $[39, p. 318]$ .

Competition within an industry is shaped by five forces: the entry of new competitors; the threat of substitute products or services; the bargaining power of buyers; the bargaining power of suppliers; and the rivalry among the existing competitors $[39]$ . It is implicit in the threat of substitute products or services and the rivalry among competitors that an innovation by one firm is likely to initiate a response by others. Thus, the search for competitive advantage not only challenges a firm to innovate, it also demands that the firm evaluate the potential risks associated with any innovation by examining the changes it will introduce and the effort required to sustain the advantage $[14, 47]$ . The response of current and future competitors must be explicitly addressed. While the introduction of a new product, process, or service may provide an initial competitive advantage to the innovating firm, the provision of substantial benefits may signal a major change in the nature of competition and, with it, substantial risks. If the firm lacks the resources of its competitors, the innovation may actually provide a competitive disadvantage over the long term, in that the firm will be less able to sustain the advantage than its competitors $[46]$ .

## 2.2. A Competitive Analysis Process

Building on this previous research, we propose a three-stage computer-supported process for this search for competitive advantage (see Figure 1). First, in the Environmental & Organizational Analysis stage, a firm conducts an analysis of the competition and of its own strategy. The outputs of this stage include environmental and organizational analysis documents, plus—for those involved in the competitive analysis process—an improved understanding of the various elements of the firm and its competitive strategy in relation to its customers, suppliers, and competitors.

During the second stage, Proposal Formulation & Evaluation, the firm builds on this knowledge to generate, evaluate, and select specific proposals. The output of this stage is a short list of promising opportunities to create competitive advantage. This stage of the search for competitive advantage has received far less attention than the analysis process of the first stage, but it is potentially the most important. One objective is to develop ideas that create synergy between organizational entities, an objective that requires creative discussion among the managers involved. Individual creativity alone, however, can be difficult to foster, and working in groups may compound obstacles to creative thought. Groups are notoriously poor at generating ideas, even when using structured techniques such as brainstorming $[37]$ or the Nominal Group Technique, making this process a prime target for improvement (for reviews of group idea generation research, see $[12, 18, 28, 32]$ ).

Figure 1. A Competitive Analysis Process

Finally, before implementing any proposals, the firm needs to project and assess their impact. In the third stage, Impact Analysis, a risk analysis and financial evaluation is performed for each proposal. This risk analysis should consider the potential responses of customers, suppliers, and especially competitors. In addition, the existence and likely responses of any other stakeholders with vested interests in the firm's actions should be acknowledged: that rationally sound business strategies have been known to fail indicates that factors other than rational ones (i.e., those in the best interest of the firm as a whole) influence strategic decision making. The interests, goals, and agendas of key organizational players need to be understood to facilitate the implementation of the proposals. By contrast, the financial analysis is more straightforward; however, it may still be difficult, since much of the analysis is based on estimates.

Who are the key managers that need to be involved in this process? Many traditional approaches presume that strategy development is a rational response to competitive forces that is conducted at the top of the firm [46]. In many cases, however, this approach may not be effective. First, the necessary information may not be present at the top of the firm [46]. Keen [26], for example, describes several cases where only top-level management was involved with the development of strategies to use information technology for competitive advantage; the strategies failed, because top management was too far away from the day-to-day operations to fully understand the issues involved. Involving lower-level managers promotes the identification of competitive opportunities that are more closely tied to the operations of the firm [26]. Second, managers may be more likely to implement smoothly a plan they help develop because they understand the plan better, and they are more committed to its success [1]. Third, organizational learning is more likely when many managers are involved in plan development; managers learn more about the goals and operations of the firm and its individual units, and can use that learning to improve performance in other ways [1, 8].

Thus, Vitale et al. [47] suggest that it is more appropriate to use an adaptive strategy that seeks to integrate information from the top, middle, and bottom of the firm, which parallels calls for firmwide participation in strategic planning [e.g., 1, 43]. Ideally, then, the managers involved in this process will be drawn from all levels in the firm, as well as all parts of the value chain. This process is a major undertaking that involves a large group of managers, some of whom may have vested interests and hidden agendas [40]. Nonetheless, it is essential to draw knowledge and information from all parts of the firm.

The challenge lies in effectively managing this large group of participants. As the size of any group increases, its effectiveness decreases, primarily because of the increased difficulty in communicating $[21, 42]$ . One option to prevent this is to break this large group into a series of small groups [e.g., 40]. This approach reduces the difficulty of large group communication by eliminating communication between various parts; parts of the group can no longer communicate and share ideas with other parts. Thus, some opportunities will likely be missed. If we partition a large group into several independent subgroups, we necessarily sever many of the information links among members. These links must then be made by participants explicitly channeling information among the various small subgroups, thereby reducing the information richness and enabling the information content to be filtered, either intentionally or unintentionally $[6]$ . Daft and Lengel $[6]$ therefore conclude that the use of one group should be more effective than the use of group members as liaisons (“integrators”) between several subgroups.

## 3. A Competitive Analysis Support System

## 3.1. Meeting Room

THE CORE OF THE COMPETITIVE ANALYSIS SUPPORT SYSTEM we have used is a Group Decision Support System, or an Electronic Meeting System (EMS) [9, 11], in which participants meet in the same room at the same time. Figure 2 presents one EMS meeting room at the University of Arizona that provides twenty-four workstations arranged in two concentric, tiered rows. Each participant is provided with a separate hard disk-based, color graphics microcomputer workstation linked via a computer network to the workstations of all other group members. Two workstations serve as the facilitator's consoles to control the EMS. Two large-screen video displays are provided as an electronic blackboard, with other audiovisual support also available (white boards, a 35 mm slide projector, and a "copy stand" video camera that acts as an overhead projector). Adjacent to the meeting room is a control room containing a laser printer to provide immediate hard-copy printouts. More details on this and the other rooms at the University of Arizona are available elsewhere [9, 34, 48].

This meeting room and the supporting software (described below) provide three distinct modes of EMS-supported meeting. With a chauffeured mode, one member of the group (or a specially trained group facilitator) uses the technology to record and structure key group ideas. These ideas are displayed on the electronic blackboard for verbal discussion by the group. With a supported mode, each member of the group uses a workstation to enter ideas that may be organized and displayed on the electronic blackboard for discussion. In this case, the EMS provides an electronic channel for group communication in addition to the verbal communication channel. With an interactive mode, the electronic channel replaces the verbal channel; all group communication is done electronically by sending typed comments and ideas among group members. Each of these three basic modes may be interspersed with one another and combined with more traditional, noncomputer-supported face-to-face discussion in any given meeting.

## 3.2. Effects on Productivity

Each of these three modes of EMS use can affect group productivity in several ways. First, the additional electronic communication channel enables parallel communication among group members $[9]$ . In traditional non-EMS group meetings, only one person can effectively speak at one time. With the electronic communication channel provided by the interactive mode (and also to some extent by a supported mode), every group member can contribute simultaneously and in parallel, thus eliminating the need for group members to compete for “air time.” For example, consider a group of twenty people generating ideas for sixty minutes. In a traditional meeting, equal participation would require that each person gets three minutes to contribute; the remaining fifty-seven minutes are spent listening. With an interactive EMS mode, each group member can contribute simultaneously for a full sixty minutes, minus the time spent reading others’ ideas. While it is true that typing comments takes longer than speaking, reading is faster than listening $[19]$ .

Second, the use of an EMS can help reduce some of the social/psychological factors that reduce group productivity. Four major impediments to group productivity are: (1) production blocking, where group members must take turns speaking, and constantly listen to other members, thus having little time themselves to concentrate and generate new ideas; (2) domination of the meeting by a few members who preclude others from contributing; (3) evaluation apprehension, whereby individuals limit their contributions because they are afraid of suggesting “dumb” or politically sensitive ideas; and (4) free riding, where some members of the group choose not to participate [12, 42]. Each of these issues can be reduced in varying degrees by interactive and supported modes. An interactive mode reduces production blocking: since all communication is electronic, group members need not wait to make contributions; and since all comments are recorded, group members can decouple themselves from the group to generate ideas, and then later rejoin the meeting without missing any group communication. Supported and interactive modes reduce meeting domination by giving all group members an equal opportunity to contribute ideas electronically. Anonymity can be provided, which has been shown to improve the outcome of idea generation by helping to reduce evaluation apprehension [5]. Free riding may also be reduced; since everyone has the same opportunity to participate, and the group can easily observe who is and who is not typing, there is a group pressure to contribute. While both interactive and supported modes provide electronic communication to reduce these problems, the supported mode does so to a lesser extent because it involves substantial verbal communication. Nonetheless, it is crucial to acknowledge the importance of verbal communication. Electronic communication may not be as appropriate or effective for some tasks, particularly for equivocal tasks in which conflicting viewpoints need to be resolved [6].

Figure 2. An EMS Meeting Room at the University of Arizona

Third, any mode of usage can add structure to a group meeting by using a formal decision model, following a structured decision process (e.g., Nominal Group Technique), or merely staying on an agenda. While non-EMS-supported groups rarely choose to add formal structure to a meeting, structure has been linked to more successful meeting outcomes, particularly for groups that do not share the same information [16, 20]. Structure can be embedded in the technology, and/or a meeting facilitator can provide structure for verbal discussions by chairing the meeting. A supported or interactive mode can also add structure informally, by encouraging participants to organize their thoughts before typing them, thereby reducing long, irrelevant comments.

Fourth, the EMS provides access to external information. Corporate databases and online policy manuals are easily accessible, in sharp contrast to noncomputer-supported meetings held in traditional conference rooms. For example, we have implemented a memory resident support tool that enables participants immediately to access any predefined information at their workstations at any stage of the EMS-supported meeting. As access to databases external to the firm may also be required $[7]$ , we have developed a prototype database inquiry tool that runs as a background task. The user enters a query for a Dow Jones database, for example, and returns to using the EMS. The inquiry tool dials the database, presents the query, stores the results, and sends a message to the user when the query is complete and information is ready for viewing. Finally, since electronic communication is easily recorded and stored, an EMS can provide meeting-to-meeting support. No comments, ideas, or opinions are lost, and no one need take notes to ensure a proper recording of the meeting. Such a recording is useful during the meeting, and it also serves as a permanent record of key information. As such, the EMS can help serve as an organizational memory.

## 3.3.3. Proposal Formulation

There are many approaches to generating proposals to create competitive advantage. Some use a specific framework to structure the process while others involve no structure. For a structured process, the TC tool described above could be used. For an unstructured process, a series of two tools, Electronic Brainstorming (EBS) and Idea Organizer (IO), are typically used. Electronic Brainstorming provides an interactive mode to reduce air time, production blocking, domination, evaluation apprehension, and free riding. Participants enter comments into many separate discussions contained in separate files that are randomly shared throughout the group. One file is initially sent to each participant, who adds ideas to this file (to a maximum of five lines) and then sends it back on the network in exchange for another randomly drawn file (Figure 5a). He or she then reads the ideas added to this file by others, and adds more comments and ideas (Figure 5b) before exchanging it for another file.

After using EBS, the group usually has many pages of potentially useful ideas. The purpose of Idea Organizer is to support the identification, formulation, and consolidation of a list of specific proposals. With IO, group members work independently to identify and organize ideas, which they submit to the group (Figure 6). Comments can be automatically copied from the EBS output. All ideas are displayed on the electronic blackboard, and are also accessible at each workstation. Members can read or add comments to these proposals, but only the facilitator can change or delete items. The process typically begins with no verbal communication, to reduce air time, production blocking, domination, evaluation apprehension, and free riding. As ideas are entered, the amount of verbal communication gradually increases, as members attempt to understand proposals, resolve equivocal opinions, and come to consensus on combining related proposals.

Once the group has a list of proposals, it may decide to prioritize them. Once again, there are several options, all of which use an interactive mode, followed by a chauffeured mode to discuss the results. With the Prioritizing tool, participants rank proposals in order of potential. With the Alternative Evaluator (AE) tool, participants rate proposals on a scale of 1 to 10 using a set of criteria (e.g., impact, risk, etc.).

## 3.3.4. Proposal Evaluation

Each proposal that is chosen for further analysis should be evaluated for its potential risk and financial impact, typically by a smaller group of managers drawn from the line and staff departments who will be involved in the implementation of the proposal. The objective of this stage of the process is to produce a detailed implementation plan for each proposal that includes a risk analysis and financial analysis. The firm can then choose whether or not to implement the initiative based on the plan.

One approach to impact analysis is Strategic Assumption Surfacing and Testing, proposed by Mason and Mitroff [30]. This technique attempts to encourage the group to consider both “rational” and “political” aspects of decision making. The Stakeholder Identification and Assumption Surfacing (SIAS) tool implements Mason and Mitroff’s technique using a supported mode. SIAS begins with the identification of stakeholders: the individuals, groups, or organizations that can affect (or are affected by) the proposal, such as competitors, customers, regulatory agencies, and groups inside the firm. The next step is to identify the assumptions held by these stakeholders that could affect the stakeholder’s response to the proposal (see Figure 7). The importance of these assumptions to the proposal and to the stakeholder are then rated. $^{1}$ After completing these three stages, the group can graphically examine the stakeholders and their assumptions to determine those that are critical to the proposal. Further analysis and redesign of the proposal may emerge from this process.

Financial analysis can be supported by a standard spreadsheet package, such as Lotus 1-2-3, or similar Decision Support System. These projections are usually rough approximations, since accurately forecasting the effects of a proposal on the financial position of the firm is often difficult. However, a rough approximation is better than none. These projections, and the key issues identified in the risk analysis, would be documented as part of the detailed planning, prior to the decision whether or not to implement the proposal.

## 4. Experiences in Supporting the Search for Competitive Advantage

WE HAVE USED THIS SYSTEM TO SUPPORT strategic planning and competitive analysis with several firms. While every component of the system has been used to support planning and competitive analysis, no one firm has used all components. This should not be surprising. A recent survey of strategic planning and competitive analysis processes in seventy-eight organizations revealed that not one completely followed a prescribed methodology [36]. Such are the realities of the field. Therefore, this section examines the experiences of five groups from three firms, each of which used different parts of the system. All sessions were held in the EMS meeting room shown in Figure 2. Table 2 summarizes the competitive analysis process and software tools used by each group. The appendix describes in detail each experience and the results generated.

## 4.1. Power Corporation (1)

Power Corporation (PC) is a publicly traded electric power utility operating in the southwestern United States. Although PC's revenues had grown steadily over the past years (over \$400 million in 1989), a series of events led to an \$80 million operating loss and the downgrading of the PC's stock and bonds to below investment grade: the nonrenewal of an important power sales contract; an unfavorable power purchase contract; an unfavorable regulatory decision; a failed sale/lease-back deal; and a highly competitive gas industry marketing campaign. When the SEC launched an investigation into the trading practices of several senior PC executives, the board of directors selected a new chairman and four new directors, and replaced the CEO and five other senior executives with executives from outside the firm.

As part of its efforts to rebuild the firm, PC used the system to support the first stage in the search for competitive advantage (environmental and organizational analysis), and to begin to develop objectives for subsequent proposal formulation. The analysis began two weeks prior to using the EMS meeting room. The top three levels of management were asked to identify twenty internal organizational strengths and weaknesses, and twenty external environmental opportunities and threats (the “SWOT” technique $[43]$ ). The CEO then selected forty of these issues for group analysis. These included items such as dedicated employees and low-cost operations (strengths), lack of strategic planning (weakness), acquisition opportunities and a good market for alternate technologies (opportunities), and a poor economy (threat).

Eighteen senior managers and two vice-presidents attended the one-day EMS-supported analysis session. For Environmental and Organizational Analysis, the group used TC to discuss and debate the forty SWOT items, and the Prioritizing tool to prioritize them. ## 4.2. Power Corporation (2)

The second PC meeting followed two days later and was attended by twelve vice-presidents and senior vice-presidents. The objective was to compare the ideas and opinions of these executives to those of PC's senior managers in the previous meeting. The agenda of this meeting was similar to the first meeting. The group first discussed, debated, and prioritized the forty SWOT items. Then they used TC and EBS to develop goals and objectives for the top three issues that arose from those analyses: financial condition, strategic planning, and corporate focus. Finally, the group discussed overall corporate goals and priorities.

## 4.3. Greyhound Financial Corporation (1)

Greyhound Financial Corporation (GFC), a wholly owned subsidiary of Greyhound Corporation, is a financial services company based in Phoenix, Arizona. GFC is a commercial lender (\$1.6 billion in outstanding loans) that is pursuing a niche-based product differentiation strategy. The focus of its expertise lies in offering innovative financing arrangements, particularly for real estate finance, communications finance, transportation finance, and corporate finance. GFC's clients are nationally based in the United States. GFC used the system to support the first two stages of the search for competitive advantage: environmental and organizational analysis, and generating proposals.

Environmental analysis was conducted by thirty managers and vice-presidents from all areas within GFC using the same “SWOT” technique as PC (i.e., strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats), with one addition (environmental and organizational constraints). The group first discussed the issues in TC, then used IO to identify the key issues, and finally ranked them with the Prioritizing tool.

Three weeks elapsed before the next meeting, during which time managers were encouraged to examine the printed reports and discuss them with their staff. The second one-day meeting to generate proposals was attended by twenty-seven managers from all departments within GFC. While many stories of successful competitive advantage have focused on bold and dramatic innovations, many innovations start small $[13]$ , and thus participants were urged to consider both bold strokes and less glamorous innovations. The managers used TC to generate ideas on how to improve linkages between departments in the value chain. Ideas ranged from sending credit analysts into the field to talk with clients, to the development of new insurance products for the communications finance market. Many ideas focused on improving information linkages vertically between departments, such as the rapid provision of customer credit information and pricing information within twenty-four hours of request. Others addressed improving linkages horizontally, such as the development of contacts in the banking industry by the treasury department to provide information on investors in the transportation area to the transportation finance department, to joint credit department/marketing department market niche intelligence reports, to the provision of legal opinions on pending legislation by the legal department to the tax department. The group reviewed these ideas, with managers informally discussing ideas with their staffs and with managers of other departments.

Each department head then addressed the group in turn. Each first gave a brief summary of the ideas that had been proposed and responded to each proposal in turn by: (a) committing to implement the proposal by a specific date; (b) explaining why the proposal could not be implemented; or (c) promising further study and committing to respond to the group by a specific date. Each manager then answered any questions that remained.

Follow-up interviews and discussions with GFC management revealed that all the proposals accepted during the meeting, as well as those promised further study, were implemented, and have contributed to GFC's financial performance. Mr. S.L. Eichenfield, GFC's CEO, and other senior managers attributed much of GFC's subsequent outstanding performance to the organizational changes that began with these meetings.

## 4.4. Greyhound Financial Corporation (2)

Two years later, GFC returned to Tucson to reexamine the competitive advantage strategies set in motion at the first meeting. Since the first meeting, GFC had become widely recognized as the industry's low-cost provider in the market niches in which it competed. GFC had also attempted to be recognized as the premier provider of customer service, an apparently conflicting goal. By making major investments in personnel training, placing a heavy emphasis on customer service, and focusing on cost containment, the dual strategy appeared to be working. Nonetheless, GFC felt it was time to reassess the strategy.

Twenty-four senior managers and vice-presidents attended the one-day meeting. The group used TC to discuss the role of efficiency in the GFC's strategy, then generated proposals to improve efficiency using the same value chain approach, before discussing (with EBS) how a focus on efficiency was hurting performance. The discussion then turned to the strategy of differentiation and whether GFC should move from its dual low-cost/high-service strategy to focus on one or the other. The group discussed new market opportunities, promoting increased innovation within GFC, improving each value chain activity, and tightening value chain linkages (both internal to GFC and externally with GFC's customers).

## 4.5. Management College

The final group was from an in-house management college (MC) of a large multinational organization. This group used the EMS for three half-day sessions to evaluate the impact of a fourteen-point strategic initiative to improve quality, and to determine whether MC should build an EMS meeting room. Since MC is not a profit center, developing competitive advantage was not an issue; the issue was one of improving the quality of service. However, the evaluation process was a good example of a process that could be used to evaluate a proposal requiring substantial organizational change to create competitive advantage through quality improvement.

Twenty-one faculty, staff, and administrators participated in the meetings, which began with an overview of the fourteen-point plan and an introduction to the meeting room. This was followed by an idea generation and discussion session using EBS, IO, and the Prioritizing tool to identify, organize, and prioritize the barriers to the implementation of the plan. The next activity used the Stakeholder Identification and Assumption Surfacing (SIAS) tool, but definitions of the terms “stakeholder” and “assumption” developed by Mason and Mitroff $^{2}$ were not satisfactory to the senior staff and much discussion ensued over a “better” definition for these terms. The senior staff chose to focus on the stakeholders within MC (e.g., MC head, faculty), using one category for “all external” stakeholders. Several stakeholders and assumptions developed by the group were thus edited or deleted to conform to these definitions before the group prioritized them. The next activity was a discussion (using TC) of what additional computing resources would be needed to implement the plan. The third day was devoted to evaluating the usefulness of EMS technology to MC. After a series of electronic and verbal discussions, the group voted 16-4 in favor of establishing an EMS meeting room at MC.

Follow-up interviews and discussion with MC management and staff revealed that MC decided to implement the fourteen-point plan to improve quality. A quality specialist to oversee the implementation of the plan was hired in late 1989. A construction planning contract for an EMS meeting room was awarded in the summer of 1990.

## 5. Case Analysis

THREE SOURCES OF DATA WERE USED to understand the impact of computer support on the meetings of these five groups. First, the meetings were observed by researchers. Second, anonymous postsession questionnaires were completed by all participants immediately after the meetings. Third, interviews were conducted with several junior and senior managers from each organization. In the discussion below, we examine several general results common to all five groups, as well as considering some of the reasons for differences among the groups.

Table 3 presents the questionnaire results. The first set of items, the overall perceptions of group members, indicates that members of the five groups perceived the computer-supported meetings to be better than previous manual processes and to help the group achieve its goals. Interviews with managers supported this belief: e.g., "I found that we accomplished 100 percent of our objectives" (S.L. Eichenfield, CEO, GFC).

Members of the five groups also reported the computer-supported-process to be more effective than the manual process at generating ideas, and identifying key ideas (see Table 3). Interviews with managers again supported the questionnaire data: e.g., "[It was a] valuable first step in kicking off the . . . process" (manager, PC). While the groups generally felt that the meetings were effective, the MC group felt the most strongly that the computer-supported process helped them generate ideas.

Members of all groups were quite satisfied with the computer-supported process and to a lesser extent with the overall problem-solving process adopted by the group (see Table 3). These opinions were very consistent among the groups for the computer-aided portion of the process, but much less consistent for the overall process, with the first GFC group reporting the highest and the MC group the lowest.

Why did these effects occur and how can the differences among the groups be explained? In section 3.2, we suggested that the system could provide several functions to improve meeting outcomes. The first of these was the ability of electronic communication to reduce problems due to air time and social/psychological factors, such as production blocking, domination, evaluation apprehension, and free riding. The reduction of these problems should have enabled more equal participation, greater involvement, and increased commitment by more group members. The postsession questionnaire results indicate the extent to which the members of the five groups felt involved and committed (see Table 3). While members of all five groups felt moderately confident that the outcomes reflected their input, there were differences in perceptions of responsibility, fairness, and commitment.

There are at least four factors that could account for these differences and the differences in efficiency and overall process satisfaction. If we compare the two GFC groups, we note that they had similar group membership, similar tasks, and used similar software tools. Nonetheless, the first group had higher opinions of meeting efficiency and the overall problem-solving process, felt more responsible and committed to the decision, and believed the process to be more fair. The participants in both groups were asked to provide information and suggest ideas and, for the first group, to make specific decisions. The seventeen senior managers in the first GFC group had authority to select or reject proposals to be implemented by their departments, and actually made commitments to specific proposals by the end of the meeting. We conclude that this immediate decision making was one factor that increased members' perceptions of efficiency, satisfaction, fairness, responsibility, and commitment. In contrast, managers from the second GFC group noted that the lack of a conclusion or wrapup to the meeting reduced commitment, as did managers from other firms; e.g., “[I] didn’t get a sense of ownership of ideas and a sense of accomplishment because the session ends [sic] inconclusively” (manager, PC).

A second factor was the composition of the groups. The first PC group and the MC group were not normally direct participants in the strategic decision-making processes of their organizations. During these meetings, their role was to provide information and analysis to senior managers who would then take this information and make a decision. No decisions were made during the meeting, and members knew that after the meeting, the decision authority lay elsewhere: “What senior management does with these ideas now is the key to its success” (manager, PC); “If there is follow through by upper management, this could have been a critically important exercise. We’ll see” (manager, PC). Thus, they reported lower personal responsibility, but were more likely to perceive the process as being fair, given their increased participation compared to the normal decision-making process.

A third factor was the specific tools and how they were used. For the most part, the groups used tools with interactive modes of communication. The interactive mode provided solely anonymous electronic communication, which we argued could reduce problems due to air time, production blocking, domination, evaluation apprehension, and free riding. When electronic communication was used, every member participated. Managers from all groups attributed much of the meetings' success to this anonymous electronic communication: "People usually reluctant to express themselves felt free to take part, and we were surprised by the number of new ideas expressed . . . . There is no question that it stimulates interchange and creative thinking" (S.L. Eichenfield). "I think it would be a pleasure to have anonymous board/management interaction" (manager, PC).

In contrast to the other groups, the MC group spent substantially more time using tools providing supported modes. The supported mode combines electronic communication with verbal communication which still suffers from these problems. During periods of verbal communication, senior group members dominated the meeting; few junior members spoke. The senior members also used the verbal discussion to change the direction of the group's deliberations by changing definitions. While one senior MC faculty member felt that anonymity was not necessary, the junior faculty we interviewed felt it was very important. Thus, we conclude that problems due to air time, production blocking, domination, evaluation apprehension, and free riding were not reduced during these periods of verbal conversation. However, these problems did appear to be reduced when electronic communication was used, and the MC session organizer observed that the “computer process has speed to explore problem areas.” Thus, the differences in perceived efficiency, satisfaction, and involvement and commitment may also be due to the substantial use of tools with a significant verbal component that did not offset these inhibitors of group productivity.

A fourth factor was the structured techniques used. All groups used tools providing structure. PC and GFC used TC structures for environmental and organizational analysis (e.g., SWOT) and the GFC groups used variants on Porter's value chain model for proposal formulation. The MC group used the highly structured SIAS technique for impact analysis, as well as a more loosely structured approach for analyzing computing needs. While these techniques were not specifically mentioned by the participants as being important, we believe the use of the value chain elements in particular to structure the generation of proposals by the GFC group was one key to success. Without this structure, participants might not have focused on proposals linking value chain activities, and, if they had, the rapid collection and organization of proposals to better link specific departments would have been more difficult.

The MC and PC groups also encountered problems using these structured techniques. The MC group had problems using SIAS, owing to disagreements over the definitions of stakeholders and assumptions. These disagreements resulted in unproductive discussions that delayed the meeting and lowered efficiency. Several PC managers mentioned that the initial structure for the top forty SWOT items was not well formed, e.g.: “A better focus at the outset would have avoided the confusion in results. [I] don’t believe we can rely on the priorities since the categories were overlapping.”

Structure was also provided by the facilitator who chaired periods of verbal discussion. The MC group found the role of the facilitator to be much more important than did the other groups (see Table 3). This is likely due to the fact that the MC group had substantially more verbal discussion than the others had. In interviews, senior managers from both GFC and MC reported that by chairing the meeting, asking probing questions during the verbal discussion, and helping the group leaders to develop meeting agendas, the facilitator was a key aspect of the system.

The final contributions of this system, the ability to use external information and accurately to record information for use after the meeting, were somewhat important.

While no external information (e.g., databases) were used by any groups, printouts of meeting results were used by all groups in subsequent efforts. The organizational and environmental analyses were used by GFC managers before the proposal-generation meeting as background information, while the printouts from the proposal-generation meeting were used for several weeks after that meeting. PC managers used the meeting printout in subsequent planning activities, as did senior MC managers.

## 6. Implications and Conclusions

IN GENERAL, THE EXPERIENCES OF THESE GROUPS suggest that EMS technology can be useful in supporting the search for competitive advantage. However, since no group used the system for all phases of the search process, and since the MC group experienced some difficulties, this technology also has limitations. We draw four conclusions for the future application of this technology:

First, EMS technology has the ability to enhance one of the most important aspects of the search for competitive advantage: the rapid collection and organization of a large number of ideas and opinions from a wide range of participants. Anonymous electronic communication appears to be an effective means to collect input from a large number of participants drawn from many departments and levels within an organization without many of the impediments usually encountered by large diverse groups. However, the combination of electronic and verbal communication proved less effective than electronic communication alone. While verbal communication may be important in the development of consensus and commitment $[6]$ , we suspect that many of the activities leading to commitment may not be public activities conducted in large-group settings; instead, they are more likely to involve one-on-one negotiation. This technology may be most effectively used to collect and organize information regarding a proposal that will be analyzed in subsequent stages by smaller groups of managers responsible for implementing proposals.

Our second conclusion is that it is important to develop a sense of commitment and to instill a sense that “something will be done” with the outputs of the meeting. At first glance, this conflicts with our first conclusion to conduct some analysis and decision making in venues other than the EMS gathering. Our recommendation is to commit to smaller proposals immediately, while promising to make decisions on larger proposals by a specific date. This may satisfy a group’s need for a sense of involvement, while such a public commitment to future action may encourage managers to accomplish further analysis and decision making on a timely basis.

Third, while the meetings described here were planned with the group leaders, the problems encountered in using the SIAS tool and, to a lesser extent, the lack of consensus on the PC SWOT items underscore the need for thorough planning of all agenda items. The conceptual definitions for “stakeholder” and “assumption” were accepted in general terms prior to the meeting, but during the meeting, with budgets, power, and political issues riding on the outcome, these definitions were less than satisfactory. Had the terms been discussed during meeting planning within the context of the specific problem area, some confusion and delay might have been avoided, and the meeting could have run more smoothly.

Finally, the use of structured techniques both for the conduct of the meetings and for the analysis of the issues involved contributed to the success of the meetings. We believe that the use of EMS to deliver this structure provided synergy: the combination of rapid communication and structured analysis resulted in highly focused discussions that enabled all participants fully to contribute their knowledge and skills in a minimum amount of time.

APPENDIX: DETAILED DESCRIPTIONS OF THE SEARCH

FOR COMPETITIVE ADVANTAGE

## Power Corporation 1

EIGHTEEN SENIOR MANAGERS AND TWO VICE-PRESIDENTS attended the one-day EMS-supported analysis session. After an initial introduction to the technology, the group began the organizational analysis with Topic Commenter (TC). TC provided a set of ten uniquely named index-card-like windows corresponding to the ten selected internal strengths and weaknesses. The group electronically discussed and debated the strengths for sixty-five minutes, resulting in fifteen single-spaced pages of analysis. The ten strengths were then ranked in importance. The group then spent thirty-five minutes using TC to discuss the ten weaknesses (resulting in twelve pages of analysis) before ranking them. The same procedure was used for the environmental analysis: the group used TC for forty-five minutes to examine opportunities (twelve pages) before ranking them, and then fifty-five minutes to consider threats (twelve pages) before ranking them.

After lunch, the focus turned to identifying goals and objectives to improve these factors as a prelude to developing proposals. TC was used, but this time only one card was discussed at a time. These activities asked members to define specific objectives for the five highest-ranked issues: financial condition (thirty minutes; five pages); corporate focus (twenty-five minutes; three pages); strategic planning (fifteen minutes; one page); an existing power contract (twenty-five minutes; three pages); and regulation (twenty minutes; four pages). Next the group used EBS for twenty minutes to discuss important corporate values (seven pages) and for fifteen minutes (seven pages) on overall corporate goals.

## Power Corporation 2

The agenda of the second PC meeting was similar to that of the first meeting. During the morning, the group used TC to discuss and rank internal strengths (forty-five minutes; nine pages); internal weaknesses (forty minutes; nine pages); external opportunities (thirty minutes; eight pages); and external threats (fifty minutes; eight pages). After lunch, the group used TC (one card at a time) to develop goals and objectives for the top three issues that arose from these analyses: financial condition (thirty minutes; three pages); strategic planning (twenty-five minutes; three pages); and corporate focus (thirty minutes; three pages). After a verbal comparison of this group's rankings with those of the previous group (moderated by the facilitator), the group used EBS to deliberate management priorities (thirty-five minutes; eight pages), and overall corporate goals and values (twenty minutes; four pages).

## Greyhound Financial Corporation 1

Environmental analysis was conducted using the SWOT technique by thirty managers and vice-presidents from all areas within GFC. Since there were only twenty-four workstations, six workstations were shared by two managers. TC was used to provide a set of uniquely named index-card-like windows corresponding to the key SWOT categories. Issues relating to these categories were identified and debated electronically for ninety minutes, resulting in fifteen pages including ninety strengths, eighty-seven weaknesses, twenty-six opportunities, thirty-two threats, and twenty constraints. The group then used Idea Organizer for forty-five minutes to organize these results into a fifteen-item short list of key issues. The Prioritizing tool was used for thirty minutes to prioritize these items twice, first, by order of importance, and second, by order of short-term to long-term priority.

Three weeks elapsed before the second one-day meeting to generate specific proposals. Since the volume of comments was expected to be large, it was necessary to find a way for Department A to propose an idea to Department B, and for both parties to be able to conduct a dialog without interfering with other departments doing the same. TC was used, with each index-card window assigned to a major department or value-adding activity in the firm. If someone in Department A wanted to enter an idea that would improve the linkages between Department A and Department B, she or he selected the window for Department B, and entered the idea. Each department was responsible for monitoring its own window for ideas proposed by other departments, and for responding to them. All departments were also expected to examine ideas proposed in other windows, in case those ideas could be developed to apply to other areas of the firm. While some overlap could have occurred (e.g., A suggesting an idea in B's window at the same time as B suggested the same in A's window), this did not prove to be a problem.

Idea generation with TC was conducted in two sessions. During the first ninety-minute session, managers entered ideas to improve linkages between departments by identifying ways that other departments could assist them to improve their performance, while during the second forty-five-minute session, managers entered ways that they could help other departments. Sixteen pages of ideas were generated in the first session (a total of 219 ideas), and eleven pages were generated in the second (a total of 185 ideas).

## Greyhound Financial Corporation 2

Twenty-four senior managers and vice-presidents attended the second one-day meeting. After an opening discussion by the CEO, the meeting began with a thirty-minute structured discussion of the importance and the role of organizational efficiency in GFC's strategy using TC (resulting in eight pages). The group then spent twenty-five minutes identifying specific proposals to improve efficiency using a similar value chain approach as in the first meeting: TC with one card per department or area (resulting in five pages). Next, the group used EBS for twenty-five minutes to discuss the areas in which the focus on efficiency was impairing effectiveness (nine pages), followed by another fifteen-minute EBS on how to improve the existing efficiency/productivity measures (eight pages). The last session before lunch was a fifteen-minute idea-generation session using EBS to suggest new market opportunities (seven pages).

After lunch, the group began with general discussion (using EBS) of how to improve linkages within the internal GFC value chain (twenty-five minutes; seven pages), followed by an EBS debate of whether GFC should move from its dual low-cost/high-service strategy to focus on one or the other (fifteen minutes; seven pages). This was followed by two TC sessions: (1) a fifteen-minute session identifying possible customer service measures for each value chain area (three pages); and (2) a fifty-minute session identifying specific proposals to build tighter linkages to customers (eleven pages). The meeting closed with three EBS sessions: (1) a ten-minute session discussing general techniques to improve information linkages within the value chain, both internally and with the customer (six pages); (2) a twenty-five-minute session discussing ways to promote innovation (seven pages); and (3) a fifteen-minute debate of changes to organizational structure (six pages).

## Management College

Twenty-one faculty, staff, and administrators participated in the meetings, which began with an overview of the fourteen-point plan and an introduction to the meeting room. This was followed by an idea-generation and discussion session using EBS to identify the barriers to the implementation of the plan. The group spent thirty-seven minutes to produce twenty-two pages (a total of 313 ideas/comments). The group then spent about an hour using Idea Organizer to categorize these comments into fourteen major areas (e.g., understanding the plan, building teamwork, resistance to change), which were prioritized using the Prioritizing tool.

The next impact-analysis activity (on the second half-day) used the stakeholder Identification and Assumption Surfacing (SIAS) tool. The group spent about ninety minutes identifying stakeholders and their assumptions regarding the plan. This analysis began with each member of the group entering a list of stakeholders, which was displayed on the front screens for the group to discuss verbally. A total of eleven internal stakeholders, plus one “all external” stakeholder, were identified. This consolidated list of stakeholders was then sent to each participant. Working individually, each participant identified assumptions for each stakeholder. After much debate, some assumptions were reworded and others deleted, leaving a total of thirty-seven assumptions (e.g., “Most will support the program,” “No resources or relief from current workload [will be provided]”).

The group then determined the relative importance of each assumption to each stakeholder, and assessed its potential impact on the proposal. Group members individually rated each assumption for importance to the stakeholder and importance to the plan, although not every participant chose to rate each assumption. After the participants finished rating the assumptions, the facilitator presented the results in a graph format that highlighted the issues critical to the success of the plan. The participants individually perused the information, followed by a general discussion of the important issues.

The final activity on the second half-day was a discussion of what additional computing resources would be needed. The group spent thirty minutes using TC to generate five pages of needs. The third day was devoted to evaluating the usefulness of EMS technology to MC. After a series of electronic and verbal discussions, the group voted in favor of establishing an EMS meeting room at MC.
