# The Utilization of Information Systems Technology in State Financial Management: An Empirical Assessment

JOHN M. STEVENS, ANTHONY G. CAHILL, and JOSEPHINE M. LAPLANTE

JOHN M. STEVENS is Professor of Public Administration at the Pennsylvania State University. He has published research on public sector information systems technology, public management, business-government relations, policy implementation, financial management, and other topics, in Public Administration Review, Academy of Management Journal, Policy Studies Review, and other journals. He is the coauthor of Information Systems and Public Management and Business-Government Relations and Interdependence: A Managerial and Analytic Perspective. He serves on the editorial board of two journals: Public Productivity and Management Review and Computers, Environment and Urban Systems.

ANTHONY G. CAHILL is Assistant Professor of Public Administration at the Pennsylvania State University. His most recent publications on the use of information systems technology in state and local governments have appeared in Computers, Environment and Urban Systems; Knowledge: Creation, Diffusion, Utilization; and Public Budgeting and Financial Management, as well as in the edited volume Managing Disaster: Strategies and Policy Perspectives. He is coauthor, with John M. Stevens and E. Sam Overman, of Information Resource Management in the Public Sector: The Impact of Information Technology in the 1990's (forthcoming).

JOSEPHINE M. LAPLANTE is Associate Professor of Public Policy and Management at the University of Southern Maine. Among her recent publications are “Policy Analysis and State Executive Budgeting” in Public Productivity Review, and “Factors Associated with Financial-Decision Support Systems in State Government: An Empirical Exploration” in Public Administration Review.

ABSTRACT: Many of the budgets of state governments are larger than those of a number of Fortune 500 companies. Budgeting offices in the executive branches of state governments are increasingly utilizing a wide variety of information systems technologies to manage and track funds, engage in financial planning, and undertake sophisticated financial analysis. These offices are the “nerve centers” for financial planning, evaluation, and management of funds for state-level programs and policies. This report explores two distinct issues within the context of technology and management in state budgeting offices. The first is an examination of the role that technology plays in the organizational structures and practices of these offices. In this regard, the thirty-eight budgeting offices in the sample demonstrated a comprehensive pattern of information systems use that compares favorably with technology use in other levels of government and reflects the multiple roles that public managers in these offices must play. The second issue is an assessment of the association between environmental, organizational, and technological factors, and the adoption and utilization of information systems technology. Surrogate variables for each of the three theoretical factors were found to be associated with increased adoption and use of technology in the offices surveyed. An argument is made for a theoretical perspective on the adoption and utilization of technology that attempts to integrate each of these three perspectives.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: government finance, information systems, public sector.

## 1. Introduction

## The Growth of Information Systems Technology in Government

THE INCREASING USE OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS technology in public sector agencies at all levels of government has become increasingly well documented by researchers from a number of disciplinary perspectives over at least the last two decades. Despite the attention that the relationship between technology and governance has received in the academic community, valid generalizations frequently remain elusive. The “public sector,” after all, is a vast, complex, and often amorphous entity, with an array of widely divergent goals, legal mandates, and available resources. While the use of technology at the federal level has received considerable, if diffuse, attention, both from within government agencies $[23, 24, 25, 26]$ and by researchers outside government $[40, 78, 88, 90]$ , technology in American local governments has received by far the most widespread and systematic attention. Two groups of “core” researchers—one led by Kraemer $[47, 49, 50, 68]$ and the second led by Norris $[62, 63, 64]$ —have contributed to the development of a reasonably systematic and cumulative knowledge base.

While the Kraemer group has concentrated primarily on large-scale survey-based research, and the Norris group has concentrated more heavily on intensive interviews in smaller-scale settings, both groups have: documented the introduction and use of technology and its rapid growth in local government agencies; identified and assessed key positive and negative impacts of that technology on the work performed by local government agencies and on the organizations themselves; explored the implications of technology in the public sector generally for graduate public affairs education; and attempted to determine the factors associated with the adoption and diffusion of technology within and across public sector organizations.

In contrast to the local and federal levels of government, however, the role of technology at the state level has received relatively little attention $[6, 81]$ . Conclusions concerning the introduction, use, or impacts of information technology are based on a smaller, more widely scattered, and generally more diffuse body of research literature that shows little of the systematization, longitudinal analysis, or cumulativeness seen in the research literatures concerning the federal and especially local levels of government. The emerging state-level research literature includes annual reports by the National Association of State Information Systems containing descriptive information on hardware and software used by state governments; current universitybased national surveys of technology in state government $^{1}$ ; a nascent periodical literature including special issues of journals such as Public Administration Review in November 1986, and Public Productivity Review in January 1985; and case studies in textbooks [83].

This article attempts to add to the body of knowledge concerning technology and governance in the states by focusing on the introduction and use of that technology in thirty-eight state executive-branch budgeting offices. The next section places the work that these offices do in the context of external and internal trends affecting both financial policy making and financial management in state governments. Section 3 briefly documents the types of uses to which information technology is being put in these offices.

Section 4 examines an issue that is increasingly occupying the attention of researchers exploring the introduction and use of technology in public sector organizations: what positive and negative factors are associated with successful adoption of technology in public sector organizations? This section examines a number of competing theoretical perspectives on factors that influence the adoption and use of technology in the public sector, and then examines the empirical relationship between a subset of those factors and the adoption and utilization of information technology. The final section places the findings of this and other empirical studies of utilization within the context of a developing but still incomplete theoretical framework that can be used to examine issues of adoption and use of information technology in public sector agencies.

## Data Collection

Data and conclusions in the article are based on responses given by executives of state budgeting offices to items in an instrument completed and returned by thirty-eight of the fifty budgeting offices, a response rate of 76 percent. Follow-up calls to the nonresponding states indicated that the respondents either did not have the requisite information system capability, or had only limited experience with such systems. The instrument was pretested in one state and then revised before distribution to all states.

Given the relatively homogeneous nature of functions performed by these offices, the response rate of 76 percent was not considered a problem, so far as representativeness was concerned. Previous research has found a statistically significant relationship between population size and information systems technology use in state government agencies [82, p. 17]. A further check on the representativeness of the responding states relative to population size was performed. The mean population size for the thirty-eight responding states was 4,677,157 with a standard deviation of 4,693,143; while the mean population size for all fifty states was 4,518,220 with a standard deviation of 4,715,028. These figures suggest that there are few or no differences between population size in the sample states and those in nonsampled states that would produce bias in the data.

The instrument contained several types of items designed to produce information that would address each of the propositions discussed above. These included:

\- a mix of descriptive items on numbers and types of computer hardware and software used and the use of varying applications in a variety of organizational settings; and

\- attitudinal items regarding the perceived role, importance, and impacts of technology on key decision support functions, organizational structures, and organizational procedures and routines.

Attitudinal items were measured with either five- or seven-point Likert scales, while other variables measuring the external environment were derived from census data. More detailed information regarding selection of items is presented below.

## 2. The Environment of Financial Management in State Executive Budgeting Offices

MANAGING FINANCIAL RESOURCES IN STATE GOVERNMENTS is becoming an increasingly difficult task. As state governments move into the 1990s, they are faced by several divergent (and even conflicting) sets of demands. An emerging “new” federalism that has its origins in the presidencies of Richard Nixon and Jimmy Carter in the late 1970s, and has been intensified by Ronald Reagan and George Bush, has significantly shifted both policy setting and financing roles to the states in numerous areas. $^{2}$ An increased demand for accountability from citizens through lobbying and special interest groups has led to both the need for more publicly available information on spending priorities or levels, as well as restrictions on taxing levels or spending priorities.

Rapidly changing economies are shifting resources from traditional manufacturing sectors to service and information sectors, leading to urgent increases in spending for economic development efforts. Unlike the federal government, the majority of states may not use deficit financing in an era of increasingly scarce tax resources. Nonetheless, the budgets of many state governments exceed all but the largest of the Fortune 500 corporations. Finally, shifting demographic patterns are forcing some states, particularly in the South and Southwest, to finance vast new service and infrastructure programs while others in the traditional “rustbelt” are faced with funding services to increasingly elderly or poor populations with declining tax bases.

Executive-branch budgeting offices in the states are increasingly at the forefront of these diverse and complex influences. Within the highly centralized patterns of spending authorization, budgeting, and accountability in state governments, these offices are at the center of policy setting, management, and evaluation in every substantive policy area. Personnel from these offices are centrally involved in the highly political process which is the essence of budgeting and financial management in all organizations, whether public or private. In addition, they are formally charged with forecasting revenues, tracking both revenue and spending levels, and a wide variety of other tasks central to managing the financial resources of state governments.

In accomplishing these tasks, these offices must balance competing demands of executive agencies, constituency and special interest groups, and the public as a whole in an environment of insufficient resources. They are forced to negotiate with often fractious state legislatures, and to comply with state or federal judicial rulings that have enormous fiscal impacts (such as the recent rulings to improve funding for education to desegregate school systems in Louisiana). In sum, executive budget offices must engage in a wide range of activities with an intensity not imagined in even the recent past [51, 82]. Even in the early and mid-1980s, the use of information technology was identified as an increasingly critical part of financial management in state government, particularly in the development of budgets and the tracking of spending [3].

Three additional factors contributed to the potential for extensive use of technology in state budgeting offices: the availability of relatively inexpensive small computers, the introduction of widely available “user-friendly” software that did not require the assistance of programmers, and the increasing knowledge of and familiarity with technology held by many of those taking entry-level positions in state government. This is particularly true for those with masters degrees in Public Administration or Business Administration, who increasingly possess skills in the use of information systems technology $[38, 46]$ .

## 3. Patterns of Information Systems Technology Use in State Executive Budgeting Offices

ONE PORTION OF THE INSTRUMENT sought descriptive information on three basic issues in the use of information systems technology, developed from previous empirical work. The first issue is simply the extent to which that technology is being used for traditional public sector budgeting and financial management applications. Previous studies have detailed the transfer of technology across and between state governments $[81]$ , and patterns of information systems technology use in a wide variety of state-level organizations $[3, 7, 30, 53, 57]$ . These studies have provided significant evidence that the use of technology, viewed from the perspective of an innovation in public sector agencies, has reached the point at which it has become institutionalized within the infrastructure of most public sector agencies $[63, p. 556]$ .

The second issue we sought to examine is the extent to which the use of technology in these offices is highly centralized, or is becoming increasingly decentralized. Until the arrival of relatively low-cost, readily available personal computers in the early 1980s, the nature of the tasks that these offices performed mandated the use of large mainframe computers, often shared with other executive branch agencies. A central data-processing staff maintained the computing equipment and performed the analyses requested by all connected offices.

In the last several years, however, public sector agencies—like their private sector counterparts—have witnessed a radical decentralization of computing $[69]$ . While mainframes remain an important source of analytic power, personal computers (either in a stand-alone mode or connected in local area networks) have allowed much of the work of these offices to be disseminated to office personnel outside a traditional centralized work plan. The effects of this decentralization, as well as attitudes of office executives to it, have gone largely unexplored.

In general, the thirty-eight state budgeting offices included in the study demonstrated a pattern of information systems use consistent with the findings of previous empirical studies, and one that reflects the multiple roles that public managers must play. The number of employees in the thirty-eight offices ranged from a low of 46 to a high of 226, with a mean of 125. All thirty-eight offices make extensive use of centralized mainframe computers for major tasks such as preparation of the overall state budget and tracking of expenditure flows.

As shown in Table 1, use of computerized applications is spread across organizational staff in a fairly comprehensive fashion. Analysts reported highest use of computer applications, while, somewhat surprisingly, accounting personnel reported relatively less use of computer applications as a percentage of the total workday. At the same time, personnel from all thirty-eight offices utilize smaller personal computers in a stand-alone mode for a variety of functions. Somewhat surprisingly, twenty-nine of the offices have installed local area networks that have both micro-to-micro and micro-to-mainframe communication links for sharing programs, data, and links with other agencies.

This relatively sophisticated (and recent) use of office technology requires specific types of technological expertise and sophistication on the part of general office personnel within the organization. Even the most “user-friendly” and transparent network applications require all users to be trained in basic computing communication concepts, routines, and procedures—a level of expertise not necessary for basic single-application uses of computers such as basic spreadsheet analysis or word processing.

Table 2 presents data on applications for which personnel use stand-alone or networked personal computers, replacing mainframe use. The results yield a picture of a reasonably decentralized environment for technological applications. Personnel in these offices use computerized applications across a wide range of areas and functions, many of which go far beyond the traditional functions of tracking and analyzing annual budgets.

4. Factors Associated with the Adoption of Information Technology in State Budgeting Offices

## Theoretical Perspectives on the Utilization of Information Systems Technology: The Diffusion of Innovations

WHILE THERE IS A RELATIVE PAUCITY of empirical research and theory building concerning the issue of factors associated with the successful adoption and use of information systems technology in state governments per se, there is an extensive theoretical literature from other fields of study that addresses the question of how and why organizations choose to adopt innovations in general, and technology-related innovations in particular. It is a rich and diverse literature stemming from multiple disciplinary perspectives and functional policy areas $[7, 35, 36, 50, 81]$ . Within the literature, three distinct sets of factors associated with the successful utilization of technology have been identified: characteristics of the technology itself; factors in the external environment of the organization; and factors within the organization. We briefly examine each of these in turn.

The late 1970s and early 1980s saw the development of what were called “natural trajectories” for the adoption and integration of technology in organizations [58, 59]. Natural trajectories are in essence deterministic rates of adoption, increased use, and finally integration of technology by organizations, and are driven by the “technological imperative” [50, p. 76]—the need to adopt technology caused by the obvious benefits that the technology itself presents. Factors such as the number of computers already in place, expenditures on computing, and the relative “match” between the complexity of the technology and the tasks required of it are believed to reinforce more extensive utilization.

A similar model rooted in the role of technology itself was developed by Glaser et al. [22]. This framework, which emphasized what Glaser called “technological determinism,” focuses on technical improvements and availability of both hardware and software, and identifies these as the driving force behind adoption of information technology. The Glaser-Torrance-Schwartz (“GTS”) model covers a continuum that has basic batch, expanded batch, online inquiry, and distributed computing stages that they use to trace the development of computing in organizations. These stages suggest that technological sophistication is the major determinant of adoption. That is, the cost and efficiency dimensions associated with sophisticated information systems technology produce visible benefits that are attractive to organizations.

Technological determinism has been countered by the development of broader theoretical perspectives that move beyond the attributes of technology itself to examine the role of factors both within and outside an organization associated with successful adoption of technology. Those who advocate the role of factors external to the organization are symbolized by the work of Feller and Menzel [19] on “diffusion milieus”: “factors in the external environment which exert pressure on the organization to change its existing practices [and which] are then filtered through the internal . . . leading to decisions to adopt or reject new products and procedures” [35, p. 57]. These factors, including financial resources available in the larger governmental environment of which the organization is a part, as well as the political environment of that larger government, are believed to shape and constrain the environment within which an organization makes decisions to utilize technology [9, 15, 29, 43, 70, 72, 88].

Finally, other researchers $[47, 52, 73, 92]$ argue that factors within the organization, including the role of executives and other management personnel and the extent to which the role of technology is clearly defined by organizational personnel as it is introduced, play a predominant role in the predisposition of those in organizations to accept new forms of information technology. King and Kraemer $[40]$ , contrasting this position with those who argue for the preeminence of technological determinism in the adoption of technology, make their position clear:

we believe that organizational context is the dominant force in the evolution of computing. By this we mean that the prevailing organizational conditions that computing can affect are more powerful determiners of whether and how much computing will be used than are the components of technological infrastructure. Regardless of the significance of new technological developments from the standpoint of innovation or opportunity, such developments will not be incorporated into organizations unless prevailing organizational commitments permit and encourage their adoption. [p. 20]

These three theoretical perspectives on the adoption and use of technology share one key common theme, and are at the same time the subject of extensive debate. Shared among them is the proposition that adoption and use of technology follows a progressive (if only partially specified) trajectory, with initial adoption of technology in organizations followed by some measurable progression in which those using technology typically become more satisfied with its value and thus undertake even greater levels of use. $^{3}$ At debate, however, is the absolute as well as relative association between each of the sets of factors and adoption and use of technology in various public agency settings.

Within this broad context, we undertook an assessment of two key dimensions of technology adoption stemming directly from the research literature. Given its historical prominence in the research literature, we first focused on the hypothesized relationship between elements of the technological environment and perceived improvements in organizational performance. Second, we examined the relative associations between each of the three sets of factors and technology adoption.

## Links between the Technological Environment and Perceptions of Benefit

The hypothesized relationship between the “technological environment” and enhanced organizational performance, while often discussed, has been measured only rarely in public sector organizations. We attempted empirically to examine the relationship between elements of the “technological environment” of state budgeting offices and the positive benefits of technology on enhanced organizational performance.

Table 3 presents the results of correlation analysis that examines the association between the “technological environment” of the state budgeting offices and perceived benefits of use of that technology. The construct “technological environment” was operationalized through four surrogate variables: the number of years computers had been used; the number of computers used in the organization (both mainframe and micro); and annual expenditures for mainframe and for microcomputers. The enhancement of organizational performance through the use of technology was operationalized through five Likert items that asked respondents the extent to which the use of technology has had a positive or negative impact on the ability of the organization to:

\- use organizational resources more effectively;

\- engage in more effective organizational planning;

• perform more effective evaluation of financial data on policies and programs;

\- improve the ability of the organization to coordinate the activities of office personnel; and

\- improve the overall quality of decision making within the organization. $^{4}$

The results of Table 3 are striking. For three of the four surrogate variables comprising the technological environment, increases in the use of technology as measured by longer use, more computers, and expenditures on mainframe computing are associated at statistically significant levels of 0.05 or greater, with perceived positive impacts of technology on the ability of the organization to engage in effective resource allocation, improved planning, evaluation, and coordination, and positive increases in the overall quality of decision making within the organization. Within the limitations of the data gathered here, a clear and compelling case exists to accept the relationship between increased use and positive attitudes concerning the efficacy of technology within these organizations.

Perhaps even more striking, however, are the associations seen between expenditures on microcomputers and each of the five surrogates for improved organizational performance. The limitations of both the item used (expenditures for microcomputers may stand by itself or be seen as a surrogate for use of microcomputers) and the analytic methods used here (correlational analysis, which is severely constrained in its ability to establish causal relationships) both mandate caution in interpreting this finding, and force us to engage in informed speculation about its substantive significance.

Nevertheless, neither the consistency of results nor the strength of associations can be ignored. We suggest that the strong, across-the-board negative associations between financial support for microcomputers and improved organizational performance can be examined most fruitfully within the ongoing debate concerning the impact of information technology on centralization versus decentralization within public sector organizations discussed earlier. The implications of technology use of all kinds for management control and relations between top management and other staff have been explored by a number of researchers $[1, 8, 41, 48, 74]$ . Clearly, the transition from a total mainframe environment to regular use of stand-alone microcomputers by analysts within these state budgeting offices is one component of this ongoing debate, in that such use is likely to have an impact on the ability of managerial personnel to monitor and coordinate the activities of subordinates.

The freedom that microcomputer-based technology gives to individual budget or other analysts to perform relatively sophisticated analyses without the involvement of central data-processing staff (and potentially management personnel as well) is a double-edged sword. It can certainly improve both the quality and the quantity of analysis. Nevertheless, we speculate that five factors may account for the perceived negative impacts of the use of microcomputers seen in Table 3.

\- Increased use of microcomputers can also lead to duplication of efforts, as two or more analysts pursue similar lines of inquiry and spend time entering, manipulating, and analyzing identical data sets without the knowledge of each other or their superiors.

\- Such use may increase tendencies toward organizational entropy, as analysts pursue projects that are interesting to them but may be outside the stream of activities that senior management personnel deem important to achieve larger organizational goals.

\- It may increase errors within the organization, as analysts with sophisticated substantive skills but relatively fewer skills in computing as a whole and specific application software enter erroneous data or propagate computational errors that are subsequently used without checking by other analysts.

\- It may also lead to lower productivity either on a temporary basis (as office personnel spend more time learning new computer-based applications) or more permanently (as personnel increasingly choose to adapt new versions of software or acquire more advanced hardware in what might be called the “technological junkie” syndrome).

\- Finally, increased use of microcomputers by individual analysts may lead to the production and dissemination of sufficiently large amounts of information within the organization that “information overload” increases the time that must be spent on contrasting and assessing divergent analyses.

It must be stressed again that given the constraints of the data presented here, these possible explanations remain only informed speculation. Nevertheless, the pattern of findings relative to microcomputer expenditures in Table 3 speaks to the need for further investigation of the specific impacts of different types of technology use.

Environmental, Organizational, and Technological Correlates of the Adoption of Information Technology in State Budgeting Offices

We turn now to an assessment of the relative associations between environmental, organizational, and technological factors, and adoption of information systems technology. The variables operationalizing each of these factors (shown in Table 4) were taken from the relevant literature discussed earlier. These variables, with one exception (“State Per Capita Income”) reflect responses given to Likert items included on the survey. The construct “Adoption of Information Technology” was operationalized in two ways. Five variables were used that represent “attribute”-based definitions of adoption and utilization, and focus in part on the configuration of technology in place within an organization [21, 22, 53, 60, 61, 62, 63]. These are:

• the number of computers present;

\- the number of years computers have been used;

• expenditures on computing hardware and software;

\- the presence of an integrated general management information system; and

\- the presence of an integrated accounting, budgeting, and payroll information system.

Three additional variables were used that focus not on the presence of technology or attributes of that technology, but on what has been termed a “decision-support” perspective on utilization—one that focuses on the impacts of technology on ongoing organizational processes and activities as perceived by personnel within the organization [9, 11, 12, 13, 20, 28, 29, 32, 43, 65, 86, 87, 90]. These variables include:

\- perceived positive impact of technology on overall decision making within the organization;

\- perceived positive impact of technology on the timeliness of decision making within the organization; and

\- perceived positive impact of technology on coordination of complex activities within the organization.

These variables chosen as surrogates for “adoption” reflect the multitrait nature of technology use and the fact that adoption or use is not a dichotomous “use/no use” proposition, but rather a continuous variable that reflects differing levels of, and commitment to, the regular use of technology.

Before discussing the relationship of these findings to other relevant research, we briefly discuss a number of interesting conclusions regarding each of the three factors.

Examination of environmental factors associated with adoption and use leads to the conclusion that overall, external factors as operationalized in this study cannot be considered to be a strong correlate of technology use, since only half of the possible twenty-four associations were significant at the 0.05 level or above. Nevertheless, two interesting conclusions deserve mention:

\- As common sense might dictate, “State Per Capita Income” is positively associated with two key demographic measures of use—the number of computers in each office and expenditures on hardware and software—and two key attitudinal measures of the positive impact of technology on organizational decision making and coordination of activities.

While the two variables selected as surrogates for technological factors associated with adoption and utilization of technology do not permit extensive analysis, the perceived complexity of hardware and software shows a dynamic relationship with utilization in the significant negative associations shown with seven of the eight utilization variables. This problem is one that takes on special significance when offices move away from standard, “off-the-shelf” application, such as spreadsheet analysis, to more complex, integrative information systems that typically introduce customized software packages and applications to organizational procedures and routines. Individuals are forced to move beyond a relatively simple computerized application of a substantive procedures with which they are already familiar (e.g., budget analysis, statistical analysis), to use of technology to assist in the general management of the office as a whole.

Despite the limitations imposed by the noncausal nature of the correlational analysis used, the evidence presented here partially supports previous studies that have examined the roles of at least two of the three factors outlined here. Cahill, Stevens, and LaPlante [7] found that a mix of environmental, organizational, and technological variables accounted for utilization of information systems technology in key decision support functions. Stevens, Cahill, and Overman [81] found that both environmental and organizational variables played a role in the transfer of customized software programs among agencies in different states. Rahman and Cahill [69] found that internal, organizational factors as well as environmental factors both influenced the adoption of information systems technology in the public sectors of fourteen developing nations.

Lawless [52] investigated both the role of intraorganizational support and characteristics of the technology in a decision support system in criminal justice. Laudon [53] found that environmental and organizational variables each played a part in initial decisions to adopt technology as well as the role technology played in organizational decision making. Ives, Hamilton, and Davis [33] reviewed 300 doctoral dissertations on information systems technology in public and private organizations and found a pattern of technology use related to, and dependent on, factors both within the organizational environment as well as in the larger external environment.

## 5. Propsects for Integrating Theories on the Utilization of Information Systems Technology

theoretical perspectives offered in the research literature on the adoption and utilization of information systems technology. The results presented here verify that while environmental variables are indeed associated with utilization, they do not show that external variables are equally or more important than organizational or technological factors. Indeed, the moderately strong negative associations found between perceived political influences and five of the eight indicators of utilization, combined with the associations found among the organizational correlates of utilization, make a strong inferential case that those who stress the importance of organizational factors in the adoption of information technology in public sector agencies are also correct in their interpretation. In the same way, at least one technological variable is strongly associated with indicators of utilization.

We conclude by discussing the implications of these findings for both organizational practice as well as future research. In terms of organizational practice, one key implication is that the processes by which information technology is adopted and integrated into an organization are amenable to deliberate control and guidance. The use of information technology, in that it is driven by a combination of external, organizational and technological imperatives, can be shaped by initiatives made by those who control and shape the organization. While external factors will shape basic constraints and opportunities within which the utilization of technology takes place within any organization, internal factors amenable to the control of managers, such as allocation of resources, personnel levels, and planned expenditures, are also factors that will do much to contribute to the application of information systems technology in the states.

In terms of the implications of the findings presented here for future research efforts on technology use in public sector organizations, we believe they take on particular significance within the context of a recurring theme that has occupied much of the research literature on technological innovations in public sector agencies over the last decade. There is an urgent need for cumulative, comprehensive progress in constructing an integrated, cumulative approach to the study of technology in public sector organizations $[7, 11, 47, 50, 68, 81, 92]$ ; a theme that echoes criticisms directed at progress in general innovations research $[16]$ .

The authors subscribe to positions held by Tornatzky et al. [84, 85] that there is no empirical evidence that supports an exclusive focus on any one set of factors—environmental, organizational, or technological—as the prime cause of the adoption and increased use of information systems technology. Nor is there any reason to accept on an a priori basis, for purposes of exploring and explaining adoption and utilization of information systems technology, any theoretical perspective that focuses on a single, exclusionary set of factors thought to be determinants of adoption or factors associated with successful adoption and utilization.

To do so would be to overlook the highly contextual nature of technology utilization in a wide variety of organizational, political, economic, and substantive contexts. Information systems technology, as it is utilized in public sector agencies, is not a static phenomenon; indeed, the opposite is true. Information systems technology in any one organizational setting is a complex, interrelated “bundle” of hardware, software-based application programs, and other program-based capabilities such as electronic communication. Individuals shape that hardware and software, as well as the procedures that govern the use and purposes of that technology, to meet multiple, divergent, and changing needs. It is a highly malleable innovation that is subject to ongoing “reinvention” [34, 50, 53, 71, 77].

We argue that the highly complex nature of technology, as well as the great number of divergent settings into which it is placed within the public sector as a whole, highlights the need for a theory of technological innovations in the public sector. This framework should explicitly recognize the existence of equally malleable and changing combinations of factors that lead to the adoption and utilization of information systems technology. It also explicitly integrates multiple theoretical perspectives on adoption and utilization, and attempts to assess the relative role and importance of these factors in a variety of settings and contexts. The concerns expressed relative to the paucity of cumulative empirical research will only be addressed when researchers take a wider and more holistic view of the concept of “utilization.”
