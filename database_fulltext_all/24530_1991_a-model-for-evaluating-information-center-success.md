# A Model for Evaluating Information Center Success

SIMHA R. MAGAL

SIMHA R. MAGAL is Assistant Professor of Management Information Systems in the College of Business Administration at Bowling Green State University. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Georgia, and has been published previously in Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Data Base, and Information and Management. His current research interests include various aspects of end-user computing and information systems effectiveness.

ABSTRACT: With the growing amount of resources consumed by end-user computing (EUC), its support organization, the information center (IC), is under increasing pressure to justify its existence. This study proposes an instrument and a model for evaluating IC success, based on the user satisfaction construct. Three dimensions of satisfaction are identified: Quality of IC Services, Quality of User-developed Applications (UDA), and User Self-sufficiency. Five hypotheses concerning the relationship among these dimensions and IC success are proposed. The instrument and the model are empirically tested. There is strong evidence of the validity of the instrument. Results indicate that the three dimensions are good predictors of IC success. All of the hypotheses were supported to varying degrees.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: end-user computing (EUC), information center, evaluation, user satisfaction.

END-USER COMPUTING (EUC) is an alternative to traditional sources of acquiring information. An information center (IC) is an established means of supporting EUC activities. Primarily because of advances in technology and a more aware and informed user community, the ICs have seen substantial growth in the last few years. To sustain this growth, the IC's consumption of an increasing portion of an organization's resources must be justified. To date, there have been few attempts to identify methodologies to evaluate ICs. The literature has focused on instances of success and failures, critical success factors, and limited dimensions of IC effectiveness.

Hoopes [19] reviewed the literature on the various measures of IC evaluation, and reasons for such evaluation, and proposed a model to measure IC success. The model identifies three reasons for evaluating ICs: to justify an IC's existence; to improve IC performance; and to motivate IC staff. The model also presents a variety of evaluation techniques suitable for use in the three situations. While Hoopes argues that any one method may not provide a “complete and robust” measure of IC performance, she suggests that user satisfaction with the IC is a useful criterion in all three situations.

In this study, a model for evaluating IC success, based on user satisfaction, is developed, and specific hypotheses are proposed. Three dimensions of satisfaction are identified: quality of IC services; user self-sufficiency; and quality of user-developed applications. Three primary hypotheses relate these dimensions directly to IC success, while two secondary hypotheses deal with interrelationships among the dimensions. The model and the hypotheses are empirically tested.

## Information System Success

PRIOR STUDIES HAVE IDENTIFIED VARIOUS MEASURES of information system success. These include measures of economic impact (cost/benefit, ROI, etc.), system utilization, and user satisfaction. Economic variables, however, are difficult, if not impossible, to operationalize and measure $[17, 25]$ . As a result, system utilization and user satisfaction are the two most widely used measures $[17]$ . Of these, user satisfaction has been preferred over system usage. The reason for this preference is not always clear; however, the following arguments are offered: (1) The operationalization of utilization in terms of the number of times a record, file, or program has been used, etc., ignores the context in which work is accomplished and the extent to which information is actually used $[25]$ . (2) System utilization is only valid when such use is voluntary, and thus may not be appropriate in all settings $[17, 21]$ . (3) Utilization is not an appropriate measure of performance because it is one of many variables that ultimately affect performance $[34]$ , and because success leads to utilization, not vice versa $[5]$ . Galletta and Lederer $[17]$ identify three critical reasons for the importance of user satisfaction: (1) management might have limited direct measures of MIS success; (2) user satisfaction may improve the quality of work life; and (3) voluntary use of information systems increases with user satisfaction.

As a result, user satisfaction has received the greater attention. The most commonly used instrument for measuring user satisfaction is one proposed by Ives, Olsen, and Baroudi [21]. Baroudi and Orlikowski [6] proposed a modified short-form instrument that includes three dimensions: satisfaction with EDP services; information product; and user involvement/ knowledge.

## Traditional versus IC Environment

WHILE USER SATISFACTION IS A POPULAR CONSTRUCT for measuring information system success, it has not been used to evaluate information centers. In adapting user satisfaction measures to ICs, several key differences between the traditional IS environment and the IC environment must be considered. These differences relate to the manner in which systems are developed and used. Doll and Torkzadeh [15] identify three key distinguishing characteristics of the end-user computing environment: direct user interaction; end-user development tools; and a shorter systems development life cycle. In the traditional environment, the systems personnel are responsible for development, with users sometimes participating in the process, while in EUC, users have greater and more direct control over the development process. In EUC, users initiate, develop, and implement systems themselves, with IC staff providing training and technical advice. The availability of user-friendly tools makes it easier for the end users to develop their own systems with minimal reliance on IC staff expertise. The availability of such tools and the reduced involvement of systems professionals in the development process shortens the development cycle and makes it less rigid. Typically, end users use an evolutionary method of development involving the creation of an initial system quickly, using the system, and refining the system as needed.

Doll and Torkzadeh [14] developed a measure of end-user computing satisfaction based on the Ives, Olsen, and Baroudi instrument. They argue that the EDP staff/services and user involvement/knowledge items are inappropriate for an end-user computing environment, and focus on the information product dimension. They identify information content, accuracy, format, ease of use, and timeliness as dimensions of end-user computing satisfaction. However, Doll and Torkzadeh focus on users' satisfaction with a specific application developed, and not the IC. Their instrument measures the quality of the applications, but does not measure the quality or performance of the IC. While quality of applications developed is important in evaluating IC success, other dimensions of IC support and services are relevant.

The services provided by IC staff may be the most visible and critical function of an IC. Numerous studies have highlighted the importance of IC support to users $[8, 24, 33]$ . Rivard $[30]$ and Rivard and Huff $[32]$ found that user satisfaction with support for EUC activities is one of the two most important factors affecting overall user satisfaction. Support includes a range of forms (training, tool selection, design and debugging assistance, etc.), as well as factors involving the quality of support (knowledge of software, understanding of users' applications, etc.). Thus, the IC staff/services measure (akin to EDP staff/services) is an essential component of user satisfaction with the IC.

User involvement/knowledge is substantially different in the IC environment than in the traditional IS environment. By definition, users are involved in EUC activities. A premise of the IC is that users will develop their own applications if provided with training and education in the use of technology $[18]$ . The value of an IC is in the knowledge that it provides users to make them self-sufficient in developing applications independently. Any evaluation of ICs must therefore include a measure of users' self-sufficiency. Rivard $[30]$ and Rivard and Huff $[32]$ highlight the importance of such a measure. In their study, independence from data processing had the highest correlation with overall satisfaction with the EUC support.

## An A Priori Model for IC Success

THE PROPOSED MODEL TO EVALUATE IC success is based on the Ives et al. instrument for user satisfaction. Although their instrument is not directly applicable to the measurement of satisfaction with information centers, many of its components are relevant.

The dependent variable in the proposed model is IC success, defined as the overall success of the IC in fulfilling its functions. The dimensions of IC success include quality of user-developed applications (QUDA), quality of IC services (QICS), and user self-sufficiency (USS). The components of each dimension are listed in Table 1 and discussed below.

## Quality of IC Services

An information center supports EUC through a variety of services. The services are diverse and include hardware and software support, data management assistance, training and education, and functional (applications development) support $[10]$ . The specific types of services change over time, with some being discontinued and others being added $[10]$ . The quality of the services provided by an IC is one dimension of IC success in the proposed model.

The Ives et al. instrument identified EDP staff and services as a dimension of satisfaction with traditional information systems, and consisted of the following specific variables: relationship with EDP staff; attitude of EDP staff; communication with EDP staff; convenience of access; processing of requests for changes; and time required for new developments. The last two are not relevant in the context of an IC as users themselves develop and modify their applications. The first three relate to a “people orientation” identified as important in end-user computing $[31]$ and to improved relations $[3, 11, 12]$ .

There are other service-oriented variables that have been identified as relevant in the literature in the context of an IC. These are technical competence of the IC staff [7, 31], relevance of services offered [31], and responsiveness of the support staff [3, 12].

Therefore, the following specific measures are included in the quality of IC services dimension of IC success:

\- relationship with IC staff;

\- attitude of IC staff;

\- communication with IC staff;

\- convenience of access to IC services;

• technical competence of IC staff;

• relevance of services offered by IC;

• responsiveness of IC staff.

Quality of UDA is analogous to the information product dimension defined by Ives et al. Of the specific measures used by them, relevance of applications is not included, because users, not the IC staff, determine the type of applications developed. Quality of UDA thus includes the following measures used by Ives et al.:

## User Self-Sufficiency

User self-sufficiency has been offered as a goal of an information center $[16]$ . Rivard and Huff $[32]$ found that being independent of the information systems function was the most important factor affecting user satisfaction with the UDA experience. One aspect of independence is having control over applications development $[3, 12, 32]$ .

In the Ives et al. study, this relates to the knowledge and involvement factor and includes degree of training provided, users' understanding of DP, users' sense of participation, and processing of requests for changes. The variable processing of requests for changes is not relevant in evaluating ICs because the IC staff does not implement changes to users' systems. User self-sufficiency is thus defined as the third dimension in the model and includes:

• degree of training provided;

\- users' understanding of IC concept and services;

• feeling of participation by users;

• feeling of control over UDA;

• independence from traditional IS function.

## The Hypotheses

FIGURE 1 ILLUSTRATES THESE DIMENSIONS and relationships constituting the proposed model. Five hypotheses formally stating these relationships are discussed below. The first three address the direct link between the three dimensions and IC success and are of primary concern. The remaining two involve the interrelationships among these dimensions.

H1: There is a positive association between the quality of IC services (QICS) and information center success (ICS).

This hypothesis is derived from the suggestions that perceived high quality of services provided leads to higher user satisfaction with those services. Satisfaction with EUC support was found to increase overall satisfaction with the UDA experience $[32]$ and users thought ICs were performing well when they valued the IC as a source of support $[7]$ . Brancheau and Wetherbe $[8]$ found that higher-rated ICs had competent and friendly staff, while lower-rated ICs had minimal service. Magal et al. $[24]$ identify the type and quality of IC services as the most important critical success factor for information centers.

H2: There is a positive association between quality of UDA (QUDA) and IC success (ICS).

This hypothesis follows from the premise that ultimately it is the quality of the end-product that determines the value of the IC. Limited support is offered by O'Donnell and March [28], who suggest that the absence of certain quality characteristics (accuracy and reliability) in UDA will diminish the productivity of EUC. Couger [11] found that higher-quality applications were developed in organizations that were successful in supporting EUC.

H3: There is a positive association between user self-sufficiency (USS) and IC success (ICS).

Presumably, if users perceive that the IC has made them self-sufficient, then they will rate the IC as being successful. In a study of organizations that provided EUC support through the traditional IS function, independence from the IS function was found to be the most important factor affecting overall satisfaction with the EUC experience $[32]$ . Several studies have associated control mechanisms with lower performance ratings of ICs. Such controls limit the degree to which users are independent and self-sufficient in developing their applications. Brancheau and Wetherbe $[8]$ found low-control policies in higher-rated ICs. Couger $[11]$ found that “hard” control leads to an “us versus them” attitude, causing users to develop ways around such controls. Wetherbe and Leitheiser $[35]$ found that users sought support from ICs over IS departments because of greater user control in the former.

H4: There is a positive association between quality of IC services (QICS) and the quality of UDA (QUDA).

This hypothesis is based on the presumption that providing quality services, in itself, does not make an IC successful; and that it is a quality end-product (UDA) that determines the value of the IC. In support of this, Couger [11] found that the quality of information improved in firms that provided a variety of support services (hardware/software support, training, etc.)

H5: There is a positive association between quality of IC services (QICS) and user self-sufficiency (USS).

User self-sufficiency is a state where users are able to develop applications with minimal reliance on IS professionals. This ability presumes knowledge and understanding of tools and techniques needed for developing applications. Nelson and Cheney [27] found that providing different types of training (college, in-house, vendor) increases the user's ability to create applications. Also, Couger [11] found that users were better able to tailor applications to their needs in organizations that provided support services.

## The Study Method

DATA WERE GATHERED THROUGH A QUESTIONNAIRE administered to users from 270 ICs. The ICs were selected randomly from membership lists of IC user groups [13] across the United States.

The questionnaire contained several demographic questions, one classifying the end users, the sixteen evaluation measures, and one overall measure of success. A four-category classification of users was adapted from one developed by Rockart and

Flannery [33]:

1. nonprogramming end users: those who use software provided by others;

2. command-level users: those who satisfy their information needs through limited use of command languages such as FOCUS, EXPRESS, SQL, etc.;

3. end-user programmers: those who develop their own applications using command and procedural languages;

4. functional support staff: those sophisticated programmers who support other users within their functional areas.

The other two categories in their classification (EUC support personnel and DP programmers) were not included because they represent IC staff, not IC users.

The users rated the sixteen evaluation measures on two seven-point Likert scales. The perceived level of importance of each measure was measured on one scale and the perception of the level of performance on the other. The IC's overall performance was measured on another seven-point scale ranging from a low to a high level of success.

The questionnaire was pretested using seven local information centers and some minor changes were incorporated into the final instrument. Since the IC success measures were based on a previously tested and validated instrument, this small number was considered sufficient to evaluate the questions for clarity.

## Analysis

THE ANALYSIS INVOLVED SEVERAL STEPS. First, descriptive statistics on the demographic variables and the evaluation measures were compiled. Next, the two scales (importance and performance) were correlated with the overall measure of success to determine which best represents the users' perspective. Then the instrument was evaluated for reliability and validity. Finally, the hypotheses were tested using regression analysis.

## Demographics

One hundred and thirty-six of the 270 ICs (50 percent) responded in the study. However, users from only sixty-five (24 percent) ICs returned a total of 174 usable questionnaires, while the other ICs provided reasons for not participating. These reasons included absence of a formal IC (12 percent), time constraints (10 percent), corporate policy against such participation (2 percent), and other reasons (3 percent). While this response rate may be considered low, it is typical of studies of this nature [24, 29].

Figure 2 contains demographic data for the participating organizations, including type of industry, geographic location, and the type of users. The responding organizations represent a variety of geographic areas and industries.

Thirty percent of the users were nonprogramming end-users, 44 percent were command-level users, 17 percent were end-user programmers, and 9 percent functional support staff.

## Evaluation of the Instrument

The instrument was evaluated for construct, convergent, discriminant, and content validity, and for reliability.

Construct validity is established by showing that the instrument is in fact measuring the construct it is intended to measure, and was evaluated in two ways. First, correlations between each evaluation measure and the overall measure of IC success were used. This assumes that the overall measure is valid. High correlations indicate construct validity. In this study, the smallest correlation was 0.36 and most (thirteen of sixteen) were at least 0.50, all significant at alpha = 0.0001. This suggests that the instrument is valid to the extent that the overall measure of success is valid.

The second method of determining construct validity is through factor analysis. A principal components analysis using a varimax rotation was conducted. Three factors emerged with eigenvalues of greater than one. The different measures of success loaded onto the three factors as suggested in the model. Table 4 presents the results of the factors analysis. Only one of the sixteen measures had loadings of less than the conventional 0.50. Loadings on eleven of the sixteen measures were greater than 0.60. These factors explained about 63 percent of the variance. The reliability of the factors was measured using Cronbach's alpha. The reliabilities (alpha) of the three factors are: QICS = 0.87; USS = 0.82; QUDA = 0.86; all of these are above generally accepted levels. The results of the factor analysis provide strong evidence for construct validity.

Convergent validity is the extent to which each measure correlates with other measures in the same construct (factor). High correlation among measures within each factor provides evidence of convergent validity. In this study, all correlations were significant at alpha = 0.0001 (see Table 5). All correlations among the QUDA measures were greater than 0.50, nine of ten correlations among the USS measures were greater than 0.40, and sixteen of twenty-one correlations among measures of QICS were greater than 0.40. This provides evidence of convergent validity.

Discriminant validity is the extent to which each measure is different from measures in other constructs (factors). This is determined by counting the number of times a measure has a higher correlation with a measure from another factor than with measures in its own factor. Campbell and Fiske [9] suggest that a count of less than one-half the potential comparisons is acceptable. An examination of the correlation matrix (Table 5) indicates that in 48 of 166 comparisons the correlations were higher. This suggests that the instrument has discriminant validity. Evidence of content validity consists of internal consistency and correlations of measures with other measures of the construct. High item-total correlations indicate internal consistency. In this study the item-total correlations were at least 0.67, with most (fourteen of sixteen) greater than 0.70. This, combined with high correlations of each measure with the overall measure of success, is indicative of content validity.

## Hypothesis Testing

The hypotheses were tested via regression analysis. The following three regression functions were evaluated:

$$
1. \text {   SUCCESS   } = \text {   QICS   } + \text {   QUDA   } + \text {   USS   }.
$$

$$
2. \mathrm{QUDA} = \mathrm{QICS}.
$$

3. USS = QICS.

The results are presented in Table 6. All three regression functions were significant (prob. > F = 0.0001). The first regression function indicates that the three dimensions in the model account for 74 percent $(R^{2})$ of the variance in IC success. Of the three, QICS is the best predictor of IC success (beta = 0.58). QUDA (beta = 0.16) and USS (beta = 0.22) contribute less to the prediction of IC success. The first regression function provides strong support for the first three hypotheses, indicating definite direct links between the three dimensions and IC success. The second regression function examines the impact of QICS on QUDA. The explanatory power of this function $(R^{2} = 0.36)$ , while considerably less than that of first function, is still significant and supports our fourth hypothesis. The last function supports the fifth hypothesis, with QICS explaining 55 percent $(R^{2})$ of the variance in USS.

## Discussion

THE USER SATISFACTION CONSTRUCT is a widely used tool for evaluating information technologies, and is applicable in evaluating ICs. This study provides a means for evaluating ICs based on user satisfaction.

Three dimensions of user satisfaction with ICs are identified: quality of IC services; user self-sufficiency; and quality of UDA. With regard to IC services, the model suggests that while an IC must provide relevant services and the staff must be technically competent, nontechnical traits (relationship, communication, attitude, response) of the IC staff are valued highly by users. This has significant implications in staffing decisions, since it highlights essential technical and nontechnical qualities for IC staff.

The dimension of UDA quality need not be emphasized since it is a basic necessity for IC success. User self-sufficiency, however, merits some discussion. If an IC succeeds in making its users self-sufficient, has it then rendered itself dispensable? Very likely not, particularly in a rapidly changing end-user computing environment. User self-sufficiency is a dynamic state. As users become self-sufficient in some aspects and technologies of end-user computing, others areas will arise where they will need the assistance of the IC. An important implication is that the IC must constantly reevaluate its mission and the services it offers. As users become less dependent on the IC, some services, such as basic training, may become unnecessary, while others, such as specialized training and research on new products and uses, may take on greater emphasis.

There is strong evidence of construct and content validity, and for the reliability of these dimensions of satisfaction with ICs. However, there is limited support for convergent and discriminant validity, which are not evaluated for the Ives et al. instrument; this issue merits further investigation. Refining the measures used and including additional measures could improve the overall validity of the instrument.

The direct effect of the three dimensions on IC success is of prime importance. For the sample in this study, these three dimensions accounted for 74 percent of the variance in IC success, which is substantial. While the evidence of the direct impact of the three dimensions on IC success is significant, support for some of the intermediate relationships hypothesized is moderate. QICS has the greatest influence in the model, reinforcing prior findings that there is no substitute for quality IC services. The effect of quality of UDA on IC success, although significant, is weaker. There are two possible explanations for this finding. First, users may feel that making them self-sufficient is more important than an IC's direct contribution to quality of UDA (through involvement in development). Second, users may believe that the quality of UDA is ultimately in their own hands, and therefore they do not hold the IC responsible for it in any significant way. This indicates that users understand that the role of IC staff in applications development is passive, rather than active.

Historically, the role of the IC has been to facilitate (support) and coordinate (monitor and control) EUC activities $[22, 24]$ . The results of this study suggest that the support provided must be aimed at making users self-sufficient in developing applications.

These results are based on a sample with widely varying characteristics, likely to affect the relationships depicted in the model. For instance, user characteristics (type of users, level of expertise, etc.) and organizational characteristics (IC policies and strategies, etc.) appear to influence end-user computing effectiveness $[4]$ . The instrument developed provides a means for studying such effects. As a preliminary examination of the influence of type of users on the three dimensions of satisfaction, the data were analyzed using the general linear models (GLM) procedure. The results revealed no significant (alpha = 0.05) difference in the ratings of the three dimensions between the various types of users. This is only an illustration of the utility of the proposed instrument and the finding should be treated with caution due to the small number of users in each type.

Finally, ICs have a dynamic quality. Studies have proposed that ICs evolve over time, characterized by changes in goals, services, type of users, policies, etc. [20, 23]. The proposed instrument can be used to trace changes in user satisfaction as an IC evolves, to identify any sources of dissatisfaction, and to find solutions. For instance, if user self-sufficiency is rated poorly, this might indicate a need to change the type and extent of training offered.

## Conclusion

THE PROPOSED MODEL PREDICTS a considerable portion of the variance in IC success. Given the number of organizations that support EUC through information centers, and at a time when IC managers are feeling pressures to justify their existence, this model provides IC managers with insights into how users evaluate their ICs. In general, to be considered successful by its users, an IC must provide services aimed at making users self-sufficient in developing quality applications. However, the specific manner in which users evaluate their ICs will depend on a variety of user and organizational characteristics. The proposed instrument facilitates IC evaluation and provides a basis for studying the effects of other variables on IC success.
