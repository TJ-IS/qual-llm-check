# A Contingency Model of DSS Development Methodology

BAY ARINZE

BAY ARINZE is Assistant Professor of Management Information Systems in the Department of Management at Drexel University. He holds an M.Sc. and a Ph.D. in systems analysis from the London School of Economics and Political Science. His current research interests include DSS design methodologies and applications, and knowledge-based systems and their uses in operations management. He has published articles in the Journal of Management Information Systems, IEEE Transactions in Engineering Management, Computers and Industrial Engineering, Industrial Marketing Management, and the ACM Sigart Special Issue on Knowledge Acquisition.

ABSTRACT: Decision Support Systems (DSS) are now an established part of the information systems mainstream, both in research and in practice. The DSS development approach differs from traditional systems development not only with regard to its associated paradigms and orientation, but also in the methods of requirements analysis it employs. This paper surveys the major methodologies used for DSS development. It analyzes them by structure, paradigm, and orientation, and discusses their underlying assumptions. A research model is then developed and used to relate the functions of DSS methodology to decision-making environments and the relevant processes within them. Finally, a contingency theory is presented to show how each of the surveyed methodologies reduces the lack of structure in the decision-making environment, and how this may be used by DSS developers for selecting a DSS methodology. Other components of the research model are discussed, to identify further prerequisites for improved understanding of the DSS role and application.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: decision support systems, DSS development methodology, DSS requirements analysis.

## 1. Introduction

THE AREA OF DECISION SUPPORT SYSTEMS (DSS) is currently enjoying the combined attentions of both business and academia. As organizations expend increasing effort on developing and implementing DSS, corresponding developments have continued to take place in the methodological area. The general consensus among the various strands of DSS research is that the traditional methods of systems analysis and development are largely ill-suited to the unique requirements of this class of systems $[9, 10, 11, 17]$ .

Several researchers have proposed DSS development methodologies to aid developers building such systems. These methodologies provide a variety of tools for DSS requirements analysis, specification, and design, and are targeted primarily at the semistructured, managerial problem domain. While broad similarities exist among them, they differ according to paradigm and the means of requirements analysis employed. As the DSS field enters maturity, with an accompanying proliferation of systems, a framework for analyzing the role and purpose of DSS methodology will prove helpful. Such a framework would assist in describing the nature of its links with the decision process, the real world, and the computer-based DSS.

This paper aims at satisfying these needs. It begins by carrying out a survey of existing DSS methodologies, and describes them with regard to paradigm, orientation, structure, and methods of requirements analysis employed. A research model is then developed to describe the role of DSS methodology. Finally, several DSS methodologies are analyzed with respect to their capability for reducing the lack of structure in DSS development, forming the basis of a contingency theory for methodology selection. The intended benefits of the research model and contingency theory include a more effective selection and use of DSS methodologies and the requirements analysis methods used within them. Other benefits include a clarified understanding of both the DSS function and the dynamics of the DSS development process.

The rest of this paper is outlined as follows. Section 2 surveys the major DSS methodologies and examines them, using specific criteria. Section 3 develops the research model defining the role of DSS methodology. Section 4 then introduces the contingency theory for methodology selection. In section 5, further issues from the research model are identified, while section 6 presents the paper's conclusions.

## 2. A Survey of DSS Methodologies

THE SURVEY OF DSS METHODOLOGIES carried out in this section spans the period between 1971 and 1989—approximately nineteen years. Ten DSS methodologies are identified, and while the list is certainly not exhaustive, the major DSS research strands are represented by the group. Three criteria were chosen as the basis for analyzing the methodologies, namely: paradigm, structure, and orientation. In selecting criteria for analyzing DSS methodologies, care was taken to use those criteria that highlight the variance among the methodologies and provide maximum descriptive power. These criteria and the rationale for their selection are discussed next.

The paradigm refers to the models underlying the methodologies, and gives insights to the type and usage of formalisms within them. This dimension helps to illustrate the differences in emphasis in the methods used, and to capture their essence. The four paradigms recognized in the literature are the decision-driven; process-driven; data-driven; and systemic methodologies.

The second dimension of structure is also an important descriptor for analyzing DSS methodology, and indicates whether a stage or contingency approach is used for guiding the DSS development process.

The third dimension involves the developmental guidelines adopted by DSS researchers in the area of DSS orientation. While one school of thought promotes the early adoption of normative models in DSS implementation, another promotes a gradual migration from the descriptive model to the normative. This represents an important indicator of the methodology's emphasis in problem modeling.

## 2.1. Criteria Selection

In performing a comparison of IS methodologies, Wood-Harper and Fitzgerald [29] use a set of assessment criteria, comprising the paradigm, model, and objective of the methodologies. These describe “a specific way of thinking about problems” (paradigm); a subjective means of representing reality (model); and the objective of the methodology, which could be analysis or problem solving.

While their major distinction is between systems and science paradigms, it is recognized that in the DSS area, prevailing methodologies are primarily nonscience, and a set of more subtle distinctions may be identified by way of the four basic paradigms discussed below. Furthermore, these selected paradigms have the additional benefit of indicating a means by which reality is abstracted; this is similar to Wood-Harper and Fitzgerald's model continuum. Their objective dimension is discounted as being of insufficient descriptive value to the taxonomy as is their “craft” versus “noncraft” differentiation of methodologies. In the first case, the understood objective is typically an improvement in decision-making effectiveness, while in the second, the nonscience DSS methodologies universally exhibit greater requirements for “craft” skills. Rather, the structure and orientation of DSS methodology, indicating progression guidelines and modeling orientation (normative versus descriptive), have been identified as two important criteria for describing and distinguishing among DSS methodologies.

## Analysis by Paradigm

Wood-Harper and Fitzgerald [29] indicate that the paradigm underlying a methodology is a major contributor to understanding the working of that methodology. As used here, it may be viewed as a reflection of a set of formalisms resulting from a specific perspective toward the world. The earliest identifiable paradigm is that of Gerrity's [7] methodology, namely, the decision-driven paradigm. In viewing the decision and its improvement as the raison d'être for DSS, the decision-centered focus was naturally preeminent in earlier approaches. The focus in this paradigm is on a comprehensive analysis of the decision processes, and their subsequent support and improvement via computer-based DSS.

The process-driven paradigm, on the other hand, emphasizes the identification of DSS representations as the central component of DSS development, the key representation being the processes that later comprise DSS functions. The methodologies that utilize this paradigm variously employ “verbs” [13], and “operators” [25, 30] to specify the processes that represent the DSS capabilities.

The third distinct paradigm is the systemic paradigm, embodied in the approaches of Ariav and Ginzberg [2] and Sauter and Schofer [21]. Here, a systemic view is taken of the DSS, with an analysis of internal components and environmental influences. In the latter methodology, this results in a contingency-based approach, with decision focus, environmental stability, and experience serving as parameters that determine the form of a suitable DSS. The former methodology (discussed later) is similar in nature. The last paradigm identified is the data-driven one [16], which, as indicated, is given marginal recognition in the DSS literature. This paradigm views the identification, collection, structuring, and management of the DSS database as the key requirement for effective DSS development, with other activities revolving around it. While the importance of the DSS database is not underestimated, the data-driven paradigm is not generally embraced by any of the major approaches.

## Analysis by Structure

Structure represents the second property used to describe DSS methodologies. What is referred to here is the type of prescriptive guidelines for DSS developers constructing DSS. The first grouping of methodologies includes those that are stage-driven, signifying that a sequence of steps or processes are specified for the DSS developer to follow. All except the systemic methodologies are stage-driven and iterative, and include the following steps in some form:

1. entry;

2. analysis/predesign;

3. DSS design;

4. prototyping/experimentation;

5. implementation (for "institutionalized" DSS);

6. maintenance and evolution.

The second grouping of methodologies includes the two systemic methodologies, which identify particular DSS configurations to satisfy specific environmental and task characteristics. They are therefore not prescriptive in the previous sense, but specify a means to select DSS developmental options and configurations based on the context.

## Analysis by Orientation

The third criterion for grouping DSS methodologies is orientation, which pertains to whether the methodology has a normative or a descriptively oriented emphasis. The former type, which stress the early adoption of normative models, all fall under the decision-driven paradigm (see Figure 1). Every other paradigm, however, possesses methodologies that are descriptively oriented—that is, they focus on the support of descriptive decision models with only a gradual migration, if any, to normative models. Stabell [26] is the most vigorous advocate of the earliest possible use of normative models after the initial investigation, the diagnosis of changes implied by the normative models being used to develop a functional specification for the DSS.

## 2.2. DSS Methodologies—A Representative Survey

This section goes on to describe, in some detail, one representative DSS methodology from each of the four paradigms, and to list six other methodologies included in the survey.

Stabell: “A Decisional Imagination”

Stabell [26] identifies support as the common theme of the DSS definition, and advances a decision-centered methodology for developing such systems. Emphasis is placed on changing the existing decision process to increase decision-making effectiveness, which is viewed as the central objective for DSS development. Stabell argues that “managers cannot improve their decision-making unaided,” and prescribes the use of “decision research” to describe and understand organizational decision making. This concept involves an early adoption of normative models after an initial investigation. Stabell’s decision-centered methodology encompasses eight stages, which include entry; data collection; problem modeling; and a derivation of required changes in decision making. The diagnosis of changes implied by the normative models then yields the DSS functional specification; the DSS is subsequently designed and built, then implemented, and, finally, monitored and evaluated after implementation.

Perhaps anticipating a criticism of the early adoption of normative models and possible misapplication, Stabell urges the use of a “decisional imagination” by the DSS developer for rapidly producing a working diagnosis, with only a limited exposure to the problem. “Decision channeling” is the term coined for shifting the existing decision process to the specified norm, and the IDC model is employed as a part of this multistage methodology.

## Ariav and Ginzberg: A Systemic Approach

The methodology proposed by Ariav and Ginzberg $[2]$ builds on the organizational analyses advanced by Keen and Scott Morton $[13]$ , focusing explicitly on the second “S” in DSS. They urge the simultaneous consideration of five systemic aspects of DSS, namely: environments, role, DSS components, their arrangement, and required resources. They advocate an initial study of the environment, followed by an analysis of the required DSS components and their arrangement. The “ideal” specification produced may then be developed using available resources. In their view, the DSS components and arrangement (the inside) must follow from an understanding of the environment and role (the outside). The contingency-based model and methodology are clearly systemic in outlook, exist at a higher level conceptually than most other methodologies discussed, and lack detailed guidelines for performing requirements analysis.

## Menkus: A Data-Driven Methodology

Menkus [16] diverges substantially from the DSS mainstream in perceiving DSS as “specialized advanced design types of information retrieval systems.” He views the determination of the decision maker’s information requirements as the main prerequisite for successful DSS development. This methodology initially identifies users of information and their requirements, and then derives a “logical information analysis structure.” Subsequently, information sources are selected, information is collected, and is then organized in conformity with the previously defined structure. Finally, data access mechanisms are developed for the user, together with a feedback mechanism to reflect changes in requirements and data sources. The eight stages have as their aim a comprehensive specification and definition of information structures that will, it is hoped, satisfy user needs. This data-driven methodology can be characterized as descriptive, paying little attention to process modeling, and, therefore, is at odds with the major research DSS strands.

## Young: A Contemporary Process-Driven Approach

The DSS methodology presented by Young [30] is the most up-to-date of those surveyed, and represents a distillation of major ideas from DSS theoretical and empirical research. Young argues that current technological developments have eliminated a part of the DSS design problem, with current availability of “a ready-made repertoire of generic capabilities.” Most large organizations, he notes, possess this “inventory of DSS resources.”

Young's three-phased methodology incorporates organizational and other analyses via an initial situational analysis. In phase 2, the identification of the DSS functional elements is carried out using Simon's IDC models and representations similar to the ROMC elements advocated by Sprague and Carlson [25]. The idea is to develop enough representations for a base-level prototype. The final phase consists of iterative DSS use, refinement, and assessment. Early value analysis of the type presented by Keen [10] is also advocated as part of this descriptively oriented methodology.

## 2.3. Other DSS Methodologies

Other methodologies included in the survey are those of Gerrity [7], Keen and Scott Morton [13], Martin [14], Sprague and Carlson [25], Meador et al. [15], and Sauter and Schofer [21].

Gerrity's methodology is the earliest (chronologically) of the ten methodologies in this survey, is decision-driven, and emphasizes normative modeling. Keen and Scott Morton's work, on the other hand, represented the mainstream of DSS research for many years, and still affects much thought in the DSS area. Their process-driven methodology placed considerable emphasis on organizational analysis, within a detailed predesign stage. The work of Martin is rooted in system dynamics, employing decision graphs to create the decision model. Martin's methodology is, as shown in Figure 1, decision-driven, and normatively oriented.

Sprague and Carlson have advanced one of the most comprehensive DSS methodologies to date in the ROMC approach, a process-driven approach with a descriptive orientation. They also specify different DSS configuration types and describe levels of DSS tools. The methodology of Meador, Guyote, and Keen, however, is defined by its authors as “a tactical plan for the DSS life-cycle,” and results from the conclusions of a survey of DSS use in nineteen firms. It is decision-driven, comprises a stage model, and is normative in orientation. Finally, the methodology of Sauter and Schofer, like that of Ariav and Ginzberg, is also systemic in outlook, applying a contingency approach to DSS development, and is descriptive in its orientation.

## 3. The Research Model for DSS Methodologies

THE TEN DISCUSSED DSS METHODOLOGIES are not deemed exhaustive, since our aim is primarily to cover the major strands of DSS research and practice. As Young [30] indicates, these strands include methodologies that continue to evolve, representing a moving target. In addition, Moore and Chang [17] contrast the MIS and DSS paradigms and highlight the uniqueness of the DSS design process, as do Keen [10] and, indirectly, Checkland [4] via the “soft” systems approach. The dynamic nature of these methodologies and their specialized focus therefore comprise the context for the survey performed.

Following this comparative viewing of the methodologies, it is possible and desirable to develop a research model of the role, function, and purpose of methodology. Such a DSS research model would require the identification of the entities and processes within the DSS context and the nature of their linkages to methodology. These links will represent an agenda of issues for DSS research and development and provide a schema with which to organize the many methods and models that have been advanced.

In developing this research model, it is instrumental to examine the entities and processes involved in DSS development. Three entities are identified as relevant, namely:

1. The real world—in which the decision maker and the wider environments exist. In these environments, resources, constraints, and organizational/systemic issues may be identified. The link of DSS methodology to the real world is its provision of techniques with which to study and abstract from it. One major element for which study and abstraction are typically carried out is the decision model.

2. The decision model—an abstraction of the decision process(es) carried out by the decision maker. These models may be normatively or descriptively oriented, as illustrated in the preceding methodologies, or in fact a combination of both. The methodology-originating link comprises the formalisms and methods that are used to develop the decision model(s), which then serve as the basis for the third entity, namely the DSS.

3. The DSS—the computer-based system (hardware, software, people, procedures, and data) developed to support the requirements of the selected decision model. This model may be descriptively or normatively oriented. The structure of the DSS is generally viewed as comprising the user interface, model base, and database. Methodology provides the techniques for building, implementing, and maintaining the DSS.

There are, in addition, three relevant processes observed for DSS development methodology:

1. The decision process—existing in the real world and a focus of study of the initial investigation. This may be formalized and analyzed using a variety of methods. Understanding the existing decision process is an important initial objective for the majority of the surveyed methodologies.

2. The change process—regardless of whether change is gradual or abrupt, a major objective is an improvement in effectiveness of the current decision process. This implies an intervening process of change, to move to the desired decision process. The DSS methodology specifies the required change process, often through contingency-based or staged approaches. Other development techniques such as prototyping may also form a part of this process.

3. The decision-supported process—represents the new decision process supported by the DSS. This new decision process is shaped by the form of the decision model used, and is maintained by the DSS methodology. Furthermore, it continues to evolve after DSS implementation, particularly for “institutionalized” DSS.

Some of the elements above are identified in other DSS-related work. For example, Ariav and Ginzberg $[2]$ base their approach on the systemic aspects of the real world. The normative and descriptive orientations mentioned earlier also reflect a continuum for the decision model. With regard to the decision process, representative formalisms may range from Stabell's "decision research" $[26]$ to Martin's decision graphs $[14]$ . Likewise, the change process may be engineered in a directed or nondirected manner $[23]$ . Finally, while Stabell $[26]$ would view the normative model as representing the ideal decision process, others take a less dogmatic and more flexible viewpoint [e.g., 13] The model (see Figure 2) therefore represents an updated approach at integrating both the important elements and the processes in DSS development.

These six elements also help to illustrate both the role and the function of DSS methodology, and to identify further issues for continued empirical and theoretical research via several highlighted “links.” The integrative role of DSS methodology, as illustrated in Figure 2, shows six sets of relevant links in the DSS development process. Link [1] denotes the real-world analysis that initiates DSS development and aids understanding of the decision process (link [2a]). Methodology subsequently provides a means of describing this or an “ideal” decision process through link [2b]. The decision model further evolves from this understanding via links [3a] and [3b]. The decision model and process provide information and insight into the nature of the required change process (links [4a] and [4b]), which is specified by the methodology through link [4c]. The DSS architecture is then built and implemented on the basis of the decision model (link [5a]), the change process (link [5b]), and methodological guidelines (link [5c]). The “target” decision-supported process evolves from the change process (link [6b]), and the implementation and use of the constructed DSS (link [6a]), and is attained and maintained using the DSS methodology via link [6c].

Figure 2. An Integrative View of the Role of DSS Methodology

Each of the methodology-originating links shown in Figure 2 represents a group of methods that are provided in varying degrees by DSS methodologies. Link [1], for example, covers methods of systems investigation used to elicit information about the decision process and environments. These comprise the initial part of requirements analysis, including primary methods such as interviewing, process tracing, etc. Link [2], on the other hand, includes methods frequently used to identify and overlay key decisions across key continuums of structurability and decision focus.

Link [3] represents a further component of DSS requirements analysis, utilizing various formalisms for the specification of the decision model. Examples of link [3] formalisms are the ROMC elements, which essentially are the system. Link [4c] incorporates the iterative and adaptive strategies DSS design and prototyping advocated by Hurst et al. [9] and Keen and Gambino [12], among others. Links [5b] and [5c] describe methods for DSS development, with respect to the hardware, software, and people resources that are deployed to build the DSS. Finally, link [6c] refers to those activities that are necessary to ensure DSS integration into its environment, and the operational support required to ensure successful DSS use and evolution.

## 4. Reduction of Unstructuredness using DSS Methodologies

THE DIMENSION OF UNSTRUCTUREDNESS, as described by Simon [24], has been a key concept underlying the development of DSS theory. Structure, or a lack of it, may result from existing, or propagated, uncertainty in any of the decision-making stages (i.e., intelligence, design, or choice). Semistructured and unstructured decisions represent the natural DSS constituency, in which computer-based systems are used to supplement human decision-making capabilities. Ariav and Ginzberg's [2] identification of structurability has since proven to be a valuable refinement to the original concept, recognizing the context-dependence of structure.

Each of the methodology-originating links of the DSS research model in Figure 2 can therefore be seen as a set of methods for handling uncertainty encountered in ill-structured problem solving, and of coping with and/or reducing this uncertainty. Davis [5] has described a classification framework for various groups of methods based on the degree to which they reduce uncertainty and thereby enhance requirements determination. The contingency framework he proposes covers a wide spectrum from situations possessing low to high uncertainty. In the latter type, he calls for experimental/prototyping methods to handle this uncertainty in requirements determination.

This latter situation type is the focus of this paper. It is possible to extend contingency theory further into this (the DSS) area to produce finer distinctions concerning the applicability of DSS requirements analysis methods. This may be done by carefully examining the uncertainty associated with each methodology-originating link, paying particular attention to those used for requirements analysis, as follows:

\- Link [1] represents the real-world analysis described earlier, and potentially possesses several factors contributing to uncertainty in the DSS context. These include the ability of users to describe requirements accurately, particularly when the decision is novel or unusually complex. Furthermore, in ill-structured decision-making environments, greater dynamism is often found in constantly changing requirements and processes.

\- Link [2b] illustrates methods for describing the decision process, and may be subject to similar problems. The decision process may not be clearly understood by the decision makers, or it may not be clearly explained, even when it is understood. It may also be dynamic, or currently inadequate. Furthermore, the analyst may lack suitable tools or skills with which to analyze the decision process. Finally, the decision process may be extremely complex, requiring a greater investment of effort to describe and improve.

\- The third link, link [3b]—the formalisms used to create the decision model—may likewise be beset with difficulties. Poor-quality decision models may result from the analyst's lack of skill, or inferior modeling formalisms. As is also often the case, there may be no available precedents to use as an example. In addition, complexity may confound simplistic modeling assumptions and demand greater investments of time and effort. An accurate descriptive decision model may further serve to entrench a poor-quality decision process [see 26], making the selection of required normative models more demanding.

\- Link [4c] subsumes methods of specifying or defining the required change process, and suffers primarily from propagated uncertainties from previous links. The change process implicitly recognizes a target DSS and decision process, specified using the three earlier sets of methods. These uncertainties, together with uncertainty in matching the most suitable change methods to the problem situation, may limit the effectiveness of designed systems.

## 4.1. A Contingency Model for DSS Methodology Selection

The extension of the contingency theory into the area of ill-structured decision making seeks to show how each of several methodologies handles and reduces lack of structure. The framework shows, for each of the methodology-originating links, how the presence or absence and comprehensiveness of the various formalisms, structures, and techniques provided by the methodologies indicates their effectiveness. The benefit of such a framework and theory is the enabling of a more informed selection and usage of the methodologies, based on problem context and their individual strengths.

Table 1 depicts the assessment of the capabilities of seven methodologies, covering the four paradigms, for each of the links indicated. The assessments or ratings for each methodology have been derived from a careful study of their descriptions in the literature.

In the area of real-world analysis, the methodologies of Keen and Scott Morton $[13]$ , and of Ariav and Ginzberg $[2]$ appear the most thorough via their organizational and systemic analyses, with the other approaches exhibiting progressively less emphasis in this area. Likewise, the proposals of Stabell $[26]$ and Martin $[14]$ effectively address the task of abstracting the decision process, with other approaches providing merely adequate, to inadequate, facilities for this methodological link.

Martin's decision graphs and Sprague and Carlson's [25] ROMC formalisms represent the most developed methods for creating the decision model, while all the methodologies provide some support for defining the change process. In the latter methodological link, the three process-driven methodologies (i.e., Sprague and Carlson; Keen and Scott Morton; and Young), and Ariav and Ginzberg [2] provide the fullest proposals.

In DSS development, these three process-driven methodologies possess adequate guidelines for both model base and database design and construction, while Menkus [16] naturally handles DSS database development the most extensively. Clearly, the most comprehensive methodologies overall are the process-driven methodologies. However, the strengths of other methodologies include that of Menkus, in database development; Ariav and Ginzberg, in real-world (systemic) analysis; Stabell and Martin, in abstracting the decision process; and Martin, again, in the creation of the decision model (using decision graphs). The DSS developer may also be urged to avoid certain methodologies when weaknesses or omissions exist in certain areas. Examples of these include Stabell's and Martin's omission of proposals for DSS design and maintenance; and Menkus's inadequate coverage of DSS requirements analysis. Used together with the analyst's evaluation of uncertainty of different developmental aspects, a DSS methodology may then be selected that is best suited to the problem at hand.

## 4.2. Application of the Contingency Approach to DSS Methodology Selection

Use of the contingency approach is now demonstrated by means of two examples. Each scenario is accompanied by a table (Tables 2 and 3) in which one column describes key problem characteristics and three further columns provide the required analysis for DSS methodology selection. The second column identifies the inferred methodological link(s), while the third indicates the uncertainty associated with the considered methodological stage. The final column then recommends the extent of DSS methodological features required (via the “links”) to handle the highlighted uncertainty in this stage. The “key” problem characteristics are identified by matching problem aspects to the methodology-emanating links, followed by an assessment of the uncertainty implied in the link.

1. A food manufacturing company with limited IS investment and experience, primarily in transaction-based systems, has traditionally relied on money-back coupons for promoting its products. This means that when customers purchase a manufacturer's product, they simply return the product label to the manufacturer, who, in turn, refunds part (25 to 50 cents) of the purchase price to the customer. Management would like to employ computer-based models to evaluate the profitability of such a promotional strategy—that is, should it continue this promotional strategy, and, if it should, for what product, which region, and how much rebate, etc. Design a DSS to support the evaluation of the promotional strategy.

In the outlined scenario, there is little uncertainty associated with situation analysis (link [1]), as a result of the stable data sources typically associated with the consumer marketing area. In addition, the general availability of consumer marketing models, combined with a measure of IS experience, implies a low-moderate uncertainty in abstracting the decision process (link [2b]). Regarding the creation of the decision model (link [3b]), methodological capabilities are required to supplement the limited experience in the organization, and to cope with the moderately complex modeling required. In addition, limited expertise with DSS development signifies considerable uncertainty in links defining the change process and DSS building ([4c] and [5c]), necessitating stronger methodological features for these links. The DSS operational phase (link [6c]) does not require much support due to the apparent one-off or ad-hoc nature of this DSS. Using Table 1, this appears to call for the DSS methodologies of Sprague and Carlson, or Young, which match the expressed requirements most closely.

2. Following poor sales performance by a manufacturer of special-purpose microcomputers, the MIS department has been asked by the company president to develop a DSS to improve the interface between the Production and Marketing departments of the firm. ## 5. Further Issues from the Research Model

FURTHER ISSUES FOR CONTINUING RESEARCH are identifiable from the proposed DSS research model and associated contingency framework for aiding methodology selection. First, it may be worthwhile to consider whether the different methodologies might be combined to produce “composite” DSS methodologies that are more effective than their constituents, and the form of such interactions. Further empirical study might also survey real-world DSS implemented using the described methodologies, and assess their effectiveness using various performance measures. These studies may serve both to refine the contingency framework and to indicate further enhancements to the existing methodologies.

Work by Checkland [4] also indicates the importance of user perspectives (or "weltanschaaung") in problem definition. This, too, may be further explored, along with Mumford and Weir's [18] sociotechnical model of information systems, specifically in the DSS context.

Link $[5]$ represents DSS developmental aspects, with regard to required hardware, software, and people resources. As the technology continues to advance at a rapid pace, it is necessary to understand its effects on DSS building and the shape of the methodology. Young $[30]$ notes that these advances initially made DSS feasible, and continue to make certain methodological options available, or, in other cases, redundant. The proliferation of such tools as fourth-generation languages, CASE and graphics packages, LANs, microcomputers, prototyping $[e.g., 12]$ , and related techniques all call for updated research involving the selection and application of DSS tools. For example, new CASE tools (e.g., Excelerator), which automate to a high degree activities ranging from organizational analyses to code generation, provide increased opportunities for faster and more comprehensive DSS prototyping, while LANs and microcomputer technology will affect the form of Group Decision Support Systems (GDSS). Further efforts should seek to determine their potential contribution to the form of the DSS development effort and their impact on DSS acceptance and usage.

Finally, links [6a], [6b], and [6c] connect to the new decision process supported by the DSS. Swanson's view [27] of implementation and maintenance as distinct and important processes in the systems life cycle conveys their importance to the DSS developer, and is supported by many of the methodologies surveyed [e.g., 13]. Further research should have the objective of assessing the types and amounts of resources that are required to implement, maintain, and integrate DSS effectively into a variety of organizational contexts.

## 6. Conclusions

THIS PAPER HAS SURVEYED AND EXAMINED ten DSS development methodologies with regard to their paradigm, structure, orientation, and methods of requirements analysis. The classification scheme for the proposed methodologies forms a useful organizing framework for developers and researchers seeking to evaluate the suitability of specific methodologies to different problems.

The survey was used as the basis of proposals for a DSS research model, illustrating the interrelationships between DSS methodology and other elements of the decision-making context. This model assists in the identification of important issues for DSS theoretical and empirical research, by the analysis of the interrelationships or “links.”

In addition, a contingency framework was developed for evaluating the capabilities provided by DSS methodologies in the DSS development process, with regard to their handling of encountered unstructuredness. This framework is thought to offer developers assistance in matching existing DSS methodologies to the encountered problem context.

In summary, the paper has aimed at the following:

1. Improving current understanding of existing DSS methodologies and the relevant assessment criteria for their classification.

2. Indicating, by means of a proposed research model, the capabilities required of DSS methodologies (the methodology-emanating “links”) in the DSS development cycle.

3. Analyzing comparatively the capabilities provided by DSS methodologies in each DSS development stage, with a focus on requirements analysis.

4. Proposing a contingency framework and approach (with case illustrations) to selecting a DSS methodology in diverse problem contexts.

Finally, three issues were highlighted for further research, namely: extensions to the proposed contingency framework; a study of the interaction and effects of technology on DSS development; and further work on DSS implementation and maintenance.

As managers continue to seek new ways of increasing decision-making effectiveness, the effective application of DSS methodologies will become more crucial in leveraging DSS technologies for competitive advantage. This paper has employed a DSS research model and contingency theory for DSS methodology selection to enable DSS developers to achieve this objective.
