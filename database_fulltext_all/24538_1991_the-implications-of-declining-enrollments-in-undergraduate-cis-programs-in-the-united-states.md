# The Implications of Declining Enrollments in Undergraduate CIS Programs in the United States

EDWARD G. CALE, JR., CHARLES H. MAWHINNEY, and DAVID R. CALLAGHAN

EDWARD G. CALE, JR., is Associate Professor of Information Systems, and the Faculty Director of the Center for Information Management Studies at Babson College. He holds a DBA in computer-based systems from the Harvard Business School. His research interests include management of the computing resource, diffusion of technological innovation, end-user computing, and computer personnel issues.

CHARLES H. MAWHINNEY is Assistant Professor of Computer Information Systems at Bentley College. He holds a Ph.D. in Business Administration from the Joseph M. Katz School of Business at the University of Pittsburgh. His research interests include end-user computing management, decision support systems, executive information systems, and computer personnel research.

DAVID R. CALLAGHAN is Associate Professor of Computer Information Systems and Director of Academic Computer User Services at Bentley College. He holds a Ph.D. in accounting information systems from the University of Massachusetts. His research interests include public domain microcomputing security software, end-user training methodologies, and the decline in information systems college enrollments.

ABSTRACT: A shortage of qualified entry-level computer information systems (CIS) personnel is beginning to occur as a result of the recent decline in student interest in computer-related careers. This paper discusses the impending shortage, its implications, and actions that can be taken to reverse this development.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: MIS education, MIS employment projections, MIS careers, computer personnel.

DURING THE PAST DECADE, FEWER AND FEWER COLLEGE STUDENTS have been electing to major in the fields of science, technology, and mathematics in higher education in the United States. Many explanations are offered for this trend. First, there is a substantial shortage of qualified science and technology faculty at the primary, secondary, and collegiate levels [9]. Also, the proportion of women and minorities attending college, who have historically been underrepresented in science and technology, is increasing [14]. Finally, with its leveraged buyouts and stock market surge, the decade of the 1980s saw an increasingly large proportion of students pursue degrees in business. In a world where technological leadership seems inextricably linked with economic leadership, the declining interest in technical fields could have a severe and negative impact in financial, social, and political spheres [9].

This article concentrates on one specific technological area, computer information systems (CIS), which we broadly define to include the spectrum of disciplines from computer science to management information systems. First, we compare forecasts through the end of the century for CIS graduates from four-year colleges with predictions for labor market demand over the same period. Because of the great disparity between predicted supply and demand, we then examine the impact these projected shortfalls will have on industry, higher education, and the CIS profession. Finally, we discuss some of the implications to the profession of this decline in student interest, and suggest a range of actions for addressing the problem.

## Forecast for Number of Graduates

ANNUAL SURVEYS OF FRESHMAN STUDENTS conducted by UCLA [1, 2, 3, 4] indicate that student interest in computer careers grew in the late 1970s and early 1980s, peaked in 1982 at 8.8 percent of respondents, and has subsequently declined below the 1977 levels to 2.6 percent. Figure 1 is a graph of the UCLA data. While this trend can be attributed to many of the same global causes of the decline in interest in science and technology, other specific reasons include student perceptions that the field is appropriate only for the most intellectually gifted, that the career path is too restrictive and involves working in isolation from other people, and a general lack of awareness of the financial rewards of the profession [21, 22], as well as a backlash to the exaggerated expectations for computer science careers in the early 1980s [11].

Based on the UCLA data, we have prepared a model to project the number of CIS majors who will graduate from four-year colleges from 1987 through the year 2000. $^{1}$ Our model for predicting the number of college CIS graduates is:

$$
C _ {t} = H _ {t - 4} \times F _ {t - 4} \times I _ {t - 4} \times R _ {t}
$$

where $C_{t} = CIS$ graduates in year t; $H_{t-4} = high$ school graduates in year t - 4; $F_{t-4} = ratio$ of first-time college freshmen to high school graduates in year t - 4; $I_{t-4} = interest$ level in CIS programs in year t - 4; and $R_{t} = ratio$ of CIS graduates in year t to freshmen enrolled as CIS majors in year t - 4.

Figure 2 depicts the resulting number of CIS graduates ( $C_{t}$ ) through the year 2000. It shows both the actual number of CIS graduates from four-year colleges for the years 1981–86, as measured by the Department of Education [10], and our projections for the “most likely” level of graduates through the year 2000 [6]. Our projections for the number of graduates between now and 1994 should be quite accurate because they reflect the interests of students already surveyed by Astin [1, 2, 3, 4] and currently in the college pipeline.

THE DEMAND FOR PROGRAMMERS AND ANALYSTS has grown steadily during the last fifteen years. Figure 3 shows the total number of programmers and analysts employed during the period 1972–86 as reported by Hodges [13], as well as the “low,” “moderate,” and “high” projections of Silvestri and Lukasiewicz (Bureau of Labor Statistics) [29] for the years 1987–2000. These projections are consistent with other BLS forecasts that growth will continue through the year 2000 [5].

The continued growth shown in the BLS estimates is a matter of some controversy. It has been argued that the advent of end-user computing and programming productivity tools will diminish the need for software development personnel. The counterargument holds that this overlooks the currently installed base of systems, mostly written in COBOL, which will not go away. Further, information systems organizations are no different from other groups, and are likely to resist changing to new tools despite their possible value. In spite of the controversial nature of BLS data, we have chosen to use them due to the lack of alternative statistical projections by those who question the BLS figures.

More important than the total number of positions is the number of open positions that must be filled each year. A simple estimate of the number of new positions can be made through pairwise subtraction of the data shown in Figure 3. To estimate total available positions, the replacement of employees who leave the profession must also be included. The Bureau of Labor Statistics [5] estimates the annual separation rates to be 8.9 percent for programmers and 9.1 percent for analysts. Given the relative closeness of these two separation rates, we chose to use a separation rate of 9.0 percent applied to the total of analysts and programmers. ## Implications

WE BELIEVE THAT THE SHORTAGE OF ENTRY-LEVEL, college-trained CIS majors in the United States will have a profound impact. To help understand the implications of this shortage, we examine the potential impact on various interested parties: (1) industrial users of information systems; (2) the software industry; (3) the CIS profession; and (4) higher education.

## Industry: User Organizations

The prime market for entry-level, college-trained CIS majors consists of midsized and large business firms (Fortune 1000 and others), where the MIS department acquires and operates transaction processing and management information systems to support the operation of the organization. As such, these user organizations will be the first to feel the squeeze caused by the shortage. Anticipated impacts will be as follows.

A shortage of application and maintenance programmers and system analysts will cause the already painful application and maintenance backlog to grow even larger. In reaction to the growing backlog, companies will be forced to develop resource allocation methods that ensure that scarce CIS human resources are being used in the most appropriate fashion. To ensure that efforts are being focused on the potentially most beneficial projects from an organizationwide perspective, companywide steering committees, long-range information services plans linked to the long-range corporate plans, and more formal attempts to justify and rank potential applications will be essential $[15]$ . In addition, organizations will have to become increasingly aware of available software packages, and the “buy versus make” decision will become more strongly biased toward the purchase option. In situations where organizations might have previously developed custom applications, off-the-shelf systems that are “good enough” or that can be semicustomized will be favored.

Also in reaction to the growing backlog, end-user computing (EUC) will continue to grow. Frustrated by long delays in obtaining services, user departments will increasingly take matters into their own hands. Taking advantage of increasingly user-friendly software packages (spreadsheets, database management systems, communication/networking systems, fourth-generation languages [4GLs], and application generators), users will continue to force the decentralization of information services. The offloading of small applications from mainframe to micro-based systems and the downloading of segments of mainframe databases will continue to grow.

The increase in EUC will, in turn, exacerbate an already frustrating problem of controlling information services. Major problems can be expected in areas such as application quality assurance, database data integrity and consistency, security, and disaster recovery planning. Without the coordination and control procedures mentioned above, companies will increasingly find themselves facing “islands of technology” that are uncoordinated, incompatible, unmaintainable, and inflexible.

Clearly, within the remaining formal information services department, once priorities for application acquisition are set, the drive will be for increased productivity. This drive will certainly include a preference for software packages where possible, and the increased use of 4GLs, computer-aided software engineering (CASE) tools, and application generators where satisfactory packages cannot be identified. Except in mature, highly stable environments with little application development, application maintenance and system documentation will experience pressures for cutbacks that, if given in to, will lead to a less relevant [27] and less maintainable portfolio of applications [25].

Finally, faced with a shrinking supply of entry-level CIS personnel, companies will be required to take aggressive action to attract and retain a sufficient quantity and quality of CIS professionals. Salary levels will clearly rise, and companies will be increasingly forced to develop their own CIS personnel, rather than depend on the educational institutions to meet their demands. The development of attractive career paths in information systems that are meaningful, upwardly mobile, and visible will be essential if bright, able employees who lack formal information systems education are to be interested in moving into information systems positions.

## Industry: Software Development Firms

As users of information services, the software industry will feel all of the above painful effects caused by the shortage of entry-level CIS personnel. As producers of products supporting information services, however, the software industry will be faced with some particularly acute challenges as well as several attractive opportunities.

As the general business community increasingly turns away from custom software application development and toward packages, software firms may well find a strong growth in demand for their products. However, faced with a shortage of personnel, and therefore a shortage of production capacity, software suppliers will become increasingly risk-averse in their development of new products. As personnel constraints limit new product development, the successful software developers will be those who, despite a small product line, can consistently produce high-demand products. To choose the right applications, successful software companies will have to maintain more than just technical expertise; they must have a clear understanding of the market and their customers. Their insight must include a clear business savvy about what is needed, as well as the expertise to respond to that need. Thus, extensive customer contacts, marketing intelligence, and strategic planning will rank with technical genius as hallmarks of successful software companies in the future.

Once they have properly set their priorities, software companies will find programmer and analyst productivity extremely important. We can expect the increased use of reusable code, object-oriented languages, CASE, 4GLs, and application generators to be vital for success in the software industry. With the continuing historical decline in the cost of computer hardware and the increase in costs of personnel-related activities, the sacrifice of machine efficiency to support human efficiency should represent an acceptable trade-off.

## The CIS Career

As industry in general and the software industry in particular react to the shortage of entry-level CIS professionals, a number of changes can be anticipated in efforts to attract the needed personnel. The classic reaction to a labor shortage is a rapid increase in salary levels. In addition, flexibility, such as part-time work, job sharing, and telecommuting, as well as a variety of different acceptable career paths, will become essential.

The nature of a professional career in CIS will also change. The movement away from centralized and tightly controlled information services toward more decentralized processing, and the greater reliance on EUC and software packages, will change the day-to-day activities of many CIS professionals. Already widely known as a field involving intense time and performance pressure, the projected increase in application and maintenance backlog and the decrease in control implicit in EUC can only increase the pressures and frustrations felt by CIS professionals. Not only will the demand for their services increase, but the number of individuals and departments vying for their attention will also grow. As EUC increases, professionals will find themselves working with users who have an increasingly higher level of technical experience, but even higher expectations—often failing to appreciate the difference in complexity between a simple spreadsheet versus a production system. Thus, the CIS professional will find him or herself with more work to do, and with less appreciative users for whom the work is done.

As information service work moves toward EUC, the role of the information systems professional will increasingly take on consulting, as opposed to technical, aspects. Rather than designing systems themselves, professionals will more frequently find themselves advising end-users on alternatives, helping them out of problems, and debugging the work of others. These demands will increase the requirement for solid communication and management skills, as well as the expected technical skills. The successful EUC consultant will have to possess an abundance of business understanding and political savvy $[16, 17]$ . While it has been understood for some time that there are strong advantages in combining business with technical skills $[18]$ , the trend toward a consultancy role for the information systems professional will increase the importance of combining these perspectives. This trend may well lead more information systems professionals to seek formal business training via community colleges, part-time MBA programs, or other vehicles.

## Higher Education

During the early and mid-1980s, undergraduate interest in IS-related courses and majors mushroomed. To keep up with the demand, colleges and universities expanded their information systems offerings by adding courses and majors, and, consequently, hired additional information systems faculty to support this expansion. Now, after several years of declining enrollment, college and university CIS programs face some severe challenges. The subsequent decline in student interest is approaching the point at which many institutions will be forced to reduce course offerings below the level required to support a full-fledged major. At that point the institution will no longer be able to prepare CIS professionals adequately, or to serve the needs of nonmajors who desire more information systems background.

A decline in the number of CIS classes taught will reduce the need for information systems faculty. A reduction in CIS faculty staffing will have several negative effects on higher education programs. The information systems profession is so young, and the rate of technology development is so rapid, that it seems vital that academic institutions be continually infused with the new blood of recent doctoral graduates. Should the demand for CIS staff significantly reduce the hiring of junior faculty, we would expect a concomitant decline in the ability of institutions to deliver cutting-edge programs. In addition, a decrease in faculty staffing could well diminish the capacity of academic institutions to respond to future increases in student interest.

## Reducing the Impact on Industry and Academia

DURING THE NEXT DECADE, AN IRONIC PROBLEM will exist: industry will face a shortage of college-qualified CIS graduates, and academia will face a lack of demand for their capacity to educate CIS professionals. This paradox presents an opportunity to address the problems of both groups simultaneously. Three potential approaches are: (1) to employ excess CIS faculty as information systems professionals in industry; (2) to employ excess CIS faculty as educators in industry, helping to cross-train employees into the information systems field; and (3) to leave excess CIS faculty in academia, and form a cooperative program that would utilize faculty and campus facilities to aid industry in the development and delivery of special programs to retrain existing employees into the CIS profession.

The first alternative, employing CIS faculty as information systems professionals in industry, has little merit. First and foremost, the total number of CIS faculty in the United States is quite small: the MISRC/McGraw-Hill 1989 Directory of Management Information Systems Faculty lists only 1,850 faculty names for the United States and Canada [23], while in 1986, the total number of doctoral degrees awarded in the “Computer and Information Sciences” area was only 374 [24]. Compared to projected annual hiring shortfalls in the hundreds of thousands (see Table 1), the impact of transferring several hundred CIS faculty members into industrial positions would be minimal. A second significant shortcoming of this approach, to be detailed below, is that it is hard to imagine that many CIS faculty would look forward to such a move, or would be particularly talented at filling the industrial positions.

The second alternative, employing excess CIS faculty in industry as educators, has some advantage over the first approach, but still has several shortcomings. A significant advantage of this approach is that, despite the small number of CIS faculty who might participate in such a program, their effect could be relatively large. An individual faculty member routinely teaches dozens, if not hundreds, of students per year. In industry, without the overhead of research, publication, and committee work, a person could annually teach numbers reaching into the middle hundreds. A second advantage of this approach is that faculty would be employed in positions closer to those for which they were prepared, as educators, not as CIS line professionals.

Therein, however, lies the major flaw of the second alternative. As products of university doctoral programs, most of their graduate study consisted of preparing to become academic researchers. These are individuals who spent many extra years in education, often at great expense, and who sacrificed the opportunity for significantly higher industrial salaries for the primary purpose of pursuing an academic career. Whether it was primarily to pursue research, or to work in an institution dedicated to helping students achieve an education, or to gain the relative independence and flexibility of academic life, faculty members have gone to great expense and trouble to pursue an academic career. Given their preparation and motivation, it seems unlikely that many faculty members would look forward to a long-term career as a corporate trainer, or that they would be particularly successful in that role.

One possible means for improving the likelihood of success for this second alternative would be to reorient current CIS doctoral programs significantly so that they produced graduates capable of and interested in industrial education. Those teaching in doctoral programs, many with tenure, would have to be convinced of the need and provided with resources to retool both their programs and, in some cases, themselves. In addition, given the typical four-year pipeline of a doctoral program, a student body with significantly different interests would have to be recruited and then educated toward the industrial environment. Therefore, such an approach would take significant time to implement and would require substantial reorientation of current faculty and programs.

The third alternative, leaving the faculty members in academia but using college and university facilities and personnel to help industry cross-train and prepare meaningful career tracks, appears to be the best. First, as general college enrollments fall during the early 1990s, as expected [12], academia will have the excess physical capacity to handle industrial clients. Second, by leaving the faculty members within the collegiate community and fashioning a hybrid career involving some industrial education and some traditional academic work, the role of faculty will be more consistent with their preparation and expectations. Third, by not decimating the capacity of academic CIS programs, we leave in place the capability to respond to increasing enrollments should the current decline in student interest be reversed.

There appear to be several other advantages to an industrial/academic cooperative program. As previously mentioned, there are advantages in educating CIS professionals in management as well as technical issues. In addition, as also mentioned, for industry to interest its employees in cross-training into CIS, more flexible, attractive career paths will be required. By forming a cooperative program, industry could take advantage of both management and organizational development faculty, as well as CIS faculty, to craft a successful program. A final advantage to such a cooperative program is that it would better familiarize the academic community with the real needs of the commercial world, which has long maintained that academia is out of touch.

There is evidence that academia could effectively participate in such a cooperative program. During the past decade, continuing and executive education programs have already grown significantly, as academia has gained the skills and capacity to educate older students. However, many of these programs are noncredit, nondegree in nature, and are not normally considered to be a part of the faculty workload. Academia may need to rethink its position on this issue.

## Reversing the Decline in Enrollments

IN THE LONG TERM IT IS REASONABLE to assume that information systems enrollments will begin to grow again as a consequence of the rapidly rising starting salaries resulting from the shortage. However, many actions can be taken now that could hasten that recovery. The four independent variables described in the model at the beginning of this paper provide a good framework for discussing such actions.

## Number of High School Graduates

All the persons who will graduate from high school in the next decade are already in the educational pipeline. Thus, projecting the number of high school graduates is relatively straightforward. One testimony to this is that the current decline in the number of high school graduates has been anticipated for several years. The only variable in this part of the projection equation that can be influenced is the dropout rate. Industry in general, and the information systems profession in particular, can play a role in reducing this rate, as well as in encouraging high school graduates to go on to college, as will be described toward the end of the following section.

## Ratio of College Freshmen to High School Graduates

Faced with a declining number of high school graduates over the past decade, institutions of higher learning have been under pressure to fill empty classrooms. This pressure can be expected to grow as the number of high school graduates continues to decline until the “first harmonic” of the post–World War II “baby boom” begins to take effect in 1995. Colleges have already shown creativity in filling seats, as demonstrated by the growth in the ratio of high school graduates going on to college during the period 1973–85 [6]. Given the continuing movement away from an industrial economy and toward an information-based economy, it can be argued that higher education will become increasingly important in the future, and that making such education available to a wider proportion of high school graduates will benefit both them and society as a whole. However, to attract a broader base of entering students, colleges will have to go beyond the traditional “college track” high school graduates. This will mean that more minority, women, and foreign students, as well as students currently considered to be academically marginal, will need to be attracted, supported, and integrated into American institutions of higher learning $[14]$ . To do so successfully will require that colleges broaden recruiting practices and create or expand programs for tutoring, mentoring, English as a second language (ESL), and remedial skills. In addition, programs will have to be developed to inform high school students of the possibilities and what they need to do to take advantage of them. Industry in general, and the information systems profession in particular, can play an important role by participating in outreach programs with high schools, by developing internship and summer jobs programs that will help interest students and provide financial assistance, and by working with school systems to foster appropriate cooperative programs.

## Interest Level in CIS Programs

As shown in Figure 1, student interest in CIS programs abruptly rose and fell during the period 1977–89. The reversal of the current trend will require a strong, proactive approach to change substantially flawed and distinctly negative student perceptions of computer careers [20, 21]. We believe that a major outreach program, with strong industry as well as academic participation, is necessary to change perceptions of computing careers, particularly before students enter college. The aim of such a program would be to expose students to the realities of the field and to correct misconceptions. It is important that the public be made aware that the field is comprised of much more than programming. In addition to communicating these career path opportunities, the public should be informed about the relatively high salaries, future growth, and relative importance of the profession.

There are many mechanisms for accomplishing this. One way is to target high school guidance counselors, as illustrated by the recent program initiated by the Boston Chapter of the Society of Information Management $[30]$ . Another mechanism is for academics and practitioners to write articles informing students and parents of the opportunities in the CIS profession. To be effective, these articles need to appear in the popular press and in journals read by high school counselors and teachers. Another mechanism is to encourage local school “career nights” where representatives from industry would talk with students, parents, and counselors. Colleges can also bring high school students to their campuses for college and career awareness programs.

Once interested in the field, high school students must be given an opportunity to take introductory courses in the area. Working together, the ACM and the IEEE have developed models for high school courses and teacher certification programs $[7, 26]$ . These model programs are narrowly oriented toward computer science, however, and should be expanded to include other aspects of IS. Clearly, one major problem in making these courses available is the lack of qualified high school faculty $[9]$ . This is an area where both the information systems industry and college faculty might contribute, both for teacher development and for course delivery.

## Ratio of CIS Graduates to Freshmen Enrolled as CIS Majors

The last part of the model is the ratio of students who graduate with a bachelor's degree in CIS to those who display an interest in the career as freshmen. This ratio can be increased in two ways: (1) by recruiting students into the major after they have arrived on campus; and (2) by increasing the retention rate for those who are in the major.

## Recruiting More Majors

Even after they have entered college, much more can be done to attract students to the information systems field. Many popular college programs, such as business administration, require students to take an introductory computing or information systems course. This course, in essence, is the last great opportunity to attract students to the profession. Colleges should consider placing their best information systems faculty in this course, and should also consider making curriculum changes that emphasize the appeal and potential of the profession. The use of information systems professionals and managers as guest speakers, who could discuss their experiences of information systems careers, should be an integral part of such a course. Such conversion attempts must be carefully orchestrated so as not to violate the objectivity of the teaching/counseling role or jeopardize relations with other disciplines.

## Increasing the Retention Rate

We believe that increasing the retention rate of students in CIS programs will require a change in faculty attitudes as well as in CIS curricula.

As the enrollments in CIS programs rose in the early 1980s, the ratio of those who graduated relative to those who entered as freshmen dropped rapidly from a peak of 0.25 in 1983 to a low of 0.19 in 1986 [6]. Many CIS programs became oversubscribed in the early 1980s, and faculty often adopted a harsh “weed out” approach toward interested students, with “killer” courses in the freshman and sophomore years. As a result of the decline in student interest, faculty will need to reevaluate their curriculum and approach toward students—not only to keep the students already enrolled in the major, but also to recruit new entries from other disciplines. CIS faculty may well have to change from this “weed them out mode” to a “marketing mode” to rebuild their enrollments.

Regarding CIS curricula, the traditional gateway into the profession has always been through computer programming. This has generally been true in both academia and industry. Undergraduate programs typically require programming as the first course in the major, and the first job the typical graduate takes is as a programmer or programmer/analyst. Consequently, the public at large often views the profession as being synonymous with computer programming—a view that we believe is detrimental to the profession. This is further supported by a recent study indicating that entering college freshmen business majors “effectively expressed an aversion to the workstyle of a computer programmer” [21]. It has also been well established that, compared with other business professions, CIS tends to attract introverts with strong “growth needs” which many attribute to the aforementioned “programming gateway” [8, 19, 20].

To address this problem, we propose two courses of action: “cosmetic changes” to the “programming gateway,” and different career entry paths for those with little interest in programming. The cosmetic modifications consist of changing the programming course title to something less threatening, such as “introduction to systems development,” and changing the entry-level job title to something like “junior systems analyst.” $^{2}$ On the issue of curriculum modification, while we agree that persons pursuing careers in software development need a rigorous technical foundation, we argue that systems analysts require less technical depth, and more emphasis on business and communication skills. Curricula and job entry positions must be developed that emphasize these aspects, and thereby attract and retain a more nontraditional student to the CIS profession.

## Conclusion

THE CONTINUING DEMAND FOR ENTRY-LEVEL information systems personnel and declining interest in the field by college freshmen will clearly result in serious shortages of entry-level personnel for the next three to four years. Given the interest of students currently in colleges and universities, we see little chance of a dramatic turnaround.

If and when a rebound occurs will depend on how the profession responds to the factors identified in our model of CIS graduates. The variable in the model most easily influenced is the interest in the career as displayed by entering freshmen. It is crucial that concerned persons and organizations begin to provide information about information systems careers to junior high and high school students, parents, and counselors. Without a sufficient supply of entry-level personnel, the information systems profession can only expect greater pressures on a staff that is already overloaded.
