# The Impact of Office Information Systems on Potential Power and Influence

SOONCHUL LEE

SOONCHUL LEE is an Assistant Professor of MIS at the Hong IK University, Seoul, Korea, and Assistant Professor of MIS at Boston University. He received a M.S. in Industrial Engineering from Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology, and a Ph.D. in MIS from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. His major research focus is the impact of information technology on organizational control. His current research interests also include strategic information systems and computer-aided approaches to information systems design.

ABSTRACT: This paper explores a model of the impact of office information systems (OIS) on potential personal power and influence in an organization. The focus is on how OIS can increase the potential power and influence of an individual. I have drawn from power base theory to identify five bases of power that would, hypothetically, provide the potential for an individual's influence in an organization: (1) resource provision; (2) irreplaceability; (3) authority; (4) network centrality; and (5) expertise. To explore this conceptual model, data were collected from 136 users of well-established office information systems. This pilot study provides general support for our model that the impact of OIS on potential personal influence can be explained through its effects on the five power bases. In particular, the data indicate that network centrality is the most significant contributor to potential personal influence for administrative personnel, while resource provision is the most significant factor for technical personnel.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: office information systems, organizational power.

## 1. Introduction

WHILE THE EMERGENCE OF OFFICE INFORMATION SYSTEMS (OIS) has perhaps been a major factor affecting office workers during the last decade [22, 48], there have been almost no studies on the effects of OIS on power or personal influence of office workers. The few exceptions are studies of the power of system developers over their users [12, 24, 25, 26, 36, 39] that have investigated the power difference between management information system (MIS) departments and user departments: their focus was on intergroup power relations. No studies have attempted to link the usage of office information systems to the power of office workers within a group.

The terms “influence” and “power” are used in this paper to denote the same construct because, as Tannenbaum [41] has noted, they have often been used interchangeably in the literature. The notion that power and influence are largely based on some set of power resources, such as control over organizational resources, has received theoretical attention and empirical support [9]. There is a body of literature on the subject of power that is potential, not enacted [8, 35, 46]. The present study focuses on the relationships between an individual's potential influence within an organization and power bases. Our study's scope is limited to middle- and bottom-level management and does not include top management.

Given that little is known about the relationship between OIS and individual influence, this study is an initial investigation whose purpose is to determine whether there is a pattern of correlation between potential personal power and the effect of OIS usage on the five power bases selected. Since there is no existing literature directly addressing the topic of this study at the individual level, the five power bases used in the study were derived from the literature on intergroup power relations, from interviews, and from a reasoned assessment of what seemed to be the most sensitive independent variables.

In the next section of this paper, relevant literature is discussed and a model is presented of how OIS affects the potential influence of individuals in an organization. Five correlations are hypothesized between potential individual power and the effect of OIS on bases of power. Section 3 describes the research design used and Section 4 presents data analyses and results. The hypotheses were tested through (1) correlation analyses based on questionnaire data, and (2) multivariate analyses to establish the relative influence of each power base on perceived potential power. Sections 5 and 6 are comprised of a discussion of the findings and conclusions.

## 2. A Model of the Impact of Office Information Systems on Potential Influence

MANY RESEARCHERS HAVE ASSUMED THAT POWER is realized only in dyadic relationships between social actors. Wrong [46], however, asserted that power need not be exercised to exist. Even though power is usually defined as the capacity to control others, he emphasized that there is a distinct difference between the capacity to control and the actual practice of control. The distinction is between potential and enacted power [35]. Cobb [8] extended this concept by differentiating power study methodologies into an episodic approach and a dispositional approach. The episodic approach examines the execution of power. Its focus is on the means through which power is used [9, 20, 45]. This approach, therefore, corresponds to Provan et al.'s [35] notion of enacted power, which requires the existence of dyadic relationships among social actors. The dispositional approach, on the other hand, views power as the capacity or potential to affect change if required, which corresponds to Provan et al.'s [35] notion of potential power.

The focus in this paper is on how office information systems can support the individual in increasing influence or power potential, not on the actual usage of power in a specific setting. Hence, we choose to adopt the dispositional approach $[8]$ .

The central assumptions of this study are that personal power and influence are based largely on some set of power bases (resources) and that an office information system (OIS) can enhance personal influence through its impact on power bases. For example, a user of office information systems may be able to increase personal influence because office information systems permit him or her easier and speedier access to relevant information. Another user may have gained influence through his or her expertise in skill related to OIS usage.

In classical behavioral theory, personal power in bureaucratic organizations is thought to be based on de jure authority related to roles. Weber [44] claimed that a bureaucratic role conferred on an incumbent the right to act in a clearly defined sphere of competence and, in a hierarchy, higher-level incumbents had more authority than lower-ranking incumbents. While classical behavioral theory assumes that power is delegated from above, neoclassical theory emphasizes the importance of acceptance of power by the target population [40]. Personal power or influence, although linked to one's position, is not the same as legitimate authority. Kanter [23], for instance, claimed that power in an organization did not imply hierarchical domination but rather the ability to get things done. Similarly, Cartwright and Zander [7] and Porter et al. [34] defined influence as the extent to which a target seriously considers the wishes of the agent. Thus, power is not rooted in the structure of roles, but it depends on such personal factors as skills and expertise.

We selected the five sources of power used in the present study in the following manner. The work of Astley and Sachedeva [3] provided three of them—resource control, authority, and network centrality. On the basis of Pfeffer and Salancik's work [32], we subdivided resource control into two separate bases of power: namely, resources provision and irreplaceability. The fifth power base, expertise, was identified by means of interviews with twenty managers and their subordinates from four major corporations.

The five bases of power are discussed in detail in the following subsections. Hypotheses for this study are presented following the discussions in each subsection. Even though the five bases of power are believed to be determinants of power of all types of social actors, their contribution to individual power may differ, depending upon the task characteristics each individual social actor is performing. Therefore, in the empirical analysis the contribution of OIS on potential personal influence through the power bases will be explored for two categories of social actors: the ones who are directly related to the organization's core technology (i.e., engineers) and the ones who provide administrative support (i.e., accountants).

## 2.1. Resource Provision and Irreplaceability

The resource dependency perspective $[1, 2, 32]$ provides a suitable general framework for studying OIS impacts on potential power. The perspective applies ideas from social exchange theory $[5, 14]$ to interpersonal dependencies created by the needs of all social actors to acquire scarce resources $[35]$ . The resource dependency perspective has dominated the power literature $[32]$ . This perspective suggests that the individuals who can provide the most critical and hard-to-obtain resources attain power in an organization $[30, 31]$ .

Pfeffer and Salancik [32] identified two related elements that are critical in determining the dependency of others on a social actor: the importance of the resources and the extent to which the social actor has discretion over resource allocation (resource provision). Arguing from the perspective of resource dependency, Judith Hackman [17] asserted that influence is inversely proportional to the substitutability of the social actor (i.e., the extent to which resources are available from other social actors). As we argue below in the section on irreplaceability, OIS may decrease the substitutability of an individual.

## Resource Provision

Pettigrew [28, 29] investigated the communication patterns in the process of choosing a new computer and reported that power accrued to the individuals who served in the role of gatekeeper. The gatekeeper can affect the decision-making process by restricting or distributing the information and organizational resources required by others. OIS can enhance an individual's power through the ability to provide information valuable to others. Many studies that investigated the relationship between information system designers and users reported that the designers usually had relatively more power because they provided the information flow of the organization [12].

## Irreplaceability

Irreplaceability refers to the difficulty with which the duties of a person can be performed by a substitute person. The less easily the duties of one person can be performed by another, the more power the former will develop [13, 14]. A person's unique ability to cope with computer-based information systems may be an irreplaceable skill. Saunders [37] claimed that although OIS automated and routinized simple tasks, nonsubstitutability may be enhanced by increasing the variety and complexity of departmental tasks through enhanced information processing capability. Therefore, we speculate that a computerized environment can contribute to irreplaceability of a social actor, even though it is also possible for the computerized environment to contribute to his or her replaceability.

Thus, it is suggestive that the following relationships may hold true:

H1: An increased level of the impact of OIS on resource provision will have a significant positive effect on potential personal influence.

H2: An increased level of the impact of OIS on irreplaceability will have a significant positive effect on potential personal influence.

## 2.2. Authority

Astley and Sachedeva [3] claimed that the popularity of power-dependency theory should not allow one to overlook the long-standing central importance of hierarchical authority because Weber's [44] bureaucratic authority power lies at the heart of hierarchical differentiation of power. Astley and Sachedeva's [3] term—“authority”—refers to the power that derives from occupying a higher position in the organizational structure. In the context of the present study, authority corresponds to responsibility, since OIS cannot directly change an individual’s hierarchical position. OIS can, however, affect the authority or responsibility of an individual by increasing his or her information-processing capability.

In a field survey of the impact of information technology on processing plants, Zuboff [49] reported that the lower subordinates assumed more power and had increased responsibility because they had greater operational proximity to the relevant decisions and were provided with accurate data and procedures.

Hence, the following hypothesis seems reasonable:

H3: An increased level of the impact of OIS on authority/responsibility will have a significant positive effect on potential personal influence.

## 2.3. Network Centrality

Power is dependent on a social actor's position in an organizational network. Tichy and Fombrun [43] argued that organizational power relationships can be analyzed by studying networks of interactions. Actors located at highly interconnected nodes in the network gain power because their immersion in multiple interdependencies (i.e., pervasiveness) makes them functionally indispensable [3, 13, 18, 27]. Hinings et al. [21] claimed that pervasiveness is a determinant of power since it describes the interdepartmental communication and other interactions among subunits.

The actors located at central nodes in the network exert considerable power. Computerized communication can help organizational members become more active and attentive by eliminating the obstacles to voluntary participation, such as the fear of embarrassment, insecurity, and other influential factors in human interaction [19]. Increased frequency of contact with others facilitates a social actor's ability to provide resources for others, and hence enhances his or her power. In their case study of divisions of General Motors, Foster and Flynn [15] reported that changed organizational communication due to the implementation of information systems caused the flow of power to the obvious centers of communication. Hence, it seems reasonable to hypothesize as follows:

H4: An increased level of the impact of OIS on network centrality will have a significant positive effect on potential personal influence.

## 2.4. Expertise

Expertise refers to the knowledge the social actor brings to the organization. Several studies have noted that power relations take place within the context of a larger environment. For instance, Benson [4] applied Zald's [47] political economy approach to interorganizational power relations to argue that power in interorganizational networks is based not only on internal network exchange, but also on external linkages to the larger environment [35]. These external linkages to the environment can be augmented through the understanding of the problems faced by a social actor.

The argument can be applied to the context of the impact of OIS on personal influence. A social actor who exists in the open system can increase his or her power not only by increasing pairwise dependencies between social actors, but also by maintaining links through information systems to the larger environment [4]. Expertise can be viewed as a major source of linkage with the environment. Consequently, the social actor who can cope with the uncertainties arising from the external environment will have more power.

Information systems may enable a social actor to learn specific skills outside of jobs to which he or she is assigned. Gerrity [16] studied the impact of portfolio management information systems on bank managers, and concluded that the managers developed extended knowledge with the information systems. We believe that the newly developed knowledge may help a social actor accrue power.

Hence, the following hypothesis was constructed:

H5: An increased level of the impact of OIS on expertise will have a significant positive effect on potential personal influence.

## 3. Methodology

GIVEN THE DIVERSITY OF POWER BASES mentioned in the literature, the paucity of empirical research, and nonstandardized survey items, we decided to explore the impact of OIS on potential individual influence using a combination of interviews and surveys. The first phase of this study consisted of structured interviews with office information system users. The major goal was to tap the experience of office system users in order to develop questions for the questionnaire to be administered to a similar sample of workers in the second phase.

The research was conducted at seven departments in four manufacturing firms. These seven departments were human resources, legal support, sales support, corporate planning, legal services, engineering, and purchasing. This was a convenience sample among departments that used office information systems. At each site, at least three users were interviewed in the first phase. These structured interviews lasted between thirty minutes and one hour. In the second phase, questionnaires were given to 180 users of office information systems. Job tasks of participants included primarily data and text entry, file maintenance, word processing, general clerical functions, graphic analysis, and programming functions. The office information system provided capabilities such as graphics, spreadsheet programming, word processing, and electronic mail. The responses from each site were subjected to ANOVA tests and no significant differences were found to exist across case sites. Interviews and questionnaires were not answered by the same people. Since the major purpose of the first phase was to develop questions for the second phase, we will focus in the following on the procedure and findings of the second phase.

## 3.1. Procedure

The item pool for a Likert-type questionnaire was constructed to measure the five $a$ priori factors identified through literature review. The users could answer eleven statements descriptive of the impacts of OIS with a Lickert response format with seven response alternatives ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree. Three general questions specifically asked for the respondent's perceived impact of OIS on influence.

Questionnaires with attached cover letters and stamped return envelopes were mailed to 180 users of information systems at the seven case sites. Of the 180 questionnaires sent, 136 were completed and returned, representing a response rate of 75.6 percent. The sample size was later reduced to 110 by omitting respondents who had left an excessive number of items unanswered, and those who had responded consistently in a specific scale over a successive number of items. Pairwise elimination was used in the treatment of individual missing data.

## 3.2. Measures

The items that constituted the questionnaire used in this study are summarized in the appendix to this paper. The means and standard deviations of the item scores are as shown. A new set of questions was constructed for this study because we could not find existing ones that were appropriate. We wanted to measure the change in potential power/influence due to OIS usage. The opinions were to be sought from the users themselves because we were interested in measuring perceptions of potential power, not actual realization of power. Therefore, the perceptual measures instead of hard measures were appropriate for the purposes of this study.

Because this questionnaire had not been used in the past and the number of questions was relatively small, a series of tests was conducted to ensure that the questionnaire measures exhibited desirable properties.

## Convergent and Discriminant Validity

Following Campbell and Fiske [6], there are four criteria that should be achieved for the convergent and discriminant validity. First, the validity diagonal (different items, same trait) is significantly different from zero and large enough to warrant further investigation. That is, there are significant correlations across items for a given trait. Validity diagonal correlations, between 0.571 (the smallest one between NC1 and NC2) and 0.762 (the largest one between RP1 and RP2), pass this test (p < 0.001) and provide evidence of convergent validity. For discriminant validity, three criteria need to be satisfied: (1) the validity diagonal values should be larger than other values in rows and columns in their own heteromethod blocks; (2) the same patterns of high and low correlations should be found through the matrix; and (3) values for same trait-different methods should be higher than different trait-same methods.

Discriminant validity was tested using a revised form of the MTMM approach $^{1}$ by counting for each construct the number of times that an item of one construct correlates more highly with items of another construct than with items of its own theoretical construct. None of the ninety-four comparisons violated the discriminant validity test. In other words, each of the correlations between different items of the same construct was greater than the correlations with items of different constructs. In each case, the values shown in Table 2 meet these three criteria of discriminant validity. Therefore, the items for all five factors displayed good convergent and discriminant validity.

## 4. Analysis and Results

IN ORDER TO TEST THE HYPOTHESES PRESENTED, the analysis focused on the development of relationships between the effect of OIS usage on personal influence and the five underlying factors identified. The average scores of item measures were used as single measures for the five independent variables, and the average score of GQ1, GQ2, and GQ3 was used as the dependent variable (GQ). To test the hypotheses, correlation analyses of power base measures with influence measures and multiple regression analyses were performed.

Hypotheses 1 through 5 state that the greater the effect of OIS impact on a power base measure, the greater will be the effect of OIS usage on potential personal influence, where the power base measures are resource provision (RP), irreplaceability (IR), authority (AU), network centrality (NC), and expertise (EX). To test the hypotheses, we had to establish that GQ (the average scores of GQ1, GQ2, and GQ3) was highly correlated to each power base measure. Table 3 provides the correlations between the power base measures and the effect of OIS usage on potential personal influence. The results show that all five power bases were significantly correlated with potential personal influence (p < 0.01). The correlation table provided general support for these five hypotheses (H1 through H5). A multiple stepwise regression analysis using the five power base measures provides support for the claim that OIS affects potential personal influence through five power bases, as shown in Table 4.

In addition, we tested separately the relationships for two types of social actors: those whose jobs are directly related to the organization's core technology, and those whose jobs were related to administrative concerns. In this study, technical personnel are defined as personnel who use organizational core technology, and administrative personnel are those whose main responsibility is the support of the core activities. Even though there were few differences in OIS used by the technical and administrative personnel and in power bases affected by OIS (statistically not significant at $p < 0.05$ ), the results of correlations and multiple regression analyses revealed interesting differences for two types of personnel.

Judith Hackman [17] defined centrality as the closeness with which the purpose of a unit matches the central mission of the institution. Groups closest to the central mission are called core groups and other groups are called peripheral groups. After investigating resource allocation within a university, she reported that core programs, such as academic programs, gained internal influence when they acquired environmental resources that contributed to their own purposes. On the other hand, peripheral programs, such as administrative offices, gained influence internally when they focused on broader institutional needs and brought in external resources that contributed to the whole.

Therefore, the tests were also carried out separately for the administrative and technical personnel. Technical personnel were central to the organizational core technology; therefore, they gain influence by enhancing their technology. The influence of administrative personnel is often not dependent on organizational core technology but on the linkage between various organizational functions. Therefore, we hypothesized that these two types of organizational personnel differ in their sources of power. Specifically, technical personnel gain influence by increasing their expertise, and administrative personnel accrue power by having more connections to other people.

To test whether the correlation coefficients in Table 3 were statistically different for administrative and technical personnel, we calculated Fisher's z scores. The correlations between GQ and network centrality were significantly different (p < 0.01) for administrative (r = 0.776) and for technical personnel (r = 0.355).

Resource provision was strongly correlated with the influence measure GQ, for both administrative $r = 0.733$ and technical personnel $r = 0.643$ . The result, therefore, supported a general resource dependency theory: that is, a social actor who is able to provide valuable resources gains more potential power.

The correlations between irreplaceability and GQ and between authority and GQ were similar for both administrative (r = 0.413 and 0.510) and technical personnel (r = 0.468 and 0.594). The correlation between expertise and GQ (r = 0.399 for administrative and r = 0.487 for technical personnel) was slightly different for the two classes of personnel, but all were positive.

Since the sample size for the administrative personnel was small, the statistical power was calculated. The statistical power is defined as the probability that a statistical test will correctly reject a null hypothesis. In our case, a null hypothesis means that there is no relationship between a proposed power base and personal influence. The statistical power was calculated by using Fisher's z transformation for the correlation coefficient at alpha = 0.05 and for the sample correlation [10]. The statistical power is reported in Table 3. Cohen [10] claimed that studies that employ high power levels (0.80 or higher) offer advantages in interpretation of results. In our case all the correlation coefficients except two (irreplaceability and expertise for administrative personnel) showed good statistical power. Thus, for administrative personnel, only the findings on the relationship between personal influence and irreplaceability and the relationship between personal influence and expertise should be interpreted carefully.

## 5. Discussion

TABLES 3 AND 4 SHOW A REMARKABLE DIFFERENCE in the relative importance of each power base in predicting influence for technical and administrative personnel. Therefore, the two types of personnel are discussed separately in the following sections. However, the findings of administrative personnel should be interpreted with care because the sample size was small (n = 26).

## 5.1. Administrative Personnel

For administrative personnel, network centrality was the measur most significantly related to GQ (r = 0.776) and expertise the least significant (r = 0.339). The work flow is mainly informational for administrative personnel. Thus, the possibility of obtaining critical information can be enhanced through increased contacts with others. The correlation between resource provision and network centrality was high (r = 0.824, p < 0.01). The high correlation between the two suggests that the ability to get the most valuable resource—namely information—can be enhanced with a position of network centrality. Saunders and Scamell [38] examined power bases and power in universities and oil-and-gas companies. In their study, pervasiveness, operationalized by similar questions of network centrality, was more significantly related to power in universities, where work flow was mostly informational, than in the gas-and-oil companies. Thompson [42] claimed that the managerial (administrative) function services the technical function by: (1) mediating between the technical suborganization and those who use its products, and (2) procuring the resources necessary for carrying out the technical functions. These two correspond to network centrality and resource provision in the context of OIS impacts in our study.

The results of regression show that network centrality was the most important contributing factor to potential personal influence perceived with OIS usage. Authority was another significant contributing factor to potential influence. The power base factors, irreplaceability and expertise, were not significant in the regression analysis.

As discussed earlier, one of the most valued resources for administrative personnel is information. An administrative person who can provide relevant information will be able to shift the pattern of communications in the organizational network toward himself or herself as a center. Similarly, increased expertise with OIS usage can also contribute to being located in the center of the communication network, thereby increasing personal influence. Irreplaceability was not a significant factor. This is not surprising since administrative personnel do not engage in organizational core activities and OIS tends to automate administrative activities.

## 5.2. Technical Personnel

The correlation matrix for the technical people as shown in Table 3 revealed that resource provision was the measure most significantly related to influence (r=0.643), followed by authority (r=0.594), expertise (r=0.487), and irreplaceability (r=0.468). The stepwise multiple regression analysis in Table 4 again showed that resource provision was the most significant contributor to influence. Authority was significant while network centrality and expertise were not.

In organizational core functions, resources are derived from the knowledge of the core technology of the organization (expertise), and expertise manifests itself in the form of power through one's ability to provide knowledge (a resource). This relationship is particularly evident for technical personnel since managers can delegate more responsibility if they are convinced that the technical subordinates possess the expertise to carry out the tasks. Thus, expertise remains an important contributor to personal influence for technical personnel through its links to resource provision and authority. This argument was further supported by the high correlations between authority and expertise ( $r=0.617, p<0.01$ ) and between resource provision and expertise ( $r=0.535, p<0.01$ ).

Network centrality was not a significant factor of influence for technical personnel. Thompson [42] argued that the closed-system aspects of organization are seen most at the technical level. Technical personnel are closed off in the technical core and are least significantly affected by the environment. Therefore, having the position of network centrality is not an important source of influence compared to other aspects of power bases.

## 6. Conclusions

IN GENERAL, THE EMPIRICAL DATA did not allow us to reject the general model that the impact of OIS on potential personal influence can be explained through its impacts on resource provision, irreplaceability, authority, network centrality, and expertise. Correlation analysis of the data as shown in Table 3 and multiple regression analysis in Table 4 provided general support for the model. For administrative personnel, the direct contributors to potential influence were network centrality and authority, whereas for technical personnel, the direct contributors to potential influence were resource provision and authority.

Office information systems appear to affect the potential influence of administrative personnel through their effect on the centrality of these people on the organizational network. OIS can serve to increase or decrease network centrality. For example, the use of electronic mail may help a social actor in an organization to access or disseminate information and thus he or she can serve the role of information gatekeeper. The same piece of technology may decrease a social actor's influence because the shift in communications can bypass him. However, the sample size for the administrative personnel was small, and more studies are required to support this claim.

For technical personnel, the effect of OIS on potential influence is primarily through resource provision. We hypothesized that the impact may be primarily through expertise since this is the traditional base of power for technical personnel. The contradiction may have arisen because the information technology examined in this case was primarily office automation systems that had little effect on technical expertise. Nevertheless, the correlation analysis suggested that expertise enhances influence indirectly through its contributions to resource provision and authority. The link is plausible since technical expertise is a valuable resource that a person can provide to other members in the organization and that expertise brings more responsibility to the system's users.

For both administrative and technical personnel, the regression results in Table 4 indicated high $R$ -square coefficients, especially considering the fact that we have not controlled for the attenuating effects of measurement error. The significantly higher $R$ -square coefficient for administrative personnel suggests that the power base variables were a better set of independent variables to explain OIS's effects on influence than they were for technical personnel. The difference appears to stem from the characteristics of the information systems used by the respondents. Had the systems studied been oriented more toward the technical people, the results might well have been reversed.

The positive results we obtained from the empirical data suggested that the model we drew from the perspective of power base theory is a good predictor of the effect of OIS usage on potential influence. Thus, the study also provided further support for the power base school of the power/influence field. The correlation results in Table 3 and the multiple regression results in Table 4 indicated important differences in the mechanisms by which OIS affects influence for technical and administrative personnel. The significant differences stemmed from differences in tasks performed and roles played in the organization. Therefore, the power base models may have important intervening variables that have yet to be explained in the literature.

The items in the appendix provide a useful instrument for further studies of OIS impacts on influence. The instrument appears to be robust, although minor modifications could be made based on the empirical analysis that we performed. Further study is warranted to elucidate this important area of OIS impact. We need to expand the study to look at alternative forms of OIS and alternative types of task and roles. We also need to strengthen the understanding of causality with a research design that incorporates pre- and posttest measurements over time, in that the respondents were asked to assess their own perception of the impact of OIS on power bases and influence.

The results of this study provide useful and interesting implications from the managerial perspective. The change of an individual's potential influence in an organization appears to be driven by the pattern of use of technology and not necessarily by the technology itself. For example, Gerrity [16] reported that while his Portfolio Management System had been designed to help facilitate portfolio management, each user adapted the system to his or her own needs and evolved new applications that were different from the original purposes. It also appears that shifts in potential personal influence in an organization due to OIS usage are brought about through not one, but several variables. Thus, the ability to predict the outcome in the potential influence pattern due to OIS usage is quite difficult. The results of this and other future studies should help shed some light on this area.
