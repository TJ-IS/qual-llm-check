# Students as Surrogates for Managers in a Decision-making Environment: An Experimental Study

CARY T. HUGHES AND MICHAEL LUCAS GIBSON

CARY T. HUGHES is Professor and Chairperson of Computer Information Systems at Middle Tennessee State University. He received his Ph.D. from Arizona State University. Dr. Hughes is active in several information systems organizations. He has published articles in several leading information system journals such as Journal of Management Information Systems, Information & Management, and Journal of Information Systems Management.

MICHAEL LUCAS GIBSON is Associate Professor of Management Information Systems at Auburn University. He received his D.B.A. in management science at the University of Kentucky in 1984. Dr. Gibson is active in research in information technology management, information engineering, computer-aided software engineering, database management systems, decision support systems, and artificial intelligence. He has published many papers in academic and professional journals and presented papers at international, national, and regional meetings of many organizations. He is a member of ACM, DSI, ORSA/TIMS, AAAI, ACE, ASM, and IRMA.

ABSTRACT: Administering and controlling a field experiment in the area of information systems is a continuing problem. A solution for many researchers is to use students in controlled laboratory settings as surrogates for real-world decision makers. This practice is often questioned in many business and social sciences disciplines. Research results from attitudinal studies suggest that students' attitudes are not the same as those for whom they are surrogates. Still, some research demonstrates that students and nonstudents respond similarly during decision making. This paper contains the results of an experiment to decide if students and industry decision makers made decisions similarly before and following a training program in the use of a DSS generator. Our analysis of the results suggests that students were not adequate surrogates for industry managers in the decision-making process. Consequently, we dispute the claim that students and nonstudents unequivocally perform similarly during decision making.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: decision support systems, decision process attributes, managerial decision making, student surrogates, training.

## 1. Introduction

RESEARCH CONDUCTED IN THE ACADEMIC ENVIRONMENT of management information systems (MIS) has often relied on students acting as surrogates for industry managers.

For example, the famed Minnesota Experiments $[14]$ relied heavily on the use of student surrogates. Abdel-khalik $[1]$ states two reasons for the desirability of using student surrogates: (1) the lack of cooperation by many managers in experimental settings, and (2) the somewhat high cost of conducting field experiments. As a result, he suggests that it is far easier to use students as surrogates for real-world decision makers. Yet researchers in many disciplines question the acceptability of using students as surrogates. Their availability and the cost-effectiveness of their participation must not determine their suitability as appropriate substitutes for industry managers.

Researchers frequently argue that experienced managers ensure the validity of decision-making experiments $[3, 20]$ . According to McFarlan $[34, p. i]$ , “While laboratory experiments performed with young MBA students are neat, convenient, and relatively cheap to execute, the very simplicity of those environments means that for many problems the insights are highly suspect if one seeks to generalize from them to actual practice.”

Assessing the adequacy of using students as surrogates for managers is important. However, no universally recognized procedures or guidelines are available for this purpose. Many researchers believe that there is significant evidence that students are valid surrogates when the focus is the decision process, although some researchers' results attest to the contrary. The objective of this study is to obtain further evidence regarding the uncategorical assumption that students are valid surrogates for industry managers when the focus of the research is the decision-making process.

Our study does not address the validity of field over laboratory research as studied by Dipboye and Flanagan $[15]$ . We agree with Greenberg $[24]$ that the artificiality of many field studies causes their results to be just as questionable concerning external validity as the results of artificial laboratory experiments.

## 2. Related Literature

THREE MAIN SUMMARIES [4, 10, 22] APPEAR IN THE LITERATURE regarding the use of students as surrogates in experimentation. They exhibit conflicting conclusions about the soundness of using students in this capacity. Section 2.1 illustrates the confusion regarding the major research undertaken concerning this issue, such as the studies of Ashton and Kramer [4], Burnett and Dunne [10], and Gordon et al. [22]. Section 2.2 provides additional examples of continuing confusing signals from the literature.

## 2.1. Major Research Regarding the Use of Students as Surrogates

Ashton and Kramer [4] provide a summary of available literature before 1980 that suggests that studies using students as surrogates fall into one of two categories: (1) experimental research regarding the attitudes and attitudinal changes of student surrogates and managers, and (2) research regarding the actual process of decision making. They find that the results of research in these two categories suggest that while there is a significant difference between managers and students in relation to changes in attitude, there are considerable similarities in the decisions reached by the two groups. They imply that students may not be valid surrogates with attitudinal studies, but they are valid surrogates when the focus of the study is the decision-making process. They conclude that, “given the results reviewed here, one could argue that students are likely to be good surrogates for the real-world individuals of interest in tasks involving human information processing and decision making” [4, p. 3].

Burnett and Dunne [10], Alderfer and Bierman [2], Cunningham et al. [12], and Park and Lessig [37] dispute the assumption that students are valid surrogates when the focus is the decision-making process, and point out that differences emerged between students and nonstudents in the study of Khera and Benson [30] regarding the final decision on recommending or adopting a product based on a sales film. Researchers often cite this latter study as evidence of the adequacy of students as surrogates. Our experiment explicitly addresses whether this assumption is uncategorically true.

Ashton and Kramer's study confirms the observations of Zelditch and Evan [43] that comparing student behavior to the behavior of industry managers is similar to comparing the genetic process of fruit flies to that of humans. Zeldith and Evan find:

The geneticists find the fruit fly a strategic and convenient research subject because of the rapid completion of the experimentation process, the availability of many replications, and the readily confined and observed subjects. What is so important about the fruit fly is that its genetic process is not fundamentally different from the genetic process of humans. Neither its size nor its structure is correlated with the process investigated....Of the college student, it must be asked, are the ways in which they differ from other people correlated with the effects investigated? [43, p. 59]

Burnett and Dunne [10] examine the use of students as surrogates in marketing research. They point out that Enis et al. [18] showed that students were subjects in over half of consumer-behavior research, while their own study indicated that approximately 17 percent of the research in major marketing journals during their study period used students as surrogates.

Burnett and Dunne [10] state that research in marketing progressed in two directions: (1) “the effect of employing particular sampling frames on the development of marketing theory,” and (2) “comparing the results produced by student subjects with those produced by some other group of subjects” [10, p. 331]. Burnett and Dunne provide a review of marketing literature regarding the second approach. Their study contrasted students with multiple groups and concluded that students should only be used when they are the subjects of interest. They caution journal editors and readers that if authors do not provide appropriate comparative evidence, drawing valid conclusions from research is questionable.

Gordon et al. [22] reviewed thirty-two studies in which students and nonstudents participated as subjects. They attack the generalizability of student-surrogate experiment results by stating, “The most pervasive evidence of external validity of applied research involving students is to be found in existing empirical work that contains student and non-student data” [p. 192]. They contend that:

1. It is difficult to replicate student sample behavior with nonstudent subjects.

2. Background variables may influence the cognitive assessment of experimental tasks.

3. Respective authors often attribute between-group differences to the differences in student and nonstudent experience or familiarity with experimental tasks.

4. Research should rely on trained experimental subjects to improve external validity.

5. Researchers should use clinical debriefing to reveal subject perceptions, understandings, and meanings concerning the research situation.

6. The external validity of student-based research may be improved by employing subjects with demographic and interest profiles similar to the population to whom researchers wish to generalize.

They also caution scholars and academic evaluators against using students as surrogates. Greenberg [24] points out several areas of contention with the Gordon et al. research:

1. Those arguing against using student samples assume that studies involving nonstudent samples typically draw on representative samples that allow greater generalization, which does not always happen.

2. Bass and Firestone [6] assert that research findings based on a particular sample do not necessarily imply the generalizability of those findings; generalizability is an issue for future investigators to establish.

3. The similarities between students and the nonstudents they replace in experiments are greater than the differences between them.

4. Showing between-group differences does not make a convincing case against the use of student subjects.

5. Between-group differences may constitute a valuable source of information about the qualifying conditions of the theories pertinent to the studies cited.

6. Berkowitz and Donnerstein [7] assert that theoretically oriented studies require greater attention to the internal validity of the research operandi than to the representativeness of the sample.

7. Fromkin and Streufert [21] contend that application is best approached by building strong theories based on internally valid studies, not by attempts to simulate real-world conditions.

8. The degree of homogeneity of the sample that adds no extraneous variance to experimental behavior is a strength, not a weakness.

Gordon et al.'s rejoinder [23] to Greenberg's criticism points out that the problem of external validity stems from no standard definition of external validity. Furthermore, they conclude that the problem stems from the differences in the definition of external validity within “universalistic” and “particularistic” research.

Dobbins et al. [16] further attack the position of Gordon et al. [22] with the following conclusions:

1. Laboratory research should not be evaluated by the extent to which it imitates organizational scenarios, but by the extent to which it increases our understanding of the processes in work behavior.

2. Gordon et al. [22] erroneously cited Christensen-Szalanski et al. [11] as evidence that results obtained with students do not generalize to nonstudents; a better interpretation is that technical judgment is affected by specialized training.

3. Dipboye and Flanagan [15] assert that laboratory and field research are equally artificial.

4. Locke [32] asserts that laboratory research is generalizable when expert-review agrees that research results are generalizable in field settings.

5. Berkowitz and Donnestein [7] assert that the meaning subjects assign to the situation and their behavior better determines the generalizability of experimental outcomes than does the demographic representativeness of the sample or the realism of the experimental setting.

They also disagreed with Gordon et al.'s caution to editors and academic evaluators, stating that it would lead to a decline in laboratory experiments and be "extremely dangerous for our field" [16, p. 285].

## 2.2. Additional Research on the Use of Students as Surrogates

Miller [35] suggests that students should be used as surrogates for managers only when the intention of the research is (1) to understand something about the students, or (2) a pilot study testing the research methodology. Examples of the first type are studies concerning the behavior of students concerning more effective ways to teach and the environmental conditions that affect the education process. The Minnesota Experiments [14] are an example of the second category.

Enis et al. [18] say students may only be appropriate surrogates when internal validity is more important than external validity. Ferber [19] and Shuptrine [40] suggest using students for exploratory research or pretesting of experimentation.

Birnberg and Nath [8] suggest three ways in which managers and students may differ: skills, personality traits, and experience. Alderfer and Bierman [2] and Kahneman and Tversky [28] add that there are also differences in risk aversion regarding wealth between students and nonstudents. Birnberg and Nath [8], with Ashton and Kramer, suggest that the research project might include student subjects with real-world managers of interest. The use of both groups allows the evaluation of the importance of factors such as experience, wealth, and age. Thus, students may be included when the purpose of the research is not to use students as surrogates; their use would simply add to the sampling population on topics about students and nonstudents.

Burnett and Dunne [10] express a far greater concern regarding the use of students when they assert that students “may be less appropriate as subjects for experiments . . . because the conditions under which they participate influence their reactions to research events” (e.g., students trying to ruin the study and “super-good subjects who will bend over backward to give the researcher what he or she wants”) [10, p. 331]. Further fueling the dispute, Remus [38] provides additional research results regarding the use of students and concludes that MBA students can safely be used as surrogates in decision-making tasks such as those in production scheduling.

## 2.3. Summary of Related Literature

The studies regarding the use of students as surrogates and responses to them send confusing signals to researchers in all functional areas of business. Too often, the researchers state too strongly their opposition to one side of the issue. The results of one experiment should not be an indictment of either side of the issue. In addition, the fact that conflicting results appear from different functional areas illustrates that no area is free from the conflict. Our study was undertaken to show that MIS researchers should be cautioned regarding this issue. We expected that the specific results of our study would be generalizable only for the type of study conducted. We expected that our results would provide additional evidence regarding the validity of uncategorically assuming students to be valid surrogates when the focus of the experiment is the decision-making process.

## 3. Research Methodology

OUR STUDY COMPARES THE DECISION-MAKING RESPONSES of students and managers involving a training program on the use of a particular decision support system (DSS) generator. Specifically, we studied whether students and managers responded similarly during the decision process before and following training in the use of the DSS generator.

## 3.1. Research Question

Our major research question is: Are students suitable surrogates for managers in decision making following training on the use of a DSS generator? The major hypothesis tested was:

There is no significant difference between decision performance scores of managers and students during decision making when subjected to the same training program and same problems to solve.

## 3.2. Research Instrumentation

Empirical experimentation was conducted comparing changes in the decision process between students and industry decision makers concerning participation or nonparticipation in a training program on the use of a DSS generator. The DSS generator used in the experiment was a high-level, interactive financial planning language.

In general, the benefits of a financial planning language are:

\- access to powerful reporting capabilities;

\- ready testing of opportunities and weaknesses of available alternatives;

\- ease and rapidity in changing the values for variables;

• information tailored to the attributes of the user regarding the decision; and

\- availability of deterministic and/or probabilistic solutions to experimental cases.

These characteristics are important to managers during the process of making decisions.

## 3.3. Selection and Training of Subjects

The experiment was conducted at a major university in the southwestern part of the United States. The student subjects were graduate MBA students with no demographic concentration. Questionnaires were used to assess if the student subjects had any previous experience in the use of a DSS generator of the type used in the experiment. If they had, they were eliminated from participation in the study. Similar precautions were taken with the participating managers. Managers were middle-level and upper-level decision makers from a nearby city. The training program was one commonly provided to organizations using a DSS generator on a trial basis. Control groups of students and managers were selected to participate in both pretraining and posttraining tests, but not in the DSS training sessions, or in the use of the DSS generator. For both groups receiving training, the same training was administered at the university site from an instructor experienced in the use of the financial planning language.

The training was divided into a morning session and an afternoon session. It began with a presentation of the types of decisions managers face. This was followed by a discussion of the advantages of using modeling in making these decisions and the advantages of using a DSS generator for modeling. The morning session concluded with a demonstration of how to use the financial planning language.

The afternoon training session started with a thirty-minute review of the morning session followed by a one-hour group workshop during which a model was designed, and a one-hour computer terminal session in which participants received “hands-on” experience using the DSS generator.

## 3.4. Decision Process Attribute Measurements

Because of the difficulty in measuring changes in actual decision processes, certain attributes of the decision process were used to measure differences in experimental subjects. Two attributes were selected to assess differences between student and nonstudent performance. These attributes were the time it took to reach a decision, and the number of alternatives generated by subjects during the decision-making process. The literature provides considerable justification for using these particular attributes $[9, 14, 29]$ . The measurement of the attributes was made operational by administering a pretraining and posttraining decision-making test case to the participants. A pretraining test was conducted to find the length of time the subject groups used to reach a decision, and the number of alternatives generated without the aid of a DSS generator. Subsequently, a posttraining test was conducted to find the length of time the same subjects used to reach a decision, and the number of alternatives generated with the aid of the DSS generator.

The pretraining and posttraining cases used were similar and neither had an optimal solution. As such, the characteristics of the test scenarios did not affect the results, and no measure of the quality of the decisions was present.

## 3.5. Location of the Experiment

The pretraining tests for both groups were conducted at the university site. The morning training session was preceded by pretraining tests conducted on both groups. The control group was released following the administration of the pretraining test. One week following training, students responded to posttraining test cases at the training site and the managers were subjected to the posttraining cases at their respective job locations under the supervision of the researchers. Neither group had access to the DSS generator until testing, subjects were isolated at both test sites during experimentation, and experimental location lacked any measurable affect on experimental outcome.

## 4. Statistical Analysis of Experimental Results

## 4.1. Analysis Variables

FOUR VARIABLES WERE USED TO MEASURE THE RESULTS of experimentation:

1. the pretraining time (PT) required for subjects to reach a decision regarding the experimental case;

2. the pretraining number (PA) of solution alternatives generated during evaluation of the experimental case;

3. the posttraining time (TP) required for the subjects to reach a decision on the experimental case; and

4. the posttraining number (AP) of solution alternatives generated during evaluation of the experimental case.

Fifty-three students and sixty-three managers participated in the study, with variables measured on two groups of students and two groups of managers. The student and manager control groups did not participate in the training program, but participated in both the pretraining and posttraining case experiment simultaneously with those who received the training. Table 1 shows the mean scores on the four variables for both the control groups and the experimental groups.

## 5. Discussion

THIS RESEARCH EXHIBITED TWO NOTICEABLE OUTCOMES. The first is that it cannot be uncategorically assumed that students are suitable surrogates for managers in performing MIS research regarding the decision process. Both the student and manager control and research groups in our study exhibited significantly different behavior in the decision-making process. Second, the number of solution alternatives generated by students and managers is significantly different. Both the control and research groups generated a significantly different number of solutions in the decision analysis of the test cases.

A less noticeable outcome of our research concerns the contention by Burnett and Dunne [10] that students seem to provide better results. Students in our research did not produce better outputs than managers. For the participants in the control group, managers generated more alternatives in more time in the pretest case and more alternatives in less time in the posttraining test case. For the experimental subjects, managers generated fewer alternatives in less time for the pretest case and fewer alternatives in similar time in the posttraining test case. Thus, it cannot be assumed that students perform better in experiments than managers in all situations. We contend that the control-group managers generated more alternatives in less time in the posttraining test case because they became familiar with decision making in a case scenario and reacted more quickly, as normally occurs in volatile business situations.

We also believe that the DSS-trained managers behaved as expected: they took longer to make a decision following training but required the generation of fewer alternatives. Managers in business frequently exhibit these characteristics in decision making aided by a computerized DSS. They take alternatives generated by a DSS seriously and evaluate them longer, but usually require the generation of fewer alternatives.

Our results add to the continuing concern about using students as surrogates for managers in the decision-making process. They show that it cannot be uncategorically assumed that students are valid surrogates when the focus of the research is the decision-making process. These results are supported by other reports in the literature $[5, 13, 17, 25, 26, 27, 30, 31, 36, 39, 41, 42]$ .

We agree with the suggestion of Gordon et al. [23] that the suitability of student subjects should be clinically evaluated before testing. When researchers want to use students as surrogates, we suggest that they conduct a pilot study both in the academic laboratory using students and in the field using industry managers. A statistical analysis of the results of the two pilot studies should find if the students are proper surrogates and if they respond similarly during experimentation. Thus, the researchers can take advantage of the availability and cost-effectiveness of using students and avoid the problems associated with conducting full field studies. If, however, analysis of the pilot study results indicates that students are not suitable surrogates for managers in the research domain, then either a full field study should be conducted or the researcher should consider abandoning the research. The fact that students are readily available and provide a somewhat inexpensive source for experimental subjects is not sufficient reason for researchers to ignore questions of validity in the selection of research subjects.

Further research needs to be conducted to determine if students are suitable surrogates in other decision-making situations. When using students as surrogates, researchers need to examine the performance variables pertinent to demographic concentration of subjects such as sex, age, years of experience, and education level.

We doubt that an absolute answer will ever be reached on the suitability of using students as surrogates during the decision-making process. Our results agree with those of Burnett and Dunne $[10]$ , that the suitability of students as surrogates in the decision process depends on case-specific circumstances. We do not take as strong a position against the external validity of student-oriented laboratory research as Gordon et al. $[22]$ or Burnett and Dunne $[10]$ . Burnett and Dunne go so far as to say that students should only be used “when they are the subject group of interest” $[10, p. 341]$ . Student surrogates are useful for both internal and external validity, providing their profile and performance are shown to be similar to those of the population of interest. We also choose not to “prematurely discuss the applicability of our research to other settings and subjects as is the tendency of behavioral scientists” $[23, p. 162]$ . Our results only extend within the boundaries of our experimentation, as asserted by Bass and Firestone $[6]$ . However, our research provides additional evidence against the uncategorical assumption that students are valid surrogates when the focus of the experiment is the decision process.

## 6. Conclusion

THIS PAPER HAS PRESENTED THE RESULTS OF EXPERIMENTATION regarding the suitability of using students as surrogates for industry decision makers. Students and industry managers were tested relative to their performance before and following training in the use of a DSS generator. Test cases were administered for groups of managers and students who received or did not receive training in the use of the DSS generator. The results of these test cases were evidence of significant differences in the behavior of students and managers during the decision-making process.

The question of whether students can be used as surrogates for managers in empirical research still represents a viable research topic. Our results agree with those of other researchers that the suitability of students as surrogates during the decision-making process is situation-specific. These results are part of the slowly accumulating evidence regarding the suitability of using students as surrogates for managers for experiments involving the decision-making processes. We suggest that further research be conducted to determine if certain situations in research scenarios lend themselves to the suitability of surrogate use.
