GERARDINE DESANCTIS, MARSHALL SCOTT POOLE, HOWARD LEWIS, AND GEORGE DESHARNAIS

ABSTRACT: Quality improvement programs are regarded by many businesses as mechanisms for improving organizational competitiveness in the face of tightening budgets and more global market structures. Recently developed computer-supported group problem-solving tools, or Group Decision Support Systems (GDSSs), offer the potential to reduce the effort involved in applying quality improvement methods by providing automated means to enter, record, and operate on ideas generated by team members during face-to-face meetings. To explore how GDSSs might be used by quality teams, the Internal Revenue Service and the University of Minnesota conducted a multiyear study of GDSS use in small-group meetings. This paper presents some initial findings on the extent and types of uses of the technology made by teams based on the first seven months of our study. This preliminary analysis is presented within the framework of adaptive structuration theory and considers the relationship among inputs, processes, and outputs of group interaction as a GDSS is used.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: group decision support, group problem solving, quality teams.

## Introduction and Research Questions

QUALITY IMPROVEMENT PROGRAMS ARE REGARDED BY MANY BUSINESSES as mechanisms for improving organizational competitiveness in the face of tightening budgets and more global market structures. These programs vary in their particular design and style of implementation, but most emphasize common principles: union-management cooperation, participative decision making, and systematic problem solving. To bring about quality improvement, managers and employees meet in small groups to identify problems and chart methods for solving problems and implementing positive change. Participants take time off from their regular work duties to engage in a lengthy series of face-to-face meetings. Structured group process techniques and an atmosphere of participative, consensus-based decision making are used to help make these meetings efficient and productive [4, 9]. To apply these techniques successfully and maintain the desired meeting atmosphere requires special training and a continual effort on the part of the team. Recently developed computer-supported group problem-solving tools, or group decision support systems (GDSSs), offer the potential to reduce the effort involved in applying quality-improvement methods by providing automated means to enter, record, and operate on team member ideas during face-to-face meetings. GDSSs provide support for brainstorming, issue prioritization, root-cause analysis, strategy selection, and other techniques that quality teams are expected to use. The opportunity for all team members simultaneously to contribute ideas and opinions to the group discussion is designed to yield open participation and an atmosphere conducive to creative thinking and consensus-based decision making. In addition, GDSSs provide extensive documentation on team meetings and decision procedures [6, 8, 9]. GDSSs are sometimes characterized in terms of basic and advanced group support features. Basic, level 1 features support communication efficiency through support for agenda setting, group scratchpad, idea gathering/brainstorming, idea evaluation, commenting, and file saving and recovery. More advanced, level 2 features provide structured decision processes, such as stakeholder analysis, problem formulation, and multi-attribute utility modeling [5].

The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) and the University of Minnesota explored how a GDSS might be used to facilitate quality team meetings in a multiyear study conducted at the IRS Manhattan District Office. This paper presents some initial findings from this research project, considering the extent and types of uses made of the GDSS during its initial implementation. The research model for the study is given in figure 1. The model is premised on adaptive structuration theory (AST), which provides an input → process → output framework for the study of group use of advanced information technologies such as GDSSs [12]. The model states that the outcomes of GDSS use, such as satisfaction with the technology, are a function of not only the extent to which the system is used but, more importantly, the manner in which the system is used. There may be some direct relationship between extent of system use or types of features used and team satisfaction with the technology, but the relationship will not be clear-cut or consistent across teams. The process of use must be examined as well. In this study we consider three aspects of the GDSS use process: (1) whether system use was at the suggestion of a team member or a person in authority (such as a team leader or outside facilitator); (2) what instrumental uses, or purposes, teams made of the technology; and (3) the sentiments (positive or negative) expressed by team members as they used the GDSS. These three processes are part of what AST describes as appropriation.

According to AST, the structures of a GDSS (e.g., level 1 versus level 2 features) are adapted by teams as they use the technology for particular purposes. AST assumes that GDSS structures are not simply unchanging tools but that the act of bringing structures into action is the key to their ultimate impact. The process by which structures enter into action is termed structuration. Structuration is the subtle process of giving social meaning to technologies by incorporating technology structures into human action [3, 11]. Structuration is difficult to observe and is best studied in the long-term use of a technology by groups, organizations, or even societies. Appropriation of a technology is the more immediate, visible process of its use; appropriation evidences deeper structuration processes [10]. A key aspect of appropriation is the reasoned uses for which people choose to bring technology structures into action. For example, why does a team choose to use a GDSS in its group meeting? For what purpose does the team apply the technology? The team appropriates the GDSS for different instrumental uses, or purposes, and the team members may vary in their attitudes toward appropriation of the GDSS. The team might use the GDSS to accomplish task activities, manage communication and other group processes, or to exercise power or influence; their attitudes toward the technology as they appropriate it may be positive or negative. The appropriation construct includes the intended purposes, or meaning, that groups assign to technology as they use it. By identifying instrumental uses we can begin to understand not only what GDSS features are being used and how they are being used, but also why they are being used—the reasons or purposes for which groups elect to bring GDSS or other structures into action. To the extent that these use processes vary across teams, the outcomes of GDSS use will vary.

Appropriation processes are subtle and difficult to observe, but they are evidenced in the interaction that takes place during a group discussion. Appropriation processes are not always obvious in just a few speech acts. Typically they are revealed through analysis of meeting phases, or extended periods of discourse within a given group meeting. Therefore, to study appropriation processes, we must closely examine the discourse of team meetings and interpret the discourse in light of a theoretical framework such as that provided in the “process” component of figure 1.

Using the constructs outlined in figure 1, our study investigates the following questions:

## System Usage (Inputs to Use)

Q1. To what extent was the GDSS used by the quality teams? By staff (nonquality) teams?

Q2. Did GDSS use vary depending on the timing of the introduction of the technology in the quality process—either in the early, middle, or later phase of the team's life?

Q3. To what extent did teams make use of advanced level 2 GDSS features, as opposed to more basic level 1 features?

## Satisfaction with the GDSS (Effects of Use)

Q4. Were the teams comfortable in their use of the GDSS? Did they enjoy using it?

Q5. Did the GDSS provide the right kind of support for the teams?

Q6. Did team members receive sufficient training to make effective use of the GDSS?

## Initiation of Use, Instrumental Uses, and Use Sentiments (Process of Use)

Q7. Did team members take responsibility for initiating GDSS use, or did use only occur at the suggestion of the team leader or outside facilitator?

Q8. How exactly was the GDSS used during meetings? For what instrumental uses, or purposes, did teams use the GDSS?

a. Was it used to accomplish the work tasks of the meeting?

b. Was it used to manage the meeting process, or procedures?

c. Was it used by certain members to control or exercise power over the team?

d. Was it used to establish or maintain social relationships among members?

e. Did members use it for private, individualistic work, as opposed to group work?

f. Was the GDSS a distraction to the team—used as a toy (for fun)? Did it just confuse the group and get them off track?

Q9. Did the types of instrumental uses vary as a function of team experience in using the GDSS?

Q10. Did the pattern of instrumental use vary depending upon who initiated GDSS use—team members versus a leader or outside facilitator?

Q11. To what extent did the teams exhibit positive or negative sentiments as they used the GDSS?

Q12. Did these sentiments vary as a function of the team's experience with using the GDSS, or as a function of who initiated its use?

Q13. Were sentiments related to the team's apparent satisfaction with the technology, in terms of comfort, support, and training?

Questions 1 through 6 explore the inputs and outputs of GDSS use for the entire set of teams in our study. Questions 7 through 13 explore how the teams use the GDSS (i.e., the process of use) via close analysis of a sample of twelve recorded meetings, drawn from our larger data set. Our analysis considers seven types of instrumental GDSS uses and how teams differ in these depending on factors such as their phase in the quality process, their experience in using the GDSS, and who initiates GDSS use in team meetings. In this way, we can begin to make inferences about use–satisfaction relationships during GDSS implementation.

Background to the Study

## The IRS/NTEU Joint Quality Improvement Process

THE IRS/NATIONAL TREASURY EMPLOYEES UNION (NTEU) Joint Quality Improvement Process is an active collaboration between the NTEU bargaining unit employees and IRS supervisors and managers in order to improve the quality of the internal and external work product in the IRS. The process applies a small-group problem-solving approach to achieving positive organizational change. Newly created, crossfunctional teams of eight to ten volunteers, consisting of both union and managerial employees, work together from six months to two years addressing one of any number of organizational issues, such as worker morale, personnel policies, forms processing, or computer systems management. They meet for approximately two hours every few weeks to identify and implement ways of improving quality, efficiency, and the work environment. Decision making is done by consensus. Teams members, leaders, and outside facilitators receive training in an eight-step quality method developed by Juran [9]. They are also instructed in problem-solving techniques [4], and given training in group dynamics, leadership, and interpersonal communication. Each team has a designated or team-appointed leader, as well as an outside facilitator who provides feedback and support to the group during their meetings.

## Group Decision Support: The SAMM System

Software aided meeting management (SAMM) is a multiuser software system designed for use in small face-to-face meetings to support activities such as agenda setting, note taking, problem formulation, idea generation, solution development and evaluation, planning, and negotiation [12]. The system consists of a set of menu-based modules that facilitate the execution of group problem-solving techniques, such as those used in the IRS quality-improvement process. Participative, consensus-based decision making is encouraged by providing anonymous recording and display of ideas, numerical opinion polling, and systematic idea reduction and prioritization. The group assembles at a horseshoe-shaped conference table with a terminal and keyboard for each group member to enter ideas, comments, votes, or notes. At the front of the room is a large screen where summary reports from the group's activities are displayed. SAMM is a “user-driven” system, which means that teams can operate the technology on their own, without a specially trained technician. Teams do not entirely rely upon SAMM during a meeting but rather use a subset of the features at various points during their discussion.

The SAMM system was selected for use at the IRS because of the close match between the system's design and the techniques being used in the quality process. Teams (except in the case of demonstrations) are provided with training and a detailed manual that explains how quality-improvement steps and techniques can be accomplished with the SAMM system. Although a technician is not required for system operation, at the IRS quality-program administrators often are available to help teams with SAMM use. The administrators are available to aid teams in selecting features for use, in applying features to specific problems or tasks, and in general system operation and troubleshooting. The administrators are either in the meeting room, in an adjacent room, or accessible by phone during many (though not all) meetings.

## Overview of the Study

The IRS Manhattan District Joint Quality Improvement Process and the University of Minnesota began the research project in January of 1989. Five existing teams and five newly formed teams participated in the study. The five new teams had SAMM available to them since their initial formation. Of the five older teams, two were introduced to SAMM during the last few months of their participation in the quality process, and three were introduced to SAMM in the middle of their life. All of the teams met regularly in the SAMM facility since joining the research study. In addition to the ten teams who were a formal part of our study, the GDSS facility was made available to other quality teams in the district and to staff teams who were interested in using the facility.

All analyses reported in this paper are based on data gathered between September 1989 and March 1990, which represents a period of SAMM use following system installation and team training in how to use the system. Analyses are based on computer system use logs, observations of a sample of videotaped meetings, and postmeeting questionnaires of quality-team satisfaction with the technology. Some system use data were also available for nonquality teams who used the SAMM facility, and this was added to our analyses for Questions 1, 3, and 4–6.

System Usage (Inputs to Use)

## Extent of Use

Although the quality teams included in our study were trained in SAMM use and agreed to have their meetings videotaped, actual use of the system was always optional. As a global measure of system use, counts were made from computer log data to determine the number of meetings held by the ten teams in which one or more features within the SAMM system was actively used. As another interesting measure, we also counted the total number of SAMM-supported meetings by teams both inside and outside of our study. This latter figure includes twelve staff teams (as opposed to crossfunctional quality teams) who used the system for specific meetings outside of the quality-improvement program and whose activities were logged by the system administrator.

## Types of Features Used

Based on computer log data, we classified the types of features used in meetings as either level-1 or level-2-type structures [5]. We were able to capture these data for both the ten quality teams included in our study as well as twelve staff teams who used the SAMM system during the seven-month period of interest.

## Satisfaction with SAMM (Effects of Use)

After every second or third meeting, team members completed a thirteen-item “system response questionnaire,” which consisted of items developed by Sambamurthy [13], Aldag and Power [1], and Doll and Torkzadeh [7]. Sambamurthy provides reliability and validity information for the questionnaire [13]. The questionnaire contained a series of statements (e.g., “I enjoyed using the SAMM System”) to which the respondent was asked to indicate degree of agreement on a seven-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). Three dimensions were considered as part of this analysis: (1) degree of comfort and enjoyment when using the SAMM system; (2) extent to which the software provided the “right support” for that particular meeting; and (3) degree to which members think they have adequate training to use SAMM effectively. Items for each dimension were averaged to create a “score” for that dimension. Higher scores indicated more positive degree of comfort and support for the first two dimensions, and an adequate level of training for the third dimension.

## Initiation of Use, Instrumental Uses, and Use Sentiments (Process of Use)

Process of use measures were obtained from in-depth analyses of videotapes from twelve quality-team meetings. The twelve sample meetings were arbitrarily selected and included two meetings from each of five teams and one meeting from each of two additional teams. Thus, a total of seven teams were represented in the twelve-meeting sample. For each meeting, transcripts were constructed to document the sequence of meeting activities and the content and temper of the team's discussion. From these transcripts, we made counts for the following variables:

## Initiator of SAMM use

We first segmented the meeting transcriptions into those portions in which SAMM was being used in the discussion (including keyboarding, looking at screens, discussing information in the system, or discussing how the system should be used), and segments where the group was not making any use of the system (open discussion, use of a flip chart, or discussion focused around paperwork, such as handouts). "Use" segments referred to periods of group interaction with SAMM, not simply a single keystroke or menu choice by an individual member. Any given period could be as brief as a few minutes, or it might extend for more than an hour. Conceivably, it could extend for the entire meeting, but this never happened; the typical use of SAMM occurred on several occasions during a meeting, with intermittent open discussion before and after use. In our sample, the total number of SAMM segments varied from as few as two to as many as eighteen use periods in one meeting. Finally, only initiatives that were acted on by the group were counted. For example, if someone said “Let’s take notes in the SAMM scratchpad,” and the group rejected or ignored the initiative, the suggestion was not counted.

Since SAMM is designed to be self-operated by the group, we were interested in determining how often system use was a result of a team member suggesting that the group make use of the system, versus the initiative to use being made by the team leader or outside facilitator. Since participation and self-management are a goal of the quality process, greater initiation by team members is desirable. For each period of use, we simply made a note of who initiated the team's use of SAMM: a team member, or the leader or outside facilitator.

## Instrumental Uses of SAMM

Each segment of SAMM use that occurred in each meeting transcript was classified as one of five types of use, applying a typology of GDSS use developed previously by Poole and DeSanctis [12]. Two additional categories were added to this scheme, to yield a total of seven types of uses, as follows:

## a. TASK

Definition: Use of the GDSS to facilitate substantive work on agenda setting, problem definition, solution generation, or other decision-making operations.

Example:

EILEEN [moving on to the next topic]: We should be prioritizing our problems.

HAL: How does the group feel about selection one of these six problems without a quorum?

JOE: I'm for it.

EILEEN: Me too.

HAL: You're all comfortable with going ahead?

All agree. Eileen says it would be in SAMM for them to review; they could use rank and weights. Hal tells them to chose Idea Evaluation and then Problems and Issues from the SAMM menu. Hal leads the group through the Assign Weights procedure. The period of SAMM use continues. The group first agrees to prioritize, then moves to SAMM to do ranking and weighting.

Includes: periods when the group first decides the activity they will undertake, then moves to the technology to facilitate accomplishment of the activity.

Does not include: periods when the group looks to the technology to determine how they should proceed. In this way, task uses are distinguished from process uses.

## b. PROCESS

Definition: Use of the GDSS to manage communication and other group processes. Example:

Gary and Jerry talk about their experiences and perceptions of the problem.

JERRY: Those procedures worked years ago . . . but don't seem to work anymore.

GARY: No ... people are building a little kingdom ... a dynasty.

JERRY: Sending back cases [after processing] for nonsense reasons.

GARY: Yeah.

They talk more about forms and procedures.

PATTY (interrupts): But does that have anything to do with [pauses; she is trying to get them back on track]. Maybe a Stakeholder Analysis on who's involved in . . .

JERRY: That may not be a bad idea.

Patty and Gary talk.

PATTY: Even without all the information, it might be an approach. How do you do this, do you know how?

They start to fiddle with keyboards. Jerry holds up a memo and talks about it. Dave returns with papers from the copy machine. Lena reviews their discussion while Dave was gone.

MARTY: It was suggested that we brainstorm on who has a stake in it, the agent, the

Period of SAMM use continues. The group is engaged in an open, unstructured discussion of issues and Patty suggests they use Stakeholder Analysis to structure their thinking.

Includes: periods when the group is on a tangent, or floundering about how to proceed and then looks to SAMM to help them to decide how to proceed.

Does not include: periods when the group first decides an activity, or how to proceed, and then looks to SAMM to accomplish the activity.

## c. POWER

Definition: Use of the GDSS by an individual on the team to influence the team's thinking or move them forward in the meeting.

Example:

While Harriet tells about something she learned in a seminar last week and other group members listen, Jan is typing something into SAMM.

MIKE (to Harriet): Were any handouts given?

HARRIET: Yeah. [Explains.]

Jan continues to type. Mike holds some sheets of paper up to show the group. Ellen shows fishbone diagram.

BOB: How about the matrix? Should we show those things? HANK: Yeah . . . show them how you came up with the solution. Don't create anything special just for the presentation.

ELLEN: Right, yeah.

BOB [looks through the group's past notes, turns pages]: We had a questionnaire, root cause matrix ...

JAN [stops typing]: I came up with five things [points to SAMM large screen and smiles] without much thought.

DAN [joking]: Imagine if you'd thought!

JAN: Since there are five of us, I came up with five things.

All read large screen quietly. Jan's inputs into SAMM have influenced the group's direction and they go on to talk about the large screen list. Jan directs the group's conversation for the next period.

Includes: SAMM use where the user(s) deliberately intended to affect the general discussion or others' opinions.

Does not include: a group member's individual use of the system which is never used to deliberately influence the group.

## d. SOCIAL

Definition: Use of the GDSS to establish or maintain social relationships among members, such as to joke, or laugh, or tease one another; use of the GDSS for socioemotional expression. Example:

Brenna is reading information from the large screen aloud to the group. Others follow along looking at items on the screen. Then the discussion eases away from the content as they joke about “double meanings” and misspellings they made on entries. Joking is about their choice of words, spelling, clarity of ideas, etc. Members enjoy the divergence. There is a tension release as they laugh with one another over their SAMM entries.

Includes: laughing and joking together as they input into SAMM or discuss outputs, shared jokes in the context of SAMM use.

Does not include: socializing that has not been brought about by, or directly involves, use of SAMM.

## e. INDIVIDUALISTIC

Definition: Use of the GDSS by an individual purely for private reasons, such as to take personal notes or to explore the system features. Individualistic use is not intended to have the group move to GDSS use and may be due to boredom.

## Example:

Group talks about problems they are experiencing in getting the information they need. Paul enters into SAMM: "Inaccessibility of departmental resources" Jane spells "inaccessibility" for him. BEN: Make the entry more detailed.

Throughout all this discussion, Marsha keys into Private Scratchpad of SAMM. Charles asks where they are.

BEN: We're trying to describe problems.

OUTSIDE FACILITATOR: Are you brainstorming?

BEN: Last time we found out these are not problems, but solutions. Now we're trying to find problems under solutions.

Paul and Jane explain further to Charlie. Jane, Ben, and Paul talk about problems. Marsha types on SAMM through all this. She's out of the discussion. Marsha never reveals or discusses what she is doing on SAMM with the group.

Includes: individual task-related as well as fun/exploratory uses of SAMM

Does not include: individual uses that are used to influence the group (as in uses for power purposes).

## f. FUN/EXPLORATORY

Definition: Use of SAMM for its own sake, with no specific goal in mind other than to “play” or “understand how the system works.”

## Example:

While the group is discussing their agenda, Mark and Pat start to tinker with SAMM. MARK: Is there anyone here who knows how to use this thing? [referring to SAMM]. Mark and Darron talk about how saving files works.

MARK (does a test file save, enters something, views it): Let's see if it corrects.

Does it. They view.

MARK: No, it adds it. OK, let's do delete option. [He deletes, then enters and views. Looks at large screen] Okay, now the next question is can we just take this away . . . let's go to Utilities.

[ Goes in and out of various menus, reads menu labels aloud] What does initialize system do? [Tries it and reads SAMM screens aloud.]

They are using SAMM features in an aimless way, just to learn about how it works.

Includes: laughing at incorrect or inept uses of SAMM, as well as using SAMM to send comments in order to make others laugh.

Does not include: exploratory uses that are conducted by one person. This is distinguished from individualistic uses in that most, if not all, group members are involved in the fun/exploratory activity or are attentive to it. This is not a case of one individual member using SAMM while others are engaged in another discussion. It is distinguished from social uses in that the activity is not characterized by a sense of group cohesion or shared affection.

## g. CONFUSION

Definition: A period of disorientation in the group when there is no clear focus of attention, with SAMM being used during this disorientation. Such periods are usually characterized by several simultaneous conversations and/or by people asking questions about what the group is doing.

## Example:

Gabbie is talking with Carl about SAMM and typing.

PAM: I'm typing in mine now.

JAN (to Gabbie): Stop messing with our entries.

Pam types for a while. She tells them to hit View.

JAN: Hit F1 to record.

Pam asks why hers aren't up on the screen and Marty and Jan tell her how to View. Carl adjusts large screen projector. Marty reenters his ideas. Pam asks about how to insert.

PAM: Dare not remove that tape or you'll go blind.

Pam asks Jan to enter her ideas. Jan says she's all right.

## Results

System Usage (Inputs to Use): Questions 1–3

THE SYSTEM LOG RECORDS INDICATED THAT 136 SAMM-supported meetings were held during the seven-month period of the current analysis, with an average of 20.6 meetings per month (see figure 2). Of these, approximately 7.8 meetings per month, or 38 percent, were conducted by the ten quality teams involved in our study. An additional 3.4 meetings per month, or 17 percent, were conducted by staff teams that used SAMM for more than one meeting and allowed recording of meeting log files (see Table 2). The remaining 45 percent of meetings during the seven-month period did not record log files. These included demonstrations (approximately 25 percent) and one-time staff or quality-team meetings (approximately 20 percent). Note that the decline in use in months 3 and 4 corresponded to the Thanksgiving and Christmas holiday season, when many employees take vacations and fewer meetings are held.

The summary statistics in Table 2 suggest that the timing of SAMM's introduction was important to the team's likelihood of opting to use the technology during its meetings [2]. Teams who were introduced to SAMM later in their work in the quality process were less likely to use the technology in their meetings and tended to stick with basic level 1 uses. On the other hand, teams who were introduced to SAMM in the early or middle part of their life were much more likely to opt to use it and to use the advanced level 2 structures.

Like newer quality teams, staff teams often chose to use advanced level 2 structures in addition to basic level 1 structures. Although the staff teams typically were not new—in the sense that members had worked together in the past for a long time before using SAMM—their SAMM-supported meetings were almost always accompanied by a special SAMM facilitator. This was not the case with the quality teams. The data seem to suggest that more newly formed teams are more likely to venture into using level 2 GDSS structures, and that in cases where SAMM is used in well-established work groups, special facilitation may be needed to encourage level 2 uses.

## Satisfaction with SAMM (Effects of Use): Questions 4–6

Results from system response questionnaires immediately following SAMM-supported meetings indicate that teams felt quite comfortable and enjoyed using SAMM, and teams were satisfied that SAMM provides the “right support” for their quality meetings. But teams also felt that they needed additional training in how to use SAMM most effectively. Average scores for these three dimensions for quality and staff teams were 5.50, 5.29, and 2.64 (on seven-point scales), respectively.

Observer ratings of the twelve sampled meetings were slightly lower but reflected the same pattern across the three dimensions; means were 4.25, 4.25, and 2.80, respectively. These ratings varied across meetings, and we show three clusters of teams and meetings on the satisfaction dimensions in Table 3. The clusters represent groups of teams and meetings that followed similar patterns on several of the research variables, such as initiation to use SAMM and types of instrumental use (see below). The clusters illustrate the variation in teams' reactions to SAMM use that are not evident by simply observing mean scores. The teams seemed to follow one of three patterns in their use of the SAMM system.

## Initiation of Use: Question 7

Who was most likely to initiate SAMM use successfully in the team? The results of our in-depth analysis of twelve sample meetings indicate that both authority figures (group leaders and outside facilitators) and team members were successful in this regard. In our analysis, the meetings seemed to fall into three clusters, or types: (1) those with a balance between member- and leader/outside facilitator-initiated use; (2) those where a team member was most likely to initiate use; and (3) those where the leader or outside facilitator was most likely to initiate use (see Table 4). Meetings in these clusters correspond to those indicated in Table 3. Most meetings fell into the first cluster. Likelihood to initiate use was not related to the point in the team's life when SAMM was introduced in that there was a mixture of early, middle, and late users of SAMM across the three clusters. In fact, the two meetings that fell into the third cluster, where the leader or outside facilitator initiated all uses, was a team that had been using SAMM since early in its life.

## Instrumental Uses: Questions 8–10

Table 5 summarizes the kinds of ways in which SAMM was used in the twelve sampled meetings. Overall, teams used SAMM primarily for task purposes, first identifying what they wanted to accomplish in the meeting and then turning to the system to aid them. Process uses were next most frequent. That the predominant uses of SAMM were to accomplish tasks and provide the teams with a meeting process is a desirable finding; SAMM was not, in general, used as a toy, as a source of confusion, or as a means for individuals to “tune out” and work on their own. Fun/exploratory and confusion uses were infrequent and concentrated in a few meetings. Power uses were relatively high and fairly evenly distributed across the meetings. On the surface, the high frequency of power uses might be disturbing, in the sense that SAMM is designed to promote democracy and participation in the group, not undue influence of one individual. However, a closer look at these uses of SAMM to influence others indicates that in nearly all cases the power move was positive: SAMM was used by one individual to direct the team to specific task activities (such as in the example given earlier), to get the team back on track, or to move the team beyond discussion and into action. In other words, periods of power were often followed by periods of task uses in the discussion.

## Discussion

OUR ANALYSES SUGGEST THAT, ALONG SEVERAL IMPORTANT DIMENSIONS, the SAMM system has been a success in the IRS quality program. For the seven-month period we examined, voluntary use of the SAMM system was quite high, and, in general, these uses included not only the more basic level 1 structures but also the more advanced level 2 system features. Second, groups most often used the system for task- and process-related activities, as opposed to individualistic, social, or power purposes. Uses of the system for fun/exploratory purposes or within periods of confusion or disorientation did occur, but they seemed to be confined to specific meetings and they did not occur at all in most of the quality-team meetings. Third, initiation to use the SAMM system came largely from group members themselves; most teams did not have to rely on their leader or outside facilitator to suggest possible uses of the technology. Finally, self-reports of satisfaction with the technology were, on average, quite high, and members provided many positive statements about the technology during their meetings.

This represents the “good news,” and on the surface it is quite pleasing. On the other hand, our analyses show that the use patterns in the teams varied quite a bit. There were teams who used the system frequently and at their own initiative, expressing positive sentiments about its use, but there were also teams who used the system infrequently, usually at the suggestion of a leader or facilitator, and who appeared to be low in their comfort and enjoyment of the technology. Consequently, statements such as “This technology will be heavily used,” or “Use of this GDSS leads to high satisfaction,” cannot be uniformly made. Consistent with the prediction of adaptive structuration theory, teams' sentiments and satisfaction with the technology are related to how the GDSS is used (e.g., who initiates system use and what instrumental uses are made of the technology). The kinds of analyses presented in this paper can be used to shed light on the subtle aspects of GDSS use processes. Finally, our data suggest that teams may be more likely to opt to use a GDSS if they start using it early in the quality process, rather than later in their group life.

To understand the constructs and relationships outlined in figure 1 more definitively, a larger sample of teams and meetings, over a longer period of time, should be studied. The analyses presented here are preliminary at best. Also, the data we have presented here are cross-sectional, and longitudinal analyses of individual teams are an important direction for further research. Other directions for further research include detailed study of groups' appropriation moves, perhaps using an interpretive coding scheme [12] and comparisons of SAMM-supported and non-SAMM-use meetings; although we were unable to gather data on control groups (quality teams without access to SAMM) in our study, we can conduct analyses within teams to explore differences between meetings where SAMM is used and where it is not used. In terms of instrumental uses, further analysis might consider the patterns in these uses over time within a given team and how learning affects instrumental uses; for example, early fun/exploratory or confusion uses may eventually lead to task uses in later meetings. Finally, a study of GDSS use would ideally contain some assessment of team performance or productivity. Although we do not have such data at present, this is something we are working on and hope to include in later analyses.

Our results suggest that SAMM is being actively and productively used in the quality teams involved in our study. The findings are particularly encouraging in light of the fact that SAMM is part of a long-range change effort in a rigid, bureaucratic organization. We believe that, like the larger quality method it supports, SAMM can succeed as a technology innovation, given sufficient time, effort, and organizational commitment. Moreover, we believe that instrumental use analysis, which is rooted in adaptive structuration theory, is a useful way of beginning to understand the technology adoption process. In our future research we plan to refine the measurement and analysis procedures presented here and hope to deepen our understanding of quality-team use of GDSS structures.
