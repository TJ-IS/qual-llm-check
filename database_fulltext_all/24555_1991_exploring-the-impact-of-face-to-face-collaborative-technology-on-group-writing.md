# Exploring the Impact of Face-to-Face Collaborative Technology on Group Writing

MARJORIE HORTON, PRISCILLA ROGERS, LAUREL AUSTIN, AND MICHAEL MCCORMICK

ABSTRACT: This exploratory study examines the impact of face-to-face collaborative technology on group writing. We compared small groups writing managerial memoranda in a computer-supported meeting room, and with conventional writing tools. The computer environment, the Capture Lab, allowed us to study how a technology designed for broad functionality and flexibility in group process affects collaborative work. We find that the technology alters the writing process, resulting in less initial group planning, more individual work, and more revising than when conventional tools are used. This affects negotiation and consensus-reaching processes. The technology also reduces speech and group focus. User feedback suggests the technology has both positive and negative effects on group interaction. The technology did not affect overall document quality, although one measure suggests it can enhance writers' audience adaptiveness. Comparisons of how different groups used the technology suggest that these patterns influence document quality. In the conclusion, we discuss what groups need in order to use this kind of collaborative technology effectively and future research directions.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: Capture Lab, collaborative technology, collaborative writing, computer-supported cooperative work, computer-supported meeting room, group work.

DESPITE THE INCREASING NUMBER OF EXPERIMENTAL COMPUTER-SUPPORTED meeting rooms for face-to-face group work, we still have much to learn about the impact of these tools on collaboration. The focus of this study is on collaborative writing. It is a rich research domain because collaborative writing is complex and potentially difficult, requiring group planning, problem solving, negotiation, and decision making. Composition and communication scholars cite a need to study face-to-face collaboration and the impact of technology [13, 22]. Recent empirical surveys of writing in the workplace reveal that collaborative writing occurs quite frequently [7, 15]. This finding is supported by observations of computer-supported meeting room users [17].

Prior research on the writing process, although usually focused on individuals, provides a starting point for the study of collaborative writing. The writing process has been described as consisting of three iterative (rather than sequential) phases: planning, composing, and reviewing/revising $[27, 43]$ . While expert writers plan more than novice writers $[27]$ , the approach to the writing process varies greatly across both experts and novices $[3, 20]$ . Thus, composition scholars are careful not to advocate one “right” way to complete a writing task. For example, Flower et al. concluded, after in-depth analyses of writers’ planning processes, that planning aids should not “model or dictate a prototypical overall path for the planning process....[A] step-by-step planning procedure could have initial value as a teaching tool or for very well-defined, limited tasks....[However] the price of simplicity could be a planning aid that subverts the recursive and opportunistic process we see in experts” [20, p. 49].

These findings and the complex nature of collaborative writing suggest that many writing tasks are best pursued in a “low-structure” setting. Low-structure computer support is designed for broad functionality and is not explicitly designed to structure the processes by which groups work $[2]$ . For example, in the Capture Lab, meeting participants may work with both a shared computer and private workstations, using whatever conventional software they find most suitable for their work $[31]$ . In contrast, “high-structure technology” provides specialized tools to structure particular types of work, constraining how a group completes its work, and, explicitly or implicitly, the group interaction process $[2]$ . Examples include Colab’s Cognoter for planning presentations $[44]$ , the SAMM system for structured decision making $[10]$ , and the GroupSystems tools for brainstorming, voting, issue analysis, etc. $[35]$ . It is possible that when collaborative writing tasks are part of highly structured processes, such as software-requirements definition or strategic planning, more specialized tools like these may be better suited. Other writing tasks for which structured processes either are not available or are not appropriate are better pursued in a low-structure setting. In the current study we examine groups creating managerial memoranda in a low-structure computerized meeting room.

Although collaborative writing in the workplace is prevalent and increasingly common in the classroom as well $[9, 14, 19, 21]$ , it is fraught with coordination and group process difficulties for collaborators $[15, 22, 23]$ . Respondents to surveys on workplace collaborative writing have described it as “a slow, ponderous way” $[15, p. 33]$ to work and as “frustrating” $[1, p. 77]$ , yet also an important vehicle for professional development and intellectual stimulation $[15]$ .

The kinds of collaborative writing studied to date typically involve much asynchronous work $[1, 7, 23, 33, 36]$ , although, increasingly, research evidence suggests benefits of synchronous and face-to-face collaboration for all aspects of the writing process. For example, both case studies of collaborative writing teams in business $[8, 38]$ and more controlled experimental research on collaborative writing technologies (e.g., $[23]$ ) demonstrate the need for face-to-face planning. Experimental research comparing written and spoken media for revising documents also suggests that the richer context provided by face-to-face meetings can improve documents more than the asynchronous, written annotations $[5]$ that coauthors often provide each other. Surveys of collaborative writing projects $[1, 7]$ indicate that coauthors do at times plan and revise their documents in face-to-face meetings, whereas drafting is typically a solitary activity. Coauthors may choose to work alone during the drafting of documents for many reasons, including the size of the documents, the scope of the writers' expertise, logistical constraints, and so on. Yet those who choose to draft together may realize several benefits $[42]$ and produce superior documents, as Fleming's $[19]$ comparison of student writing groups suggests.

Lack of useful tools for composing collaboratively may contribute to the fact that coauthors appear to avoid drafting together. With the emergence of new collaborative technologies, this may change. Early users of Colab's Cognoter, which supported the creation and editing of text in private windows only, reported frustration at their inability to observe the ongoing writing and editing of their collaborators. Stefik et al. concluded that “To an important degree, it seems participants need access not only to the product of each others’ writing, but to the writing process itself” [44, p. 45]. This observation suggests that a shared view of the work in progress is important for maintaining the group focus.

The technology of the Capture Lab offers writers a unique environment for relatively small groups collaborating on all aspects of a shared document, by providing collaborators with a shared public screen for collective work and private workstations for individual work. Among other things, a shared workspace may help collaborators to build a “shared bridge of meaning” [12], as they develop and refine their ideas in public for everyone to view and question [42]. It may also enhance a collaborative writing group’s ability to consider the needs of its audience and to function as the “first-line” audience [1], reading the document as it emerges on the public screen, and revising it accordingly.

This kind of collaborative technology may also reduce some of the group process problems observed in collaborative writing groups. Forman and Katsky [22] note that group members may sometimes assume either too little or too much control and ownership. Ideally, the public workspace provided by the shared computer and screen will provide individual group members with a sense of ownership, and equal access to the shared document will preclude excessive control or ownership by any one member.

But there are potential drawbacks to working with such tools. One concern is information overload, as group members can rapidly generate large volumes of text individually and/or collectively. The private workstations may also fragment a group's work efforts, for example, by allowing individuals to tune out from the group dialog, and to work alone at a time when the group needs to be working together to reach consensus. The computers themselves may also lead a group to avoid some of the necessary, but difficult, upfront planning, by jumping too quickly to the drafting process $[25]$ .

In this preliminary study we explore the potential benefits and disadvantages of an environment like the Capture Lab for collaborative writing. More broadly, the study addresses how small groups use low-structure, face-to-face collaborative technology to accomplish a complex task and how this technology influences the nature and quality of their work. To investigate this question, we completed a comparative analysis of groups writing managerial memoranda with computer technology and with conventional writing tools. We focus on the impact of the computer technology on the writing process, group interaction, and document quality.

## Methodology

## Experimental Design

TO CONTROL FOR POSSIBLE GROUP EFFECTS, we used a within-groups design, comparing two conditions for collaborative writing. All groups produced one document using conventional writing tools (the noncomputer [NC] condition) and one document using the collaborative technology of the Capture Lab (the computer [C] condition). Groups produced both documents in the same meeting room. In the NC condition each group member had a pad of paper, a pencil, and a pen. A flipchart on an easel, markers, and tape were also provided. A clock was mounted on the wall. In the C condition each group member had a private computer (Macintosh II™) consisting of a display monitor, keyboard, and mouse. This equipment was positioned around a large rectangular conference table. (The groups used the Capture Lab technology moved to a conventional meeting room, since the Capture Lab was under reconstruction. The monitors were not embedded into the conference table as they are in the Capture Lab.) Group members also shared a public Macintosh II. Its output was projected on a large monitor at the front of the room. All of the central processing units were stored remotely. In addition, groups had access to a laser printer in the room.

All group members had equal access to the public computer. To access the shared computer, a user pressed a special key on his or her keyboard. Pressing this key a second time returned the user to his or her individual computer. Only one user could control the public computer at any one time. If one user had control of the public computer and another user pressed the access key, the first user was reconnected to his or her personal computer and the second user gained control of the public computer. A clipboard transfer function allowed users to move sections of a document between their individual computers and the public computer using the Macintosh cut and paste functions. All computers displayed a small clock in the menu bar. MS Word $^{™}$ was open on the public and private computers at the start of the session. No traditional writing tools such as pens or paper were available. For this initial investigation, we intentionally kept the two conditions distinct to allow an explicit comparison of collaborative writing with each set of tools, although we acknowledge that this design constrained subjects from choosing which tools to use.

## Subjects

Five groups of advanced business and liberal arts undergraduates participated in the study. Three of these groups participated as a course requirement for a business writing course. Due to unexpected low enrollment in the course, students from another business communications course were recruited and paid for their participation. Three groups included four members, one included five, and one included three.

All of the groups had some prior experience working as a group. The extent of this experience ranged widely, from a few class assignments for four of the groups, to several lengthy class projects over the course of one year for the most established group. Four of the five groups could be considered relatively immature, in terms of group members' knowledge about the abilities and values of the other members [28].

Prior to their participation in the first session, subjects completed a brief profile that included questions about their proficiency with computers, typing, and word-processing software, and their attitudes toward writing. Analysis of these profiles revealed the groups were comparable in terms of proficiency, but varied somewhat in their attitudes toward writing.

## Writing Tasks

We used two comparable writing tasks, and to the extent possible with five groups, we counterbalanced order of task, order of condition, and type of group (paid versus course requirement). Each task was a short case that involved groups in a politically sensitive business situation that compelled them to write a persuasive response in memorandum form. The tasks were developed by management communication experts to model real business situations that would warrant a group writing effort. Each was constructed to contain roughly the same amount of information on the group's role, the motivation for the memorandum, and information about who would receive it. (In pilot testing, groups performed similarly on the two tasks, in terms of time spent on each and assessed document quality.)

For one task, group members were assigned the role of a sales management team, writing to an executive committee to argue against proposed changes in a vital sales training program. For the other task, group members were assigned the role of regional directors, writing to store managers to dispel rumors about changes in hiring practices. Groups were given up to two hours to complete each task and were instructed to leave all materials they produced. (The groups that were paid for their participation were told they would be paid for the entire session even if they finished early.)

## Training

Observational and experimental research on the Capture Lab has shown that lack of expertise with this technology can be an impediment for users $[2, 16]$ . To minimize such effects, all groups participated in an hour-long training session on the Macintosh and MS Word. For many subjects, this training was a review. All subjects were computer literate before beginning this project. $^{1}$

Training and practice specific to the collaborative technology were provided at the start of each group's technology session. The training included general comments about possible ways groups could use the public and private computers (e.g., using multiple windows, taking notes or developing ideas on one's private machine). For purposes of control, in the NC condition the trainer also commented about ways groups could use the conventional tools (e.g., taping flipchart pages on the wall, taking notes or developing ideas on individual notepads). No other explicit training or practice at the start of each group's NC session was considered necessary.

Subjects were clearly at different points on the learning curve for the C versus NC conditions, since they all had years of experience writing with conventional tools and no prior experience using this kind of groupware. Not providing more extensive training allowed us to explore the creative strategies and patterns subjects developed on their own. However, any conclusions about the effectiveness of the technology should be considered tentative in light of the subjects' inexperience.

## Data Collection

A video camera positioned in one corner of the room recorded the sessions and transmitted a video image of the session to a remote observation station. In the C condition, the output of the public screen was displayed on a computer at the observation station via Timbuktu™ software. In addition, all of the subjects' screen activities on both the public and private computers were recorded with Screen-Recorder™, a program that creates a software "tape" of computer screen activity that can be played back at a later time.

## Logging of Group Activities

Overall, there were four observers during the course of this study. During a particular session, at least two observers alternated logging the group's activities for half-hour periods, using a custom-built software logging tool.

Logging involved watching the group from the observation station and recording specific collaborative activities. Every thirty seconds a tone would sound and the observer would log any of these activities occurring during that interval by selecting the appropriate code buttons. The logging activities that we report on here were organized into two categories—group interaction (speech and group focus) and tool use (private versus public tool use). Additional comments or annotations could be recorded in a comment field. The logging categories were refined over several pilot sessions. We have noted consistency between the results from analyses of the logging data and our findings from the videotape scenarios described below. $^{2}$

Revisions to the group document were subsequently logged after the experimental sessions by consulting the session videotapes, ScreenRecorder files of public screen activity, and each group's final documents and members' working notes.

## Questionnaires

A postsession questionnaire using both open-ended and multiple-choice questions on a five-point Likert scale focused on the subjects' evaluation of the writing tools, group dynamics, collaborative writing process, and final product. $^{3}$

## Videotape Analyses

The videotapes from all of the experimental sessions were reviewed independently by three researchers, who individually wrote scenarios describing each group's writing process, patterns of tool use, and group dynamics. Comparisons of these scenarios indicate a high degree of consistency between the researchers, which was confirmed by extensive follow-up discussions. $^{4}$

These scenarios, in conjunction with more fine-grained analyses of the videotapes, the ScreenRecorder files of computer activity, and each group's final documents and working notes, were used to characterize the nature of the groups' writing processes.

## Document Assessment

Writing consultants from the Michigan Business School Managerial Writing Program, all experienced evaluators, used holistic and analytic measures to evaluate the quality of the final documents. Each document was scored independently by two different evaluators. When their ratings differed, a third evaluator independently scored the documents, unaware of the previous ratings. To ensure that these evaluators were blind to condition, all the memoranda were retyped in the same font.

These measures were developed to evaluate managerial documents. Holistic measures included a general seven-point holistic assessment and a within-group comparative evaluation $[24]$ .⁵ The analytic measures $[41]$ include the Quality of Reasoning Measure, the Persuasive Appeals Measure, and the Persuasive Adaptiveness Measure, which are based on evaluation schemes developed and empirically tested by Connor $[6]$ , and the Tone Measure, which derives from Perlman $[39]$ .⁶

## Findings

IN OUR EFFORTS TO UNDERSTAND HOW GROUPS can use a low-structure computer-supported meeting environment for collaborative work, we have collected a wide variety of data. In this paper we present the subset of findings we believe tells the most informative story about the impact of this technology on collaborative writing. Our findings suggest that the technology significantly alters the group writing process and, more generally, that it affects the nature of group interaction. However, it may only slightly change the quality of a group's final product. We believe these preliminary findings have implications for how groups work on many different problem-solving tasks using face-to-face collaborative technology.

## Collaborative Technology Alters the Writing Process

Our most significant discovery may be that groups used different patterned processes to complete the writing tasks in the two conditions. All of the groups planned, produced, and revised to some degree in both conditions. However, the amount of time spent on these activities, the amount of group versus individual work, and the patterns of tool use varied dramatically by condition. Below we examine groups' planning, producing, and revising, in turn. However, we should stress that these are often iterative processes. (In discussing data for individual groups, we will refer to Groups 1–5 as NC1–NC5 and C1–C5 to relate the group IDs to condition.)

## Patterns of Initial Planning

As anticipated, we found that groups typically planned throughout the writing process. We will focus here, however, on initial planning, planning prior to text generation, for two reasons. First, small-group researchers have found a positive relationship between planning task strategy (planning what the group is going to do) and task performance [26].

A reasonable extrapolation is that writing planning (planning what the group is going to convey through writing) is also positively related to writing task performance. This assumption is supported by Carey et al. [4], who found that the quality of individuals' initial planning was related to writing effectiveness. Second, both the extent and the nature of initial planning varied considerably between conditions. (For our purposes, initial planning pertains to the writing situation and the written document. Because the focus of this study is on the task of writing, we chose not to include planning task strategy, for example, whether to divide up the work, what writing tools to use, etc.)

Initial planning was defined as verbal or written planning prior to the onset of writing memorandum text. Group planning involved group members working together to plan their document, whereas individual planning involved group members working alone.

We found that the NC condition prompted initial group planning. In contrast, in the C condition, group members typically planned individually, if at all, and often without subsequently sharing their plans. Table 1 summarizes these results.

In the NC condition, all groups engaged in initial planning as a group, for an average of 22.8 minutes. Groups generated brainstorming lists and outlines. Three groups used the public flipchart extensively; in another group, members took private notes. Only one group failed to write down any of its group planning work. We saw no individual initial planning. By contrast, in the C condition, most groups usually began producing text with little or no initial group planning; across the five groups, the average time spent was 7.8 minutes. This difference in amount of initial group planning across conditions was marginally significant (paired t = 3.6, p = 0.06, two-tailed). As Table 1 shows, in the C condition one group spent no time, while two other groups only planned as a group for up to four minutes. One of these groups (C2) spent just one minute and only discussed the memo header. Another group (C4) spent a total of 11 minutes planning as a group, but this time was interspersed with periods of individual planning in silence. Four of the five groups did not use the public computer to record any of their group planning. The fifth group (C5) was a clear exception to this general pattern. Members of C5 spent 23 minutes on initial group planning, but only after a considerable period of individual planning.

In the C condition, members from three groups spent time on initial planning as individuals, using their private computers to generate brainstorming lists and outlines. Across the five groups, the average time spent on initial planning as individuals was 5.0 minutes. However, only one group (C5) spent time explicitly reviewing and merging the work from its period of individual planning.

Questionnaire data indicate that subjects were discontented both with the quality of the planning and the functionality of the writing tools for planning in the C condition. Subjects rated the quality of their planning in the C condition lower than in the NC condition. When asked to evaluate aspects of their groups' writing process, individual subjects rated planning as only "poor" to "good" after the C session (mean = 2.90), but as "good" to "very good" after the NC session (mean = 3.35), as can be seen in Table 2. $^{7}$

Moreover, after participating in both conditions, subjects were asked which set of writing tools (public and private computers or the conventional tools—flipchart, notepads, pens) they would prefer to use for planning group documents. A surprising 75 percent selected the conventional writing tools. This may be due in part to the fact that when our subjects write individual papers on computers, they typically plan on paper before writing, according to their self-reports. Another reason may be that computers constrain the nature of plans and planning notes to text and very simple graphics for all but the most sophisticated users. In reviewing some of the research on writing with computers, Neuwirth concludes that writers need environments that “support the creation of arrows, boxes or other diagrams for displaying conceptual relationships among ideas and the suppression of detail” [33, p. 186].

We do not argue that less group planning is detrimental to a group's ability to write collaboratively. In fact, research on brainstorming has shown that individual work can be more effective and result in higher-quality results than group work [11, 45]. However, we hypothesize that some of the difficulties groups had producing a draft in the C condition stemmed from their lack of group planning and consensus-reaching early in the writing process. For example, Group 3 was unable to agree on an approach to writing the memo in the C condition. They continually tried and abandoned different approaches without achieving consensus on any one approach. By the end of their two-hour session, they had only managed to produce a brief collection of sentences and sentence fragments.

## Patterns of Producing

We also find quite different patterns of group versus individual work in how groups produce their memoranda. In the NC condition, four of the five groups composed primarily as a group. Typically one or two members of the group served as the scribe, recording text on the “group notepad” as it was generated verbally by the group. A group notepad is any notepad used for recording text for the group memorandum. (One of these groups used the flipchart as the “group notepad” for much of their session.) Across groups, individual efforts were relatively minor in the NC condition, consisting of ideas occasionally jotted down for future reference.

We saw only two salient examples of extended individual effort in the NC condition. In one case (NC2), one group member individually edited the final group document. In another case (NC5), group members explicitly divided the task and assigned sections for teams (pairs of students) to produce. (This group used the computer technology for their first session, and they used the same basic task approach of dividing the work in their second, NC session.) Neither of these instances suggests a pattern of individual producing of the kind we observed in the C condition.

In the C condition we see a distinct tendency for groups to produce individually for extensive periods of time. All groups engaged in sporadic periods of individual work throughout the writing process. In four of the five groups, subjects drafted alone for significant periods, occasionally conferring with other group members. Three of the groups (C1, C2, and C5) made an explicit decision to work individually, either dividing the sections of the memorandum or each writing complete memoranda for later comparison and integration. For two of these groups, the decision to work individually came after they had difficulty composing as a group. For another group (C3), no systematic plan of attack emerged and members worked more haphazardly, at times on their private computers and at other times sharing the public computer. Only one group (C4) came close to producing as a group. This group tried to generate text together, but one member's ongoing efforts at revising a copy of the group's draft on his private computer fragmented the process.

The logging data for private versus public tool use confirm these findings from the video analysis. The total amount of sustained individual production was determined for each group. A period of “sustained” individual production was defined as four or more minutes of consecutive private tool use, allowing for occasional brief pauses, after the period of initial planning ended. This analysis did not consider private tool use by teams of group members as individual production. Using this measure, C groups spent an average of 41.2 minutes on individual production, compared to only 6.9 minutes for the NC groups. These results are summarized in Table 3.

In comparison with other emerging technologies for collaborative writing, such as group editors [37], the Capture Lab may encourage more individual effort. Only one user at a time can edit a given version of a document. In contrast, group editors allow users concurrent access to the same copy of a document, which may focus a group's attention and effort more on the same aspect of the task.

## Patterns of Revising

The patterns and extent of revision differed across the two conditions, reflecting both the different styles of production and the relative ease of revising in each condition. In the NC condition, group members often discussed the wording of their memorandum extensively prior to writing it down. That is, wordsmithing was very often verbal, preceding the actual production of text. This particular pattern of group negotiation and producing, compared to the frequent individual producing in the C condition, may be one reason why we see less frequent revisions in the NC condition. The logging data in Table 4 show that groups engaged in more revision in the C condition (29.5 percent of the logging intervals) than in the NC condition (11.8 percent). (Revising was defined as handwriting, typing, pasting, erasing, or deleting to change existing text in the group memorandum.) In both conditions, the vast majority of revisions were at the word or sentence level. However, in the C condition, writers also moved large pieces of text more frequently.

The video scenarios suggest that group members were more focused and attentive in the NC condition as revisions were being made to the group document than they were in the C condition. The fact that in the C condition everyone could see the public document and everyone had easy access to it may have made individuals feel more comfortable making changes without explicitly consulting their colleagues or getting consensus. In the questionnaires, 90 percent of the subjects reported a clear preference for the computer writing tools for revising group documents. This is to be expected, considering the relative ease of revising with even the simplest word-processing tool. The erasing and copying over one experiences with traditional writing tools is considerably more difficult by comparison.

## No Time Savings with Computers

Using the computer(s) to generate their memoranda did not save the groups time overall. The average times to complete the tasks were 1 hour, 55 minutes for the C condition, compared to 1 hour, 43 minutes for the NC condition. While the amount of time spent completing the task did not vary significantly with condition, the ways groups spent their time did differ, as we have seen. Given the small sample size and the single session our subjects had working with the Capture Lab technology, further research is needed to determine the impact of such tools on a group's efficiency.

## Summary of Findings on the Writing Process

Our analysis suggests that groups generally employ different collaborative writing processes when using conventional tools versus collaborative computer technology. Groups engaged in less initial group planning in the C condition and often began producing texts almost immediately. Our video scenarios suggest that this lack of initial group planning delayed group negotiation and consensus-reaching in the C condition. Often groups did not discuss key questions about the nature of the writing situation or reach consensus about appropriate communication strategies until after individual texts had been produced. This made negotiation and consensus-reaching more difficult. In the NC condition there was almost always a great deal of negotiating prior to anyone producing much actual text, as well as during periods of group producing. We also found that the C condition promoted more revising. Taken together, these findings indicate that technology significantly alters the collaborative writing process. The differences in the writing process between conditions are illustrated in figure 1.

## Collaborative Technology Alters Group Interaction

We also find that the computers have a considerable impact on the nature of group interactions. This is consistent with findings from previous studies on computer-supported meeting environments and computer-mediated communication, which have shown that this technology reduces interpersonal communication and group interaction $[29, 34, 40]$ .

## Less Speech and Group Focus with Computers

Groups engaged in speech significantly less often in the C condition than in the NC condition. (Speech was logged if at least one person spoke during the 30-second interval.) As Table 5 illustrates, in the C condition, speech was logged 86.2 percent of the time, whereas in the NC condition speech was logged 97.7 percent of the time.

The amount of speech in the C condition is quite high, especially given the extent of private computer use, individual work, and the placement of the display monitors on the conference table, partially interfering with group members' views of each other.

Across all five groups, the extent to which groups achieved group focus did not differ significantly between conditions. (Group focus was logged if all group members were attending to the same aspect of the same task, not working independently, for the majority of the 30-second interval.) In the NC condition, groups were logged as focused 67.3 percent of the time, and in the C condition, 52.8 percent of the time. However, analyzing the data excluding Group 5 (the group that worked almost exclusively in small teams and individuals in the NC condition), we find that group focus is much more frequent in the NC condition (79.4 percent) than in the C condition (49.4 percent) (see Table 6). These data on group focus do not necessarily mean less task focus, but they do suggest that the computer technology provided a more distributed work approach for the group task.

## Contrasting Communication Effects

Informal feedback from users suggests that, despite the logging data results showing less speech and less group focus in the C condition, the collaborative technology can help group members to communicate their ideas and work together more easily. In response to questionnaire items about how their groups worked together and the effects of the writing tools on one's ability to communicate one's ideas, members from four of the five groups commented that the computers helped individuals to express ideas, to view ideas, and to have a complete exchange of individual work that would not be practical without the technology. They also reported that the computers provided a greater equality of communication among members.

A counterexample to this feedback was Group C4. One member began privately revising a draft of the group's memorandum which he had transferred to his private computer. For much of the session he worked alone without telling his colleagues what he was doing. Consequently, toward the end of the session, the group realized it had generated two versions of the memorandum, one developed collaboratively by three group members and another developed individually by one group member. This situation placed the group in conflict over which document to use. This conflict was eventually resolved by integrating sections of each document.

Comments from members of Group C4 convey this conflict and the group's difficulties collaborating. The member who worked individually admitted in his questionnaire to “removing” himself from the group. Another member complained about this, “I . . . didn’t like that Don didn’t contribute when we were initially writing together . . . [he] only contributed at the end . . . I didn’t like that at all.” As the group worked to integrate the two texts, their conversation reflected this conflict with frequent references to text as “mine,” “ours,” “yours,” and the like. Two members also complained explicitly that the C condition impaired communication.

## Summary of Findings on Group Interaction

The writing process and collaborative patterns that typified the groups in the C condition suggest that the computer technology may both hinder and enhance group interaction. It prompts group members to begin working individually almost immediately, often without sufficient group planning, coordination, and consensus. It also reduces the amount of speech and group focus. However, the individual work prompted by the technology may be important in allowing group members to develop their ideas without premature pressures for consensus. As Finholt, Sproull, and Kiesler $[18]$ noted, when there is pressure for consensus among group members, the effectiveness of individual members who may have particular expertise can be reduced.

In addition, the ability to transfer work quickly and easily between the public and private computers can reduce the coordination and transaction costs $[30]$ inherent in group work. The informal feedback from users supports the notion that computer capabilities provide additional channels for distributing information among group members, for merging the efforts of individuals, and for improving and equalizing communication. Occasionally, however, we did witness some confusion about who was currently in control of the public computer, especially when someone pressed the access key without any kind of verbal signal to the group.

## Collaborative Technology Does Not Alter Product Quality

Neither holistic measure revealed significant differences in document quality between conditions. With one exception, the documents scored in the mid-range on the seven-point holistic rating scale (1 being the lowest quality, 7 the highest), as seen in

Table 7. Mean ratings were 4.6 for the C condition and 5.0 for the NC condition. Two groups (C1 and C2) received slightly higher ratings on the documents they composed in the C condition, one group (C5) received equivalent ratings in both conditions, and one group (C4) received a slightly lower rating on the document they composed in the C condition. The exceptional group (C3) received a much lower rating on the document they composed in the C condition, perhaps in part because they did not finish the document in the allotted time. The comparative holistic measure did not reveal any overall effects of condition on document quality.

Only one of the analytic measures, the Persuasive Adaptiveness measure, suggests a difference in the documents produced in the two conditions, as shown in Table 8. This measure is a five-level scale for scoring the linguistic and rhetorical features of a document that address reader concerns. A well-adapted or highly “reader-focused” document receives a score of five, whereas a document without any reader adaptation receives a score of one. Four of the five group documents composed in the C condition received an adaptiveness score one level higher than those group documents composed in the NC condition. Documents from the fifth group were scored the same in both conditions. These data are summarized in Table 8.

This finding that four of the groups scored higher on adaptiveness in the C condition suggests that the collaborative technology may facilitate reader focus. Perhaps the ability for group members to review their document simultaneously on the public screen, as well as to review printouts of it individually, enabled them to see the document as their reader(s) would see it.

We analyzed the groups' dialogs during the writing process to explore the nature of their reader analysis in more depth. All verbal exchanges that explicitly referred to the reader were excerpted from the videotapes. These exchanges were units of dialog involving the initiation of a topic explicitly pertaining to the reader, such as who the reader was, the readers' beliefs and expectations, and so on. They ended when the topic shifted away from the reader. The results of this analysis surprisingly conflict with the Persuasive Adaptiveness measure, in that we found more reader-related exchanges in the NC condition. Number of exchanges in the C and NC conditions were compared using an Analysis of Covariance, with the total number of utterances, defined as conversational turns, in a session entered as the covariate. $^{8}$ Mean scores across all groups, adjusted for the total number of utterances in a session, were 7.3 for the C condition and 16.3 for the NC condition. Although this difference is not significant, given the small number of groups, it is suggestive.

One speculative interpretation of this discrepancy in results is that in the C condition especially, explicit discussion about the reader may not be essential for the creation of a reader-focused text. Working on either their private computers or on the public computer, group members see their document in a more “finished” form as it evolves. That is, they see it as their reader will see it. The computer technology forces writers into the role of readers—the source of the message also becomes the receiver of the message—in a way that is not possible in an NC environment, where the group’s emerging product, handwritten, does not share the same physical resemblance to a final memorandum form. It is also possible that the greater ease of revision with computers enables writers to tailor their text more toward their reader, again with the advantage of seeing the text as their reader would, as it approaches completion.

## Tool Preferences Parallel Performance

Subjects' tool preferences are consistent with the groups' patterns of tool use. We compared the postsession questionnaire responses of subjects from groups producing better memos with the computer technology with the responses of the subjects from groups producing better documents with the flipcharts, notepads, and pens. When asked which set of tools they preferred for composing, 86 percent of the subjects from the groups producing better memos in the C condition selected the computer tools and only 14 percent selected the conventional tools. In contrast, subjects from the groups producing better memos in the NC condition showed some preference for the conventional tools: 62 percent selected the conventional tools and only 25 percent selected the computer tools. (Thirteen percent reported no preference.) These results are shown in Table 9.

The extent to which this preference for the conventional tools would diminish as groups worked with the computer technology over time is an important question, especially with respect to training and experience, as we discuss below. Our subjects were computer literate at the outset of the study, but did vary somewhat in their tendency to draft documents with a computer versus with paper and pencil, according to their self-reports.

Summary of Findings on the Written Product

In general, this collaborative technology did not yield a detectable difference in the quality of the written product. With the exception of the Persuasive Adaptiveness results, neither the holistic nor the analytic measures revealed significant differences between conditions. In light of the quite small sample size, further research is needed before we can conclude that using such technology cannot improve product quality.

## Conclusions

## Suggestions for Using Collaborative Computer Technology

SUBJECTS' COMMENTS DURING THE EXPERIMENTAL SESSIONS indicate that they expected to find the group writing easier in the C condition and that they were surprised at some of the difficulties they encountered coordinating their use of the technology. Subjects also reported that the writing tools in the C condition diverted attention from the group task. Members of several groups also commented that they had difficulty with information overload—that is, with too many ideas “thrown up on the screen” from the individual computers. Others felt that the computers provided too much freedom to edit and revise.

Although the individual computers seem to beckon users to do private work, extended individual work without group negotiation and consensus-reaching can be premature and costly for groups. Difficulties with information overload, duplication of effort, ownership conflicts, and lack of consensus are some of the problems that we observed. We have also seen how groups using the technology generate much work privately, but then fail to share it among group members or delete it prematurely before everyone has a chance to review it. These difficulties may stem largely from the groups' inexperience working with the technology and, for four of the five groups, from their immaturity as groups.

Several suggestions emerge from this exploratory study for how groups can avoid these difficulties and work effectively using computer support for face-to-face meetings. Following these suggestions should reduce some of the group process problems $[22]$ that often arise in collaborative projects. We also believe these suggestions are applicable to many kinds of small-group tasks.

Groups need to engage in initial planning as a group, regardless of whether they also pursue individual planning. Based on this study, adding structure that encourages initial group planning when completing a collaborative writing task may be warranted. We might also educate groups about the need for initial group planning. Another alternative is to provide brainstorming or outlining software for initial planning, incorporating techniques that can accommodate individual and/or group planning efforts $[32]$ . Providing conventional planning tools such as flipcharts may also promote planning.

Group members also need to make systematic decisions about when to work as a group and when to pursue individual work, and to communicate openly about the nature of their individual work. When groups do engage in significant individual work, they should develop a plan for systematically reviewing and integrating this work, to increase participation and buy-in among group members, as well as to minimize members' wasted efforts.

For groups who need or want to work face-to-face, dividing the task and working individually some of the time may yield better-quality results than working exclusively as a group, as our comparison of groups' work patterns suggests. Groups who adopt this strategy may be more satisfied with the technology as well.

## Directions and Questions for Future Research

Although our findings are based on a small sample using one particular collaborative technology, they strongly point to the need for research that addresses the unique problems groups encounter when seeking to collaborate effectively with new technology. What kinds of benefits could groups achieve, working in a computer-supported meeting environment, given training that incorporates the suggestions we offer here? How much direction or facilitation do groups need in order to work effectively with this kind of technology? These remain important issues for further study, and the findings may very well depend on the particular constellation of task, technology, group size, group history, and other factors involved.

Experience working with the technology over time will likely be even more critical than training. As we have noted, our subjects were at a disadvantage in the C condition, in terms of their lack of prior experience working with these new group tools. Groups do not initially understand how the technology can support their interaction. For example, our subjects did not know how the public screen can be a focusing tool. Instead, the public screen became a diversion at times as users switched haphazardly between public and private work, without adequate communication.

Additional experience will introduce users to the range of possibilities for collaborative work that do not exist with conventional tools. It will take time in such a new environment for a group to find its rhythm, to develop working patterns to take advantage of the tools, and to have the tool use become second nature.

Longitudinal research focusing on how groups use computer-supported meeting rooms over an extended period of time is most critical in identifying the real benefits (and costs) of this technology for collaboration. An important component of this continuing research will be to provide users with a toolkit of innovative software for both synchronous and asynchronous collaboration. Studying groups in field settings and providing conventional tools as well should make the research more realistic and avoid some of the limitations of the laboratory setting. Discovering how and when groups choose to work together versus apart, and what tools they choose to use to accomplish their work as the groups mature and become more experienced with the technology will deepen our understanding of collaboration and hopefully provide new insights about what new technologies we should develop.
