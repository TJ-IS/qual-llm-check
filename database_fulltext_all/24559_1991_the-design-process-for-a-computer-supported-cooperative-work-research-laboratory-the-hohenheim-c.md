ABSTRACT: The design of an environment that supports computer-aided meetings of teams requires multidisciplinary cooperation between specialists in the field of computer-supported cooperative work (CSCW) and conference room and furniture designers in order to master all requirements of such an environment. With this in mind, this paper describes the design process and the experiences gained while designing the Hohenheim Computer Aided Team (CATeam) Room. This room offers a computer-supported meeting environment. In an iterative process of defining the CATeam Room requirements and developing design alternatives, a round room and conference table design were found to be most useful for the given requirements.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: architecture, CATeam room, computer aided team, computer-supported cooperative work, conference rooms, conference tables, design, electronic meeting systems, group decision support systems,

## 1. Introduction

THE PROCESS, ISSUES, AND PROBLEMS OF DESIGNING A MEETING ROOM for computer aided teamwork are of major interest to CSCW researchers and practitioners who plan to build such a state-of-the-art meeting room, since the physical environment is important to group work [22, 25]. Organizations are increasingly concerned about their physical appearance, including their offices and meeting rooms [2]. Wineman [31] surveyed existing literature on architecturally related behavioral aspects of work settings and delineates (1) physical comfort and task instrumentality, (2) privacy and social interaction, and (3) symbolic identification as three important areas of present research and interest. Steele [28] pointed out that the experience of physical comfort is associated with physical as well as social and personal factors. The composition of an environment can balance the amount of privacy and social interaction, especially the amount of contacts among teams and the quality of communication during the contacts, as Fuchs [9], Stone [29] and Allen and Gerstberger [1] have illustrated. According to Sundstrom and Altman [30], even the character and mission of teams can be influenced by the environment. The contributions of Doxtater [7], Sommer [26, 27], Hatch [12], and Hall [11] provide insights in the spatial workplace structure and the personal space of the people therein.

This paper describes the experiences in designing such a facility, named the CATeam Room, as a cooperative design process at the University of Hohenheim in Germany $[8]$ . This study involved information systems (IS) researchers at the University of Hohenheim and interior and furniture design architects of the State Academy of the Arts in Stuttgart. These two parties cooperated to develop the CATeam Room in a way that enhances group work. Where needed, specialists in psychology and ergonomics were consulted concerning issues in teamwork, the relationship between technology and humans, and other factors relevant to the room design.

## 2. The CATeam Project

CATEAM IS VIEWED AS A COLLECTION OF CONCEPTS and computer-supported tools to improve the productivity of group work through the use of information and communication technology [15]. The Hohenheim CATeam Room is intended to provide a laboratory to perform research on CATeam tools. The CATeam tools aim to support the teamwork process as a whole. That process includes meeting phases as well as individual work phases, often performed in offices at remote locations, and the transition between these phases. While the CATeam Room itself is predestined for research on meeting support, the complete CATeam environment with networked remote offices allows for research on all phases of teamwork. Within that research, the room will be used to help answer the following research questions:

1. How can work in teams be supported, coordinated, and improved by the use of information and communication technology (ICT)? How can these changes, especially productivity gains, be measured and evaluated?

2. Which changes in (classic) meetings are possible through ICT use? Which changes in (classic) meetings are necessary for ICT use? Which changes in (classic) meetings result from ICT use?

3. How do certain tools work and are they acceptable to the users?

4. To what extent can results of empirical studies performed in the United States be applied to Europe, especially Germany? Do studies need to be replicated because of cultural differences?

We expect to develop an understanding of group work and how it can be supported through the use of information and communication technology, to provide new tools to knowledge workers, and to evaluate these tools. Similar purposes are covered by the target areas of prevalent CSCW research reviewed by Krcmar [16]. The character of the human-computer interface of an environment that includes computer technology for meeting support is influenced by what people perceive in this room. Therefore, special attention is paid to the environment during the design of the room if the technology interface is not supposed to be an impediment to group work but rather an enabling environment to empower people to do better teamwork [17].

## 3. The CATeam Room Design Process

ROOM DESIGN IS A CREATIVE PROCESS. Lorenz [18] distinguishes development from scratch, enlargement, and remodeling of existing rooms as three different design contexts that offer varying degrees of freedom for a designer. While development from scratch allows designers to follow a sequential process of creating designs according to human, organizational, and technological needs, the two corrective approaches of enlargement and remodeling usually require the designer to regard given constraints. Therefore, activities of a design process include requirements and constraints elicitation, alternative design creation, design evaluation and ranking, and visualization and testing of favored

Steps of the CATeam design process conducted thus far have been:

\- Determine the general CATeam Room and the conference table requirements.

\- Survey the facility layout.

\- Determine choices in the arrangement of public screens, in the seating layout, and in the conference table design.

\- Generate different design alternatives and evaluate them in discussions.

\- Visualize and test the favored alternative with a mock-up.

\- Finally, produce work drawings for the conference table.

At the beginning of the cooperative CATeam Room design, the IS researchers and the architects shared their experiences from preparatory work that included visits to group decision support systems (GDSS) research laboratories in the United States in 1988. These included the Colab at Xerox PARC, the Decision Laboratory at Claremont College, the MISRC Lab at the University of Minnesota, the Capture Lab at CMI, Ann Arbor, and the Plexsys Decision Lab at the University of Arizona. In documenting these visits, Krcmar [14] concluded that elements of spatial design may influence research results, and therefore that the joint development of the meeting facilities with a research agenda as a background was essential. Visits to conventional conference rooms of major companies in the Stuttgart area (e.g., Bosch, Landesgirokasse [a bank], and Standard Electric Lorenz [SEL, a division of AL-CATEL]), and a review of the literature and videos about GDSS laboratories completed the preparatory work [13, 15]. Mack [20] and Olson et al. [23] also provide an overview of existing GDSS facilities.

## 3.1. Requirements

## 3.1.1. CATeam Room Requirements

The CATeam Room design can affect the process and outcome of group meetings. It is likely that several environment designs may be equally appropriate, but the design must be compatible with requirements for its use. The interior design details affect the performance of teams using a meeting room supported by information technology. Thus, room design has to be considered as a controlled variable for later research into meetings and their dynamics $[4, 6, 15, 21, 22, 23]$ .

Design choices for the room were limited by:

1. the requirements of the IS researchers concerning:

—the basic assumptions of the research study,

—the desired scope of computer support for teamwork,

—the physical characteristics of the room and its equipment,

—budgetary constraints; and

2. the desire of the design architects to create a unique, easy-to-use room, with a clear concept incorporating the IS researchers' requirements.

According to Lorenz [18], basic principles that guide the workspace design process are functionality and attractiveness. Functionality is specified as flexible adaptation to accomplishing different tasks to satisfy different needs. In particular, the following functionality and attractiveness requirements of the design architects and IS researchers, derived from the results of the preparatory work and literature study, as well as the research assumptions as documented by Krcmar [15], guided the design process:

1. The room must provide an observation and a meeting facility.

2. The observation facility must be accessible and functional and when not in use must not disturb meetings in progress. It can also be used to store equipment not necessary inside the meeting room.

3. The observation facility must allow complete and unbiased capture of the meeting processes through one-way mirrors.

4. The meeting room should accommodate a maximum of ten to twelve participants.

5. The meeting room must be functional for computer-supported teamwork as well as for conventional meetings. Therefore, it should be possible to conceal the technology in the room when the devices are not in use.

6. Each meeting participant should have immediate access to a personal computer. All personal computers are linked by a local area network providing communication capabilities and a video network, managing access to large public screen displays. Special Groupware and off-the-shelf applications will be made available.

7. Use of the personal computer should be optional and the decision of which technology to use in a meeting should be left to each individual participant or to the group, with easy transition possibility.

8. The monitor of the personal computer should not obstruct view and communication. Also, the personal screen should be occluded from “peeping” by other participants, thus providing privacy.

9. Various conference situations (democratic, hierarchical), forms of teamwork (debate, presentation, or parliamentary form of teamwork), and team sizes should be supported by the flexibility of the room and conference table.

10. Large public screens can serve as a focal point capturing the attention of the teams.

11. Modern conference room equipment and technology such as an electronic writing board, a document video camera, regular writing boards, flipcharts, a scanner, and the like, need to be integrated. Teams should be offered a wide range of technology support choices that enhance the flexibility of media usage in the CATeam Room.

12. The meeting room and its conference table should be integrated into a homogeneous design.

13. The concept of CATeam should be evident in the room's design.

14. The meeting room should be as spacious as possible.

15. Technical devices should be concealed, but not to the extent that a cozy atmosphere or boring neutrality is created.

The intention was to create a friendly atmosphere for teamwork in the meeting room by its design, offering a smooth transition from non-information-technology-aided to information-technology-aided teamwork, thus allowing an evolutionary approach of improving groupwork. Social interaction and communication among meeting participants should be maintained or even invigorated. The target user population of the CATeam Room is natural teams with middle- and upper-management members of business organizations and university-affiliated groups (e.g., students). In general, natural groups should be used for tool evaluation and student groups for feature evaluation [15]. Interestingly, many of the requirements overlap with the primary design considerations listed in Olson et al. [23]. The CATeam room is also intended as a showcase for advancing the ideas of meeting support.

## 3.1.2. Conference Table Requirements

The conference table should be integrated into the CATeam room's design. Each of the maximal twelve workspaces of the table has to accommodate a desktop personal computer with a twelve-inch monitor, a keyboard, and a mouse. This equipment must be easily accessible and easily removable when not in use, thereby allowing computer-supported as well as noncomputer-supported conferences. This allows research into media usage and media choice of computer aided teams. Each participant should be able to view only his or her monitor and the monitors should not obstruct communication between participants. Arrangement of the monitors on the conference table should allow for glare reduction. During the design, the need for a “plug-in” or portable computers was added.

## 3.2. The Facility Layout

The facility available for remodeling is located in the basement of a wing of the baroque-style Hohenheim palace. The room needs complete remodeling since it was not furnished at all but used for storage. The room is 6.93 meters deep and 8.43 meters wide, with an effective total height of 3.20 meters. Windows on one side of the room lead to light shafts. There is an unremovable pillar, 0.62 meter wide on each side, in the middle of the room. The existing entrance door can be relocated and widened. Even a second access from the corridor could be built, if necessary.

## 3.3. Basic Choices for the Public Screen Arrangement, Seating Layout, and Conference Table Design

The 3M Meeting Management Team [19] recommends taking particular note of seating configurations for meetings. For determining the appropriate configuration, different forms of teamwork interaction in meetings have to be distinguished. When selected participants are the primary focus point for the conference team, the form of teamwork can be characterized as “presentation” or sometimes “training.” If all participants are actively engaged in a conference, small teams usually use a form of teamwork that can be named “debate.” In a debate of small teams it is usually not necessary to sequence team member participation in advance. Debates can become more fluent by a process facilitator (moderator) and a technical facilitator (chauffeur or assistant). On the contrary, large groups often need to negotiate or implicitly agree in advance on a protocol or agenda of member contribution to a debate; designated facilitators are needed and speakers take turns to “present” their team input. This behavior is known from parliamentary forms of government and is therefore called the “parliamentary” form of teamwork.

The seating layout has to be configured so that the various roles people play in the intended form of teamwork are supported by the physical arrangement of their workspaces and the public screens. Figure 1 shows the suitability of the different seating designs and the recommended number of large public screens for the different forms of teamwork with small numbers. For larger teams (more than twenty members), the classroom, auditorium, or theater arrangement is recommended [19]. The degree of suitability depends on how well social interaction and communication between meeting participants are maintained or even stimulated in a specific type of teamwork. An overview of the characteristics of conference tables and seating arrangements used in other CSCW research labs is presented in Ferwagner et al. [8], Lewe and Krcmar [17], as well as in Mack [20] and Olson et al. [23].

U-shaped seating arrangements support parliamentary-type meetings of large groups and presentations because they allow a dedicated speaker position in front of the U. One public screen will usually suffice and serve as a focal point in front of the participants. A debate can be enforced in this seating arrangement, but unequal status of the seating positions is implied. Therefore, the U-shaped conference table design causes an implicit hierarchical structure in conference teams since the seating positions do not offer equal chances for drawing attention (and therefore power) to a participant's location, nor do they offer equal meeting control and visual meeting room overview. As Mantei [21] reported from her observations in the Capture Lab, the rear seating position is the most powerful position because, unlike at other positions at the table, everybody can be observed and the front screen can be seen at the same time. Because of the resulting hierarchical power structure, a U-shaped seating arrangement does not suit democratic debates.

An oval seating arrangement can be used for debates but suitability is reduced because of the danger of coalition formation. At least two public screens on opposite sides are required to give equal ease of visual access to the public screens. Presentations and parliamentary-type meetings can be performed with an oval seating arrangement, but it is difficult to provide a good position for a speaker. A speaker position on one of the two smaller sides of the oval can be used but suitability is limited because some participants have to move their chairs away from the table in order to face the speaker $[21]$ . A third public screen behind the speaker should be provided for the speaker's use. During presentations, the team can focus on this third public screen and during debates they focus on the two others. This solution is expensive because it is inadvisable to turn around one of the unused projectors of the two other screens and use it for display on the third screen. Every projector movement necessitates a difficult focusing procedure and interrupts the teamwork process.

The round seating arrangement is very suitable for debates. Two large public screens on opposite sides of the room should be provided. This arrangement provides almost equal status along with equal viewing possibilities at all positions around the table.

Almost equal status is necessary so as not to obstruct “democratic” meeting situations. A single public screen leads to a focus of the team on this screen, just as it is forced in a non-debate-promoting U-shaped seating arrangement. Nevertheless, the round arrangement can also be used for presentations if the speaker stands up, moves backward a little (maybe to the closest public screen), and the neighbors of the speaker turn toward the speaker. Suitability is limited to situations in which this behavior is acceptable. Parliamentary-type meetings, however, are not very well supported as there is no easy way to gain space for a moderator. A larger number of participants requires huge round tables, which disrupts cross-table conversations.

With rearrangeable conference table elements, a reconfigurable seating layout is attainable. Flexibility is gained by placing the table-elements on rollers. For an even larger flexibility, interchangeable table tops of different shapes are required $[4, 23]$ .

## 3.4. Design Alternatives

Starting with first sketches of ideas about conference table designs, several CA Team Room design alternatives were generated. The final round shape of the conference table evolved from a metamorphosis of the popular table design of conventional rectangular or trapezoidal meeting rooms. In parallel, the architects and IS researchers developed detailed room design alternatives in the sequence presented below. They evolved from a discussion of the major characteristics of each alternative, an evaluation regarding their fulfillment of the requirements mentioned above and improvement of the negative aspects in each advanced alternative. This was an iterative and cooperative process for the IS researchers and architects.

## 3.4.1. "Roman Church" Alternative

The cross-shaped layout of the “Roman Church” meeting room design alternative, shown in figure 2a, creates the impression of a comfortably large room by the cross shape. The pillar is not an irritating nuisance inside the meeting room. The conference table seating arrangement has a U-shape with an opening to the large public screen in the front. Observation is possible from one side and from the front in a sound-insulated partition of the facility which has its own access through a small entrance hall. The hallway also allows access to the meeting room. The meeting room is elevated two steps from the ground on a double bottom designed for physically and mentally “lifting up” the participants when they enter the room as well as to provide space for cables.

The architecture, table design, and seating arrangement support meetings with presentations and debates. The narrowness of the room is positively enlarged by “apses” on both sides. The U-shaped conference table design, however, causes an implicit hierarchical structure in conference teams since the seating positions do not offer equal chances for drawing attention (and therefore power) to a participant’s location, nor do they offer equal meeting control and visual meeting room overview.

## 3.4.2. "Bistro" Alternative

The “Bistro” design shown in figure 2b includes round, movable, and self-contained conference table units on rollers positioned around a cable-connection-hydrant in the center of an almost round meeting room.

The Bistro design offers a nice, “informal” working atmosphere. It seems to provide flexibility in adapting the CATeam Room to different group sizes since the units can quickly be moved in and out of the room and connected or disconnected from the supply-hydrant for the session. The seating arrangement also has greater flexibility since the self-contained units can be quickly moved around in the room. The formation of several small subgroups can be supported. This can be helpful for meeting and planning methodologies that encourage movement and rearrangement during the meeting. Moderators can make use of action in the room and the informal setting to stimulate participation and creativity. The flexibility gained by movable units can create a sense of a provisional arrangement, which can lessen the participants’ motivation to work in this room. Furthermore, the movement of the units could require so much effort that the units will hardly ever be moved. Therefore, a stable table design is preferred and, because of technological constraints, the Bistro alternative was rejected for further consideration at the time of design.

The rapid technological advances in cable-less LANs, battery life, computer weight (notebook and notepad computers), and input devices (speech input) allowed us more vigorously to pursue the idea of movable workspace units on rollers. A supply-hydrant would not be needed except for docking conventional hardware which needs power supply and network access by cable. This idea was picked up in the further development of the final conference table design. At present the segments of the conference table are moveable by placing them on rollers and the table top was made removable from the segment to allow diverse shapes to be placed on top. For various seating configurations, a continuous worksurface can be achieved by the removable tabletops, similar to the ELMER at the University of Michigan [4, 23].

## 3.4.3. "Classic Double" Alternative

The “Classic Double” room shown in figure 3 is equipped with a classic oval conference table and two public screens on each wall of the longer table side. Visual access to the public screen is not obstructed as the screens are placed on the wall well above the heads of seated participants. There are three levels of viewing (monitor level, face-to-face contact between other participants, and public screen level) that enhance interlocking of all teamwork interaction, according to Burden [3], Gebessler [10], Hall [11], and Sommer [26, 27].

The Classic Double alternative tries to offer more equality of position by offering two coupled public screens. The room size is enlarged by using one of the lightshafts for a garden and to supply daylight. The main room entrance has been relocated and now offers a larger entrance hall that can be used for coffee breaks.

This room design makes good use of the available space. It is well suited for cooperation and debates since there is almost equal opportunity to view the public screen and actions in the room from every seat. The room entrance is separated so that no one is seated with his or her back to the entrance. The oval shape of the conference table and the two opposite public screens could foster the creation of two opponent parties in meetings. This formation of coalitions could have a systematic adulteration effect on later CATeam research results. Meetings including presentations or training situations are not well supported by this design.

## 3.4.5. "Roundabout" Alternative

In our tests of workspace ergonomics, we found that the roundness of the conference table can be variable as long as the resulting outer diameter of the table does not exceed an acceptable distance from which people can be seated from one another. In small experiments with up to ten team members, we could demonstrate that intense conversations across the table can be sustained up to a distance of four to six meters if there is no visual obstruction. The minimum distance at which we seated people next to each other around the table was about one meter. This amount of personal space was acceptable to the subjects in our tests and is consistent with results reported by Mantei [21]. Our study results suggest modifying the oval table design of the Winging It design to a ring-shaped table design with ten to twelve movable segments depicted in figure 5.

The shape of the conference table is reflected by the round layout of the meeting room with the axis “trance-pillar-conference table.” This room layout was already introduced in the Bistro alternative. A spacious design for the meeting room was achieved by inscribing the greatest circle possible. The pillar is integrated into the meeting room with round panels that also reflect the shape of the conference table. The ideas and advantages of mounting the table segments on rollers and integration of two opposite public screens of the Classic Double alternative are picked up in this design.

Our solution for the desktop of the conference table workspace segments is shown in figure 7. We can accommodate a monitor, a keyboard, a ring binder or a stack of papers, a mouse, and a personal disk drive. In addition, this arrangement houses switches or infrared remote control units for direct control of electronic conference room devices such as the large public screens, video equipment, or lights.

An empty desktop, as is usually provided for conventional meetings, is available when the personal monitor, the keyboard, and the mouse are moved into the table body and covered by a flap. When computer support is made available, the monitor usually is furthest away from the meeting participant, whose paper documents and keyboard can be switched back and forth, depending on whether computer usage and keyboard input or handwriting is predominant. Each workspace needs to be 120 cm deep to provide sufficient space for papers, the keyboard, the monitor, and perhaps even a coffee cup. Each workspace is distinguished from its neighbor by a small strip of different color.

Figure 8 shows a model of the resulting table, as selected for display by the jury of the 1989 “Anno 2000 Office Design Competition” in Milan. Also, as already mentioned, the complete table is now composed of single workspace elements on rollers allowing a flexible arrangement.

This alternative offers a homogeneous room design and a democratic seating arrangement providing equal status to the participants by the ring-shaped table. Each seat offers a good view of one of the two public screens, face-to-face contact is enhanced, and personal monitors can be viewed only by the closest neighbor, if at all.

As the 3M Meeting Management Team [19] point out, acoustics, lighting, and ventilation also need careful consideration in the design of a meeting room. ## 4. Conclusion

TEAMWORK PERFORMANCE, WHICH CAN BE IMPROVED BY COMPUTER and other technology support, is highly dependent on the design of the environment. Therefore, expertise from CSCW researchers as well as from interior design architects and specialists of ergonomics was used to find a design that best suits all the requirements established before and during the iterative development effort. In this collaboration process, special attention was paid to the seating and public screen arrangements favoring different team sizes and forms of teamwork. Another important issue is media usage flexibility, which can be provided by offering a variety of technology with immediate access and conference room workspace ergonomics, which are an integral part of the room design.

The Roundabout design for the Hohenheim CA Team Room presented in this paper evolved gradually from first design sketches oriented toward traditional conference room settings while incorporating more and more requirements of such an environment. This joint effort of IS researchers and interior design architects attempts to provide a CA Team environment that tempts teams to prefer working in this environment rather than view it as a merely technical prerequisite for more efficient and cooperative teamwork.
