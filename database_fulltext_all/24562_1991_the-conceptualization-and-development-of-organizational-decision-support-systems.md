# The Conceptualization and Development of Organizational Decision Support Systems

JOEY F. GEORGE

JOEY F. GEORGE is Assistant Professor of MIS at the University of Arizona. He earned his bachelor's degree from Stanford University in 1979 and his Ph.D. in management at the University of California at Irvine in 1986. His research focuses on information systems and the workplace. His current research interests include group decision support systems and their use in non-American cultures, the effects of computing on decision authority in organizations, computer training and support, and the effects of extensive computerization in work groups, including computer-based monitoring. His work has been published in several journals, including Communications of the ACM, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, and MIS Quarterly.

ABSTRACT: Organizational decision support systems (ODSS) are a new type of decision support system (DSS) focusing on the organization rather than the individual or the group. In this paper, various conceptions of ODSS are reviewed, as are several architectures suggested for their construction. The paper ends with a discussion of the information technology on which ODSS can be based.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: organizational decision support systems, decision support systems, group decision support systems.

## Introduction

THE TERM “DECISION SUPPORT SYSTEM” (DSS) has been in use for almost twenty years. Over time, it has come to mean primarily a computer-based system for supporting individual decision making. The past decade has seen the development of computer-based systems to support groups of individuals in several tasks, including, but not limited to, decision making. The next logical step would seem to be the development of computer-based support for decision-making processes at a broader level that surpasses the group, moving up to divisions, departments, or even organizations. The name for such systems has already been suggested: organizational decision support systems (ODSS) (although as we will see, many who write about such systems prefer alternative names).

An earlier version of this paper was originally published in the Proceedings of the Twenty-Fourth Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences (IEEE Computer Society Press, 1991).

This paper is primarily a review of the ODSS literature to date. Its purpose is to examine what has been written about ODSS and to bring the important parts of the literature together in one place. There are three parts to the paper. The first is a discussion of the various conceptualizations of ODSS, with an eye to synthesizing these views into a single coherent and consistent conceptualization. The second part reviews some of the architectures that have been suggested for the development of ODSS. Some of these system architectures are conceptual, but some represent ODSSs that have been developed and are currently operational. The final section reviews the information technologies that have been identified in the literature as potential components of an ODSS. If this paper succeeds in its purpose, it will serve as a starting point for researchers who are interested in pursuing the development and study of decision support systems that have an organizational, rather than an individual or group, focus.

## Conceptualizations of ODSS

ONE OF THE FIRST MENTIONS OF ORGANIZATIONAL DECISION SUPPORT comes in a 1981 paper by Hackathorn and Keen [7]. Here Hackathorn and Keen distinguish among three types of decision support: individual, group, and organization. They maintain that computer-based systems can be developed to provide decision support for each of these levels. They have this to say about organization decision support:

Organization support focuses on an organization task or activity involving a sequence of operations and actors (e.g., developing a divisional marketing plan, capital budgeting) . . . Each individual's activities must mesh closely with other people's. Any computer-based aid will be used as much for a vehicle for communication and

coordination as for problem solving. [p. 24]

It was recognized early that providing computer-based support for an organization task or activity would require more than just support for decision making or problem solving. Hackathorn and Keen predicted that the provision of support for communication and coordination would also be important.

Not much was done in the literature with the concept of providing organization support with DSS for the next seven or so years. Then, beginning in 1988, the idea began to be the focus of work for several different researchers. The rest of this section serves as a brief tour of nine different conceptualizations of ODSS, developed from 1988 to 1990. Because these conceptualizations vary, the end of the section represents an attempt to find some common ground in them, developing in the process a minimalist definition of ODSS.

The first notable conceptualization of ODSS came in 1988, in the preface to the aptly titled Organization Decision Support Systems [9]. A.M. McCosh described how the earliest DSSs were indeed focused on the work of a single manager, but because of the huge expense and size of computers two decades ago, any such system would have to be built for a very senior manager. Managers of lesser stature could not command the use of such a major corporate resource for their needs. Since the managers for which the first DSSs were developed were of such high status in the organization, the DSS more than likely had a broad focus and involved several organization units. In that sense, the first DSS were also ODSS.

As computers became smaller, more powerful, and cheaper, managers at several organizational levels were able to have their own DSS developed. And because computing became less of a major corporate resource, the DSSs these managers used tended to focus almost exclusively on the problems of their own particular business units. DSSs became more narrow and specialized. In parallel with these developments were improvements in networking and data communication, making it possible to develop DSSs that could support groups of individuals, each of whom could work at a separate personal computer and share information. The focus of these efforts was on the work group or team and their joint business problems. Further technological developments have made it possible for the sharing of information and computer support across many different groups and individuals, returning the focus to decisions that affect many organizational units and address corporate problems. Figure 1 illustrates this evolution in the development of DSS. The figure shows how the costs to build a DSS have fallen over the past 20 years, and at the same time, the focus of these systems has shifted from the organization to the individual to the group, and back to the organization.

Also in the ODSS book, Bots and Sol [1] define an ODSS as “more than a computer system: it is a collection of hardware, software, people, and procedures that keeps the organization alive” (p. 139; original emphasis).

Later in 1988, Philippakis and Green suggested an architecture for organizationwide decision support systems [12]. The architecture took the form of a pyramid into which were organized the four types of DSS they had delineated: corporate planning systems (CPS), executive information systems (EIS), functional DSS (FDSS), and local DSS (LDSS) (figure 2). CPS have a corporatewide scope and a long-term horizon. EIS focus on the short term and on performance. FDSS provide support in a functional area, such as production or marketing, and LDSS are locally developed and controlled, with little formal structure.

These DSS types are organized as they are in Philippakis and Green's organizational pyramid for specific reasons. CPS and EIS are on top, making up the pyramid's capstone, because they cut across organizational functions. The FDSS and LDSS are distributed within functions because they are limited to particular functions, run by individuals or teams within them. All four types of DSS draw on organizational DSS resources, which include human resources, software/hardware, models/tools, and databases. Philippakis and Green maintain that this is the main reason to have an organizationwide DSS architecture, to emphasize the need for organizational planning, design, and control of the DSS function.

The topic of organizational decision support systems received concerted, focused attention in 1989 as part of the Information Systems and Decision Processing (ISDP) effort, sponsored by the Institute of Management Science and the National Science Foundation. The ISDP effort enlisted the aid of several dozen academic specialists in DSS to consider the current state and future of such systems. One of the five task forces into which this group was divided focused specifically on ODSS. This task force, chaired by Jay F. Nunamaker, Jr., produced six working papers and a summary report.

Each paper had a decidedly different perspective. Two of these papers $[8, 13]$ appeared in different forms in the Proceedings of the Twenty-Third Annual Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences (HICSS), along with three other papers $[15, 16, 17]$ . These nine papers represented nine different conceptualizations of ODSS, six of which are presented next.

Watson [16] defined an ODSS as “a combination of computer and communication technology designed to coordinate and disseminate decision-making across functional areas and hierarchical layers in order that decisions are congruent with organizational goals and management’s shared interpretation of the competitive environment” (p. 111).

Walker [15] defined an ODSS as “a DSS that is used by persons at several workstations in more than one organizational unit who make varied (interrelated but independent) decisions using a common set of tools” (p. 120). Walker’s definition is less stringent than Watson’s. It is interesting to note that Walker was basing his definition on an ODSS he helped develop. Watson, on the other hand, was conceptualizing in his paper about the design and architectural requirements of a generic ODSS.

Swanson, in both his ISDP [14] and HICSS [13] efforts, saw ODSS as specifically supporting distributed decision making. He preferred to call such systems distributed decision support systems (DDSS). In his HICSS paper, he says:

an organizational DSS should not be thought of as a manager's DSS. Rather it should be viewed as supporting the organization's division of labor in decision making.... A distributed decision support system (DDSS) is defined here as a decision support system (DSS) which supports distributed decision making. It constitutes one perspective or view of organizational DSS. [pp. 129–130]

Another paper on ODSS that suggested an alternative name was Weisband and Galegher [17], who say:

We prefer the term organizational information support systems (OISS). We subscribe to the idea that providing computer-based systems support for the range of information processing tasks carried out in most modern organizations means emphasizing diversity and flexibility, rather than just aiming to create ‘a system’ that will be useful across this vast domain. [p. 138]

Notice that the focus of ODSS has shifted in this definition. It has moved from providing decision support to providing computer-based support for the range of information processing tasks undertaken by modern organizations. To accommodate this broader emphasis, the authors have adopted a broader name for such support systems.

Still another approach is taken by King and Star [8]:

The concept [of ODSS] in principle is simple: apply the technologies of computers and communications to enhance the organizational decision making process . . . .In principle, ODSS takes the vision of technological support for group processes to the higher level of organizations, in much the same way that GDSS extended the vision of technological support for individual action to group process. [p. 143]

Yet, in the succeeding examination of the relationship of individual decision processing to that of groups, King and Star conclude that the way groups make decisions is very different from the way individuals do. Group decision processes are more complex because there is an additional need for communication, as well as an additional need for judgment rules to resolve differences or conflict among members. They go on to say that, just as one cannot scale up from DSS to GDSS, one cannot scale up from GDSS to ODSS. Organization decision processes are much more complex than those for groups, differing along several key dimensions. Representatives of groups will tend to act in an organizationally suboptimal fashion when it comes to organizational decision processes because their first loyalty is to their groups, and not to the organization. The implication for designers is that ODSS should not be designed as GDSS for big groups.

Finally, the contribution of Fedorowicz and Konsynski [5] is noteworthy because of their attempts to categorize ODSS, which they call simply OSS (organization support systems). According to Fedorowicz and Konsynski, there are four types of OSS:

Type 0: any basic computer-based support system that reenforces traditional norms. These OSS are structure-enforcing.

Type 1: information technology (IT) used at the organizational level for organizationwide purposes; structure-preserving. An example is an EIS used to analyze organizationwide data.

Type 2: IT that spans the organization, used by individuals outside of functional or hierarchical boundaries; structure-independent.

Type 3: any IT that changes existing or enables new organizational structures; structure-transforming.

Although at first glance it may appear difficult to synthesize these different conceptualizations of ODSS, there are actually several similarities. Among the more common features:

\- The focus of an ODSS is an organizational task or activity or a decision that affects several organization units or corporate issues.

\- An ODSS cuts across organizational functions or hierarchical layers.

\- An ODSS almost necessarily involves computer-based technologies, and may also involve communication technologies.

These common elements of the conceptualizations we reviewed do not, however, necessarily help us recognize an ODSS when we see one. An individual DSS is relatively easy to recognize: in its simplest form, it runs on one machine for one user. A GDSS is likewise easy to recognize, especially in its more common form, the decision room. However, once a GDSS extends into the dispersed, asynchronous arena, it becomes more difficult to identify. Does one look at the software to make the distinction? If so, what aspects of the software identify the system as a dispersed GDSS and not just some networked microcomputers? There are similar problems in cleanly identifying an ODSS, since an ODSS would probably run on several connected machines, some of which may not even be in the same organization. In addition, an ODSS could have several components, some of which could be individual DSS or GDSS in their own right. If we accept the Hackathorn and Keen [7] description of organization support, then an ODSS might support a sequence of operations and several actors. Must all of the operations in the sequence be supported by computers? All of the actors? Where in the chain of events does one begin to look for support?

Despite these problems, however, these common elements at least provide a place to begin in determining a consolidated concept of ODSS. It also might be helpful to look at some system architectures for ODSS derived through conceptualization and through developing operational ODSS.

IN THE ODSS LITERATURE IDENTIFIED SO FAR, several models and system architectures of ODSS have been developed. In this section, we first examine two architectures and a model based on what their authors believe ODSS can and should be. We then look at three architectures and physical configurations based on the design and implementation of actual ODSS.

The first conceptual architecture we will review is for a generic ODSS, developed by Swanson and Zmud $[14]$ , based on their ideas of what an ODSS should be (figure 3). Their architecture is based on the use of two sets of computer-based tools: generic and situation-specific. The ODSS is designed to facilitate multiple and concurrent distributed decision-making situations. The focus seems to be primarily on distributed support for participants in these situations.

The generic tools are divided into four classes: communication (e-mail, voice mail, etc.), data handling (capable of handling analytical, textual, and graphical data), public data management (allowing for the creation, modification, and maintenance of public data), and process governance (which provides for building groups and resolving tasks). The situation-specific tools are divided into three classes: a database facility, participant/task representation, and a situational context facility. The database facility provides for the creation and maintenance of private databases. Each participant in a decision situation is likely to maintain several private databases, even for a particular decision situation. The participant/task representation facility allows participants and tasks to be tracked across decision situations. According to Swanson and Zmud, this facility would provide the following for each active decision situation: a listing of all participants, a listing of all tasks, a listing of task-participant responsibilities, and a sequence diagram relating a situation's tasks. In addition, the participant/task representation facility would include a data-gathering/scanning capability that tracks important task-related and external events, updating relevant fields in both public and local databases. Finally, the situational context facility promotes the evolution of a single contextual frame for all of the participants for each active decision situation.

Whereas the architecture devised by Swanson and Zmud revolves around their basic idea of an ODSS to support distributed decision processes, a second conceptual architecture developed by Watson [16] revolves around a GDSS. Watson's architecture is reproduced in figure 4. As can be seen from the figure, the heart of Watson's system is the GDSS, or meeting support system. Closely associated with the GDSS is a task and meeting knowledge base. Watson describes this as an expert system that helps the group using the meeting support system to select the proper structure for a particular problem and to help the group resolve problems in an on-going meeting.

On the other side of the meeting support system in Watson's architecture diagram is the corporate communication network, which provides two key features of decision support: (1) it allows group members to access and use the expertise of individuals who are not in the meeting and to access corporate and public databases; and (2) it provides access to the decision database. The decision database is a unique feature designed expressly for an ODSS. It captures and records information flows that occur during group decision making, records past decisions and keeps track of current problems, and stores key facts and assumptions relevant to each particular decision. The decision database, as conceptualized by Watson, should capture what is currently occurring in the organization, since it reflects the thinking behind current decisions and the actions being taken to implement decisions. The corporate communication network also allows access to the expertise database, which contains information about individuals who are experts in a particular problem domain. Groups can get additional information on the problem they are dealing with by finding out who in the organization has relevant expertise and then by consulting that person. Finally, groups may also access corporate and public databases that may contain additional information they want.

The model of an ODSS developed by Miller and Nilakanta $[10]$ was first presented in a paper that was part of the minitrack on ODSS at the 1991 HICSS conference (figure 5). Their model illustrates the three organizational subsystems of knowledge, data, and models, which they state “interact over a rich and varied telecommunications backbone” (p. 66) to constitute an ODSS. Knowledge, data, and models can originate both inside and outside the organization, and they can come from both public and private domains. They argue that an ODSS will rely on the corporate database, and they devote most of their paper to describing a data-extraction scheme that will allow models in the ODSS to communicate directly with the corporate database.

The first of the architectures based on actual ODSS that we will consider was developed by Walker [15]. This is the physical configuration of the Enlisted Force Management System (EFMS), developed by Walker and others for the U.S. Air Force (figure 6). EFMS helps members of the Air Staff in the Pentagon make decisions related to their enlisted personnel. The project to develop EFMS started in 1981, with implementation beginning in 1986 (and still continuing when Walker's paper was published in 1990).

EFMS supports functions spread among five major, somewhat independent, organizational units, under the command of four different two-star generals in three geographically dispersed locations. It has three major components: the model base, an interlinked system of small models, each designed for one specific purpose; the database, which is centralized and provides input to the models and retains their output for management reporting, and is available for direct inquiry by the users; and the user interface, which is designed to be uniform so that each user can do the same things in the same way for each type of model. The general physical configuration provides for end users in geographically dispersed sites, using microcomputers, to interact with the database and the models using a high-level English-like command language. The model base and the database both reside on a mainframe computer.

![](/api/attachments/P8DDZ3S7/fulltext/images/8fa094b288af72c164fab2192fb86a0612622144ef5defad3888680f873759f5.jpg)  
Figure 6. Enlisted Force Management System (EFMS) Physical Configuration (from Walker [15])

The other two operational ODSSs are very similar to the one described by Walker. They both provide models that can be accessed by users in various organizational units. It seems to be this focus on shared models, and to some extent shared data, across organizational units that allows these systems to be seen as ODSS. These systems are less grandiose in scope and function than the systems described by Swanson and Zmud $[14]$ and Watson $[16]$ . For example, the system described by Pagani and Bellucci $[11]$ was designed to support the corporate strategic planning process at Telettra (figure 7). It was to augment the existing DSS, which was used by the Strategic Planning Staff for top management. There were four major components: the database, which enables users to access both private and public databases (the public databases are read-only, however); the model base, which allows for group models to be used and private models to be created; office automation tools: word processing and graphics; and communications, which provides for the transfer of data and reports from one subsystem to another and for e-mail.

The interesting thing about the Pagani and Bellucci $[11]$ ODSS is its incorporation of both a public and a private environment. Individual users would have access to the four components of the system for their individual, private needs, using the system as they would any individual DSS, but they also have access to the group database and group models. They can communicate with other end users and gather additional information through the communication component of the system. It is this public environment that makes the system an ODSS.

The final system we will consider was also designed to support strategic planning in an Italian company $[4]$ . The specific function the system was designed to support is the definition of five-year plans for contracts in foreign markets and the related resource planning. How the system fits into the overall decision process is depicted in figure 8.

The actual decision process occurs at the strategic level. There are two outputs of the process: a plan of foreign projects to acquire, and a plan of needed resources. Inputs to the process are pertinent external variables and certain internal information (current expertise in the company, levels of resource sharing among projects in progress, constraints on the future availability of resources). Some of the internal information is provided by the Project Management Information System, which provides information on the resources needed for contracts, which is then aggregated to the departmental level. Some of this information is used as input for “benchmarks,” or reference models, which relate activities to resources on the basis of historical data and type of contract. The DSS of concern here helps manage the internal information needed in developing the five-year plans. It allows for the analysis and evaluation of a given program of foreign contracts.

Dondi et al. [4] state that their system is an ODSS because the decision process with which it is concerned involves several organizational units and therefore is a multi-decision-maker process, because the problem domain is partially unstructured, and because the relationship between five-year plans and the necessary resources is neither rigid nor routine.

What insights do these architectures, conceptual and real, provide us into ODSS? One insight is the similarity between the real architectures developed by Walker $[15]$ and Pagani and Bellucci $[11]$ . Both systems are designed to provide access to data and models for multiple users in a fairly straightforward manner. In both cases, there are organizational databases and model bases that individual users can access. Both systems are based on existing information technology, and both systems focus on applications that are of central concern to the entire organization (i.e., enlisted force management $[15]$ and strategic planning $[11]$ ). In some ways, these systems are extensions of single-user DSS, where each individual user now has access to his or her own data and models, as well as to the data and models used and developed by others dealing with similar (if not the same) problems. These systems seem to be examples of the state of ODSS development currently.

The second insight is that the conceptual architectures describe more extensive ODSS than the real architectures. Both Swanson and Zmud $[14]$ and Watson $[16]$ describe systems that do far more than simply allow users to access data and models used by others. In addition, both include technological components that are as yet unproven. For example, the Watson $[16]$ architecture includes a task and meeting knowledge base and decision and expertise databases. The objective here is to allow a work group or task team to better utilize the resources of the organization, rather than simply allow users to access other data and models. These conceptual architectures demonstrate a rethinking of the ODSS concept and illustrate possible future paths for ODSS development.

## IT for ODSS

THE LAST ISSUE TO BE CONSIDERED in this paper is the specific information technologies that can be used in ODSS. The range of IT that can be used is large. In the view of Fedorowicz and Konsynski [5], the range is almost equal to the entire range of IT that is available for any purpose (Table 1).

Culnan and Gutek [3] present a very different view. For them, an ODSS does not necessarily have to be based on information or communication technology. In their seven examples of ODSS they include organizational subunits that may or may not be supported by computer-based systems. Still another view is presented by George, Nunamaker, and Valacich [6], who describe five different specific types of IT that can be applied to general ODSS objectives:

\- Communication technologies: IT to foster team, organization, and inter-organization communication;

\- Coordination technologies: IT to coordinate resources, facilities, and projects,

e.g, groupware, calendars;

\- Filtering technologies: IT to filter and summarize information, e.g., intelligent e-mail;

\- Decision-making technologies: IT designed to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of individual and group decision making, e.g., DSS, GDSS.

\- Monitoring technologies: IT to monitor the status of organizational operations, industry trends, etc., e.g. EIS.

The IT applied, and the extent to which it is applied, depends on the particular ODSS objective under consideration. In their paper, George and colleagues discuss three specific organizational objectives that some high-profile organizations are trying to achieve today: downsizing, or the shift to flatter, leaner organizations; a shift to a team-based organization; and outsourcing some organizational activities to third-parties (as in Kodak's handing over responsibility for their mainframe computing to IBM). The authors claim that no general ODSS could handle all three objectives equally well, so they suggest a different ODSS design for each of the objectives. Table 2 illustrates the extent to which each of their five IT types is useful for each organizational objective.

At first glance, there appear to be few common areas in these three views of what IT is appropriate for ODSS. Although the Fedorowicz and Konsynski $[5]$ and George, et al. $[6]$ views both include IT categories of communications and decision tools, the focus of the former is on individual technologies, including hardware, whereas the focus of the latter is on classes of system types. The Culnan and Gutek view $[3]$ is also based on classes of systems, but some of these systems do not even have to be automated. With some effort and loss of detail, however, we could merge these three views into one that looks something like the following, based on the George et al. $[6]$ system types:

\- Communication technologies: IT to foster team, organization, and inter-organization communication, including all of the specific information technologies listed under this category in [5], as well as the computer-mediated communication technologies mentioned in [3].

\- Coordination technologies: IT to coordinate resources, facilities, and projects, e.g., groupware, electronic calendaring [5].

\- Filtering technologies: IT to filter and summarize information, e.g., intelligent e-mail.

\- Decision-making technologies: IT designed to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of individual and group decision making, e.g., DSS, GDSS, modeling and decision tools [5], and models as mentioned in [3].

\- Monitoring technologies: IT to monitor the status of organizational operations, industry trends, etc., e.g., EIS [5], ESS [3], organizational scanning units [3], customer affairs and public affairs units [3].

\- Data/knowledge representation technologies: IT for the representation and storage of data and knowledge [5], including organizational archives and common-access databases [3].

\- Processing and presentation technologies: IT for processing data and presenting information [5].

This integrated categorization preserves the best features of all three views on which it is based. The system categories identified by George et al. $[6]$ are expanded to include IT for data representation and for processing and presentation. The many individual IT identified in $[5]$ are preserved, although subsumed under specific system categories. And the idea that the components of an ODSS do not necessarily have to be automated, as expressed in $[3]$ , is also preserved. The next step is to extend Table 2 to cover the new categories of system type. The table could also be extended to cover other organizational trends and objectives.

## Conclusions

GIVEN OUR REVIEW OF THE LITERATURE ON ODSS, the question to ask now is, "How do we know an ODSS when we see one?" As we maintained earlier, the answer to this question is neither clear nor complete, but hopefully this review has made it easier to generate an answer than was the case before.

How do we know an ODSS when we see one? Based on the conceptualizations, we can safely say that an ODSS has as its focus an organizational task or activity or decision that affects several organization units or corporate issues. An ODSS cuts across organizational functions or hierarchical layers, and it generally incorporates computer-based and/or communication technologies.

From the architectures and physical configurations, we can say that an ODSS provides access for individual users to shared models and shared data. The implemented systems we looked at are very similar in this respect. Each has as its focus some aspect of strategic planning. The conceptual architectures, however, were broader in scope and represent future directions for development of ODSS.

From the ideas on what information technology is suitable for ODSS, we find that a broad range are available and suitable, but these different technologies can be categorized into seven different system types, to support communication, coordination, filtering, decision making, monitoring, data and knowledge representation, and data-processing and information presentation. This underscores the need for communication and coordination in organization support identified by Hackathorn and Keen [7]. And we see that an ODSS may not necessarily benefit from computer-based support at all.

Hopefully, by reviewing the existing literature on ODSS and by examining conceptualizations, architectures, and suitable information technologies, the image of an ODSS and what constitutes it is a little clearer. This, however, is just a starting point in the study of ODSSs. The questions that remain to be examined concern the origin of such systems and how they are designed and implemented to support organizational decision making. Decision making at the organizational level is a complex and not well understood process $[8]$ . Bowker and Star $[2]$ present an analysis of the development of the International Classification of Diseases as an example of an ODSS that has developed over the last one hundred years, spanning many organizations and nations, and until recently in its existence, without the support of information technology. Yet the issues they raise, such as the need for multiple-representational forms for information, finding the appropriate level of data categorization, and realizing that imposed standards result in workarounds, are pertinent for the design and implementation of all ODSSs. In future research on ODSSs, these issues require as much examination as the more technical ones.
