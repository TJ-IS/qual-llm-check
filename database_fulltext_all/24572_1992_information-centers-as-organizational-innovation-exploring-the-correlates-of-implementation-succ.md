# Information Centers as Organizational Innovation: Exploring the Correlates of Implementation Success

MARY K. FULLER AND E. BURTON SWANSON

MARY K. She has also taught at the University of Southern California and California State University, Dominguez Hills. Her primary research interests are in diffusion of innovation, application systems development, and end-user computing. Prior to coming to UCLA, Ms. Fuller received an M.S. in Information Systems from the University of Wisconsin–Madison and spent several years working as a systems analyst and programmer. NOT ALL ORGANIZATIONAL INNOVATIONS ARE SUCCESSES. Some may fail to be diffused and institutionalized. Others may be widely employed with little or only transitory, or even negative, impact. Even where an innovation is well conceived, its rewards are not likely to accrue equally to those who undertake it. Conventional wisdom holds that early adopters are the most likely to profit [35, pp. 381–382]. However, mere adoption of an innovation is but one stage in the process. Innovations must also be implemented and assimilated. About this aspect of innovation we know comparatively little [27]. But a rather substantial literature warns us that, especially where new technology is involved, implementation is likely to be problematic [20, 36, 39].

The information center (IC) constitutes one example of an organizational innovation based in the use of new information technology. Originated in the mid-1970s by IBM in Canada, ICs were conceived to provide support of end-user computing whereby users apply computers directly, rather than indirectly through the programming services of the Information Systems (IS) department $[15, 42]$ . Typically located as a self-contained unit within the IS $[6]$ , the IC employs a relatively small staff devoted to helping users help themselves in the acquisition and use of computer hardware, software, and data. It has been a popular innovation. Bohl $[1]$ estimates that 80 percent of large American businesses (with a billion dollars in sales) have adopted the IC concept.

While ICs have thus come to be widespread, comparatively little is known about their overall effectiveness. By most reports, ICs have been generally successful. Doubts have been expressed, however. Gerrity and Rockart $[12]$ identify certain weaknesses in the characteristic IC: it tends to be centralized whereas users prefer localized support; its staff often lacks needed functional and applications knowledge; it is an IS creation sometimes seen as an experimental palliative to user demands; it tends to be reactive rather than proactive; and it is often unaccompanied by a strategic direction. Even among enthusiasts, some see the IC as a temporary innovation, needed only where and so long as user computing skills remain underdeveloped $[4]$ . Some believe the demise of the IC will occur as support moves out into the end-user areas with the creation of departmentalized help centers $[16]$ . Recently, some ICs have evidently been destined for the “scrap heap” $[18]$ . As an innovative IS cure-all for end-user computing, the original bloom may therefore now be off the IC rose.

The present paper reports results from a study of IC adoption and implementation among twenty-seven IS organizations. It confirms that IC success, as evaluated by IC managers, has tended to be mixed among organizations, and it identifies certain correlates of success—the size of the host organization at the locations served by the

IS unit together with the total number of IC services provided—that have previously been overlooked. The findings have implications beyond the case of information centers. Specifically, they suggest some limitations of the popular “critical success factors” approach $[34]$ when it is used by researchers to study and account for the implementation success of a particular IS innovation. Beyond such success factors, we find that explaining variance in implementation success and providing effective guidance for IS managers requires that basic organizational context be taken into account.

## Background

WHAT MAKES AN IC SUCCESSFUL? Here we briefly review what has been said on this subject. While the research literature is small, it provides good motivation for our work.

Leitheiser and Wetherbe [19] report on a field study of twenty-seven IC managers who were interviewed by telephone about their specific achievements, problems, and critical success factors (those few key areas where “things must go right,” as defined by Rockart [34]; see also [5]). Most frequently mentioned as success factors were “provide whatever services are needed in a timely way” and “develop a competent staff.”

Brancheau et al. [3] approached the success factor issue from the perspective of IC users. From semistructured interviews of fifty users of ICs in five companies, they identify the technical skills of the IC staff as the most important critical success factor, from the user viewpoint. Users were also asked to rate their ICs in terms of overall effectiveness. Significant differences among the five organizations are reported, which the authors attribute in part to corresponding differences in the IC staffs' understandings of their user needs.

Carr [6] conducted a field study of twenty ICs to see how Hammond's [15] original concept was implemented in practice. The IC managers interviewed claimed the primary benefits of their units were more literate, competent, and productive end users; however, the key factors in achieving these benefits were not systematically explored. Similarly, White and Cristy [42] studied six IC installations, finding that these ICs did not conform well with the authors' ideal (formal) model; the relative success of these ICs was not directly addressed.

Munro et al. [28] studied IC implementation tactics for the expansion and control of end-user computing among thirty-seven organizations. They suggest that ICs will tend to converge over time toward a suitable balance between expansion and control tactics; however, they report that none of the units studied has reached this mature state yet. Relative success among ICs is not compared.

Magal et al. [23] and Magal and Carr [24] surveyed a large randomly selected sample of IC managers about their critical success factors. Twenty-six candidate factors were assessed. A competent staff, communication with users, and top management support proved to be the leading factors in IC success, from the consensus viewpoint. Variance in these assessments was also examined by means of a factor analysis. Five composite factors—commitment to the IC concept, quality of IC support services, facilitation of end-user computing, role clarity, and coordination of end-user computing--were identified. However, only slight differences in the perceived importance of these factors across ICs of varying age, user group size, and hardware employed were found [24]. And the IC managers made no judgments about the degree of success actually achieved by their units.

An American Management Association (AMA) survey $[1]$ asked respondents about their rationale for creating their ICs. Training and support, improving DP/end-user relations, and providing access to corporate data ranked highly regardless of startup date. About half of the respondents ranked easing the DP backlog as important. Standardizing micro hardware and software, protecting the security of the database, and supervising micro/mainframe links were more important to more recently established ICs. Save for IC age, other factors such as size or location (e.g., headquarters or branch) were not associated with significant variation in rationale for creating the IC.

As for success, the AMA survey found that mature ICs were more successful than newer ICs at improving software training, providing access to corporate data on the mainframe, and improving relations between DP and end-user departments. Newer ICs are more successful at standardizing micro hardware. Reducing the DP backlog proved to be problematic for ICs regardless of maturity.

On the whole, while the existing literature on ICs shows much concern for the success of this innovation, especially in terms of those factors judged critical to it, it contributes relatively little to the systematic explanation of this success. Variance in success among ICs is not generally addressed. Relevant contingencies remain to be explored $[33]$ . Theory is on the whole absent.

## An Exploratory Study

WE ADDRESSED THE RESEARCH QUESTION of explaining IC success as part of a larger exploratory study in which we examined the IC more broadly, both as an organizational innovation and as an IS innovation. Significantly, where an IC is established by an IS department, it is both an IS product and a process innovation [40]. New services are provided to the host organization, for which the IC is a process innovation, and work is also reorganized within IS. Both technological and organizational components are involved. Both IS and the host organization are affected. The three research questions raised in the larger study were: (1) What has been the pattern of adoption and diffusion of ICs among IS organizations? (2) What accounts for the relative success and failure of ICs among IS organizations that have adopted them? (3) How has the IC evolved over time as an organizational innovation? In a previous paper [10], we addressed the first question. In the present paper, we address the second question and touch upon the third.

## Theoretical Motivation

The study was motivated in principal part by an IS innovation diffusion model suggested by Swanson [40], in which the IS department is seen as dually linked to its professional and host organizational environments, from which it secures its expertise, resources, and expressions of organizational needs. These contexts are seen as crucial for understanding the adoption and diffusion of any IS innovation among organizations. IS departments may be either tightly or loosely coupled to their professional and host organizational environments (indeed, they may be loosely coupled to one in part because they are tightly coupled to the other). Some IS innovations may be diffused primarily through the IS professional environment; others may be effectively diffused via user groups within the host organization's industry. The popular and widely adopted IC serves as a particularly interesting example of an innovation to be studied.

In our previous paper we found that, among the adopters of ICs, earlier adopters were more likely to be associated with information-intensive industries and with a large host organization at the locations served. We attributed earlier adoption to need-based demands within the organization. Among adopters, ICs tended to be adopted first where the information needs of the host organization were greatest. (Independently of timing of adoption, adopters also tended to have larger and more professional IS units, when compared to nonadopters $[10]$ .) In the present analysis, we hypothesized that the earlier adopters of ICs will further tend to be the more successful at any one subsequent time. Since earlier adoption is more need-based, it is also likely to be a good fit for the organization, while later adoption may occur simply because the innovation is institutionalized and taken for granted as a good practice $[41]$ . Earlier adoption should also benefit from the longer period of implementation and assimilation when comparing success as of a given later date.

Nevertheless, it is by no means clear that our working hypothesis should be unequivocally supported. A counterhypothesis and argument favors the later adopter. Later adoption is probably less risky as the technical bugs in the innovation are more likely to have been worked out. Later adoption should also be facilitated by the gradual accumulation of professional knowledge about the innovation and its effective implementation (as embodied in the success-factor literature discussed above). Moreover, other factors apart from timing of adoption are also likely to be important to IC success, as suggested by the research described above. We consider four groups of other variables: (1) IS and host organizational context variables; (2) the rationale for adoption of the IC concept; (3) the current size and scope of the IC and its services; and (4) the impact, problems, and performance evaluation currently associated with the IC. Of the IS and host organizational context variables, three may be particularly salient to IC success: (i) the information intensity of the host organization business; (ii) the size of the host organization and its IS department; and (iii) the educational background of the IS department.

Information intensity refers to the degree to which an industry or company relies on information processing to create and deliver its product $[32]$ . Because the management and impact of IS technology are more important for organizations in information-intensive industries $[22]$ , here there is likely to be a greater opportunity and effort to make the IC a success.

An organization's size has generally been found to be positively related to its innovativeness [35]. It is also likely to play a role as a critical success factor. Ample size presents more options for organizing and staffing an IC to best advantage, drawing from the resource abundance and specialized skills of the larger organization. Since IC staff typically come from the IS department [6], a large IS unit is likely to be particularly helpful to IC success. Beyond the IS department, a larger host organization offers a larger and more differentiated market for IC services. It thus presents opportunities for achievement of critical mass, economies of scale, and niche marketing of IC services.

The IS department's educational background may also play a role in the success of the IC. Again, since the IC staff typically come from the IS department, a well-educated, well-trained IS staff is likely to produce a well-educated, well-trained IC staff. Such an IS staff may also provide good technical backup support for the IC staff. Higher educational levels are further associated with more cosmopolitan behavior and attitudes [8, 44] and a psychological commitment to moving beyond the status quo [31]. Such an IS department is likely to be less threatened by and more supportive of the IC innovation.

Beyond these context variables, other more immediate factors are likely to be important to IC success, including the rationale associated with its original organizational adoption. Reasons for adopting ICs differ and may not be equally good ones in shaping the successful IC. For example, reducing the development backlog may be an unobtainable goal (as suggested by the research discussed above). On the other hand, improving IS/end-user relations may be achievable and lead to a successful IC. The size and scope of the IC and its services should also be related to its success. Sufficient IC staff size is likely to be particularly important. An IC initially successful in the pilot stage could falter when put into firmwide operations with insufficient staff to meet burgeoning demands. Wisdom concerning IC staff size varies. IBM–Canada (site of the first IC) had a user-to-staff ratio of 100:1 [17]. Gillin [13, 14] reports successful cases of 90:1 and 170:1. However, Paul [30] reports the best ratio is 50:1 while Hammond [15] calls for an initial staff of three to four people and a ratio of 20:1. Bohl [1] found the typical IC startup staff consists of three or four people, regardless of the organization's size or description.

Services provided by the IC may similarly vary with different levels of effectiveness. For example, while training is fundamental to enable end users to do their own programming, Bohl [1] found less than a third of the ICs included training when initially organized. The number of requests received and processed by the IC may similarly vary with different levels of effectiveness.

Finally, differences in impacts, problems encountered, and performance goals may also lend insights into whatever success is achieved by the IC. Overall, there is thus a wide variety of likely correlates of reported success in IC implementation among organizations.

## Methodology

Our research approach was exploratory in nature and employed a conventional questionnaire survey of IS departments and their information centers. The fifteen-page questionnaire (available from the authors) included two parts: the first on the IS department and its parent organization, to be completed by the IS manager, and the second on the department's information center (if any), to be completed by the IC manager. From the IS manager, we sought background data that included the industry and size of the host organization, and the education level and size of the IS department (see also [10]). From the IC manager, we sought data on IC age, size, requests, and services, and, furthermore, judgments of impacts, problems, adoption rationale, basis of performance evaluation, and overall success. Checklists of multiple items were included for services, impacts, problems, and adoption rationale, developed from a review of the IC literature [1, 2, 3, 11, 19].

In addressing overall IC success, we asked the IC manager for a simple “bottom-line” assessment on a seven-point scale. We imposed no theory of success upon the manager; we wanted personal criteria and knowledge to be employed. While many views of IC success may be relevant here, it is the IC manager’s own personal judgment that is the most well placed and likely to reflect the differing views of users, IC staff, and the IS manager. It is moreover a judgment that is of basic practical importance to the future of the IC. Such an integrative evaluation also comes easily to the manager, who learns to do this naturally by the nature of his or her position and responsibility $[26]$ .

Because our questionnaire was being used for the first time, it was pretested and refined in reviews with two IS managers and one IC manager, followed by a pilot study involving six IS managers along with their IC managers in a large U.S. metropolitan area. It was then mailed to a random sample of 500 members of the Data Processing Management Association within the job function category of Data Processing and Operational Management. The intent was to obtain a sample of considerable representative diversity. Following initial distribution of the questionnaire, two follow-ups were sent. The final return rate was 16 percent (81 respondents). Responses were anonymous.

Among the respondents, two did not provide usable completed questionnaires. Seventeen others were further eliminated from the analysis, including one outlier organization whose size and budget were roughly triple that of any other organization, and sixteen very small organizations (less than two application programmers) whose size obviously precluded spinning off staff members to provide full-time support of end-user computing. Of the sixty-two remaining organizations, twenty-seven (44 percent) reported having ICs. Among those thirty-five organizations without ICs, four reported having either established an IC as a pilot project or having approved an IC for implementation, nine reported having an IC under study, three reported considering and rejecting implementation of an IC, twelve have never considered the idea of implementing an IC, and five organizations gave various reasons for not implementing an IC at this time (e.g., “idea on hold”). Two organizations did not elaborate.

The present analysis focuses upon the reported success of those twenty-seven organizations with ICs. This is admittedly a relatively small sample. It is also likely to be a biased one, all things considered. We must be careful in making inferences from it. We therefore examined it more closely as to its representativeness.

## Sample Representativeness

We identified four key problems in linking our analytic sample of twenty-seven organizations with ICs to the theoretical population to which we would make inferences—a specification problem, a sampling problem, a response problem, and a screening problem—as shown in figure 1. These problems are inherent to all survey research. We considered their resolution and implications in our own case.

In the case of the specification problem, resolution is in general subject to two biases associated with the survey population—those of contamination and deficiency. $^{1}$ In our case, because the relevant DPMA job function category is rather broadly defined and populated by more than half the total membership, we suspect that contamination is far more likely than deficiency. (If there is also a deficiency, it is likely to be in the underrepresentation of the less professional IS units whose managers do not belong to DPMA or other professional associations).

The sampling problem was resolved in our case by classical means. Given random sampling, our survey sample is of course likely to be representative of the survey population.

Our respondent sample is suspect, however. The relatively low 16 percent response rate raises the issue of whether it suffers from response bias, a likely occurrence even for much higher response rates $[9]$ . Low response rates are of course particularly common in mailed questionnaire surveys. Because our instrument was somewhat long and called for factual information that no doubt required time and effort to collect, we anticipated such a response problem and expected only about a 25 percent rate. Still, why did we suffer an even lower rate? In general, nonrespondents are more likely than respondents to be those for whom the questionnaire is poorly targeted (because, for example, it is sent to a manager who has recently changed jobs) or uninteresting (because, for example, the organization does not have and is not considering an IC). As we see next, we believe that our additional response problem may be explained in these terms.

In resolving our screening problem, we winnowed our respondent sample to yield an analytic sample of sixty-two. Nineteen respondents (23 percent) were eliminated as unsuitable. Significantly, these eliminations provide us with information about our larger sample. Especially given the number of too-small organizations among respondents, we estimate that more than one-fourth of our total survey sample was inappropriate to the survey. The survey population as a whole was significantly contaminated. This is not unusual among surveys and need not detract from their purposes $[9]$ . In our case, it is likely to explain our response problem and allows us to reassess our effective response rate. Because we are, of course, interested in surveying only those for whom our questions are appropriate, our overall effective response rate from this group may be estimated as somewhat higher than 16 percent. Because our overall analytic sample is nevertheless still likely to be biased, we compared early to late respondents, a conventional procedure. We found no significant differences.

In this paper we are interested only in an analytic subsample, those twenty-seven respondents (44 percent of the overall analytic sample) who have adopted the IC concept. This 44 percent adoption frequency probably overestimates the ratio of adopters in the larger organizational population. Again, because those with ICs would probably be most likely to respond to our questionnaire, we have further reason to believe that our effective response rate is very conservatively estimated at 16 percent.

While, narrowly speaking, we are interested here in whether our analytic subsample is representative of those IS departments with ICs in the larger survey population, more broadly we are interested in whether our subsample is representative of the theoretical population to which our findings should apply. We are not interested per se in organizations managed by DPMA members. Rather, the interest is in all those IS departments that are candidates to adopt the IC concept. This larger population is not well specified, in part because we do not yet know enough about ICs to know the limits associated with their adoption. This does not end our efforts to examine the representativeness of our sample, however.

We further compared our analytic sample to a much larger sample of 529 IC adopters from a survey conducted by the American Management Association [1]. This larger sample is also subject to questions of representativeness, as it is based on an even lower response rate, about 5 percent, than is our own.

Two IC attributes, IC staff size and IC age, provide good points of comparison. For our own subsample, the mean IC staff size is 9.6 and the mean IC age is about 3 years as of May 1988. For the AMA sample, the mean staff size is 6.7 and the mean IC age is also about 3 years, but as of May 1986, however, the mean staff size is 9.5 for ICs that became operational in 1983 or earlier. Older ICs tend to be larger ICs; more recently established ICs may also tend to grow somewhat faster. We find that the two samples, drawn from very different surveys two years apart, paint a largely consistent picture.

We further compared the industries associated with the two respondent samples. The largest groups represented in the AMA sample were manufacturers of industrial and consumer goods (17.2 percent), banking and financial institutions (11.3 percent), government and military (11.2 percent), insurance (10.4 percent), utilities (8.1 percent), and health care and medical providers (6.2 percent). The largest groups represented in our own sample were government (15 percent), insurance (13 percent), health care (10 percent), banking and financial (6 percent), and aerospace (6 percent), with other manufacturers scattered in more specific categories. Again, we find the samples draw from the same leading industries. We conclude that our relatively small analytic sample is likely to have good representative diversity.

What were the important reasons in the IS department's decision to implement an IC? Table 2 summarizes. Improving job productivity among end users, control of the proliferation of microcomputers, and improving computer literacy among end users lead among the thirteen listed reasons. Reducing the development backlog, which might have been expected to have been a leading reason [15], is found not to have been, consistent with Carr's [6] findings.

What services does the IC now provide and what services did it provide when it first became operational? As summarized in Table 3, among twenty-one listed services, user training is the most frequently provided service both currently and initially. The service most frequently added since the IC was first established is consulting. Establishing hardware and software standards and establishment of security practices follow behind closely. This supports Henderson and Treacy's [16] argument that EUC requires an evolving management strategy in which education and training issues give way to software standards, security, and, eventually, justification and planning concerns. It suggests also that the IC may be evolving as an innovation as suggested by Swanson [40], and/or that organizations simply tend to elaborate on their original set of IC services over time.

## Analytic Results

What are the correlates of success in the implementation of an IC? We addressed this question by means of correlational and hierarchical regression analyses. We proceeded cautiously, given our relatively small sample size. We began by normalizing and reducing certain of the data described above.

A factor analysis of the important reasons in the decision to adopt an IC identified four significant factors, which we termed (1) the service rationale, (2) the cost rationale, (3) the user-relations rationale, and (4) the control rationale. Together, these factors explain more than half (51 percent) of the total variance in the adoption rationale. Table 6 presents the important details of the analysis. An index was constructed for each of the four factors composed of salient items, as also shown. Given our small sample, both the factors and their indices should be treated with extreme caution, as instability is very likely. Results are unlikely to replicate in subsequent studies (see especially [37]). We nevertheless include them because they reduce our data and offer some exploratory insight. $^{2}$ However, our important findings do not turn upon their inclusion, as we shall see.

The IC services currently provided, the impacts on the organization, and the problems experienced to date, yielded no comparable interpretable factor structures. In the interest of analytic and interpretive simplicity, these data were thus summarized as totals across their items.

Three of the other independent variables—host size at all locations, host size at locations served, and IS staff size—were normalized by logarithmic transformations.

First-order correlation coefficients among the data were next examined. Table 7 summarizes. A variety of significant correlations are seen to exist; there is no evidence of extreme multicollinearity. Notwithstanding our relatively small sample, each variable is significantly correlated with at least one other variable. The number of significant correlations (21 at the 0.05 level) is also much greater than the number (7 at the 0.05 level) that would be expected from chance alone. (Given our small sample size, these significant correlations are also relatively large in magnitude, +/- 0.33 or greater; the analysis thus focuses upon likely larger effects.) Among the significant associations, IC success is significantly correlated with three other variables: the size of the host organization at the locations served, the total number of IC services currently provided, and the total impacts of the IC to date.

Last, a hierarchical regression was performed. We avoided preselection of predictor variables based on significant correlations in the correlation matrix, so as to avoid capitalizing on chance significance [37]. We gave five groups of from one to five predictor variables (sixteen in all) the opportunity to enter the regression equation in a pre-established group sequence. The five groups were sequenced according to their logical and temporal precedence: (1) IS organizational context variables; (2) the rationale for adoption of the IC concept; (3) the date of IC adoption; (4) the current size and scope of the IC and its services; and (5) the impact, problems, and performance evaluation currently associated with the IC. Within each group, only those variables of significance were allowed in the analysis to enter the equation. Table 8 presents the results. IC success is significantly explained ( $R^2$ equals 0.43) by a simple linear combination of two variables: the size of the host organization at the locations served by the IC together with the total number of IC services now provided.

Understandably, given our small sample, the question arises as to whether this two-predictor model would generalize successfully to other samples. To pursue this issue, we computed Herzberg's estimate of the amount of shrinkage in $R^2$ that would have been found had we cross-validated to another sample [37, p. 80]. The computed shrinkage was considerable (35 percent), but the new $R^2$ estimate of 0.28 was still more than half the 0.50 held to be good for social science research. Thus, while other, more elaborate, models may well be devised to predict a greater $R^2$ with larger samples, providing superior explanations, our own two-predictor model is likely to generalize with modest success.

## Discussion

CONTRARY TO OUR WORKING HYPOTHESIS, IC success is not significantly explained by earlier adoption of the innovation. Rather, it is explained in part by the same contextual factor that explained early adoption itself, namely, the size of the host organization at the locations served. Once this factor is taken into account, early adoption makes no contribution to the explanation of IC success.

Among the other groups of independent variables, only the current size and scope of the IC and its services proves to be of significance in the explanation of IC success. Within this group, the total number of IC services is found to be an additional correlate, although of course not necessarily a cause, of success. Other candidate groups of independent variables, representing the rationale for adoption of the IC concept, and the impact, problems, and goals currently associated with the IC, make no additional significant contribution to the explanation of IC success. Given our data, this is perhaps not surprising. In the case of the rationale for adoption of the IC concept, our exploratory factors may, of course, not have been well composed. Regardless, none had a significant first-order correlation with IC success. In the case of the impact, problems and goals associated with the IC, these were the last group of variables among the five given the opportunity to enter the regression equation and $R^{2}$ equal to 0.43 was already explained.

In summary, because of our small sample, our analysis of the effects of candidate independent variables was necessarily limited. Analyses of larger samples might well yield a more elaborate predictive model. Because the representativeness of our organizational sample might also still be questioned (in terms of both the survey population and the low response rate), firm conclusions as to generalizeability should probably also be withheld, pending additional study. Nevertheless, our findings do emphasize for researchers that certain organizational basics dare not be overlooked when one seeks to account for and comprehend the implementation success of an innovation. Organization size and scope are among these basics; their enduring importance is underscored here.

## Conclusion

OUR STUDY OF IC IMPLEMENTATION and its success correlates stands in marked contrast to the current research literature based in the critical success factors approach $[3, 19, 24, 25]$ in which managers have been asked to judge those factors important to their IC success. This success-factors approach is on the surface of obvious practical interest inasmuch as it generates the consensus wisdom of those responsible for achieving IC success. The results of our own study point to a weakness in the critical success-factors approach, however, when it is employed by researchers to explain variance in implementation success.

When managers judge individual success factors, they are of course likely to make these judgments in the specific contexts of their own respective organizations (indeed, they are usually asked to do so, and this is in fact the point of the exercise when the approach is applied for management purposes as originally advocated by Rockart [34]). Any consensus wisdom as to success factors and their importance, therefore, such as those computed in the above research studies, is necessarily an aggregate across organizational contexts. Significantly, such wisdom provides direct guidance to a manager only to the extent that it is essentially invariant, that is, to the extent it offers a universal wisdom. Unfortunately, however, such direct guidance is likely to be weak, as universal wisdom tends to take the form of platitudes (e.g., develop a competent staff, obtain top management support). For this reason, variance in success factors judgments across organizations may offer better potential for informing managers. Here, however, the indirect, contingent guidance that can be extracted from such variance requires both that the variance be systematically developed by means of sharply hewn factors that generate differences in judgments across organizations, and then that this variance be explained in terms of being matched to organizational contexts. This is no small task. In pursuing it, parsimony in the articulation of both factors and contexts should be sought; the most effective contingent guidance is likely to be of a rough-and-ready nature, focusing on relatively few rules of thumb.

While previous research explains little variance in success factor judgments in terms of organizational contexts, the present research demonstrates in contrast the importance of organizational context, in the form of organizational size and scope, in explaining variance in actual reported success. Why might this be the case? Upon reflection, there should perhaps be little surprise. In judging their success factors, managers apparently tend to focus on certain generic factors, such as “top management support,” which constitute the influencable variables they perceive to be important. They tend to take certain other factors, specifically, their own environmental and contextual constants, such as organizational size, for granted. As a consequence, although these “constants” are in fact important variables within the overall population of organizations, they tend not to be directly represented in the success factors identified by individual managers. Certain contextual success factors are apparently systematically missed, given the nature of the approach.

Nevertheless, it is less clear why variance in reported success factors should not be explainable in part in terms of varying organizational contexts. If organizational context is important, as it surely is, one would expect it to impact at least indirectly upon success factor judgments. If it does not, we would be left with universal wisdom only, one without pointed implications for managers who presumably need to understand and respond to the particular organizational circumstances in which they find themselves. The challenge to success-factor research, both for ICs and for other organizational innovations, is therefore to establish that it yields more than a set of managerial platitudes when applied to a population of organizations. This can be done, we propose, only by joining it with analysis of the form reported here, whereby organizational contexts are examined, and, beyond the present work, whereby multiple independent assessments of success are also similarly incorporated into the analysis.

This is worth doing too, we suggest. While our own analysis establishes organizational context to be of importance to IC success, it fails in its own way to identify those managerial implementation tactics (or other controllable variables beyond the services themselves) of corresponding contingent significance. This remains to be accomplished one way or another, as Nutt [29] also reminds us from his own implementation studies. Only when organizational context and implementation tactics are joined together are we likely to provide an actionable contingency theory for the implementation of IS innovations. Only then are we likely to achieve a deeper understanding of the key critical success factors.
