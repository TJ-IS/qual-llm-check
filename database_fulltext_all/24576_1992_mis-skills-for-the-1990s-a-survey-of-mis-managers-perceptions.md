# MIS Skills for the 1990s: A Survey of MIS Managers' Perceptions

ROBERT L. LEITHEISER

ROBERT L. LEITHEISER is Associate Professor of Management (MIS) in the College of Business and Economics at the University of Wisconsin–Whitewater. He received his Ph.D. in management information systems and an M.B.A. from the University of Minnesota. Dr. Leitheiser has held various industry positions and has published papers in the Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Information and Management, and Data Base. His current research interests include management of information systems, end-user computing, behavioral aspects of systems development, and interface design.

ABSTRACT: The effective management of human resources is an important challenge for MIS executives. Part of the challenge is obtaining and retaining MIS personnel with the skills and knowledge required to support the organization effectively. The purpose of this paper is to provide managers and educators with information on the current and future demand for MIS professionals and with information about the relative importance of specific MIS skills. To address these issues a survey of MIS managers was undertaken in the summer of 1990. The results include projections of demand for ten MIS job types. Also reported are the perceptions of importance of fifty-four skills for systems developers and twenty-six technical specialist skills. A model is proposed for explaining perceptions of skill need. Implications of the results are discussed for businesses, educational institutions, and researchers.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: demand for MIS professionals, human resources for MIS, MIS skills.

## 1. Introduction

INFORMATION SYSTEMS AND DATA RESOURCES have become increasingly important to business organizations. To meet the information processing needs of their firms, MIS managers must manage their human resources effectively. One of the most important challenges for managers is obtaining a MIS staff with requisite skills and knowledge [5, 8].

Universities and other schools of higher learning are being challenged to produce job candidates with skills and knowledge that are in demand. Unfortunately, there is evidence that differences in perceptions between MIS management and academics could affect the ability of educational institutions to respond to these needs [3].

Acknowledgment: This study was supported in part by the members of the MIS Consortium at the University of Wisconsin–Milwaukee.

The current study was undertaken to help MIS managers better understand their skill requirements and to provide a means for communicating those requirements to academic institutions. The following three questions are addressed:

1. What will demand be for specific MIS job categories over the next ten years?

2. What will be the most important skills for systems developers over the next ten years?

3. What will be the most important MIS technical specialist skills over the next ten years?

These questions were attacked by collecting field survey data from a cross-section of businesses in the upper midwest.

## 2. Related Research

## 2.1. MIS Demand

THE DEMAND FOR MIS PROFESSIONALS has been projected by the U.S. Department of Labor [16] to be much higher than average through the year 2000 (see figure 1). In fact, employment in the major MIS job categories of computer systems analysts, computer programmers, and computer and peripheral equipment operators is expected to grow 53 percent, 48 percent, and 29 percent, respectively, between 1988 and 2000. The analyst and programmer growth rates placed these two job categories on the U.S. Department of Labor's (DOL) list of the twenty fastest-growing occupations [13].

Department of Labor estimates were derived from consideration of four underlying forces $[7]$ . The first is the demand for computing services, that is, for computer operations and systems development. Estimates of service demand are based on projections of computer use by organizations. The second consideration is the technology and methods used to deliver these services. Improvements in technology result in the delivery of more services with the same number of employees. Productivity is combined with demand for services to produce an estimate of the demand for MIS professionals. The third element in the equation is the makeup of industries. If the economy shifts to industries that are heavy users of information systems, then the demand for computer services will grow at a rate that is faster than the overall growth of the economy. Finally, overall economic growth is factored in. The Department of Labor projects three scenarios: low, moderate, and high growth. The figures cited here are for moderate growth.

In addition to the growth rates, the other characteristic of MIS job demand that is of interest is the makeup of the employment. The largest segment is programming, with 42–43 percent of forecast positions. The DOL does not expect much change in the demand mix for the 1990s.

The projections of demand by job category present an aggregate picture of skill needs but do not help educators or managers with specific requirements. To address this issue, studies have been done to determine the usefulness of specific skills.

## 2.2. MIS Skills

The skills required for MIS professionals are determined by the tasks they are to accomplish, the organizational environment in which they operate, and the technology with which they work. Efforts have been made to identify important skills and to instill them in MIS professionals.

In 1972 the ACM Curriculum Committee on Computer Education for Management proposed a curriculum for a master's level program in information systems development [2]. After analyzing the development process and the attributes of information systems in organizations, the group of educators and practitioners generated a list of thirty-seven skills and abilities that students in a graduate MIS program should expect to acquire. The skills were placed in one of six categories: people, models, systems, computers, organizations, and society.

A program of study was proposed to meet these skill requirements. The list of skills was reviewed and slightly modified in 1982 [14]. The Data Processing Management Association has also analyzed the skills and knowledge needed for MIS professionals and proposed its own curriculum [9].

Given that firms and schools have limited resources, it is important to identify the skills that should receive the most emphasis. Studies by Henry, Dickson, and LaSalle $[12]$ and Benbasat, Dexter, and Mantha $[4]$ examined the perceived importance of ACM MIS skill categories for systems analysts. Both studies found that samples of MIS professionals rated “people” skills above “systems” and “computer” skills. A similar study by Cheney and Lyons [6] used different specific skills and skill categories and found that “systems design” skills were most important. A separate “people” category was not evaluated.

Benbasat et al. [4] also examined the influence of MIS organizational maturity (based on [10]) on the perceived usefulness of skills. They found that MIS managers in more mature organizations rated generalist skills (e.g., organizations, people, and society) as being more useful than did managers in less mature organizations. No differences were found for specialist skills and managers from both types of firms rated generalist skills above specialist skills.

Two more recent studies examined perceptions of skill importance but did not aggregate results by category. Green [11] examined systems analysts' perceptions of importance for twenty-one skills. Many of the highest-rated skills were interpersonal. Interestingly, users did not rate interpersonal skills as highly as the analysts did. Green also found that only four of the twenty-one skills were rated differently by public and private sector systems analysts.

The second study was done using a group decision support system to iteratively rank skills for new MIS hires $[18]$ . The final ranking for new systems analysts placed “systems analysis/design” skills first and “interpersonal” skills second in importance. Similar rankings were done for programmers and end-user support personnel. The interpersonal skills category was rated first for the support personnel but only tenth for programmers.

All of the above studies used logically derived lists of skills. Two studies attempted to determine the skills needed in systems development empirically. In one study, researchers attempted to identify the dimensions of job behavior that supervisors actually used to evaluate systems developers $[1]$ . Twelve major dimensions were identified and ordered in terms of importance. While technical knowledge was at the top of this list, it was immediately followed by maintaining customer relations (a “people” skill). A similar ranking for programmer/analysts placed customer relations and communications skills further down the list.

Vitalari [17] attempted to learn the categories of knowledge/skill that were actually used in the development process by systems analysts. The researcher defined 252 categories of knowledge that might be relevant to the systems analysis process. Evidence of use of these categories was sought in “talk aloud” protocols produced by analysts as they solved an example problem. The more frequently knowledge of a particular category is used, Vitalari argued, the more important that knowledge is in the analysis task. In general, categories related to determining the requirements of the system were mentioned more often than those used for considering people and organizational issues. This emphasis may, however, be the result of the experimental setting.

In summary, the prior research into MIS skills has found that MIS managers and systems analysts believe that people and organizational skills are more important to job success than technical skills. This does not mean, however, that they believe that technical knowledge is unimportant. The Vitalari study demonstrates the need for technical knowledge when performing systems analysis tasks.

## 3. MIS Skill Needs Model

DETERMINING A FIRM'S REQUIREMENTS for MIS professionals includes consideration of organizational, process, and technological factors. Figure 2 introduces a model that describes these relationships. The central idea behind the model is that the need for MIS skills is dependent on (1) the need for MIS services by the firm and (2) the type and quantity of skills required to provide those services. Other factors strongly influence these primary model components.

The need for MIS services may be defined as the demand for systems development, systems operation, technical support, and other special services. Special services may include information centers, planning, internal auditing, research and development, and the like. Skill needs can be derived by estimating the amount and type of skills required to deliver these services.

Two major influences on skill demand are characteristics of the organization and characteristics of available technology. Opportunities for using information services are defined by the business processes in an organization. The processes of a service organization are significantly different from those of a manufacturing concern. Only the latter, for example, is likely to need computer-aided design. Further influencing this demand are competition, government regulations, and the organization's growth and size.

Two studies found evidence for organizational impacts on skill importance. Benbasat et al. [4] found that organizational maturity was related to the perceived importance of generalist skills for systems analysts. Green [11] found that analysts and users in public sector organizations have a somewhat higher regard for technical skills than their counterparts in private firms. Many other organizational characteristics may be hypothesized to affect skill requirements.

The characteristics of available technology affect both the need for MIS services and the skills required to meet this need. For example, the availability of data communications and LAN technologies creates the opportunity for a firm to use electronic mail. These same technologies create a need for MIS personnel who understand how to use the technologies.

The ability of a technology to influence service demand and requirements depends on its cost, performance, and risk. The rate of development of the technology will determine when, if ever, the technology will impact on MIS skill needs. More advanced technologies present higher risks and take longer to impact on skill needs.

A final element of the model is the constraints component. When projecting MIS skill needs, managers are likely to consider resource, legal, and other constraints and adjust their estimates based on these factors.

The need for MIS skills is reflected in a number of variables. Three are used in this study: First, MIS demand is expressed as the number of professionals employed in particular job categories (e.g., number of application programmers). Second, demand is represented by the perceptions of importance of specific skills for systems development personnel. Finally, skill demand is reflected in the perceptions of importance of particular technical specialist skills.

## 4. Methodology

THE PRIMARY PURPOSE OF THE STUDY is to examine the outputs of the model: that is, (1) to describe the current and projected demand for MIS job categories, and (2) to describe current perceptions of skill importance and how importance is expected to change over the next decade. In order to achieve these ends a field study was designed and executed in the summer of 1990.

The first step was to develop an instrument that would meet the study's goals. The final instrument had three primary sections (examples of items are given in appendix 1). The first section asked for a firm's current and projected employment for ten MIS job categories (e.g., systems analysts, application programmers, etc.). The purpose of this section was to provide information on the current job type mix and on expected changes for these ten job categories.

The second section required that respondents rate the importance of a set of fifty-four skills for their “application development personnel.” The rationale for this section was to determine the importance of various systems analysis, programming, interpersonal, and business skills for employees who build application systems. The rating scale was the same as that used by Benbasat et al. [4].

The third, and last, major section asked respondents to rate the importance of a set of twenty-six specialist skills. The purpose of this section is to judge the demand for specific technical competencies that might be met by hiring or contracting technical specialists.

The skill items were created by reviewing the skill lists from the ACM Curriculum Committee [2, 14], previous empirical studies [4, 6], and a list of important technologies [15]. One desired outcome of the study was to provide detailed results that would help in the design of MIS training, curriculum, and courses. This goal led the researcher to include more technology-specific skills (e.g., programming in COBOL) than were used elsewhere (e.g., [2, 11]).

A pilot study was performed using the MIS executives of twelve leading local firms. These participants were mailed the questionnaire and later given a reminder phone call to ensure a timely response. No problems were found with the questionnaire so it was used in the main data collection step.

Three hundred and twenty midwestern MIS managers were mailed the questionnaire with a cover letter explaining the study. The addresses of the managers were obtained from a commercially available mailing list. Ninety-five usable responses were obtained, for a 30 percent response rate. This rate was deemed acceptable and understandable given the length of the questionnaire and inaccuracies in the mailing list.

Since the same questionnaire and process were used in the pilot study as in the primary mailing, the data from the initial twelve firms were added to the set to produce a usable sample of 107 MIS managers. A breakdown of respondent firms by size and industry is given in Table 1.

## 5. Results

## 5.1. Job Category Needs

THE OVERALL DEMAND FOR EMPLOYEES is shown in figure 3 and Table 2. The totals are not as important as the growth rates and the relative levels of demand for different job categories. Overall, demand for MIS professionals was predicted to be 23.1 percent higher in 1995 as compared to 1990, and another 20.3 percent higher for 2000.

For specific job categories, the expected increase in demand for application programmers for the decade is 14 percent while that for systems analysts is 87 percent. The implication of this difference is that, while application programmer is currently the largest category of employment, it will be displaced after 1995 by systems analyst. By 2000, the sample firms expect to employ 17 percent more systems analysts than application programmers. This is an important shift in employment for the MIS field.

In the other categories, systems programmers are expected to increase at a much faster rate than application programmers (+63 percent), but that is from a much smaller base of employees. Computer equipment operators are expected to decline slightly (-6 percent) in employment over the same time period.

Some of the specialized MIS job categories are expected to grow at rates even higher than that for systems analysts. End-user support personnel may be up 89 percent by the year 2000. MIS planning specialists are expected to grow by 88 percent. In the technical specialties, database specialists are projected to be up 124 percent, telecommunication specialists up 92 percent, and artificial intelligence specialists up by 582 percent. The dramatic increase in the latter group is from a very small base. Only 0.6 percent of the current total employment by the 107 sample firms is in the artificial intelligence category.

The differences in importance between Business, Programming, and Analysis/Design Skill categories were usually too small to pick one category over another with confidence. ## 5.2.2. Specific Developer Skills

Substantial and interesting differences exist within skill categories. For example, a large initial divergence in importance is reported for the analysis and design skills but the variation diminishes as managers project importance into the future (see figure 5). Skills associated with performing cost/benefit analyses, feasibility studies, conceptual designs, and information requirements determination are considered important today and remain important in the future. Semantic data modeling and object-oriented analysis and design are not considered very important in 1990 but are projected to become more useful over the next ten years. The most dramatic gain, however, is in the ability to use Computer Aided Software Engineering (CASE) tools. This skill increases dramatically in importance until by the end of the decade it is expected to be one of the most important skills possessed by systems developers.

All of the programming skills were considered moderately important, with documentation being most important for 1990 and 1995. While most of the skills were projected to maintain a constant level of importance, there were two exceptions. The ability to prototype applications was seen to grow in importance until it becomes one of the most important skills in 1995 and 2000. Knowledge of a specific systems development methodology also grows in importance over the projected period.

The interpersonal skills of listening, working with others, and writing are the three highest-rated skills for all three periods. Persuading others, presenting, responding to others' emotions, and training are also considered very important. The only skill in this set whose importance is seen to decline is the ability to work alone to accomplish a task. The message of these results is that teamwork and interpersonal communication are viewed as keys to development success in both the short and long terms.

Most of the Business Skills trend upward in an almost identical way. The ability to foresee problems with new technologies and the understanding of a business function become two of the most important skills by the year 2000. The skill in this category that increases the fastest in importance is the ability to identify applications that provide competitive advantage.

A very dramatic pattern exists for skills within the Computing Environment category (see figure 6). The ability to develop systems for a mainframe environment is one of the most important skills in 1990 but drops off dramatically over the subsequent two time periods. Replacing mainframe environment skills in importance are skills in personal computer and multiple platform environments. The importance of developing applications for Systems Application Architecture (SAA) and UNIX environments is also expected to increase rapidly. UNIX skills, however, are still not expected to be as important as other environments in the year 2000.

In spite of the low level of the importance given to the Language category, there are some very important individual skills within this skill set. Knowledge of COBOL and Job Control Languages are seen as essential for 1990. They drop rapidly, however, over the other two time periods. Meanwhile, knowledge of fourth-generation languages and SQL becomes crucial by 1995 and 2000. In fact, for the year 2000 these two languages are considered to be two of the most important skills for systems developers. Programming in C, object-oriented languages (e.g., C++ or Smalltalk), and artificial intelligence languages are expected to increase markedly in importance in the coming years to the point where they are as important as COBOL. Finally, for firms in this study, knowledge of Ada is not considered very useful. Perhaps a sample that included more defense contractors would have produced a different result. From these results, it may be concluded that skills in a few specific languages are seen as essential, but a broad knowledge of many languages is not that important.

Most of the individual application skills trend significantly upward for 1995 and 2000. The only exception is the ability to design hierarchical and network databases which has a modest decline for each time period. The ability to design relational databases rises until it is one of the most important skills for the year 2000. One of the fastest increases in skill importance is that projected for the ability to develop expert systems.

## 5.3.2. Specific Specialist Skills

The patterns of skill ratings vary tremendously for different skill categories (figure 8). Physical database design, data communication systems, local area networks, and electronic data interchange are specific skills in the Database/Data Communications category that are also top ten skills for each of the projected years. Most of the skills are viewed as modestly increasing in importance over the decade. Exceptions are electronic data interchange (EDI) and local area network (LAN) skills which are seen as dramatically increasing in importance between 1990 and 2000.

The importance of most of the specific Software skills is expected to increase substantially between 1990 and 1995. This is especially true for the Application Generator, Graphical Interface, and CASE skills, which become some of the most important specialist skills for 1995 or 2000. Systems programming stands out as the only skill in this category that is viewed as becoming less important. One interesting skill that was rated very highly was expertise in designing user-friendly systems. This finding suggests a high level of concern for making systems easy to learn and use.

The Hardware skills vary tremendously in importance and trend. Hardware Integration is rated as one of the most important skills for all three time periods. The ability to use supercomputers, on the other hand, is rated as one of the lowest. Skills in using optical storage, parallel processing, and hardware integration are projected to increase substantially in importance over the decade while hardware maintenance skills remain constant. Clearly, the importance of hardware skills depends greatly on the technology and specific need.

## 6. Conclusions

## 6.1. Research Questions

THIS PAPER ATTEMPTED TO ANSWER three research questions about the demand for MIS skills. The first question concerned the demand expected for specific MIS job categories over the next ten years. The results were summarized in Table 2 and figure 3.

It is interesting to compare the results of this survey with Department of Labor projections. For the current study, the fastest-growing primary job category is Systems Analyst, which is projected to grow by 87 percent in this decade. The Department of Labor projects growth for the same period at less than half that level (+41 percent $^{3}$ ). The expected growth in demand for all programming (i.e., application and systems programming) over the decade is projected by this study to be +21 percent, which is only a little more than half of what the DOL is projecting (+37 percent).

The operator job category has one of the greatest discrepancies because the surveyed managers predicted a 4 percent drop in the employment while the DOL forecast a 23 percent increase. In this case, part of the difference may be due to different definitions for “computer operator.” The Department of Labor’s definition may be broader than that used by most respondents in this study.

Reasons for the other differences may come from the very different methodologies used to produce each set of estimates. First, the figures for this study are based on the perceptions of MIS managers. No explicit underlying model of the economy or of job growth was used. Each manager used his or her own model and assumptions.

Second, these numbers reflect growth only in the sample firms. Overall MIS job growth is a combination of the growth in requirements for existing firms plus the requirements of newly created firms. Only the former is represented in the survey numbers but both make up part of the Department of Labor forecasts. Perhaps the skill demands for new firms are different from those for established firms (e.g., do they hire relatively more programmers than systems analysts?).

Finally, the data reported here come from one region of the country: the upper midwest. The DOL figures are for the whole nation, which may differ from the midwest. Interestingly, both of the estimates of total growth for the primary job categories (programmers, systems analysts, and operators) are relatively close: +35 percent for the Department of Labor and +34 percent for the current study. The difference between them is in the distribution of job types. Only time will tell whether the perceptions of 107 MIS managers or the modeling by analysts in the Department of Labor will produce results that more accurately reflect the future demand for MIS skills.

It should also be noted here that projecting employment out ten years is an uncertain process. Given that many firms do long-range planning for four- to five-year periods, the 1995 estimates may be reasonable guesses. The estimates for the year 2000 must be taken with some degree of caution. The only claim made for the study results is that they accurately represent the expectations of experienced MIS managers.

The second question asked what the most important skills will be for systems developers over the next ten years. Appendix 2 lists systems development skills in order of importance for 1990. The importance of categories of skills and of skills within categories are shown in figures 4, 5, and 6. The emphasis on the importance of interpersonal skills is consistent with findings from earlier studies (e.g., [4, 11, 12]). Business skills, programming skills, and analysis/design skills were grouped next in level of importance for systems developers.

The third question asked about the most important technical specialist skills. Appendix 3 contains a list of technical specialist skills ranked by importance for 1990. Figures 7 and 8 show the relative importance placed on technical specialist skill categories and individual skills. Database and data communication skills tended to be rated as more important than other skills. Other specific skills that are viewed as becoming essential are CASE, graphical user interfaces, and image processing.

## 6.2. Implications

The results of the study have implications for businesses, educational institutions, and researchers. Firms can expect to increase their employment in some job categories more than in others. By the end of the decade, there will likely be a shift to employing more systems analysts than application programmers. Furthermore, businesses should consider the level of importance placed on interpersonal skills by the survey's respondents, and evaluate their training programs and selection procedures in light of this finding.

Educational institutions must continually adjust their programs to meet the changing needs of the economy. The results of the study suggest that some development skills that are crucial now (e.g., COBOL programming and mainframe environments) will drop rapidly in importance over the next ten years. Other skills are expected to increase in importance dramatically (e.g., CASE, prototyping, electronic data interchange, LAN, and image processing). Steps should be taken to prepare students for those changes. In general, the employment picture for MIS graduates is expected to remain healthy until at least the end of the century.

One strong implication that springs from the findings is that academic institutions should focus on developing interpersonal and business skills, as well as technical skills. Writing, speaking, persuading, working with others, and understanding others' emotions are all important to the success of systems development. This is not a new result, but one that needs to be reemphasized.

Researchers are offered a number of interesting avenues for investigation. Many factors affect actual and perceived MIS skill demand. The model presented in figure 2 offers a means of identifying, organizing, and exploring these factors. One important question concerns the difference between managers' perceptions of skill demand and actual organizational needs. The results of the current study are based on perceptions and should be interpreted as such. Would objective measures of such factors as MIS service needs and service requirements produce the same results? Another avenue of research concerns the affect of different levels of interpersonal skill on systems development success. Are managers' perceptions of the importance of interpersonal skills valid? How much of the variance in the quality of systems development is due to differences in interpersonal skill level?

The most important MIS resources are human resources. Better management of these resources is essential for MIS success in the next decade. This paper has been an attempt to increase our understanding of required MIS skills so that managers and educators may better prepare for the challenges of the 1990s.
