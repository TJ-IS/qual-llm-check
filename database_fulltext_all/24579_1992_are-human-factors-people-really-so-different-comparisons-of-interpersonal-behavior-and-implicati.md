# Are Human-Factors People Really So Different? Comparisons of Interpersonal Behavior and Implications for Design Teams

POPPY LAURETTA MCLEOD

POPPY LAURETTA MCLEOD has been Assistant Professor of Organizational Behavior at the University of Michigan's School of Business Administration since 1985, after completing her Ph.D. in social psychology at Harvard University in 1985. Her current research focuses on the impact of technology, interpersonal behavior, and diversity on the effectiveness of small work teams.

ABSTRACT: This study examined differences between human-factors and machine-factors computer professionals in self-reports of their interpersonal behaviors. Three hundred and eleven computer professionals completed an instrument describing their jobs and three categories of their interpersonal behavior—Dominance, Friendliness, and Task versus Socio-Emotional. Human-factors professionals described their ideal and actual behavior as more dominant than that of other computer professionals. No differences were found for the other two behavior dimensions. Human-factors professionals on the average were more involved with research while machine-oriented professionals were more involved with product development. The findings are discussed with respect to their importance for computer system design teams. It is suggested that interpersonal factors may affect the utilization of human-factors experts.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: human factors, individual differences, MIS professionals.

WITHOUT QUESTION THE SUCCESS OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS (IS) depends on human factors. Over the years numerous writers have lamented that this truth was not self-evident to the system design community [23, 31, 65, 66, 67, 75]. More recent work has, however, noted with optimism the increasing attention to human factors in the system design process [12, 17, 58, 50, 70].

Acknowledgments: The support of the Division of Research at the University of Michigan Graduate School of Business Administration is gratefully acknowledged. I thank Marilyn Mantei for assistance with data collection and analysis and comments on earlier versions of the manuscript, Kathleen Culver-Lozo and Javier Lerch for assistance with data coding, Carol Mohr for editorial assistance, and Sharon Lobel, Janet Weiss, Dennis Severance, Randy Cooper, and several anonymous reviewers for helpful comments.

Ideally, system development teams will include members with human-factors expertise $[24]$ , but the human-factors professional has traditionally not been viewed favorably by system designers. For example, Hammond et al. $[30]$ interviewed designers and found that, even in organizations where there was a human-factors department, the designers rarely used it because they felt that human factors were unimportant or that the department did not offer real help. Gaines and Shaw $[24]$ noted that human-factors experts are seldom fully involved in system design. They compare human-factors experts to medical specialists who “only get called in when there are problems.”

There are nevertheless signs that the human-factors expert is an increasingly welcome contributor to system design. King [50] describes the current system design climate as one of increasing “dialogue between the users and DP.” He says further that “[In] this type of environment, transfers of staff between DP and user organizations become attractive and productive” (p. 155). Thus, the human-factors expert who is an integral part of system design teams is becoming more common.

This traditional distance between system designers and human-factors experts might suggest there are differences in personal characteristics between these two groups that may be associated with communication difficulties between them. Such differences would raise implications for the functioning of design teams that include both human- and machine-factors experts.

The current study asks the broad questions, in what ways do the personal characteristics of human-factors experts differ from those of machine-factors experts, and in what ways do these differences affect design team dynamics? The distinction between “human factors” and “machine factors” is based on Christie’s [12] definition of human factors as “all the aspects of using a system that affect what users understand about the system, how they feel about the system, and how they behave in regard to the system” (p. 7). The machine-factors expert, then, is someone whose work tends not to require much direct concern with the user.

There is broad agreement in the system design literature that the end user's point of view must be considered in order for systems to be successful [9, 50, 72, 73, 85]. Although the empirical evidence has been mixed [40], most writers recommend that users be directly and fully involved in the system development process [72]. In the absence of direct user involvement, the human-factors expert can often represent the user perspective.

## Human Factors versus Machine Factors

MUCH HAS BEEN WRITTEN ABOUT THE CHARACTERISTICS of IS professionals. Major topics have been their personalities [7, 13, 22, 57, 77, 83], their cognitive style [33, 44, 47, 48, 71], their values and attitudes [55, 54, 65, 82], the factors that motivate them [19, 20, 22, 27], and stress [39]. Most of these studies have either compared IS to non-IS people or have compared IS people across subspecialties or ranks. None has specifically investigated human-factors experts.

We might expect human-factors experts to differ from machine-factors experts because of differences in their educational backgrounds. Human-factors specialists typically do not receive their training in computer science, but rather are trained in psychology and related disciplines. Moreover, people with backgrounds in computer science, particularly at the undergraduate level, typically get little exposure to human factors as part of their education. For example, Booth et al. [10], in describing what topics a curriculum in computer design should contain, mentioned very briefly “social impact” as an integral part of the design process. Gibbs and Tucker [26] presented a model curriculum for a liberal arts degree in computer science that did not mention any subjects that could be construed easily as incorporating human-factors principles. Koffman et al. [51, 52] recommended curricula for the CS1 and CS2 courses that contained no human-factors attention. The most recent curriculum recommendations, however, do suggest that human-computer interaction should be a core part of the undergraduate computer science curriculum [16, 79].

The organizational and personnel psychology literatures have shown that educational and vocational choice are correlated with attitudes, personality, cognitive style, and behavior [35, 42]. Even in the IS literature, Peter Keen's work has shown that differences in professional discipline and training are related to differences in cognitive style [47]. Kumar and Bjorn-Andersen [54] have argued that the educational backgrounds of system designers are related to their value systems.

Holland [35], whose work on career choice is widely cited, views vocational and educational interests as an “expression of personality.” He has argued that people will tend to choose educational and vocational career environments whose characteristics tend to be compatible with their personality types. Holland’s theory explains the common observation that occupations tend to be populated by a preponderance of people of specific personality, value, and cognitive types [49]. For example, Kumar and Welke [55] and Kumar and Bjorn-Andersen [54] found that IS professionals held a higher concentration of economic and technical values than users, whose values were more social and political. Thus, the educational differences between machine- and human-factors experts might lead to expectations of differences in interpersonal styles.

## Interpersonal Behavior

THE MAJORITY OF SYSTEMS DEVELOPERS' time is spent communicating with other people [53], and IS professionals, no less than people in other occupations, value good interpersonal relations on project teams [81, 82]. The important role that the mix of personal characteristics on work teams plays in group process and communication has been demonstrated in past research. Hurst, Rush, and White [34] have shown that the mix of cognitive styles among members of top management teams affects organizational innovation. Jackson [41] and Jackson et al. [42] summarized literature on the effects of the mix of personal characteristics of team members on group process and performance. Jackson [41] reports, for instance, that the effects of demographic and functional heterogeneity depend on the nature of the group task. For tasks requiring creativity, such as system design, heterogeneity leads to higher-quality outcomes than homogeneity.

Studies that specifically address computer system design teams have reported similar results [17]. For example, Kaiser and Bostrom [45] and White [82] studied system design team composition based on Jung's psychological types. Both studies found that teams with members of all types were more successful than were teams in which all types were not represented. On the other hand, differences in personality or cognitive style can lead to communication difficulties and conflict [80]. Thus, the benefits of involving human-factors experts or end users in the design process depend on managing the communications and interpersonal relationships with designers effectively [11, 14, 15, 78, 84].

Past research on the effects of personal characteristics on the functioning of system design teams has focused on relatively intangible constructs such as cognitive style [45, 81] or values [55, 54, 82]. The current study, however, focuses on behaviors because these are the manifestation of values, attitudes, personality traits, and cognitive style. Knowing another person's cognitive style, for example, is important for understanding behaviors and for choosing a response, but it is to the actual behavior that one must respond. Thus, I may understand that my boss doesn't come talk to me because he is an introvert, but I still must respond to the fact that he doesn't talk to me.

The literature on interpersonal behavior and small-group psychology has identified a number of basic dimensions of behavior that govern interpersonal interactions. Interpersonal theory has a long history in the field of psychology $[8]$ . Birtchnell has traced this history and consolidated the major findings. His work, taken together with the work of the major group theorists $[4, 29, 76]$ , suggests three dimensions of interpersonal behavior that are basic to the functioning of task groups.

The first dimension of interpersonal behavior important in groups is ascendency or dominance. This dimension most often captures leadership, participation, and social hierarchy in groups. According to Shaw [76], “individuals vary in the extent to which they wish to be prominent....Persons who possess the personality characteristics associated with ascendency...attempt leadership, participate in group activities” (p. 197).

The second dimension that is key to interpersonal group process is affiliation, often correlated with cohesion. Simply put, group members need to affiliate and interact with each other. This dimension also tends to capture friendliness and attraction. Shaw [76] suggests that affiliation behavior will be associated with positive effects in groups. Levine and Moreland [56] similarly argue that affiliation behavior is a key determinant of group cohesion.

The final dimension is the classic distinction between task and maintenance behaviors, sometimes also referred to as task versus socioemotional behaviors $[2]$ . Quality of emotional expression has been found to be an important correlate of behavior in groups $[25]$ . Hackman $[29]$ has argued that both appropriate interpersonal and task skills are needed for effectiveness in a group. This dimension captures the fundamental activities of groups—accomplishing their work while keeping members motivated.

The current study examined human- and machine-factors experts for systematic differences relative to these three broad dimensions of interpersonal behavior. In the following sections, the relevance of these dimensions for human- and machine-factors experts and system design teams is discussed. The study hypotheses are also presented.

## Ascendency/Dominance

The behaviors captured by this dimension are related to participation, influence, and introversion/extroversion. In group interactions, dominant people are active and talkative. Robey and Farrow [72] and Robey, Farrow, and Franz [73] have demonstrated the relevance of this behavioral dimension for conflict and conflict resolution in system development teams. They note that it is the ineffective handling of conflict rather than the mere presence of conflict that is negative. Using data gathered from a major system development project, they showed that participation and influence had a positive effect on conflict resolution. That is, the people who were more dominant and influential were better able to help in resolving group conflicts.

King [50] presents further arguments for the importance of dominance and influence in system development teams. The so-called “egoless” programming teams—teams with no formal leader—have been effective in very few cases. According to King, without someone to provide direction, the teams lack motivation and “drift aimlessly if a natural leader does not emerge, and in most cases one does not.” Thus, the ability to exert influence and leadership—dominant behavior—is an important interpersonal skill. Shneiderman [77] also notes that “the assertive individual who is . . . not intimidated easily . . . is often seen as the superior programmer type” (p. 55).

Also discussed below, the social-need strength of IS professionals has been found to be lower than that of the average population [7]. The samples in the studies that Bartol and Martin reviewed generally would fall within the machine-oriented category used in the present study. Social-need strength is a personality characteristic associated with ascendent and affiliation behaviors [7]; thus, we might expect the ascendent or dominant behaviors of machine-factors experts to be lower than that of human-factors experts who probably resemble the general population more closely than do machine-factors experts.

Further, Goldstein and Rockart [27] note that the training of programmer/analysts typically does not include leadership skills. King [50] also noted that most computer science curricula have not taught people team and leadership skills. Although curricula today do teach these skills more often, the time frame of Goldstein and Rockart's and King's reports best fits the sample used in this study. The following hypothesis will thus be tested:

H1: Human-factors experts will report more dominant behavior than machine-factors experts.

## Affiliation/Friendliness

This dimension comprises behaviors that are positive, other-oriented, egalitarian, and cooperative versus behaviors that are negative, individualistic, and uncooperative. The small-group and organization literature generally and the system development literature more specifically show this dimension to be important for project group effectiveness. Behaviors along this dimension can have an impact on work motivation, as shown by need theories of motivation. Needs for affiliation, acceptance, and inclusion are important motivators $[1, 60, 61]$ for some people, and quality of interpersonal relations with co-workers and supervisors is a factor that contributes to job satisfaction $[32, 82]$ .

Although some evidence exists that computer professionals have lower social needs than the average population $[7]$ , the stereotype of the misanthropic programmer has not been consistently supported by the literature $[19, 20, 21, 36]$ . Ferratt and Short $[20]$ have argued that the motivational patterns of IS professionals do not differ from the patterns of other people, once organizational level is taken into account.

In a study of job satisfaction among system professionals, Goldstein and Rockart [27] report that supervisor support (e.g., extent to which supervisor is friendly and easy to approach) correlated significantly with job satisfaction. Shneiderman [77] suggests that “a friendly, warm, cooperative style will be helpful in team programming” (p. 55). And Mantei [59], in discussing structured programming teams, suggests that friendliness is important for relations with clients.

In light of the changes occurring in the computer profession since the publication of Bartol and Martin's [7] review, we are likely to see machine-factors experts who are more sensitive to other people than in the past. The study will thus test the following hypothesis:

H2: There is no difference between human-factors experts and machine-factors experts in reports of friendly behaviors.

## Task versus Socioemotional

This dimension refers to behaviors that are controlled, serious, and task-oriented versus behaviors that are expressive, light-hearted, and emotional. This dimension captures the classic distinction between task versus socioemotional behaviors from the small-group literature [2]. This dimension also correlates with the thinking–feeling dimension in the Jungian typology [69], used frequently in the system development literature [45, 44, 46, 81]. The preference for a thinking approach to decision making—using impersonal bases for making decisions—does not mean these individuals do not experience deep emotions. Rather, it means that they are not likely to be as demonstrative as a feeling type—one who prefers to use personal criteria for making decisions [49]. Groups need both types of behavior in order to be effective [3-].

Two studies in particular support the need for balance between task and socioemotional types on system development teams. Kaiser and Bostrom [45] and White [81] both found that teams with all thinking and no feeling types were less effective than were teams that had both types represented. Kaiser and Srinivasan [46] and Kaiser [44] present data suggesting that the proportion of feeling types among computer personnel is lower than in the general population. Research on the distribution of Jungian types suggests that, in technical fields such as computer programming, accounting, and engineering, contain fewer feeling than thinking types even though there are equal proportions of these two types in the general population [49]. Comparing human-factors experts to machine-factors experts would lead to the prediction that the latter are less emotionally expressive than the former. This study thus tests the following hypothesis:

H3: Human-factors experts will report more emotionally expressive behavior than will machine-factors experts.

## Job Characteristics

THE WORK OF FERRATT AND SHORT [19, 20] has demonstrated the importance of taking job context into account in studies of the characteristics of computer professionals. The current study also examined differences between human-factors and machine-factors experts in characteristic job requirements. Four characteristics were studied. First was the actual extent to which human- and machine-factors experts regularly worked as part of a team.

Second was the degree of management responsibility held. Many writers have criticized the IS profession for failing to train people sufficiently in managerial skills [77]. Goldstein and Rockart [27] call for increased attention to developing the IS person's managerial skills. King [50] blames the failure of egoless programming teams on the lack of management and leadership skills in the general programmer population. The alternative, chief programmer teams, are often ineffective because the “superstar” programmers with the technical and interpersonal skills are rare.

The degree to which human-factors experts were regular members of teams that produced actual system products was the third characteristic. The human-factors expert has traditionally resided in a department separate from actual system development $[30, 53]$ , and is not always viewed favorably by designers $[30]$ . To what extent does this pattern continue to be reflected in the current sample? Fourth, it was of interest to study whether the human-factors experts were more likely than machine-factors experts to be involved in basic research—investigation or experimentation on procedures, software, or hardware in the absence of explicit expectations that the results will be applied to specific products or practice unconnected to product development $[70]$ . This is comparable to the type of research that is conducted at organizations such as Bellcore or Xerox PARC.

The following four hypotheses regarding job characteristics were tested in addition to the behavioral hypotheses:

H4: Human-factors experts will be less likely to report working regularly as part of a team than machine-factors experts.

H5: Human-factors experts will be less likely to report having management responsibility than machine-factors experts.

H6: Human-factors experts will be less likely to report involvement in product development than machine-factors experts.

H7: Human-factors experts will be more likely to report involvement in research than machine-factors experts.

## Method

## Participants

THE SAMPLE WAS GENERATED FROM THE MAILING LIST of participants in the tutorials at the CHI'85 (Computer–Human Interface) conference. The tutorials at this annual conference allow attendees to participate in introductory and advanced short courses in topics such as user-computer interface design. The majority of the people on the list were professionals working in industry. The majority of the organizations represented in the sample were major nonacademic research labs such as Bellcore, Xerox PARC, and the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, or IS-oriented divisions of Fortune 500 companies such as the IBM Human-factors Center, Xerox Office Systems Division, or Ford Aerospace and Communications Corporation. $^{1}$ Questionnaires and a cover letter were mailed to everyone on the list except those at academic institutions or with addresses outside North America. Five hundred thirty questionnaires were mailed, and 311 usable ones were returned—a 59 percent response rate.

This sample is obviously not random, nor is it fully representative of the computer profession. The fact that they attended this conference, and especially that they participated in the tutorials, suggests that they had some interest in human factors, whether or not they were specialists. Presumably, in such a sample the machine-factors experts would be more like human-factors experts than we would normally expect. We would thus expect this bias to decrease the chances of supporting the study's hypotheses. Appropriate care will be taken in interpreting the data.

## Questionnaires

## Job Descriptions

Respondents did not provide their job titles, but rather provided in their own words brief descriptions of their jobs. Using these descriptions, the author and a colleague who is an expert in computer system design independently classified each respondent as falling within the human-factors or machine-factors category. The following operational definitions guided the coding:

\- Human factors: The work focuses either exclusively on humans or on the human side of the human-computer interface. People who study cognitive processes or human adaptation to systems, for example, were placed here. Some systems analysts, depending upon the specifics of the descriptions they gave, would also be placed here.

\- Machine factors: The work focuses either exclusively on machines or on the machine side of the human-computer interface. Programmers and hardware engineers were placed in this category, for example. Also placed here were people who developed interface software or hardware.

There were a number of respondents whose jobs fit neither of these categories. These were jobs involving documentation or graphic arts, for example. These people were coded as “other.” There was a 74 percent level of agreement between the two coders on the initial classification. Disagreements were resolved through discussion for the final categorization. Except for a very small percentage of the respondents classified in the “other” category (11 percent, n = 35), all the respondents’ job descriptions indicated that their work had some bearing on information systems.

In addition to these job descriptions, the respondents provided some specific information about their jobs. They indicated whether or not they worked regularly as part of a team (coded as a 0, 1 variable). They were also asked to rate, on 5-point Likert scales (where 1 = very little and 5 = a great deal), the extent to which their jobs involved managerial responsibility, research, and product development.

Other data gathered were age, gender, salary range, level of education, and field of education. Education field was coded into nine categories: Information Systems and Computer Science, Psychology, Other Social Science, Physical Science, Law and Humanities, Business, Education, Art, and Other.

## Interpersonal Behavior

The dependent variables in the study were self-reports of interpersonal behavior. These measures were adapted from SYMLOG [5], a system for the observation and measurement of interpersonal behavior in groups. Bales [4] has shown that SYMLOG is a valid and reliable assessment of the three basic dimensions of interpersonal behavior—Ascendency/dominance, Affiliation/friendliness, and Task versus socioemotional. Figure 1 presents these three dimensions graphically.

The work of Bales has been noteworthy for its emphasis on the influence of personality on interpersonal processes $[3, 5]$ . SYMLOG was chosen for this study because of its emphasis on behavior. It is a flexible system $[62]$ that allows measurement of “the roles of any group on the same general dimensions” $[56, p. 602]$ . It has been used to address a variety of research topics $[69]$ , including social cognitive structure $[37]$ , leadership $[6]$ , political judgments $[68]$ , and feedback $[63]$ .

The system's validity and reliability have been established through a number of studies. Bales and Cohen [5] report reliability coefficients, based on Gulliksen's [28] formula, of 0.77 for the dominance–submissiveness dimensions, 0.95 for the friendliness–unfriendliness dimension, and 0.80 for the task versus socioemotional dimension. Isenberg and Ennis [38] demonstrated that the SYMLOG dimensions, based on factor analysis, were consistent with dimensions derived through multidimensional scaling. Isenberg [37], found cross-domain and temporal stability in subjects' perceptions of the SYMLOG dimensions. Fassheber and Terjung [18] found SYMLOG to be predictive of behaviors outside of a group context.

The sum of the ratings on the submissive items is subtracted from the sum of the ratings on dominant items to yield a single score for the Dominance dimension. In the present study, the ratings on each of the twenty-six items were made on three-point scales where 0 = not often, 1 = sometimes, and 2 = often. $^{3}$ Thus, the scores on each dimension could range from -18 to +18. The calculations would be the same for the other two dimensions.

The respondents in this study rated, for each of the behavioral descriptions shown in figure 2, the extent to which they actually behaved in each of these ways, and the extent to which they wished to behave in each of these ways. They were not asked to specify any particular circumstances in making these ratings, but rather were asked to describe their behavior in general. An example of the actual question wording is shown in figure 2. For each of these three stimuli, the scores were calculated on each of the three dimensions, resulting in nine scores for each respondent. These scores were the behavioral measures of the study.

## Results

## Description of the Sample

SIXTY-FOUR PERCENT OF THE RESPONDENTS were male, and the mean age was 34. The bulk of them (78 percent) earned annual salaries between \$20,000 and \$50,000. Ninety-seven percent of them had at least a bachelor's degree, 33 percent had master's degrees, and 26 percent had doctorates.

The machine-oriented groups made up the single largest percentage of the sample overall (50.8 percent), and, as expected, the majority of them had degrees in information systems or computer science (84.3 percent). Approximately 73 percent (72.6 percent) of the human-oriented respondents had degrees in psychology. It is also noteworthy that the bulk of the respondents who held doctorates were in the psychology field (58.1 percent), and in the human-factors category (52.6 percent).

The respondents' jobs on the average involved little management responsibility (M. = 2.41, S.D. = 1.19). Most of them were involved to a moderate extent with product development (M. = 3.66, S.D. = 1.43), while very few were involved with research (M. = 1.74, S.D. = 0.91). Approximately half (53.3 percent) reported that they worked regularly as part of a team.

## Interpersonal Behavior

In order to test H1 to H3, one-way analyses of variance were conducted for each of the behavior variables, with planned comparisons to test for specific differences between the human-factors and machine-factors groups [74].

Table 2 presents the means on each of the dimensions for wished-for and actual behaviors. The planned comparisons showed clear differences between the human-and machine-factors groups on the Dominance dimension. The human-oriented groups reported that they wished to behave more dominantly than the machine-oriented groups reported, $F(1,308) = 10.66$ , $p = 0.001$ , $r = 0.18$ . $^{4}$ Further, the respondents who were human-oriented reported that their actual behavior was more dominant than was reported by the machine-oriented groups, $F(1,308) = 5.68$ , $p = 0.038$ , $r = 0.13$ . These findings support hypothesis H1. As predicted, there were no differences on the Friendliness dimension, supporting H2. Contrary to the prediction of H3, no differences on the Task versus Socioemotional dimension were found.

## Job Characteristics

There was a greater tendency for machine-factors experts than human-factors experts to be members of regular teams: 60 percent machine factors; 43 percent human factors. But this trend did not reach conventional levels of significance $\chi^{2}(4)=8.82, p=0.07$ . Thus, H4 is only marginally supported. One-way ANOVAs were conducted with extent of managerial responsibility, involvement with product development, and involvement with research as the dependent variables, and planned comparisons to test the specific difference between human-oriented and machine-oriented groups.

Table 3 presents the means for these variables and the ANOVA results. The analyses showed no significant effects with respect to level of managerial responsibility. H5 was therefore not supported. There were significant main effects for the extent of involvement with product development and research. The planned comparisons showed higher involvement in product development among the machine-oriented groups than among the human-oriented groups $F(1,308)=11.14, p=0.0009, r=0.19$ ; despite the overall low level of involvement in research across the entire sample, human-oriented groups reported more involvement in research than did machine-oriented groups, $F(1,308)=4.61, p=0.03, r=0.12$ . Thus, hypotheses H6 and H7 were strongly supported.

There was a significant interaction effect between specialization and team membership. Human-factors specialists were not likely to be involved with product development if they were on a team, while machine-factors specialists were likely to be involved in product development if they were on a team, $F(1,308) = 6.96$ , p < 0.001, r = 0.15. There was no interaction for involvement with research.

## Discussion

TABLE 1 SUMMARIZES THE STUDY RESULTS. This study found that, as predicted, human-factors experts were likely to behave more dominantly than machine-factors experts, and were equally likely to behave in a friendly way, based on self-reports. No differences in task versus socioemotional behavior appeared. There was a slight tendency for machine-factors experts more than human-factors experts to work on a team, consistent with the hypothesis. Human-factors people were more likely to be involved in research and less likely to be involved with product development than machine-factors people.

At least with respect to perceptions of their own behavior, human-factors experts are more like the general population (i.e., the end user) than they are like their machine-factors colleagues in dominant behavior. On the other hand, the human- and machine-factors people were similar to each other in their reported affiliation and task versus socioemotional behaviors. Both groups saw their actual behaviors as friendly and task-oriented, and wished to exhibit friendlier behavior. Although the similarity on the Friendly dimension was predicted, the similarity between these two groups could also partly be a function of sampling bias. The machine-factors people who attend CHI conferences may have more in common with human-factors people than other machine-factors people have. Nevertheless these findings are consistent with those of other researchers that computer professionals value friendly, cooperative behaviors on work teams as much as other people $[19, 82]$ .

Although there has been a trend toward increased incorporation of human-factors experts into the entire system design process, the data from this study indicate that human-factors experts are still unlikely to be regular members of teams working on product development. As Gaines and Shaw [24] suggested, perhaps the human-factors expert plays a consultative and support role to product development teams.

## Limitations of the Study

Most obvious is the bias of the sample. Although CHI is a computer science conference that attracts IS professionals, the conference's theme of computer-human interaction draws the segment of the IS community interested in that issue. The likely effect of this bias on the current study is to reduce the size of the differences between the machine- and human-factors experts. Furthermore, the sample bias probably most strongly affected the machine-oriented category. Future research with a more representative sample of especially machine-factors professionals might find stronger behavioral differences between the groups.

A second limitation is that all the data were self-report. Bales and Cohen [5] report that respondents tend to rate themselves as less dominant and more friendly than others would rate them. This is due to differences in perception, and the stimuli in the environment selected for attention, as suggested by attribution theory [43]. Observers would tend to see others as more dominant because of the saliency of dominant behavior. Self-serving biases lead people to see themselves as more friendly and positive than others see them. We would not expect the biases to affect this study's results regarding the differences between human- and machine-factors people, but must be considered when comparing these findings to those of other studies.

A third limitation is related to the framing of the SYMLOG questions. The respondents were asked to describe their behavior in general, rather than in a specific setting. Since behavior is partially situationally determined $[64]$ , measurement validity could be increased by asking people to describe their behavior in context. It is possible that the respondents did consider their work setting as the context, but we cannot be sure. We thus have to assume that a larger than desirable amount of error variance exists in the behavioral measures.

## Implications and Conclusions

Despite the study's limitations, there are several important implications. First, the study suggests that human-factors experts are different from machine-factors experts, but not that different. If human-factors people are not so very different from other computer professionals, then communication and interpersonal dynamics on project teams will be smoother than if otherwise. However, as the work of Kaiser and Bostrom [45], White [81], and Jackson [41] has shown, heterogeneity on teams is associated with higher-quality outcomes, especially with creative tasks. If human-factors people are pretty much like machine-factors people, then project teams gain little in diversity of personality.

Future research should take a careful look at the utilization of human-factors people in computer system design. If the trend toward increasing end-user involvement continues, what then will be the role of the human-factors expert? It appears that in some respects dynamics between human-factors and machine-factors professionals resemble those between end-users and systems people generally. If human-factors experts become integral members of design project teams, then it would be important to understand their potential impact on group process.

Huber [33] has questioned the value of studying individual characteristics such as cognitive style in MIS. Although his criticism was leveled at the attempt to design systems that are compatible with users' style, it has some relevance to this study. What is the value of knowing that human-factors and machine-factors people may or may not differ in their interpersonal behaviors? In a reply to Huber, Robey [71] agreed that there is a certain futility in trying to match systems to users' style, but argued for assessing the cognitive style of designers for the sake of project team development. Research in information systems and in small groups more generally has demonstrated the significant effect of team composition and member characteristics for effectiveness [3, 29, 45, 81]. By knowing something about the mix of personalities on a system development team, the leader or facilitator can make better decisions about how to manage that team.
