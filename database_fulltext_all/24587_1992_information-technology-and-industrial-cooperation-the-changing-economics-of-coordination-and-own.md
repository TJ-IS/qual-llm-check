# Information Technology and Industrial Cooperation: The Changing Economics of Coordination and Ownership

ERIC K. CLEMONS AND MICHAEL C. ROW

ABSTRACT: Cooperation is becoming increasingly important in the modern business environment. The resulting emergence of new forms of organizational relationships is challenging managers to understand the fundamental dynamics of cooperation in order to evaluate and restructure their industrial relationships. This paper applies transactions cost economics toward understanding cooperative relationships. Cooperation is viewed as an effort to increase resource utilization and value through higher explicit coordination of economic activities. However, increasing explicit coordination can create transaction risks: exposure to opportunistic behavior by the other party. Transaction risk limits the level of coordination that is achievable. Information technology can reduce the costs of coordination while also reducing the transaction risks associated with increased coordination. These dual effects suggest a move toward tightly coupled, cooperative relationships.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: electronic markets, intercorporate coordination, inter-organizational information systems, strategic information systems.

## 1. Introduction

THE NATURE OF INDUSTRIAL RELATIONSHIPS IS UNDERGOING a significant change. Competitive, exploitative, and unnecessarily antagonistic relationships are increasingly giving way to more cooperative, longer-term associations [19]. The increased importance of cooperation can be seen not only in traditional joint venture activity for research and development and market entry, but also in increased cooperation between suppliers and customers in many industries, and in strategic alliances and resource-sharing arrangements both within and across industries. A telling example of the increased role of cooperation is a recent issue of the Financial Times (April 26, 1991) where four of five front-page stories dealt with cooperative relationships. $^{1}$

This increase in the cooperative emphasis of industrial relationships raises many issues, important from both a managerial and an academic perspective. When is cooperation viable? What forms can cooperation can take? What factors influence the most effective form of cooperation? What is the behavior of the resulting alliance, or network (e.g., what can we say about network stability)?

These issues are of critical importance to the information technology (IT) field. Cooperation has proven important in the competitive dynamics of many IT applications, such as airline reservation systems and automated teller machines (ATMs) [5, 10]. IT may also play a critical role in other forms of cooperation, such as the emergence of electronic hierarchies [22] for managing cooperative relationships between suppliers and customers. While IT's ability to manage the interactions between firms has been widely recognized in the information systems field, a theory linking IT with the emergence and form of cooperative arrangements has not yet emerged.

This paper applies transactions cost economics (TCE) [29] toward understanding cooperative relationships. In particular, we make the following argument:

\- The level of explicit coordination is an important dimension of interactions, distinct and separate from the question of ownership. Cooperation can be seen as efforts to increase resource utilization and to create value, through closer explicit coordination with parties not fully owned by the firm.

\- The level of explicit coordination among firms is limited by the transactions costs of managing the interaction. This has been shown (e.g., [29]) to exert a fundamental influence upon the structure of firms and industries.

\- We find it useful to stress the distinction between two components of transactions costs: coordination costs and transaction risk. IT investment can affect these two components in ways different, even opposite, from the investments in explicit coordination through specialized physical plant and human capital that underlie traditional TCE analysis. In particular, IT can reduce coordination costs while substantially reducing transaction risks.

\- By reducing the costs and the risks of explicit coordination, IT can facilitate the development of stable, tightly coupled relationships among firms.

Transactions cost economics investigates how interactions among economic activities are organized, explicitly recognizing the costs of managing the interaction, or transactions costs. This makes TCE a natural starting point for investigating cooperation, which is essentially a mechanism for managing interactions. Traditionally, TCE has focused on the determination of firm boundaries, that is, the question of whether an interaction will be managed through independent firms contracting in markets or through the hierarchy of a single firm. TCE's focus on ownership is limiting when one is looking at cooperation, which can be viewed as the area between markets and hierarchies. We extend TCE to include the level of explicit coordination as an important dimension of economic organization separate from the question of ownership. Explicit coordination is the extent to which decisions reflect and are tailored to a specific relationship, and is distinguished from the implicit coordination of the "invisible hand" of market competition.

Cooperative relationships can be viewed as efforts to increase resource utilization and to add value through closer explicit coordination of economic activities. Coordination on a transaction by transaction basis, mediated principally through market pricing, is replaced by tightly coupled operations and decision processes in the context of a long-term relationship. For example, a bank can integrate its ATMs into a larger cooperative network both to increase the transaction volume in its own ATMs and to increase the value of the bank services to its customers.

Increasing the coordination of resources, however, involves transactions costs for managing the interactions, including costs of searching for an appropriate partner, negotiating the contract, implementing the processes for coordination, and monitoring performance of that relationship. We argue that it is useful to divide transactions costs into coordination costs and transaction risk. $^{2}$ Coordination costs are the direct costs of integrating decisions between economic activities. Transaction risk is the cost associated with the exposure to being exploited in the relationship. This distinction allows new insight into the role of IT in cooperation.

IT reduces coordination costs. This is not new and has been widely recognized in the IT field [e.g., 2, 3, 18, 22, 26]. However, a reduction in unit coordination costs will not lead to an increase in explicit coordination if transaction risk is increased proportionately. $^{3}$ We argue that IT has the potential to reduce coordination costs while also reducing the transaction risk created from closer integration of decisions, leading to an increase in explicit coordination and cooperation among firms.

The Economost order entry system introduced by McKesson demonstrates this ability to support an increase in highly coordinated, cooperative relationships, while reducing the transaction risk created by cooperation $[7]$ . McKesson, the largest drug distributor in the country, introduced Economost to achieve closer coordination with their customers, independent drug stores. In return for an increasing share of the store's orders, Economost offers the drug store lower prices, electronic ordering, preprinted price and shelf tags, and management reports. Deliveries arrive packaged based on shelf layout, so that the store's stocking clerk can make one pass through the store. This relationship with McKesson appears at first to be potentially risky for the drug store. Moving toward a sole supplier relationship, the retailer can no longer rely on competition among suppliers to guarantee a good price. In addition, the retailer must adapt its operations to McKesson's, including adjusting shelf layout, training employees in the operational aspects of the system, and learning how to use the management reports. But the drug store is far from being “locked in” by this investment. Major competitors offer functionally equivalent systems, and will bear the majority of the customer's costs of switching to a new supplier. They will supply the labor to adjust shelf layout and product labels. Information technology greatly facilitates switching. IT provides the flexibility to tailor the process so as to cause minimum disruption and change for the retailer. The hardware the store uses—modems and PCs—can still be used if the store switches to one of McKesson's competitors. New distributors can tailor reports easily to match the customer's needs. McKesson even has the capability for reading competitors' product codes to allow low-cost and virtually instantaneous conversion. The result is that the increased explicit coordination through IT has been accomplished without the creation of significant transaction risk, that is, without enabling the wholesaler to extract extraordinary profits at the expense of retailers. McKesson receives 99 percent of its orders through Economost and has realized tremendous internal productivity improvements from closer coordination; however, there has been no marked increase in the firm's overall profitability relative to major competitors.

Separating the impact of IT on coordination costs and transaction risk suggests that IT will support a general “move to the middle,” toward cooperative, highly integrated interorganizational relationships, and away both from less explicitly coordinated market structures and from interactions managed within the boundaries of a single organization. By reducing the costs of explicit coordination, IT will tend to lead firms to rely more on explicit coordination, both within the firm and in relationships with other firms. By reducing transaction risk, IT will tend to lead firms to rely on outside firms to perform more economic activities. In particular, IT can support the following effects:

\- Vertical quasi-integration. Existing relationships with customers and suppliers can become more tightly coupled.

\- Outsourcing. Activities previously performed within the firm due to high transaction risk may be shifted to third-party providers, in order to benefit from the higher production economies, such as scale and specialization, of those providers.

\- Quasi-diversification. Firms may cooperate across markets and across industries in order to leverage their key resources in new areas, exploiting increased economies of scale and scope in those resources. Relationships with other firms that were previously not possible due to high coordination costs or high transaction risk may become feasible.

The analysis presented in this paper builds on and clarifies previous literature in the IT field based on the TCE paradigm. Malone et al. [22] used TCE to hypothesize a “move to the market” due to IT, but also observed the emergence of tightly coupled electronic hierarchies based on IT that seemed to run counter to that hypothesis. Gurbaxani and Whang’s analysis using TCE [16] came to similar, seemingly contradictory, conclusions, observing, for example, that IT can support either an increase or a decrease in vertical integration. The “move to the middle” framework, based on the distinction between ownership and explicit coordination and the distinction between coordination costs and transaction risk, clarifies some of this confusion, and suggests directions for further research.

The paper is organized as follows. Section 2 presents a theory of the costs and benefits of cooperation in relation to transaction cost economics. We propose the level of explicit coordination as an important dimension of interactions that has not received attention in transactions cost theory. We also make the distinction between the costs of coordinating through integration of decisions and operations and the costs of managing the risks introduced by that integration. Section 3 uses the conceptual theory to investigate the role of IT in influencing cooperation. IT reduces coordination costs and transaction risk, although not necessarily to the same degree; both dimensions must be analyzed separately. These dual effects influence the degree of explicit coordination among economic activities and the ownership structure necessary for controlling risk. Section 4 summarizes the major conclusions and suggests directions for future work.

## 2. A Theory of Industrial Cooperation

COOPERATION CAN TAKE MANY FORMS, FROM FORMAL joint ventures to long-term primary supplier relationships. This section presents a conceptual theory of cooperation based on transactions cost economics. We define cooperation very broadly as any long-term agreement or understanding between independent organizations. Cooperation is the middle area between the theoretical extremes of “spot” markets (transaction-oriented markets such as stock exchanges) and pure hierarchies (centrally directed interactions within a single firm).

## 2.1. Transactions Cost Economics

Transaction cost economics (TCE) provides a starting point for looking at cooperative relationships. TCE seeks to explain economic organization in terms of the mechanisms used to manage the interactions between economic activities. Most commonly, the focus is on the dichotomy between markets and hierarchies for managing interactions. With market organization, interactions are managed through lateral negotiations among independent firms. With hierarchical organization, interactions are managed centrally, within a single firm. TCE posits that the most efficient governance mechanism for an interaction is determined by balancing production economies and transactions costs. In general, the existence of production economies, such as economies of specialization, scale, and scope, favors the emergence of specialized firms interacting in markets, that is, employing contracts between autonomous entities.

In some situations, however, market contracting can become difficult, increasing the transactions costs of managing the interaction. In particular, under conditions of high uncertainty it may be difficult to anticipate all contingencies in a contract. Moreover, there may be the risk of opportunistic behavior, such as renegotiating price or other contract terms, misrepresentation, or performance shirking. Contracting and monitoring costs increase. At some point it becomes more efficient to administer the interaction within a hierarchical organizational structure, that is, within a single firm. Williamson argued $[29]$ that ownership confers informational advantages over markets as well as the ability to structure monitoring mechanisms and incentives. Alchian and Demsetz $[1]$ showed that the rights to residual profits associated with ownership were critical in controlling opportunistic behavior. In general, hierarchical organization sacrifices some production economies in order to reduce transactions costs.

TCE has tended to focus on the question of firm boundaries $[29]$ , that is, the dichotomy between markets and hierarchies. IT literature utilizing TCE has also focused primarily on the dimension of ownership $[16, 22]$ . However, it is the area between these extremes that is of interest in looking at cooperation. We need to characterize this area more fully. This is difficult since there is no meaningful single-dimension continuum between the abstractions of markets and hierarchies. A multidivisional firm that uses internal transfer prices that reflect market conditions as its primary coordination mechanism for largely autonomous operating units has more in common with the “spot” market than does the high level of explicit coordination and integration of operations presently found between GM and its independent contractors and suppliers. Ownership alone does not provide an adequate characterization.

## 2.2. Motivations and Benefits of Cooperation

We extend TCE to include the concept of explicit coordination, or the extent to which decisions are coordinated between economic activities through processes and information that are specific to the two activities. This extension permits a better conceptualization of cooperation within the TCE framework. Cooperative arrangements are attempts to increase utilization of fixed resources, such as productive capacity, managerial capability, and technological know-how, through closer integration of decisions, that is, increased explicit coordination, among economic activities that are not jointly owned.

Analysis begins with the “perfect” commodity markets of traditional economic analysis. Market transacting is seen to be costless, so firms are organized to maximize production economies, such as scale, scope, and specialization. Coordination of decisions is through price and quantity only. In this theoretical abstraction, economic activities of buyers and sellers are perfectly coordinated, although integration of operations and decision processes is low. Buyers and sellers need not even know the identity of the counterparty, much less tailor their decisions to specific characteristics of that party. Markets such as stock exchanges come the closest to this ideal.

Often environmental and technological factors will make it possible to increase the overall efficiency of production or exchange through closer integration of decisions and operations. This increase in explicit coordination necessarily involves investments to tailor operations to specific interactions. A supplier with access to the customer's production schedule can rationalize production and distribution, reducing inventories and increasing utilization of resources. $^{4}$ However, the supplier must invest in information systems to accumulate the information and in decision processes to utilize that information for production scheduling and delivery.

The benefits of closer explicit coordination are not limited to vertical relationships within a single value chain. Utilization of resources can be increased by leveraging them in new products or markets. For example, owners of the airline computer reservations systems (CRS) quickly realized that the distribution infrastructure could be used to deliver other services, such as reservations for hotels and car rentals $[10]$ . Offering these services through the CRS increased the transaction volume through the system, lowering average cost per transaction. Moreover, offering all travel services through a single channel reduced consumer's transaction cost for planning a trip. Leveraging a common resource, such as the CRS, to produce or deliver new products improves resource utilization and value through economies of scope.

Leveraging key resources in different products or markets usually involves access to resources controlled by other firms, requires substantial adaptation of resources and operations, and entails considerable risk. Leveraging the CRS in the hotel and car rental markets required explicit coordination with organizations offering those services, including agreeing on service descriptions and linking computer systems and databases.

Except when limited by antitrust laws, resource utilization can also be improved through increased coordination of similar resources and operations. Coordinated

R&D, production, or distribution can lead to reduced capital investments, reduced risk, and increased efficiency. ATM services in most major markets are cooperative or coordinated efforts involving virtually all banks in the market. In most cases the ATM machines and switches are owned and operated by member banks, but are electronically integrated to provide seamless service throughout the network [5]. Here the benefit of increased explicit coordination comes from economies of scale. The most significant cost elements in linking ATMs and banks together are computer and telecommunications systems, which are largely fixed cost elements. Increasing transaction volume doesn't increase total costs, so average cost per transaction falls. For ATM networks, these cost-based economies of scale are complemented by significant participation externalities in that the value of the network to consumers increases with the size of the network and number of participating banks.

In summary, cooperative arrangements increase explicit coordination among economic activities in order to exploit transactional efficiencies or latent production economies. These potential benefits must be compared with the costs of achieving the cooperation, an issue to which we now turn.

## 2.3. Costs of Cooperation

There are costs and risks associated with cooperation, that is, transactions costs. According to TCE, when the transactions costs of interorganizational exchange exceed the production economies available from the separate firms, the economic activities will be merged into a single firm, with the interaction being managed within the organizational hierarchy. But transactions costs can be broken down into two parts, with different economic ramifications and different organizational mechanisms available for managing them. This is critical in understanding the role of IT in cooperation.

Transactions costs can be broken down into costs of coordination and costs of transaction risk. Coordination costs are the costs of coordinating decisions and operations among economic activities in order to improve resource efficiency. This includes costs to establish and operate information channels and decision processes. For example, the concept of just-in-time (JIT) inventory involves investment in computer linkages or other mechanisms to communicate schedules and status. At the same time, decision processes must be modified to take advantage of the improved information flow. Coordination costs are largely independent of whether the interaction occurs within a single firm or crosses firm boundaries.

Transaction risk is the possibility of opportunistic behavior by another party to the relationship, leading to uncertainty surrounding the level and division of the benefits from the increased integration of decisions and operations. As finance theory tells us, risk has an equivalent cost. Investments can be made in mechanisms to reduce the risk, but these investments themselves directly increase costs. For example, additional effort can be made to develop a more complete and enforceable contract, increasing the initial contracting costs, but reducing the transaction risk. Any risk that is not reduced by control mechanisms (including insurance) will affect costs in the form of higher cost of capital (or expected losses).

Three major sources of transaction risk have been identified in the literature: transaction-specific capital, information asymmetries, and loss of resource control. Transaction-specific capital (e.g., [20]) is investment by one party that has little or no value in uses other than the specific interaction for which it was undertaken. Returns on such sunk capital can often be appropriated by the other party, particularly in situations of high uncertainty that necessitate incomplete contracts. This can be accomplished by opportunistic renegotiation of the contract at renewal, or performance shirking in hard-to-monitor areas of the interaction. For example, a supplier may locate a plant close to a major customer to reduce transportation costs, but if the relationship is ended, the plant can only be used for other customers with higher transportation costs. Alternatively, a firm may hire an individual to manage relationships with a major supplier or customer; the account manager may be reassigned, but much of his investment in personal relationships and client history is of minimal value. Once capital is sunk, the other party can bargain away almost the entire economic return on that capital without forcing the investing party to withdraw. While the ex-ante bargaining positions may be at parity, the party committing sunk capital will clearly be at an ex-post disadvantage.

Transactions cost economics has traditionally focused on the risk created by transaction-specific capital $[20, 29]$ . However, there are other risks from closer integration of decisions and operations. Asymmetries in information can create problems in monitoring performance, which can increase transaction risk. $^{5}$ In many situations, due to the nature of production processes or limitations in information, it is difficult to measure accurately the specific contribution of inputs in generating output. This creates the opportunity for performance shirking by one party at the expense of the other. For example, a component supplier may reduce quality to improve his own profitability. If the purchaser lacks the ability to measure quality at the front end and lacks the information to track future product failures back to the offending component, this opportunistic strategy will be successful. As decisions and operations become more integrated, it becomes more difficult to assign and measure responsibility, increasing the risk of shirking. Game theory with information asymmetries (e.g., $[12]$ , ch. 4) has shown how the risk of shirking leads to higher costs. $^{6}$

Loss of resource control occurs when resources are transferred as part of the relationship, if these resources cannot be returned or controlled in the event of the termination of the relationship. $^{7}$ Most frequently, the important resources subject to loss of control will be in the form of information or know-how, since it is very difficult to control access and subsequent utilization of such resources. The history of business is replete with examples where a licensee or other cooperative partner used the relationship to acquire technology or customer relationships and then emerged as a threat to the original partner. One recent example is the partnership between General Electric and the Motoren und Turbinen Muenchen GmbH (MTM) subsidiary of Daimler-Benz AG [25]. G.E. and MTM had cooperated on jet engine development since the mid-1960s and were embarking on the development of a new high-thrust engine for long-range jumbo aircraft. However, Daimler and MTM entered into an agreement with arch-rival Pratt and Whitney, a subsidiary of United Technologies, to develop a competing high-thrust engine. Daimler offered to return all documentation concerning G.E.'s technology, but, of course, there is no way to control the use of the information content of that documentation. G.E. sued Daimler for \$1 billion. The suit has been settled through renegotiation of G.E.'s agreement with Daimler. This settlement has been valued at \$500 million for G.E. over the next fifteen years.

## 2.4. Summary—Cooperation and Transactions Costs

We have argued that transactions cost economics is a useful base for investigating cooperation. However, the focus on the theoretical extremes of markets and hierarchies does not help us in characterizing cooperation, which is essentially the area between those theoretical extremes. Explicit coordination is proposed as the important link. Cooperative relationships are based on increasing coordination through integration of decisions and operations in order to increase resource utilization or value.

Coordination involves transactions costs. These costs can be broken down into the costs of achieving coordination and the costs of transaction risk resulting from the coordination. Increasing integration of decisions and operations may lead to increased transaction risk in the form of transaction-specific capital, shirking, or loss of resource control.

The distinction between costs of coordination and of transaction risk is extremely important, particularly for understanding the role of IT. The IT field has long recognized the importance of IT's ability to reduce the costs of coordination (e.g., [2, 3, 13, 18, 22]). However, by focusing largely on the cost of coordination, the critical role of risk in determining the governance structure of an interaction is neglected. Under any governance structure, whether it be markets or hierarchies or anything in between, an investment to reduce coordination costs that does not increase transaction risk is always a good thing for all parties (of course, assuming the cost reductions provide a reasonable return on investment). One can make no interesting statements about the level of explicit coordination or division of the benefits resulting from increased coordination without investigating the risk component.

At the same time, traditional transaction-cost theorists (e.g., [20, 29]) focus primarily on the risk created by transaction-specific capital. Their arguments implicitly assume that increasing explicit coordination requires specialized (sunk) investments to decrease coordination costs, but that the sunk nature of these investments substantially increases transaction risk. This was certainly plausible when increased integration of decisions and operations was accomplished primarily through manual processes, with complex networks of idiosyncratic personal relationships, or through sunk investment in specialized physical plant or machinery. We have suggested that explicit coordination is not necessarily linked to transaction risk, and thus that the level of explicit coordination and ownership must be analyzed as separate dimensions of transaction governance structure. Moreover, there are other sources of transaction risk besides transaction specific capital, specifically the risk of shirking and the risk of loss of resource control.

## 3. IT and Cooperation

IT IS WIDELY ACCEPTED THAT IT REDUCES coordination costs, that is, the cost of accumulating, storing, and communicating information (e.g., [2, 3, 13, 18, 22]). As Malone and Rockart [19] point out, decreasing the cost of coordination leads to three basic economic effects:

\- Increased demand for coordination through IT. Firms will coordinate more. Decision processes will become more sophisticated, incorporating more information, both from within the firm and from outside the firm.

\- Substitution of coordination through IT for other resources. For example, just-in-time (JIT) inventory techniques substitute information and coordination for inventories.

\- Development of coordination-intensive structures. The above first-order effects can lead, over time, to the development of dramatically new organizational and societal structures. Already, we can see the traditional multidivisional, hierarchical firm giving way to a flatter, more flexible type of organization that has been termed a dynamic network [24].

However, as Malone et al. [22] pointed out, this increased coordination can take the form of more efficient transaction-oriented electronic markets or of more efficient highly integrated, cooperative relationships. By separately addressing the issues of transaction risk, coordination, and ownership, we can gain insight into the conditions necessary for IT to support cooperation, and thus into the forms of cooperation likely to be exhibited.

## 3.1. IT and Transaction Risk

Transaction risk results in underinvestment in explicit coordination among economic activities (the potential benefits of coordination are not achieved), or in the interaction being managed within a single organization (potentially advantageous production economies from outside suppliers are sacrificed). IT can reduce transaction risk by reducing the level of transaction-specific capital and by reducing the cost of monitoring and control among separate firms; this, as much as the reduction in coordination costs from IT, explains increasing use of IT to facilitate integration of decisions and operations.

## 3.1.1. Reduction of Transaction-Specific Capital

One way IT can reduce transaction risk is to reduce the level of transaction-specific (sunk) capital required to increase explicit coordination. $^{8}$ Investment in computers, telecommunications equipment, and other hardware is rarely transaction-specific. More importantly, fundamental economic characteristics of software reduce the investment sunk in a specific relationship:

\- Modularity and replicability of know-how. Unlike human information systems, the expertise embedded in information systems is codified and can be transferred to new applications at virtually no incremental cost. Moreover, modularity of well-designed systems facilitates isolating the most idiosyncratic functional elements, reducing the cost of tailoring systems to new situations.

\- Open standards. Standards facilitate transferability of applications, reducing sunk costs and switching costs. Most industries have developed standards for electronic data interchange (EDI), such as the Universal Communications Standard (UCS) protocol adopted in the grocery industry [14]. Even de facto interface standards are emerging, such as Microsoft Windows, that facilitate training and switching.

\- Conversion and translation. IT can support conversion and translation of protocols and interfaces at low cost. For example, many software packages, such as Microsoft Excel and Word, include the capability to mimic competing products.

\- Intuitive interfaces. As IT sophistication increases, user interfaces are emerging that require very little training, due to intuitive design and sophisticated on-line help capability. Again, the effect is to reduce the level of sunk investment in a single system.

The success and stability of the MAC ATM network, owned by Philadelphia National Bank (PNB), is largely due to the use of IT to reduce transaction-specific investment and related transaction risk $[5]$ . MAC has emerged as the only ATM network in the Philadelphia area and has expanded to become a dominant regional network, rivaled only by the New York-based NYCE network. This would appear to create significant transaction risk for participants. Success of an ATM network is largely based on scale, not only for cost-based economies of scale, but also for participation externalities, where the value of the network to consumers increases with the network size and the number of participating banks. MAC's dominant position in its markets and the fact that MAC is owned by PNB, creates the risk that PNB will be in a position to extract most of the economic benefits of the network from participants. Consistent with TCE theory, the potential dangers of such a dependency have led to most regional ATM networks, including NYCE, being jointly owned by their members. However, MAC is enormously successful and stable, and PNB is not earning the extraordinary profits that could be expected from their dominant position. A key reason for this is how MAC participants have managed transaction-specific capital. Large members of MAC maintain their own ATMs and networks. PNB's systems handle the operational integration of these independent networks using standard protocols. This architecture reduces the IT capital sunk in the relationship with MAC and gives participants the option of switching membership to a competing network or forming a new cooperative network. $^{9}$ The resulting reduction in transaction risk is a critical factor in the growth and stability of the MAC network.

## 3.1.2. Monitoring and Incentives

IT allows much more cost-effective monitoring of cooperative arrangements, which can further reduce transaction risks, particularly the risks of shirking and loss of resource control. Information that is too expensive to collect manually can be generated as a byproduct of normal operations and directed to the appropriate point in the organization at low cost. This information can be used for control, but can also be used to form more sophisticated incentives so that direct control is less necessary.

An example from the consumer packaged goods industry is instructive here. $^{10}$ Information technology, driven by the spread of checkout scanner systems, has created significant opportunities for higher integration and more cooperation. However, increasing explicit coordination creates significant transaction risks, particularly given the history of conflict between retailers and manufacturers in this industry. Using IT for monitoring and for supporting new incentive structures is emerging as a critical issue in emerging cooperative arrangements.

Checkout scanners provide retailers with unprecedented amounts of timely, detailed information on product movement. Shelf-space management systems enable the retailer to harness this information to make more informed decisions about product selection and shelf layout. Many retailers, however, lack the time and resources necessary to perform detailed analysis on all of the product categories they carry. Since manufacturers operate in a narrow range of categories, they can devote more resources to understanding consumer behavior and the market dynamics of their categories. This expertise, once developed, can be applied to the needs of numerous retailers for support in their shelf-space management; thus, manufacturers enjoy considerable economies of scale in this area. Increasingly, retailers are working cooperatively with manufacturers to determine shelf layouts. Manufacturers utilize store scanner data and information on specific market characteristics to suggest changes to products and shelf layouts in order to increase sales and profitability. It appears that manufacturers potentially could exploit this relationship, favoring their own products over competitors at the expense of the retailer. However, scanner data also provide a means of accurately measuring the performance of manufacturer recommendations. Retailers use profitability models to compare the recommendations of competing manufacturers. Once a layout has been implemented, they use scanner data to monitor product movement and analyze economic return on shelf space. Manufacturers thus have very little scope for exploiting their advisory role through opportunistic recommendations. In merchandising support, IT is both creating the opportunity for cooperation and providing the monitoring capability to reduce the transaction risk associated with cooperation.

## 3.1.3. Limitations of IT's Ability to Reduce Transaction Risk

The transaction risk created through increased explicit coordination and IT's ability to reduce or manage that risk is obviously dependent on situation-specific factors. More important, IT design decisions can greatly increase or decrease the level of risk. For example, the decision to use off-the-shelf hardware and industry standard protocols will reduce the transaction risk, while the use of custom hardware and proprietary protocols will increase the transaction risk.

One area where the use of IT for explicit coordination can increase transaction risk is the risk of loss of resource control. It may be more difficult to control the use of information and know-how stored electronically. Expertise that is codified in IT is much easier to duplicate and transfer than is human expertise. There are ways IT can help reduce this risk, particularly through monitoring and incentives. For example, in the direct marketing industry, firms that make their customer lists available to other parties frequently seed their lists with “dummy” customers, to facilitate monitoring the reuse of those lists.

## 3.2. Impact on Ownership and Integration: "Move to the Middle"

Traditional transactions cost economics has focused primarily on the single dimension of ownership (see figure 1a). This implicitly assumes that ownership and explicit coordination are linked, that investments to improve explicit coordination, and thus resource utilization, always increase transaction risk and the risk of appropriation of benefits:

\- When the net benefits of integration of decisions and operations (expected benefits from investments in explicit coordination, less the cost of this investment) were sufficiently large to counterbalance the loss of an outside supplier's production economies, firms would vertically integrate and make investments intended to gain the benefits of explicit coordination. Vertical integration would eliminate the risk of appropriation, although it would frequently preclude using a supplier with greater economies of scale, scope, or specialization. This is shown as point A in figure 1.

\- Where the net benefits of explicit coordination were not sufficient to outweigh the loss of an outside supplier's production economies, firms might outsource but were unlikely to make the investments required for full and effective coordination (i.e., Williamsonian underinvestment in integration of decisions and operations). Where there are no benefits from explicit coordination, the result is a transaction-oriented spot market. This is shown as point B in figure 1.

Malone et al. [22] have suggested that IT investments will, by reducing market transactions costs, lead to a “move to the market,” shown in figure 1a as a move from point A to point B. We have suggested that it is important to decouple the issue of explicit coordination from the question of ownership (figure 1b): IT may decouple an investment’s beneficial impact on coordination costs from its damaging impact on transaction risk. Firms can now make greater use of outsourcing, and outsource supplies of goods or services, including critical components, gaining the advantage of the supplier’s economies of scale, scope, or specialization. Moreover, when transacting with independent firms, they can make investments intended to improve integration of decisions and operations significantly, without necessarily increasing transaction risk due to these investments. This suggests that although firms will increase market transactions, they will not rely solely on one-off transactions, or gravitate to the use of transaction-oriented spot markets, but will move toward long-term, stable partnerships to increase resource utilization through greater explicit coordination. The decision to integrate decisions and operations is now effectively distinct from the decision to integrate ownership. This is shown as a move toward point D in figure 1b, which appears as a “move to the middle” on the markets and hierarchies axis of traditional TCE (the A–B axis in figure 1).

Figure 1. Ownership and Coordination Decoupled. Figure 1a illustrates the traditional view that ownership and coordination are tightly coupled. Historically, under earlier technology, this was a reasonable strategy for firms and a reasonable view for theory. Figure 1b shows that it is useful to separate the two dimensions of ownership and coordination. ## 3.3. IT-Supported Cooperation

To understand where IT can support cooperation, it is critical to understand how the technology can affect both explicit coordination costs and transaction risks. The next subsections investigate some of the possibilities for IT supported cooperation.

## 3.3.1. Vertical Quasi-integration

Perhaps the most obvious application of IT to support cooperation is in increasing the level of explicit coordination between buyers and suppliers within the value chain of a single industry. Such vertical partnerships are exemplified by the emerging relationship between Procter & Gamble and Wal-Mart (e.g., [11]). P&G has access to Wal-Mart's scanner data down to the store level. P&G monitors sales and delivers needed inventory to the warehouse and frequently directly to the store, relieving Wal-Mart of the cost of processing orders and reducing their warehouse inventories. At the same time, the information allows P&G to rationalize their own production and delivery to decrease logistics costs for their entire operations. Finally, the information allows P&G to monitor merchandising and promotion efforts to improve product sales and profit performance both for Wal-Mart and for P&G, while still enabling P&G to ascertain that promotions are used as intended. While the systems used for explicit coordination were initially largely specialized to P&G and Wal-Mart, the systems utilized industry standard protocols. Both firms are applying the systems and experience developed for that relationship toward improving coordination with other parties.

Access to such extensive information on Wal-Mart's internal operations could potentially put P&G in a position to exploit the relationship opportunistically. However, this transaction risk is greatly reduced by two factors. First, and most important, Wal-Mart's size gives them considerable bargaining power with P&G. Second, the information from the system allows Wal-Mart to monitor P&G's performance as well as the performance of other suppliers.

## 3.3.2. Outsourcing

Increasingly, activities that were previously performed within a single firm are being outsourced to specialized providers. For example, in financial services, smaller players are finding it possible to outsource certain scale-intensive back-office IT applications, such as check processing or statement processing, to larger players with significant scale advantages. Outsourcing gives small firms access to economies of scale that would be virtually impossible to develop internally. Given the increasing consolidation in that industry, the ability to outsource may be critical for the continued survival of smaller firms. Shopping out back-office computer functions is not unique to financial services or to small firms. Eastman Kodak and American Standard have outsourced much of their data processing operations. One market research company has estimated that the market for outsourced computer services was \$4.72 billion in 1990 and will reach \$10 billion by 1994 [28].

Other business functions and products are being increasingly outsourced. In the automotive industry, Chrysler now outsources over 70 percent of the value of their automobiles [17]. Some observers have even suggested that IT can lead to “hollow corporations” where firms are little more than information switchboards [30], although there is some controversy over whether this level of outsourcing is sustainable in the long run.

Both vertical partnerships and outsourcing involve changing the form of existing interactions. IT's ability to reduce the costs of explicit coordination without increasing transaction risk also makes it possible to create new interactions to exploit increased production economies, particularly scale and scope.

## 3.3.3. Cooperation for Economies of Scope

Key resources can sometimes be leveraged in new products or markets, thereby realizing economies of scope. Such synergistic diversification most often requires access to additional resources the firm does not possess. IT can support cooperative arrangements for accessing those resources without having to purchase them.

Merrill Lynch's Cash Management Account (CMA) is a classic example of cooperation for economies of scope [6]. The CMA product combined the functions of a brokerage account with the functions of a bank demand deposit account. Unused investment funds were automatically transferred to a high-yield money market account. Customers could access available funds either by check or by debit card. Merrill possessed the necessary resources and expertise for the brokerage account and money market fund functions, but lacked the transaction processing capability for checks and debit cards. Moreover, by law, Merrill could not offer banking services. To implement CMA, Merrill entered into a cooperative arrangement with BankOne, a national bank in Columbus, Ohio. BankOne provided the demand deposit account and processed all checks and debit card transactions. These transactions were transferred each day to Merrill's systems, which would shift available funds into the money market account. Merrill also processed all investment transactions. Through cooperation with BankOne, Merrill was able to define and exploit a new class of financial service that leveraged their existing resource base in the brokerage industry.

## 3.3.4. Cooperation for Economies of Scale

While outsourcing seeks to exploit economies of scale by relying on third-party providers, it may be possible to achieve these economies by coordinating the use of similar resources across firms in the same industry. Often this is a violation of antitrust laws, but there are some examples where it has been permitted. Abbott Labs and 3M have formed a cooperative venture to provide product delivery.

Rosenbluth Travel has used IT to create the Rosenbluth International Alliance (RIA), a cooperative organization offering integrated travel management services across the globe $[8, 9]$ . As mentioned earlier, Rosenbluth Travel has emerged as one of the five largest travel agencies in the United States, with IT playing an important role in that success. In the late 1980s, the increasing globalization of their corporate clients had led to increased demand for travel support for international travelers wherever they were in the world. The RIA provides that global travel support through a network of thirty-two agencies spanning thirty-seven countries. The coordination necessary to provide this service is enabled by IT. A U.S. executive needing help with travel arrangements while traveling in London can contact the U.K. RIA member. The local travel agent in London has electronic access to all information that would be available to his or her travel agent at home, including the traveler's complete itinerary, travel preferences, and his or her employer's travel policies. Each RIA member can thus offer domestic clients the benefits of seamlessly integrated global support, without actually owning agencies across the world. Moreover, IT allows the RIA to harness their global purchasing power. Reservations information is consolidated across all RIA members and is used to negotiate special rates and services with travel suppliers such as hotels and airlines based on worldwide volume. In both travel support and negotiations, IT allows RIA members to achieve the benefits of global scale and presence without ownership.

## 3.4. Cooperation in IT Ventures

Throughout the 1980s IT has emerged as an important strategic issue. New applications often are extremely expensive and alter competition and the structure of industries in many ways. Initially, the focus was on using these systems to gain competitive advantage (e.g., [23]). However, two factors suggest that such systems are important areas for potential cooperation [4]. First, such systems often provide enormous economies of scale and network externalities, which create significant benefits from cooperation. Second, expected competitive gains from proprietary development frequently prove elusive due to the difficulties in preventing imitation of IT capability. Of course, the transaction risks created through cooperation must be managed, and the framework presented earlier can be used to evaluate these risks and to design an appropriate management structure.

A fundamental economic characteristic of IT investment is that fixed costs are high and marginal costs are low, leading to considerable economies of scale. The most significant fixed cost is development cost. When several users have similar or nearly identical requirements, they have a strong incentive to pool development and operation of their applications. Cooperation can dramatically lower costs by eliminating the redundant investment that would occur should each party attempt to develop similar proprietary systems.

In some situations, the cost economies of scale of IT investment are complemented by participation externalities. Participation externalities result where the value of a system to a customer increases with the number of participants in the system. For example, airline CRSs create considerable value by providing access to most airlines. The value of the system to the travel agent (or to the ultimate customer) is directly related to the number of airlines accessible. In the limiting case, it is easy to see how a system with only one airline would not be worth very much to customers. Automated teller machine (ATM) networks also exhibit participation externalities. The value of an ATM network to the customer is related to ATM density and coverage, which increases with the number of bank participants in the system.

The potential advantages of cooperation must still be weighed against the transaction risks involved. Managers must better understand the nature of these risks and the mechanisms that are available for managing them. These mechanisms include the level of explicit coordination, the level and division of sunk capital, information channels and incentives, and ownership. Failure to understand and manage these risks can be very costly. Proprietary airline computer reservation systems in the United States controlled access to customer base, enabling United and American to earn considerable profits at the expense of other airlines $[10]$ . In Europe and Asia, this situation does not appear to be emerging, as all major travel agent CRSs will be cooperatively owned. $^{11}$ Here, ownership is necessary to protect against the very significant transactions risk.

## 4. Summary and Conclusions

COOPERATION IS A CRITICALLY IMPORTANT AND EMERGING topic in business; moreover, it is one in which IT apparently should be a significant component. In this paper we have tried to expand transactions cost economics to shed light on the emergence and structure of cooperative relationships. While transactions cost economics typically focuses on ownership structure, we have proposed that the level of explicit coordination among economic activities is a critical dimension. Cooperation can be viewed as efforts to increase the level of explicit coordination in order to increase resource utilization and value.

But cooperation can increase transactions costs, which include not only the operational costs of achieving coordination, but costs associated with higher risk of being exploited by opportunistic behavior of the other party. These transaction risks stem from the presence of transaction-specific capital, information asymmetries that make monitoring performance difficult, and the threat of loss of resource control. Transaction risk limits the level of explicit coordination that will be achieved and, in some cases, makes market contracting untenable; this leads to hierarchical organization, managing the interaction within the boundaries of a single firm.

IT can decrease the costs of coordination, that is, the costs of accumulating, communicating, and processing information. However, a reduction in coordination costs will not necessarily lead to an increase in observed explicit coordination among firms, particularly where increased explicit coordination increases transaction risks.

An important characteristic of IT is the ability to reduce both the costs of coordination and transaction risk. The reduction in transaction risk is accomplished by reducing the level of transaction-specificity in investments in explicit coordination, by reducing the costs of performance monitoring, and by providing information needed to structure more incentive-compatible reward mechanisms. The reduction in transaction risk leads to greater reliance on outsourcing, that is, on use of outside agents to achieve economies of scale, scope, or specialization. The reduction in coordination costs structures this outsourcing as stable, cooperative, value-adding partnerships. We call this combination a “move to the middle.”

New cooperative structures are not limited to vertical, buyer–supplier relationships within the value chain of a single industry. Interactions between firms in the same or related industries that previously were not achievable, due to high coordination costs or high transaction risk, may now be possible. Similar resources may be coordinated with other firms to increase economies of scale, as we saw in the case of ATMs. And resources may be leveraged in other products and markets to exploit new economies of scope, as was the case with the airline CRS expanding into hotel and rental car reservations. The conceptual structure presented here can aid managers in identifying and evaluating opportunities for cooperation, in balancing the risks of dependence against the benefits obtained from economies of scale, scope, and specialization, and in avoiding situations that are unstable or have the potential to become exploitative.
