# An Empirical Assessment of Information Systems Planning and the Role of Information Systems in Organizations

G. PREMKUMAR AND WILLIAM R. KING

G. PREMKUMAR is Assistant Professor of MIS at the College of Business, Iowa State University. He received his Ph.D. in information systems from the University of Pittsburgh and has over eight years of experience in the industry. His research interests include information systems planning, telecommunications management, and organization's adoption of new information technology. He has published in Decision Sciences, International Journal of Man–Machine Studies, Omega, Information and Management, Long Range Planning, and Journal of Data Base Administration.

WILLIAM R. KING is University Professor of Business Administration at the Katz Graduate School of Business, University of Pittsburgh. He has served as President of the Institute of Management Science (TIMS), senior editor of the MIS Quarterly, and as general chairman of the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS). His research papers have appeared in the leading journals in management science, information systems, and strategic planning.

ABSTRACT: Research in the information systems (IS) field indicates that organizations have different roles for IS in their operations and that various aspects of strategic IS planning will vary depending on the IS role. This study empirically evaluates the differences in planning, organizational support, and performance characteristics of IS planning among organizations with different roles for IS. An operationalization of the “role of IS” is developed on the basis of the notion of a “strategic grid.” The study also evaluates the relationship between various performance measures and the “fit” between the IS planning process and the role of IS in the organization. The data for the study were collected from a field survey of 249 firms. The results of the data analyses indicate that there are significant differences in planning, organizational support, and performance characteristics of IS planning among organizations with different roles for IS. The planning and support characteristics generally match the predictions implicit in the strategic grid, thereby partially validating it. Significant relationships exist between two of the performance measures, planning effectiveness and IS’s contribution to organizational performance, and the “fit” between the role of IS and the quality of the planning process.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: fit between role of IS and IS planning, IS planning, organizational support for IS planning, role of IS in an organization, strategic grid.

THE LAST DECADE HAS WITNESSED AN INFORMATION REVOLUTION that has made sweeping changes in the way organizations conduct business. Information technology (IT) is transforming the nature of products, processes, and competition and is having far-reaching strategic impact on organizations and industries [8]. In many firms, Information technology has changed the role of the information systems (IS) function from a traditional batch-oriented process providing basic support services to a strategic and critical function that impacts the firm's daily operations and its business strategies and plans [67].

The new role for IS has brought into focus the necessity to plan for the IS function so that its plans are in alignment with the firm's business plans [24, 33]. Strategic IS planning is being increasingly used to explicate the organization's business plans and strategies, to identify IS opportunities that support the business plans, to develop information architectures based on user's information needs, and to develop long-term developmental plans for the IS function.

McFarlan et al. [33] use the “strategic grid” framework to highlight the different roles for IS in an organization. They emphasize that the characteristics of the IS planning system must match with the role that IS plays in the organization. An organization may not be able to achieve strategic impact with IS using a planning system primarily focused on operational issues. On the other hand, a firm that does not plan to use IS for strategic purposes may be wasting its resources by using a sophisticated strategic IS planning methodology [54].

Evaluating the link between the role played by IS in an organization and its IS planning system has become an important research issue in IS [8, 45]. However, there are few empirical studies that have evaluated this link. Hence, the objectives of this study are:

1. to operationalize and validate the “strategic grid” as a measure of the role played by IS in an organization;

2. to evaluate the differences in IS planning systems among organizations with different roles for IS;

3. to evaluate the relationship between performance and the fit between the “role of IS” and the quality of the IS planning process.

## Background

THE RESEARCH LITERATURE RELATED TO THE “ROLE of IS,” IS planning system characteristics, and the concept of “fit” forms the bases for the study.

## Role of IS in the Organization

The evolution of the IS function and the notion that IS serves different purposes in various organizations has been extensively discussed in the IS literature $[33, 38, 39, 41]$ . Nolan $[39]$ found that the IS function goes through six stages of growth and that organizations use IS in a different manner in each stage. Along similar lines, McFarlan et al. [33] found that in some organizations IS activities represent an area of strategic importance, while for others they play a cost-effective and useful, but distinctly supporting, role. Based on this finding, they developed the “strategic grid,” which determines the role of IS in an organization by classifying organizations on two dimensions—the strategic impact of IS on the present and future operations. They also emphasized the need for a proper fit between the role of IS in the organization and its planning and support systems.

In an empirical study, using the strategic grid, Raghunathan and Raghunathan $[45]$ confirmed some of the predictions of McFarlan et al. $[33]$ . They found differences in some IS planning characteristics among firms in the four quadrants of the strategic grid. Their study emphasized the contingent nature of IS planning and the need to match IS planning characteristics with the role of IS in the organization. In another study on the IS planning of thirty-five firms, Sullivan $[54]$ found the need to match the IS planning methodology with the extent of infusion/diffusion of IS in an organization. He found that in some firms a simple methodology may be adequate, while in others a more eclectic approach is necessary to support the complex and critical role of IS.

## IS Planning System Characteristics

The core of the IS planning system is the planning process, which assesses the external IS environment and the internal requirements, identifies new system applications, and develops a set of strategic IS plans to support the business plans of the organization $[25]$ . The primary objective of IS planning is to align IS plans with the business plans $[3, 72]$ . However, Lederer and Mendelow $[27]$ found that in many organizations strategic business plans do not exist, or are not clearly documented, or do not contain relevant information that can be used in IS planning. Hence, organizations often use various support mechanisms, such as a steering committee or the participation of the IS manager in strategic business planning, to facilitate the integration of the two planning systems $[28]$ . Top management and user involvement are also found to be important facilitators for effective IS planning $[1, 5, 28, 42]$ .

The adequacy of planning resources has been a focus of attention in characterizing IS planning $[25]$ . Lederer and Sethi $[29]$ found the availability of planning resources to be a serious problem in IS planning. Goodhue et al. $[16]$ found that the allocation of adequate resources for implementation of the plan is also a significant problem that affects the effectiveness of the planning system.

Significant investments in IS and growing organizational claims of deriving strategic advantage using IS have brought the planning performance dimension into prominence in characterizing planning $[12, 65]$ . The measurement of planning performance has been a subject of considerable research in strategic planning and IS planning $[60, 65]$ . To prove the efficacy of planning, it is necessary to measure the effectiveness of planning and its impact on the organization $[24]$ . There have been a few studies that have focused on linking IT investment to objective organizational performance measures (sales growth, ROA, etc.), but with limited success $[15, 65]$ .

Researchers have had better success with perceptual measures such as using the fulfillment of planning objectives as a measure of planning effectiveness [44].

## The Fit between the Role of IS and IS Planning

The need to develop a proper “fit” between the characteristics of the planning system and the role of IS in the organization has been emphasized by researchers $[8]$ . The concept of “fit” has been extensively used in strategic management to describe linkages in strategy-structure $[9]$ , technology-structure $[68]$ , organization-environment $[57]$ , strategy-organizational culture $[51]$ , and product life-cycle business strategy $[19]$ . In IS, the concept of “fit” has not been widely used, but has implicitly formed the basis for some studies $[66]$ . Although the alignment of IS operations with business operations $[3]$ , matching organizational characteristics with IS operations $[14]$ , and matching IS plans with business plans $[23, 72]$ have been emphasized, only a few studies have addressed the fit among these variables in a formal sense. Researchers using this concept in strategic planning, have emphasized the need to develop a proper definition and a suitable data-analytic approach to evaluate the fit and its relationship to any criterion variable $[58]$ . Venkatraman $[59]$ identified six different categories of fit and the statistical techniques that are appropriate in each category.

The relevant research literature reviewed above therefore indicates that IS plays different roles in organizations, that organizations require different planning and support systems to support the various roles of IS, and that it is important to develop a proper fit between the characteristics of the planning system and the role of IS in the organization.

## Research Variables

THE DEVELOPMENT OF A DEFINITION FOR THE role of IS using the “strategic grid” framework, the first objective of this study, is described below, followed by a discussion of the other research variables.

## The Role of IS

The core of our definition of the “role of IS” in the organization is the mission of the IS function and its actual impact on the firm’s business operations. There are a variety of classificatory schemes, based on some variation of IS diffusion/sophistication, that deal with some of the aspects of our definition of the role of IS $[33, 38, 54, 71]$ . McFarlan’s $[33]$ “strategic grid” was selected as the appropriate framework for defining the role of IS because it deals with both the mission of IS and the actual impact of IS through its two dimensions—the strategic impact of the future application development portfolio and the strategic impact of existing systems. Organizations are classified into four groups—strategic, turnaround, factory, and support, as shown in figure 1.

Organizations in the “strategic” group are critically dependent on the smooth functioning of IS applications for their daily operations and have applications under development that are vital to their competitive success. Organizations in the “turnaround” group are not dependent on IS support for their current operations. However, they have applications under development that are vital for their long-term strategic objectives. Organizations in the “factory” group are critically dependent on IS for their daily operations, but their future application development is not critical. Organizations in the “support” group are critically dependent on IS for neither their present operations nor their future operations. They may have large IS departments but they are not critical to the organization’s business.

## IS Planning System Variables

Since a primary focus of the study is to evaluate the differences in IS planning system among firms that have various roles for IS, three categories of variables that describe the various aspects of an IS planning system were chosen—planning characteristics, organizational support characteristics, and planning performance characteristics.

The first category consists of three variables related to the IS planning process—the quality of the IS planning process, the planning time horizon, and IS planning resources. These variables have been widely used in prior research studies on IS planning $[14, 24, 29, 33, 42, 45]$ . These studies are subsequently discussed in the context of formulation of research hypotheses.

An organization's support environment is as important as the planning system itself, since it provides the infrastructure to create a conducive environment for IS planning and implementation [24, 30]. The five variables considered in this category are: (1) IS-business integration; (2) quality of support mechanisms; (3) top management involvement in IS planning; (4) user involvement in IS planning; and (5) IS resources. These variables also have been widely used in IS research [8, 16, 20, 28, 45, 48, 72]. Detailed discussion of each variable is deferred for the development of research hypotheses.

The last category, performance characteristics, evaluates whether there are differences in various performance measures among firms that have different roles for IS. Since the role of IS is based on the basic premise that IS has differential impact on the business operations and performance of organizations $[8]$ , it is important to evaluate the performance differences among the groups to get a comprehensive picture of the role of IS in an organization. In this study performance is evaluated at three levels: the planning level, the functional level, and the organizational level. The variables are respectively termed: (1) planning effectiveness; (2) performance of the IS function; and (3) IS contribution to organizational performance $[18]$ .

## Research Hypotheses

THE RESEARCH HYPOTHESES THAT TEST THE DIFFERENCES in IS planning system characteristics among organizations with different roles for IS, the second objective of this study, are discussed in this section.

## Quality of the Planning Process

The role of IS in an organization determines the firm's vision, priorities, and growth directions for the IS function [8]. IS planning, which identifies the long-term plans and growth directions for the function, will differ between firms that foresee significant role for IS in the future from those that do not do so. It can be expected that the quality of the strategic IS planning process, which addresses strategic issues, will be better for firms that have high expectations for the future impact of IS [33]. Hence:

Hypothesis 1a: Organizations in the strategic/turnaround groups will have better-quality planning processes than organizations in the factory/support groups of the strategic grid.

## Planning Time Horizon

Organizations that have a strategic vision for IS can be expected to have a longer planning time horizon that is commensurate with their long-term vision $[34, 70]$ . Hence, firms in the strategic and turnaround groups can be expected to have a longer planning time horizon compared with the other two groups $[33]$ . Hence:

Hypothesis 1b: Organizations in the strategic/turnaround groups will have longer planning time horizon than organizations in the factory/support groups of the strategic grid.

## IS Planning Resources

The resources allocated to IS planning are dependent on the organization's vision for IS and its commitment to IS planning for achieving that vision [23, 25, 33]. Organizations that envision a significant future role for IS can be expected to devote more effort to IS planning [33]. However, some researchers have found that a few firms have developed major strategic IS applications without significant amount of IS planning [47, 49]. Since these studies are based on a small number of case examples, more reliance is placed on large-scale empirical studies, such as the one conducted by Raghunathan and Raghunathan [45] that found that firms that plan for a strategic role for IS allocate greater resources to planning compared with those that do not do so. Therefore:

Hypothesis 1c: Organizations in the strategic/turnaround groups will use more planning resources compared to organizations in the factory/support groups of the strategic grid.

## IS-Business Integration

Organizations that plan for a significant role for IS in the future will need better integration of IS plans with their business plans to ensure that their information systems are in alignment with the business strategy of the organization $[33,45]$ . Hence:

Hypothesis 2a: Organizations in the strategic/turnaround groups will have better IS-business integration compared with organizations in the support/factory groups of the strategic grid.

## Quality of Support Mechanisms

Essential to the integration of business and IS plans is the free flow of information between top management, business planning staff, and the IS planners. Organizational support mechanisms are needed to facilitate the information flow between the two planning systems $[28]$ . Cash et al. $[8]$ suggest that firms that plan for a significant role for IS in the future should have good support mechanisms to help it to align better with the business plans. Therefore:

Hypothesis 2b: Organizations in the strategic/turnaround groups will have better-quality support mechanisms than organizations in the support/factory groups of the strategic grid.

## Top Management and User Involvement

McFarlan et al. [33] predict that there will be greater involvement of top management and users in the IS planning process for organizations in the strategy and turnaround groups. This has been empirically verified by Raghunathan and Raghunathan [45] with respect to top management involvement and support. Hence:

Hypothesis 2c: Organizations in the strategic/turnaround groups will have more top management involvement than organizations in the support/factory groups of the strategic grid.

Hypothesis 2d: Organizations in the strategic/turnaround groups will have more user involvement than organizations in the support/factory groups of the strategic grid.

## IS Resources

Organizations must allocate sufficient resources to the IS function to ensure that IS plans are successfully implemented $[14]$ . The resources allocated to an IS department are expected to be dependent on the size of its operations, and its criticality and impact on business operations. Hence, firms where IS has a significant and critical impact on the current business operations can be expected to have larger IS resources compared with other firms. However, McFarlan et al. $[33]$ argue that it is not necessarily true. They contend that there could be organizations with large IS resources but with relatively low impact of IS on their business operations. Hence we propose the null hypothesis:

Hypothesis 2e: There is no difference in the resources allocated to IS among organizations in the strategic, turnaround, support, or factory groups of the strategic grid.

## Planning Effectiveness

IS planning is often initiated in organizations to achieve a few key planning objectives, such as better integration of IS plans with business plans, better identification of strategic IS applications, and better communication of IS plans to users. The achievement of these objectives can be considered to be a measure of planning effectiveness [46]. Organizations that plan for a significant role for the IS function can be expected to have greater attainment of these planning objectives. Success in planning is to a large extent dependent on the organization's culture and level of resistance to planning [31, 52]. Ramanujam and Venkatraman [46] found that resistance to planning reduced the effectiveness of planning systems. Raghunathan and Raghunathan [45] found that the level of acceptance of planning and the perceived usefulness of planning are higher in firms that are in the strategic or turnaround groups than in those in the other two groups. Hence, firms in these two groups, because of lesser resistance to planning and greater awareness of potential of IS technology, will have a more conducive climate for IS planning, thereby leading to greater planning effectiveness. Hence:

Hypothesis 3a: Organizations in the strategic/turnaround groups will have greater planning effectiveness compared to organizations in the support/factory groups of the strategic grid.

## Performance of IS Function

McFarlan et al. [33] predict that for firms where IS has significant impact on the present operations—that is, firms in the strategic or factory group—the IS department must maintain a high level of performance, since, for example, the impact of a shutdown of the computer center can have a disastrous effect on business operations. For instance, American Airlines suffered significant revenue loss when their computers were down for twelve hours in 1989 ([32], p. 329). In these firms, the performance of the IS function in terms of quality of service, accuracy of information, and the reliability of system must be high to meet the critical current needs of the organization. Hence:

Hypothesis 3b: Organizations in the strategic/factory groups will have better IS performance compared to the organizations in the support/turnaround groups of the strategic grid.

## IS Contribution to Organization's Performance

Information systems are widely believed to have the ability to influence organizational performance in a variety of ways, such as through improved sales revenue, market share, return on investment (ROI), or customer satisfaction. Many case studies have described the competitive use of IS [7, 13, 21]. Researchers have suggested that the return from information systems is dependent on the system's strategic role [53, 64]. Firms in the strategic or factory group, such as American Airlines [8], have been found to increase their market share from strategic information systems [13]. Other illustrations of firms, such as McKesson [11] and American Hospital Supply [62], provide evidence of how information systems can impact business performance. Since this study focuses on current performance, it can be expected that firms that have a significant impact of IS on present operations will have a greater IS contribution to organizational performance. Hence:

Hypothesis 3c: Organizations in the strategic/factory groups will have a greater IS contribution to organizational performance compared to organizations in the support/turnaround groups of the strategic grid.

## Research Design

## Operationalization of Research Variables

MULTI-ITEM INDICATORS WERE USED FOR MEASURING the perception of the head of the IS department concerning the various research variables. Most of the items were measured using a seven-point Likert-type scale. The “role of IS” was assessed using the two dimensions of the “strategic grid” (figure 1). Ten items were derived based on guidelines provided by McFarlan et al. [33]. For the “future impact of IS” dimension, respondents were asked to indicate their degree of agreement with statements such as information systems will provide new ways for firm to compete, new systems are being developed to create new products/services, and the like. For the “present impact of IS” dimension, respondents were asked to assess the degree of disruption caused by computer shutdown, feasibility of manually processing the data of critical operations, and so on. Organizations were classified into one of four groups (support, factory, turnaround, or strategic) based on whether their responses along the two dimensions were either above or below the mean values of the sample.

Since IS planning process is a multifaceted activity involving the analysis of the external, internal, and technology environment and its impact on the firm, the quality of the planning process was assessed in terms of the extent of detailed analysis devoted to planning activities in these three major areas $[4, 24, 34]$ . For example, respondents were asked to indicate the level of detailed analysis devoted to the analysis of technology trends, development of information architecture, and the like. Its quality is also evaluated based on external standards of good planning practices $[24, 52]$ .

Planning time horizon was measured in years on a continuous scale. Planning resources were measured using the percentage of the total IS staff actively involved in IS planning $[23]$ . This relative measure was chosen to avoid difficulties in interpretation due to differences in the sizes of the IS activity. The resources devoted to IS were measured as the proportion of sales revenue allocated to the IS budget. Both planning and IS resources were measured using seven percentage range categories (ranging from 0–0.5 percent to greater than 12 percent). Subsequently, for easier data analysis and interpretation, the sample was classified into three broad groups (low, medium, and high).

Both user and top management involvement were measured using a single-item scale. IS–business integration was measured in terms of senior management's understanding of the potential of IS in their business operations, IS planners' understanding of the business plans of the firm, and the clarity of the business plans in providing directions for IS planning [63, 71].

The quality of support mechanisms was measured using two items—the degree of participation of the IS manager in strategic business planning and level of interactions between business and IS planners. A third item, the existence of a steering committee, was found to have very low interitem correlation with the other two items and hence was dropped.

The degree of fulfillment of IS planning objectives was used as a measure of planning effectiveness $[44, 61]$ . In this study, planning effectiveness was assessed by the extent of fulfillment of seven common IS planning objectives.

IS performance was measured using a single item. The contribution of IS to organizational performance was measured as a weighted average of its contribution to five performance measures—ROI, sales revenue, market share, operating efficiency, and customer satisfaction. The first three performance measures have been extensively used in strategy research $[60]$ , and the fourth measure is based on the traditional notion of using computers to improve operating efficiency and reduce costs. The customer satisfaction measure reflects current theory that goes beyond more traditional measures. Organizations may favor one measure over another, based on their business objectives, requirements, and constraints. For example, one firm may favor using IS primarily for increasing sales, while another may use IS for improving internal efficiency. Hence, respondents were asked to allocate 100 points among the five performance measures based on their importance to the organization. They also indicated the level of IS contribution to each of the five performance measures on a seven-point Likert-type scale. IS contribution was calculated as the average of the contribution to the five measures weighted by the importance rating.

## Data Collection

Although the “strategic grid” framework is logical and intuitive, and has been used for studying organizations $[8]$ , its validity has been empirically tested to only a limited extent $[45]$ . It is important to validate the framework and generalize the results across a wide cross-section of organizations. Hence, a large-scale field survey was chosen as the appropriate methodology for data collection. A detailed questionnaire was designed to measure the various research constructs. The items in the questionnaire were initially derived from an extensive literature review. The instrument was pretested for content validity, general format, and readability with three advanced doctoral students. Subsequently, it was pretested with three faculty. After the feedback from pretesting was incorporated, the instrument was tested with three senior practicing IS executives.

The target population included 720 companies, randomly chosen from The Corporate 1000 [56], a directory of the 1,000 largest manufacturing and service companies in the United States. The target respondents were the senior executives in the IS department. The limitations of using a single source to represent a company's position is well recognized. However, this is addressed by targeting the response from the head of the IS function, under the expectations that he or she can provide a broader organizational perspective of IS operations. The use of responses from individual departments was considered and dropped because of the potential for errors from targeting the wrong department/person, or receiving responses based on user's narrow perspective of IS operations. In order to reduce the possibility of informant bias, multi-item indicators were used to measure most of the constructs.

Two hundred and forty-nine responses were received for a response rate of 35 percent. The sample characteristics are illustrated in Table 1. The respondents were well distributed across industry groups and sales revenue levels, thereby providing greater generalizability of the results. An analysis of the respondents who provided their organizational title indicated that a significant number of them were indeed senior IS executives.

## Validity and Reliability Assessments

The items used for measuring the various constructs were tested for validity and reliability. While validity measures the extent to which the indicator measures the underlying construct, reliability measures the stability of the scale $[40]$ .

Content validity of the constructs, which evaluates if all the dimensions of the construct are being measured $[10]$ , was established through the elaborate process used in identifying the items and refining them through the various phases of pre-testing.

Construct validity was evaluated using factor analysis to determine if all the items measuring the construct cluster together and measure a single construct. Initially, the correlation matrix of the items measuring the construct was analyzed to identify outliers that have very low interitem correlations. An analysis of the correlation matrix revealed that two items measuring the “impact of IS on present operations” and one item measuring “impact of IS on future operations” were outliers. Subsequently, principal component factor analysis with varimax rotation was used to determine if the items cluster together and load on a single factor. All the constructs, except one, exhibited significant convergent validity. Factor analysis was also used to test the discriminant validity of the constructs. Based on criteria of factor loading, eigenvalue, and variance explained $[69]$ , it was found that all the constructs exhibited significant discriminant validity.

## Evaluation of Fit between Quality of Planning Process and Role of IS

The third objective of this study was to evaluate the relationship between various performance measures and the fit between the role of IS in the organization and the quality of the planning process. When fit is evaluated using an interaction perspective (in our case, as an interaction of the role of IS and the quality of planning process influencing performance), the “moderator” framework is the appropriate data-analytic approach to be used in evaluating the effect of fit $[59]$ . Hence, the regression coefficients of the predictor variable in the two groups, formed by the contextual variable, will be compared to determine the significance of the relationship $[2, 50]$ .

Separate regression analyses between the quality of the planning process and the three performance variables were performed for the support and strategic groups. The statistical significance of the difference in the regression coefficient between the two groups is determined using a t-statistic, as shown in Table 10 [2].

These results indicate that there is significant difference in the regression coefficients between the two groups with respect to planning effectiveness $p < 0.01$ and the IS contribution to organization's performance $p < 0.1$ . The difference is not significant with respect to IS performance. The effect of fit therefore seems to be most felt at the planning level and less on the levels of IS and organizational performance. The results are understandable since some direct effect of planning on planning effectiveness can be expected, and firms in the support group tend to have lower-quality planning compared with firms in the strategic group.

## Summary

THE OPERATIONALIZATION OF THE STRATEGIC GRID that is developed in this study and the results of the various hypotheses that are based on it serve to validate the grid as a way of assessing the role played by IS in an organization.

The hypothesis (1a) dealing with the quality of the planning process shows that it is significantly better for firms that foresee a significant role for IS in the future (strategic/turnaround groups) compared with those that do not foresee such a role (support/factory groups). Further analysis reveals that the mean values are not markedly different between strategic and turnaround group, or between factory and support group. This suggests that only one of the two dimensions of the strategic grid (the impact of IS on future operations) has the most significant influence on the quality of the planning process. This is consistent with the findings of Raghunathan and Raghunathan [45], who found that the “future” dimension had a greater impact on the planning dimensions. McFarlan et al. [33] also predict that the strategic and turnaround groups are more likely to be similar in planning characteristics and different from the other two groups.

Both the planning time horizon used by the organization and the level of resources devoted to planning are significantly different for the two sets of groups. Taken together, these results strongly confirm the expectations concerning the way in which firms that have chosen various roles for IS will plan for IS—namely, that firms in the strategic/turnaround group believe in planning, commit more resources to planning, have a long-term planning horizon, and perform quality planning to enable the IS function to have a significant role in the future operations of the organization.

This interpretation of the strategic grid is further validated by an analysis of differences in the IS resources in the various groups. A significant proportion of firms in the factory group (26.3 percent) are in the “high” category, This is greater than the proportion of firms in the turnaround group (8 percent). This indicates that while the planning budget is more dependent on the “future role” dimension of the “strategic grid,” the IS resources are more dependent on the “present role” dimension. These results are appealing since firms in the turnaround group are merely planning for major future investment in IS operations, while firms in the factory group must have already made a significant investment in the IS function to enable it to achieve its present critical role in the firm’s operations.

The hypotheses related to organizational support mechanisms reveal that, as anticipated, firms in strategic/turnaround groups have significantly higher levels of IS-business integration, quality in support mechanisms, top management and user involvement than firms in the support/factory groups. As noted earlier, the IS resources dimension of organizational support was found to be quite different in the four groups of the grid.

Of special interest is the result that top management involvement is the lowest in the “support” group. This is in line with general management thinking and the critical success factor approach, in which top management allocate their time on the few things that are important for the successful performance of the firm. This is reflected in a quote by a senior manager of an organization in the “support” group [33]:

There is no payoff in my spending time here. Any conceivable improvements in this department's performance will have a negligible impact on the company as a whole.

The fact that organizations in the strategic group have a greater IS contribution to organizational performance compared with the support group implies greater predictive validity for the grid, since the core of the definition of “role of IS” is based on the impact of IS on the firm’s operations.

The results of discriminant analysis revealed that most of the planning system variables were important to discriminate between the support and strategic groups and the discriminant function had significant classificatory ability, indicating that they were indeed separate groups with clear differences in planning parameters.

Another objective of the study was to determine the relationship between performance measures and the “fit” between the planning process and the role of IS. The results of the analysis of “fit” were mixed. While there was significant relationship between “fit” and planning effectiveness and to some extent with IS’s contribution to organizational performance, the relationship with IS performance was not significant.

## Implications for Practitioners

There are three major ways in which the results of this study can be used to improve organizations—to improve communications, to improve strategy formulation, and to improve IS planning and organizational processes. Communications may be improved because this study provides a practical mechanism for assessing the role of IS in the organization. There is a significant need for practitioners to be able to make such an assessment. For instance, a vendor of IS products and services needs to be able to make such an assessment if he or she is to approach a potential customer in an appropriate fashion.

Managers within an organization also need to be able to do so in order that they understand the context of top management's policies and directives, and so that they can attempt to influence IS strategy. There is no greater waste of management time and effort than activities that are based on misunderstandings of fundamental roles such as the role of IS in the organization. Such assessments can facilitate communications between users, top management, and IS management. This will reduce the "communication gap" or mismatch, described by McFarlan et al. [33], that could exist between the perception of senior management on the role of IS and the actual role of IS in the organization. It would also help senior management in determining the appropriate infrastructure for IS management in terms of: (a) level of involvement of senior management in steering committee, (b) risk profile in project portfolio, (c) level of integration with business planning, (d) rank of IS executive in the organization's hierarchy, (e) level of technical innovation, and (f) extent of performance monitoring [33].

The operationalization of strategic grid can also facilitate in strategy formulation. It is important to realize that the strategic grid is a dynamic framework and that firms can constantly be shifting from one group to another. The movement may take place at their initiative or be forced by external conditions. For instance, because of a strategic IS system, a firm can be positioned in the strategic group, but over a period of time competitors may develop a similar system, resulting in loss of competitive advantage and therefore a reduced level of impact of IS on business performance measures such as sales and market share. Sustaining the competitive advantage with strategic IS requires constant refinement of existing systems to keep ahead of the competitors $[12]$ . The constant refinement and innovation process becomes more difficult as competitors gain speed and a certain amount of saturation is reached in the technology. Hopper $[20]$ describes the evolution of the Sabre system at American Airlines, and explains the current problems in gaining additional competitive advantage from the system. Unless the IS manager continuously evaluates the firm's application development portfolio with respect to technology and its competitors, very soon the external market environment may force the firm to shift from a strategic to a factory group. On the other hand, firms in strategic group may decide, due to other business constraints, to consolidate its present IS systems and operate it effectively, thereby moving to the factory group. Koerner $[26]$ reports the experience at Merrill Lynch where the firm decided to consolidate its present systems rather than try new innovations in IS technology.

Practitioners may use the operationalization of the “quality of IS planning” developed here and the other results of the study for self-evaluation and to improve IS planning and organizational processes. This will help them to better understand what they should do to establish an appropriate fit between the role of IS and these planning and organizational mechanisms. For instance, a firm might use the “quality of planning process” instrument to make a comprehensive assessment of the IS planning system and determine the changes required to support the future operations. Firms desirous of moving from one group to another must develop a detailed migration plan. For example, if an organization is planning to shift from the support group to the strategic group, it needs to establish suitable organizational mechanisms to ensure sufficient involvement of top management/users in the planning process. It may also need to change its planning time horizon to reflect the long-term perspective, establish better linkages with its business planning group, allocate more resources to planning, and change the scope and focus of planning.

## Implications for Researchers

This study's empirical validation of the “strategic grid” framework and the operationalization of the “role of IS” provides researchers with a useful framework and an instrument for use in future studies. This study focused on the effect of the role of IS on only one component of IS management—planning. Future research could address the relationship between the role of IS and other components of the IS function such as system development practices, control systems for operation including chargeback systems, IS performance evaluation, rank of IS executive, and technical expertise/sophistication.

Another interesting area for future research is to identify the antecedent factors that cause an organization to adopt a particular role for IS in their organization. Some of the factors that could be considered are the strategic business posture of the organization, the level of competition in the industry, the information intensity in the operations, and environmental characteristics. Identification of the antecedent factors will help organizations to determine the appropriate role for IS in their organization.

Organizations can then determine the mismatch between the actual role and the ideal role and formulate suitable strategies to reach that role. Also, we could examine the impact of the level of mismatch on organizational performance.

In this study we did not relate the IS variables to business strategy. An interesting perspective would be to evaluate the relationship between business strategy and role of IS and its impact on IS's contribution to organizational performance. For instance, Floyd and Wooldridge [15] found that business strategy influences the link between IS and performance. We could examine the relationship between Porter's generic strategies [41] and the role of IS. Another possibility in the business strategy area is to examine the relationship between the Miles and Snow's typology of organizations [35] and the role of IS in an organization. We can evaluate if prospectors are more likely to be in the strategic group compared to defenders. Taking it further, if we can develop a reference profile for IS planning and operations for firms in the different groups of the Miles and Snow [35] typology, and match it with the role of IS, we can examine the impact of fit between business strategy and the role of IS on IS's contribution to organizational performance.

The results of this study do have some limitations that need to be recognized. The sample for this study was primarily from large organizations, and to that extent the results of the study can only be generalized to similar organizations. The cross-sectional nature of this study limits the ability to imply causality in the relationships. Also, the use of perceptual measures from a single respondent in an organization could result in some informant bias, and hence the results must be interpreted with caution.
