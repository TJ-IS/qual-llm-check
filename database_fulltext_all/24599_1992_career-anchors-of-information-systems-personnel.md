# Career Anchors of Information Systems Personnel

RAYMOND G. CREPEAU, CONNIE W. CROOK, MARTIN D. GOSLAR, AND MARK E. MCMURTREY

RAYMOND G. CREPEAU is an Assistant Professor of Computer Information Systems in the School of Business Administration at Western Carolina University. Currently a doctoral candidate in MIS in the College of Business Administration at the University of South Carolina, he received a B.A. in Economics from Providence College and an M.B.A. from Auburn University. He has published in the Journal of Systems Management and has presented papers at several conferences. His current research interests focus on application of expert systems in business and knowledge acquisition.

CONNIE W. CROOK is currently a doctoral candidate in the College of Business Administration at the University of South Carolina. Ms. Crook has several years industry experience dealing with end-user computing. She received a B.S. in mathematics from the University of North Carolina at Greensboro, and an M.Ed. and M.S. from North Carolina State University. She has presented papers at several conferences. Her current research interests are associated with end-user computing and communication support systems.

MARTIN D. GOSLAR is an Associate Professor of Business Computer Systems on the faculty of the College of Business Administration and Economics at New Mexico State University. Dr. Goslar has several years industry experience dealing with systems development and implementation. He has published IS research in a variety of journals including Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Sciences, Journal of Systems Management, Journal of Computer Information Systems, Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science, and the Journal of Consumer Marketing.

MARK E. MCMURTREY is a doctoral candidate in the College of Business Administration at the University of South Carolina. He received a B.S.B.A. and an M.B.A. from the University of Arkansas. His current research interests include database requirements for manufacturing planning and control systems and end-user computing.

ABSTRACT: Career research in the information systems (IS) field has primarily focused on external motivators associated with organizational factors and material incentives. IS empirical research investigating the internalized career orientations of practitioners is necessary to match individual expectations with corporate human resource planning.

Acknowledgment: This research was funded, in part, by a grant from the Division of Research, College of Business Administration, University of South Carolina. The authors gratefully acknowledge Dr. Thomas J. DeLong and the anonymous referees for reviewing earlier versions of this paper.

This study identifies several career anchors or orientations maintained by IS personnel. By administering the Career Orientations Inventory to 321 IS personnel, Managerial and Technical Competence, Identity, Service, Organizational Stability, and Variety career orientations are distinguished. These internal anchors significantly influence how IS and human resource management should (1) plan corporate IS career paths, (2) nurture IS employees during their careers, and (3) encourage appropriate communication about career advancement.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: career orientations, information systems career planning, management of information systems personnel.

## 1. Introduction

EFFECTIVE MANAGEMENT OF INFORMATION SYSTEMS (IS) PROFESSIONALS has been a major concern to those responsible for their productivity and career development. Several studies emphasize the perceived importance of IS career management. In separate Delphi studies, Couger [11] discovered that human resource executives identified better IS human resource planning as the top issue in the 1990s; IS executives noted this issue in the top four. Martin [30] found that IS executives in a broad range of industries identified human resource development as the third most important critical success factor. Dickson et al. [17] reported key information systems issues for the 1980s: specification, recruitment, and development ranked eighth. In another confirming study, Hartog and Herbert [23] found that recruitment and training ranked seventh in terms of importance by IS managers in the midwest. The concerns evidenced in these studies emphasize that, since IS growth and promotions are slowing, increasing focus on effectively managing IS professionals will continue [10].

Strategies for effective IS career management have been associated with several problems hypothesized to be information systems related: turnover, burnout, limited advancement potential, nominal corporate commitment, supervisory aversion, and exceptional compensation [42]. Interestingly, although those vested in managing IS personnel recognize that more attention toward career development is needed, there remains a lack of attention to careers and career planning in this field [20, 43].

## 1.1. IS Career-Related Research

Few studies have investigated IS career planning and development. The reported studies focused on external factors that guide individuals through their careers. Following is a summary of the primary empirical IS research dealing with career issues.

Motivation has been the primary focus of IS career issue research. Research shows that motivation factors have not changed dramatically from 1977 to 1987 (except pay and benefits, which climbed in rank from eighth to fourth). While different career orientations are based in part on the underlying motivational values inherent in individuals, Couger [10, 12] reported analysts and programmers are motivated by the following: (1) the work itself, (2) opportunity for achievement, (3) opportunity for advancement, (4) pay and benefits, (5) recognition, (6) increased responsibility, (7)

technical supervision, (8) interpersonal relations, (9) job security, (10) work conditions, and (11) company policies. In a similar vein, Green [21] reported several systems analysts' perceptions of their job skills and roles. Comparing this professional group with systems users, systems analysts reported valuing behavioral skills (e.g., diplomacy and political sensitivity) more than the perceived importance of technical skills (e.g., programming). Nonsalary incentives such as promotion, recognition from others, and personal growth and development are deemed very important by this group. Green concluded that project managers should ensure analysts are provided with performance feedback based on specific task requirements to enhance their motivation.

Ferratt and Short [19] compared motivational patterns (guidance, social, esteem, achievement, and power needs) of IS with non-IS people. They found no significant differences between the two groups. This conclusion was further supported and amplified with alternative statistical approaches [24]. However, Myers [31] found highly significant differences between IS and non-IS professionals in the area of needs. Nash and Redwine [32] noted that computer personnel research revealed vast differences in the abilities, cognitive perspectives, and personality styles of computer professionals.

Diversity has also been recognized in terms of developing unique career paths for those associated with particular computer technologies and organizational structures $[9, 34]$ . Kaiser $[26]$ found little evidence that formal paths exist. In addition, a trend toward migration to user areas was discovered. Washing and Boveington $[43]$ confirmed Kaiser's finding: IS personnel are not receiving sufficient career path communication and feel left out of existing management career development planning. Even when career paths are perceived to exist, IS professionals are willing to move in and out of different paths frequently. This trend may mean that IS professionals either do not have the requisite knowledge to identify suitable individual paths or lack flexible career planning that may compel movement to other firms $[41]$ . Yet little empirical research exists to confirm this notion.

To provide insight into individual career decisions, this study was undertaken to examine empirically the internal aspects of IS careers. The organizational career literature provides both the research foundation and the instrumentation for this study.

## 1.2. Individual-Organization Career Literature

Edgar Schein presented a set of concepts concerning the interrelated nature of organization, individual, and career in 1971 [35, 36, 37, 38, 39]. These concepts were initially derived from research conducted from 1958 to 1964 and later refined at the Sloan School of Management at MIT. From that time, a substantial body of literature has evolved that further validates what is now referred to as Schein's career anchor model. Schein [37, p. 127] maintains that career anchors contain a “pattern of self-perceived talents, motives, and values [which] . . . guide, constrain, stabilize, and integrate the person’s career.” These self-perceptions or career anchors influence career choices, employer changes, other career-related decisions, and ultimately how the future is viewed. The career anchor model underscores evolutionary changes to career orientations as workers gain work experience.

Schein [37] identified five career anchors during research conducted in the early 1960s. The first, Managerial Competence, represents the need to be competent in the activities associated with management, such as problem analysis skills, emotional stability, and interpersonal competence. A second anchor, Technical Competence, is associated with motivation for the challenge of a technical field, functional area, or content of the work (not the managerial process). The Security/Stability anchor symbolizes the desire for an organization that provides long-run stability, good benefits, and basic job security. The fourth anchor is Creativity/Entrepreneurship. This embodies the need to create something, to try new projects. Schein's final anchor, Autonomy/Independence, encompasses a person's need to be free of constraint to pursue professional or technical competence. While recognizing that work experience in early years is particularly influential in forming individual-career anchors, these dispositions also are applicable in later career stages.

Schein's career anchor model resulted primarily from interview sessions. DeLong [15] attempted to validate the career anchor model empirically. Beyond the five anchors identified by Schein, DeLong investigated three additional career variables suggested in the earlier research: (1) Identity (the desire for status and prestige from belonging to certain companies or organizations), (2) Service (concern with helping others and seeing changes that result from efforts), and (3) Variety (the desire for several different challenges). Operationalizing Schein's model through a research questionnaire, DeLong's study validated Schein's conclusions by clearly identifying the five anchors and, additionally, distinguishing these three career orientations. In some studies, DeLong found that the Security/Stability anchor emerged as two independent anchors [14, 15]. One, Stability, represents individuals who will accept an organizational definition of their career. The other, Security, is demonstrated by individuals who will move from company to company to ensure permanence in a geographical area. Finally, DeLong emphasized that the career anchor is a “composite of one’s career orientations (needs, attitudes, and values) and one’s self-perceived talents” [14, p. 58].

Empirical studies [4, 7, 16, 44] involving several occupational groups (e.g., engineers and law enforcement) have confirmed the existence of these eight or nine identifiable career anchors/orientations.

## 2. The Study

THIS STUDY IS DESIGNED TO INVESTIGATE CAREER orientations of IS personnel. Although this stream of research has been highly encouraged in the IS field, no systematic data about career anchors or orientations of IS personnel have been reported. Ginzberg and Baroudi [20] suggest ten important career research propositions to be investigated before specific IS personnel recommendations can be formulated. These propositions are strongly influenced by the career anchor approach to career planning and strategy. Additional IS personnel and career path research must be conducted to advise both IS academicians and practitioners on the efficacy of current practice and future problems. Studies in referent fields have provided a solid empirical foundation and a refined research instrument for application to IS. The dual-career ladder [2], changing career perspectives, and dynamic changes in information technologies and techniques mandate that insights for career development be available. The importance of research for both IS management and academics in terms of career planning and development firmly supports progressive study of this area [11].

This research examines whether these career anchors are operative for those in the IS field. Two research propositions are investigated:

Proposition 1: IS personnel possess a wide variety of career anchors/orientations.

While there have been a number of studies investigating career perspectives in other career fields (e.g., engineering, medicine, law, education, religion), little empirical research has been published in the IS field. Prior research noted above involving different occupations established that subjects in these groups exhibit all or most of the career anchors identified earlier, with some being more dominant than others $[6, 14]$ . Support for this proposition would establish that IS personnel are similar to personnel in other fields. Commensurate expectations for the IS profession were held a priori.

Past literature in the field has assumed a dual-career track perspective (i.e., managerial or technical). Both practitioners and academicians have based their approaches on this assumption. Recommendations concerning management of IS personnel often emphasize the dual-career ladder construct. Individuals in this occupation are viewed as either “managerially” or “technically” oriented. Analysis is necessary to confirm or refute this traditional perspective; also, investigation of this proposition enlarges the IS career research foundation for more productive insights into IS career planning. Thus, the following proposition was also examined:

Proposition 2: The dominant career orientations exhibited by IS personnel will be either managerial or technical.

Allen and Katz [2] emphasized that the dual-career ladder system has been used by industry for years. This system presumes motivational and efficiency benefits for both the organization and the individual. Feldman [18] states that there is a dichotomy between technical and managerial competence: technically oriented employees are reluctant to switch work in their specialty for managerial responsibility. IS personnel are often linked with engineers who are reluctant to change responsibilities. Individuals with a managerial orientation often view their early technical jobs as stepping stones to supervisory levels [20]. Investigating orientations of specialists in the IS field will influence the likely success of this approach. Support for this proposition is crucial if the prevailing industry view for IS staff is to be accepted.

## 2.1. Research Design

For data collection, the Career Orientations Inventory (COI) survey developed by Thomas DeLong was used (see  \( [13, 14, 15, 16] \) ). The COI has been refined and validated in several studies including Wood et al.  \( [44] \) , Burke  \( [5] \) , and DeLong  \( [14,

16]. The COI provides a pretested instrument with demonstrated high internal validity and reliability [45]. The instrument contains forty-one questions that measure five anchor and three career orientation variables described earlier (Security/Stability, Technical Competence, Managerial Competence, Creativity, Autonomy/Entrepreneurship, Identity, Service, and Variety). Demographic questions were modified for this subject sample. Six-point Likert response scales for subject responses were designed to avoid neutral answers; previous researchers have administered four-, five-, and six-point scales.

Subjects $(n=321)$ participating in this research are located in a large (over 500,000) metropolitan area in the southeastern United States. This sample was obtained from seven organizations agreeing to make their employees available. These individuals work in a variety of settings: financial, state government, public utility, U.S. military, city government and health care industries are represented. Of the respondents, 40 percent are employed in governmental units, 31 percent in financial institutions, and 29 percent are employed in other types of enterprises. Table 1 summarizes the demographics of participating subjects.

Participating organizations permitted research staff to administer the COI to IS employees on site. Subjects voluntarily met at prearranged times during working hours to complete the instrument. In larger organizations where subjects worked different shifts, several survey administration sessions were required. Respondents completed the questionnaire within fifteen to twenty minutes.

## 2.2. Statistical Methodology

Two factor analyses were accomplished using the Common Factor Model [22]. Bartlett's test of sphericity [22] and the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy [25] were employed to confirm the appropriateness of factor analysis for this data. Both tests were conducted due to sensitivity associated with large sample size. These results suggest the presence of homogeneous groups of variables and appropriate application of factor analysis.

Principal components analysis confirmed the existence of IS career anchors. Factor analysis applying varimax rotation identified eight factors using the latent root and scree test criterion. Appendix A lists survey questions used for each construct with notation for those items that did not satisfy a minimum factor loading of 0.40 (consistent with DeLong [14]). The analysis identified the following factors: (1) managerial competence, (2) technical competence, (3) autonomy, (4) service, (5) identity, (6) variety, (7) organizational stability, and (8) geographic security. Since responses associated with the creativity construct did not load on any factor, they were excluded from further analysis. Subject responses clearly distinguished two dimensions of Schein's security/stability anchor: organizational stability (i.e., long-term employment) and geographic location (i.e., remaining in one geographical location). These results are consistent with prior research (see [14, 15]).

Cronbach's alpha was calculated for each factor to establish reliability and determine internal consistency [8]. As reported in Table 2, ranges of 0.5–0.8 surpass acceptable reliability coefficient levels [33].

Second-order factor analysis $[3, 27, 29]$ was employed to summarize subject responses. This technique identifies interfactor correlations among the factors derived in the first-order factor analysis. DeLong $[14, 16]$ used this approach to determine which factors would cluster together and found that his samples clustered into distinct groups based on similarities of career orientations. A similar clustering occurred with our sample. The second-order factor analysis produced the pattern and structure matrices shown in appendix B. The data suggest a clustering of our respondents around three dimensions. Dimension 1, descriptively labeled “Leadership,” consists of the career factors Managerial Competence, Service, Identity, and Variety. The “Organizational Stability” factor defines Dimension 2, and “Technical Competence” constitutes Dimension 3. Importantly, Technical Competence is negatively correlated with the Managerial Competence factor in the Leadership dimension.

## 3. Discussion

IS PERSONNEL INCORPORATE A WIDE CROSS-SECTION of individuals performing clerical, technical, and managerial duties. This broad range of career perspectives is evident in the different subject responses by job category (computer operator, programmer, systems analyst, and manager) summarized in figure 1.

Two recommendations in the IS career literature (e.g., [1, 19, 20, 29, 40]) allude to implementing a dual-career ladder and to focusing on career planning. The career ladder concept presumably allows individuals in the field to advance without assuming managerial responsibilities while others pursue a traditional advancement approach. From a career anchor perspective, this approach suggests that IS personnel display either technical or managerial orientations in their career planning.

## 3.1. IS Career Orientations

Results of this research demonstrate that study respondents possess several, largely independent, career anchors. Eight career anchors are evident: Managerial Competence, Technical Competence, Organizational Stability, Geographic Security, Service, Identity, Autonomy, and Variety. Thus, proposition 1 is supported. In contrast to previous studies entailing other occupational groups, respondents in this sample do not exhibit a Creativity career orientation. This was contrary to a priori expectations, although comment concerning issues linked with this finding has been published by several authorities [6]. DeLong [15] notes that Creativity was the most difficult career construct for which to create descriptive statements when developing and refining the Career Orientation Inventory. Schein [37, p. 128] views the Creativity anchor as the manner in which individuals organize their career decisions around a “need to create something—a product, a company, or a service of their own.” By considering Schein’s entrepreneurial perspective, the results may not be as surprising. A possible explanation might be found in the evolutionary trend of IS duties from development to maintenance [39]. While it is true that anchors represent an “internal state,” anchors are developed over a period of time and one’s experience would have some influence over their development.

These results clearly distinguish two separate Security/Stability factors that have been labeled Geographic Security and Organizational Stability. Although DeLong

## 3.2. IS Career Dimensions

Second-order factor analysis tested proposition 2. This statistical technique facilitates identification of respondent groups that “cluster” together based on similarities in career orientations. This clustering of IS professionals’ responses around these dimensions equates to the “dominance” of these respective orientations. Instead of two dominant anchors, as postulated in proposition 2 and upon which management prescriptions are based, this research identifies three career dimensions for those IS personnel. These three dimensions represent six of the eight anchors derived in the first-order factor analysis. The first dimension is descriptively labeled as “Leadership,” and includes the four anchors of Managerial Competence, Service, Identity, and Variety, all strongly related to managerial qualities. (Appendix A contains COI questionnaire items associated with each of these constructs.) IS personnel who have internalized this orientation are focused toward analyzing problems, remaining emotionally stable, and being interpersonally competent. They want to use their interpersonal skills in service to others and enjoy many different challenges and work assignments. Not surprisingly, their status in the organization is important to them.

The second dimension, labeled “Stability,” reflects those who tend to do what is required of them by their employers to maintain job security, a decent income, and a stable future in the form of a good retirement program, benefits, and so on. By implication, such people will more often accept an organizational definition of their careers and will “trust” the organization to do the right thing for them $[37]$ .

The final dimension, “Technical,” describes an orientation toward being challenged by the actual work. Such individuals are interested in attaining competence in a particular area of expertise. Those adopting this anchor desire to be recognized for their talents, demonstrating expertise respected by those in the field. Perceptions of job competence are more important than interpersonal relationships [16].

Of the original derived anchors, Autonomy and Geographic Security did not load significantly in the second-order factor analysis. Explanation may possibly be found in the sample of respondents. Those surveyed in this study are employed by large organizations or governmental units, neither of which typically provide employees with a great deal of autonomy. Individuals who value Autonomy as a career anchor seek work situations that are “maximally free of organizational constraints to pursue” their goals $[37, p. 156]$ . They seek careers that facilitate maximum independence and are unlikely to seek employment in large, bureaucratic organizations.

These second-order factorial combinations do identify the expected orientations, Leadership (which includes the Managerial anchor) and Technical, but also include a third dominant orientation, Stability. The dual-career ladder construct may indeed be effective for IS human resource planning and management. Still, management needs to be sensitive to the other orientations found in this sample, especially Stability. Ignoring other anchors may contribute to low internal motivation and to the frustration IS personnel feel over the direction of their careers.

## 4. Managerial Implications

RESULTS CLEARLY SUPPORT THE PROPOSITION THAT INFORMATION systems personnel possess a wide range of career orientations. Within this range, managerial and technical competence are not the only focus for career decisions; other factors include identity, service, organizational stability, and variety.

The idea of career anchors or orientations focuses on an individual's realization of his or her “internalized” career perceptions. This concept is important considering that “self-perceptions guide and constrain a person’s future career decisions no matter how accurate or inaccurate those perceptions are” [16, p. 184]. Human resource planning, utilization, and evaluation in the information systems arena must involve both organizational and individual interests to avoid unproductive career decision making. With the impending labor shortage of the 1990s and ongoing turnover concerns [28], reducing IS personnel turnover and concurrently increasing productivity will become even more critical.

Ferratt and Short's [19] conclusions that the motivational patterns of IS and non-IS employees are similar is reinforced by this study. IS and non-IS personnel exhibit a wide range of career anchors/orientations. Even so, evidence suggests that career path communication and counseling for IS employees is neglected [43].

Ginzberg and Baroudi [20] took a general view of career planning based on organizational career literature. Their propositions suggested investigation of organizational careers from three perspectives: internal versus external career, the career over time, and the career cube. This research looked at the internal career only, operationalized through the career anchor/orientation construct. Evidence provided in this research strongly encourages human resource and IS management to involve their employees actively in the career planning and development cycle. As with other occupations, the dual-career concept can be an effective tool for IS employees anchored in either orientation. In addition, conscious recognition of other individual internal career anchors and incorporation of these perspectives for planning and development will yield significant benefits for both employers and employees.

This research examined the employees' perceptions of their career potential. Organizations can apply these findings to uncover relevant changes in their management practices to increase not only the employees' well-being, but to link individual needs with corporate needs. Since the occupational sample is composed of IS professionals, several personnel practices come to mind—particularly application of the dual-career ladder. In effect, individuals may be classified as either “technical” or “non-technical” due to previous experience or training rather than by their internal orientation. Less than desired (from both the employee’s perspective and often unknowingly from the organization’s perspective) staffing decisions can result.

Three career dimensions emerged from this research: Stability, Leadership, and Technical. These dimensions offer insights for IS managers who employ individuals in most of the capacities represented in the study sample (e.g., operator, programmer, systems analyst, manager). Reconciliation of differences between managerial preconceptions and individual orientations can encourage new avenues of communication. By understanding employee views in the context of these dimensions, hidden agendas can be recognized so that employer–employee aspirations can be more compatible. This literally may mean that failure to recognize these dimensions will lead to higher turnover rates at best, lackluster performance at times, and a lack of commitment. Thus, career strategy can be formulated by matching a personally valued internal career to the organizationally valued external career.

In these days of highly volatile business practices and dynamic information technologies, stability (in terms of long-term employment) can be very meaningful to those interested in long-term security, good benefits, and basic job protection. Proactive assignment of those individuals, for example, to IS areas that are projected to provide conventional information services into the foreseeable future can decrease both employer cost and employee dissatisfaction.

Interestingly enough, since Creativity did not emerge as a significant factor, management strategies to encourage innovative approaches to system life-cycle-related activities may be counterproductive. While generalizability of the results of this study are limited to the characteristics of the sample investigated, these results are consistent with other research.

## 5. Conclusions

THREE HUNDRED AND TWENTY-ONE IS PERSONNEL were administered the Career Orientations Inventory to assess the validity of using the dual-career ladder concept for the management of IS professionals. Results show that these individuals have not only technical and managerial orientations, as was expected, but they are also reliant on values such as stability, service, identity, and variety in guiding their future career decisions.

## 6. Directions for Future Research

ALMOST ALL PRIOR STUDIES OF IS PERSONNEL have focused on external careers and have ignored the internal career concepts of these individuals [20]. Future studies are required to determine the extent to which the internal career orientations of IS personnel found in this study match the external career options provided by organizations. This research constitutes an initial step toward exploring this important area.

Many worthy career anchor phenomena remain to be explored to further enhance IS career management. Validation of the emergence of Stability as a dominant career anchor and the lack of a Creativity anchor is required. Also, while the orientations identified by this study are representative of IS as an occupational group, differences among career anchors across job functions (i.e., operator, programmer, analyst, manager) may yield perspectives enabling senior IS executives to establish more effective personnel management policies. In addition, Schein conjectured that career anchors evolve and change over time. Longitudinal studies are necessary to confirm this fact and to identify specific evolutionary patterns. Explicit recognition by both employees and employers may yield increasing productivity over time. Finally, replication with a larger and more geographically dispersed sample of IS professionals is certainly warranted. It is hoped that this study will serve as a catalyst for such future research.
