# A Theoretical Perspective of Negotiation Support Systems

LAI-HUAT LIM AND IZAK BENBASAT

LAI-HUAT LIM is a Senior Tutor in the Department of Information Systems and Computer Science, National University of Singapore. He is currently a doctoral candidate at the Faculty of Commerce and Business Administration, University of British Columbia. Mr. Lim received his bachelor's degree in electrical engineering and his master's degree in MIS, both from the National University of Singapore. His current research interests include group decision support systems, negotiation support systems, and decision support systems.

IZAK BENBASAT is CANFOR Professor of Management Information Systems and Director of Research at the Faculty of Commerce and Business Administration, University of British Columbia. He holds a doctorate in MIS from the University of Minnesota (1974). He was a Marvin Bower Fellow at the Harvard Business School in 1985–86. He has served as Senior Associate Editor (for theory and research) of the MIS Quarterly and as Editor of the Information Systems and Decision Support Systems Department of Management Science. He has been involved with laboratory experimentation in information systems since 1973 investigating topics such as decision support systems, human-computer interfaces, and graphical information presentation. His other research interests include knowledge acquisition for expert systems design and managing the introduction of new information technologies.

ABSTRACT: This paper provides an overview of the existing negotiation literature under the headings of game theory, economic models, political models, and social-psychological models. Inferring from the review the need for computer support for negotiation, the paper then proposes a theory for understanding the effects due to a support system in a two-party, monolithic, and multiple-issue setting. The theory conceptualizes a negotiation support system as consisting of individual decision support systems interconnected with an electronic communication channel; accordingly, it postulates two sets of effects, one owing to the decision aid, and the other to the communication support.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: electronic meeting systems, group decision support systems, negotiation support systems.

This work was partially supported by funds from the National Sciences and Engineering Research Council of Canada. We thank Professor Jay Nunamaker, editor of this special issue, and the reviewers for their constructive comments.

## 1. Introduction

RESEARCH IN GROUP DECISION SUPPORT SYSTEMS (GDSS), also known as electronic meeting systems (EMS), has cumulatively pointed out the importance of the task variable in moderating outcomes due to the use of the technology $[13]$ . Such recognition justifies the independent treatment of supporting a particular task type—the negotiation task. Whereas design of negotiation support software has received much attention (for example, see $[10, 20]$ ), systematic studies of the effect of computer support on negotiation are rarely available (two examples are $[17, 21]$ ). There are two possible explanations to account for this phenomenon. The first explanation has to do with the relative novelty of the negotiation support system (NSS) concept. The second explanation, possibly a more productive one, points toward the lack of theory in guiding such studies. It is the intent of this paper to attempt to fill in this gap.

Section 2 provides an overview of the existing theories of negotiation. After pointing out the need for computer-based negotiation support, section 3 presents a theory of the effects of NSS. Finally, section 4 addresses the limitations of the proposed theory.

## 2. An Overview of Negotiation Literature

BARGAINING IS CONCEPTUALIZED AS A MEANS through which two or more purposive actors arrive at specific settlements or outcomes under conditions of strategic interaction or interdependent decision making [38]. The terms “bargaining” and “negotiation” are used interchangeably in this paper.

Negotiations occur in a wide variety of political, economic, and social settings. At the political level, we have seen examples such as the Camp David negotiations between Israel and Egypt, the Panama Canal negotiations between the United States and Panama, and more recently, the arms talk between the United States and the former Soviet Union. Many of the negotiations that take place at this level are characterized by two nonmonolithic parties, and consequently, there are usually three, rather than just one, agreements that have to be made. There is one negotiation across the table, and one within each side.

Negotiations at the economic level occur in many facets and in different forms in the business world. One such example entails acquisitions and mergers of firms. Even though negotiations at this level might not be as complex as those at the political level, there are still complicated and intriguing issues to be considered even for a simple merger. Finally, at the social level, the negotiation phenomena may again be found in a wide variety of situations, ranging from the simpler ones that involve, say, an argument over the apportioning of inheritance to the more complex ones like a dispute over estate development plan between a group of concerned residents and an estate developer.

Responding to the great difference in application areas, there are correspondingly many different theoretical treatments for negotiations. Generally speaking, there are four classes of negotiation theories, falling under the headings of game theory, economic models, political models, and social-psychological models.

## 2.1. Game Theory

The models of game theory are fundamentally static models in that they focus on outcomes rather than on processes. Consequently, these models are relevant for prescriptive purposes, but are inadequate for descriptive purposes. To quote Luce and Raiffa [24], game theory “states neither how people behave nor how they should behave in an absolute sense, but how they should behave if they wish to achieve certain ends.”

Young [38] points out that most game theorists regard the bargaining problem as a special case of interdependent decision making, with the result that the concept of bargaining is relevant only to a subset of the whole collection of game-theoretic models. Two-person games (as a specific situation of n-person games) can be divided into three categories [34]: (1) pure coordination game, such as the “game” between a single set of partners in bridge, (2) pure conflict (or zero-sum) game, such as the game between the two teams in bridge where one team wins necessarily at the expense of the other, and (3) mixed-motive game, such as the prisoner’s dilemma [29]. In this case, only the last two categories qualify as bargaining problems. A prominent example of models dealing with the two-person games is the Nash theory [26] which, under the concept of fairness, produces a determinate solution known as the Nash solution.

As the number of players increases beyond two, the situation gets more complicated. Possibilities of the forming of coalitions now exist, and have to be taken into account in the treatments of n-person games. The formation of coalitions is the main feature of a subclass of games which Rapoport and Orwant [30] refer to as negotiable, n-person games. With respect to these games, the theory is frequently not even prescriptive as far as the forming of coalitions is concerned. For example, as pointed out by Rapoport and Orwant, the von Neumann and Morgenstern [37] “solution” for n-person games “is in no way a prescription to any player or even to any set of players collectively concerning what coalitions they should form and what the distribution [of rewards] should be.” Solution theory is a major category in the game-theoretic literature that has relevance to negotiation tasks. A related branch is that of sub-solutions [32].

Another important category lies in the literature dealing with the Shapley value. Shapley [35] offers a method for evaluating the worth of an n-person game for any player. The related “Shapley value” can be interpreted as a measure of a priori bargaining power [16]. When applied to certain simple games (in which every coalition has its payoff either one or zero, i.e., it is either winning or losing), a player’s pivotal power is the proportion of times his resources can change a losing coalition into a winning one. Bargaining set models form the third major category of n-person, bargaining games. According to Horowitz [19], a bargaining set is “the set of all individually rational payoff configurations in which no player has a justified objection against any other member of the same coalition.” Subscribing to more severe stability requirements, other bargaining sets have also been proposed [1]. For example, one type replaces individual by coalition rationality, requiring that a coalition will not form if some of its members can obtain more by forming a coalition themselves.

## 2.2. Economic Models of Bargaining

The major characteristics of economic models can be explained in the ways they differ from the game-theoretic approach. Most of the economic models have grown out of specific substantive interests in such problems as wage determination in labor-management negotiations and price setting in various situations that resemble “isolated exchange.” This explains why these models are relatively specific in substantive terms while the theory of games heads toward the building of general theory of interdependent decision making. Also, the economic models are dynamic models that focus on the bargaining process as well as the ultimate outcome of bargaining, whereas the game-theoretic models are fundamentally static models as explained above. The last important distinction between economic models and the models of game theory lies in the difference in emphasis. The former tends to emphasize the formation of expectations about the behavior of the relevant others; the emphasis in the latter is either on the conditions that facilitate each player to make accurate predictions about the behavior of the opposing negotiators or on the characteristics sufficient to yield determinate outcomes.

Among the economic models, Zeuthen's [41] work on economic warfare under conditions of bilateral monopoly $^{1}$ is probably the most well known. He argues that each bargainer, based on an expected-utility basis, will assess the gains and losses associated with his or her bargaining strategies at any given point in a negotiation. With this premise, Zeuthen goes on to propose a two-stage model of the bargaining process that is capable of producing determinate results. Contrasting with this model, a second group of economic models of bargaining is characterized by the fact that these models ultimately fail to produce determinate results for the bargaining process. An example is Pen's [28] model. Pen recognizes that it is not at all reasonable to assume that the personal equilibrium conditions of both negotiating parties will be satisfied with the same value of the price or wage rate. It thus becomes essential of having some mechanism through which the equilibrium condition of one or both of the parties can be transformed to allow the same price or wage rate to satisfy both of them. Therefore, Pen argues that “the function of the bargaining process is to transform the relevant magnitudes and relations in such a way that the equilibrium conditions are no longer in conflict.” Despite his failure to generate a determinate solution, Pen has offered an important contribution in terms of conceptualizing more clearly some major features of bargaining.

## 2.3. Political Models of Negotiation

The political models of negotiation are concerned with testing the ability of conflict of interest to help predict political behavior. Axelrod's [2] work in this area provides an excellent example. The general hypothesis of his study is that, other things being equal, the more conflict of interest there is, the greater the likelihood of conflictful behavior. For example, a model involving the Senate and House conferences would predict that bills with high conflict of interest between Senate and House conferees are more likely to die in conference.

In formulating the political models for the different political processes, Axelrod makes use of abstract strategic interactions such as the bargaining game and the prisoner's dilemma. For example, the congressional conference committee's activities were taken to approximate the model of the two-person bargaining game. However, if the strategic interaction cannot be conceptualized as a bargaining game or a prisoner's dilemma, conflict of interest remains to be defined. Indeed, the defining of conflict of interest is an important element in the formulation of a political model. Other legitimate concerns in the formulating of such models include the determination of possible outcomes, the measurement of the participants' utilities for these outcomes, and the observation of behavior under different amounts of conflict of interest.

## 2.4. Social-Psychological Models of Negotiation

Besides game theory and economic and political models of negotiation, there is a fourth category of negotiation theories which we have called social-psychological models. Models falling into this category are largely descriptive rather than prescriptive; they interpret the negotiation process from various perspectives including sociological, psychological, learning, or even joint decision making. Bartos [4] offers a theory of negotiation from a sociological point of view. The basis of this theory is the notion that men strive to create and maintain the conditions of justice [18]. Bartos further argues that the well-known Nash solution to bargaining games is consistent with Homans's notion of justice. Therefore, the theory hypothesizes that negotiators view the midpoint between their past demands and offers as just and strive to achieve it. Also interested in negotiation process, Cross [12] sees it from a learning perspective. To Cross, negotiators are persons attempting to optimize payoffs from the situation by choosing bargaining strategies. These strategies are contingent on each party's perception of the opposing party's strategy. If the perception contains error, expectation will also change, leading to a modification of the party's strategy choice.

Spector's [36] psychological analysis attributes different bargaining behaviors to different interaction pairs of personalities, and shows that different outcome preferences are related to specific personality types. Zartman [40] interprets negotiation as a joint decision-making process that is characterized by the existence of a jointly agreeable formula. In this model, “negotiators begin by groping for this formula that will serve as a referent, provide a notion of justice, and define a common perception on which implementing details can be based.” This approach includes a place for the exercise of power in altering expectations and acceptances.

Collective bargaining is distinguished from interpersonal bargaining as involving representatives of groups [25]. Because of this differentiation, the former is also a more complicated subject matter than the latter, and warrants separate treatment. McGrath's tripolar model of collective bargaining [25] identifies three sets of forces to which each spokesman is subject: (1) R-forces, directed toward the position of the party the negotiator represents (e.g., those arising from a negotiator's attitudinal identification with his reference group), (2) A-forces, directed toward the position of the opposing party (e.g., arising from sympathy he has for the goals of the other party), and (3) C-forces, directed toward the position of the broader organization or social systems in which all parties participate (e.g., arising from the desire to set forth a solution that would be good for the general community). The degree of success of a solution is determined by the balance between these three sets of forces.

As in game theory, coalition formation has also been studied by the social-psychological models. An example of such models is the minimum resource theory $[15, 31]$ , which emphasizes the initial resources the players bring to the situation (rather than their strategic bargaining positions). The theory proposes the following central hypothesis: a coalition will form in which the total resources are as small as possible while still being sufficient. As explained by Riker $[31]$ , “participants create coalitions just as large as they believe will insure winning and no larger.” An interesting implication of this hypothesis is that the strongest player will be excluded in many situations and thus be the loser—if by strength we mean the amount of initial resources one possesses.

## 2.5. Summary

The theoretical models as presented above have dealt with negotiation as basically a situation consisting of human bargainers and their interaction. Correspondingly (and essentially), the prescriptive theories attempt to maximize the payoffs for one or more parties by relying mainly on the strategies of the bargainers, while the descriptive theories seek to understand what goes on during negotiation as an interaction process. Nevertheless, the rationality assumption of the prescriptive approach necessary for effectively forming and carrying out the strategies cannot always be met due to mainly the limitations associated with the information-processing capacity of human beings. Such limitations have contributed toward “process losses” within human interactions $[27]$ . The role of DSS and NSS in alleviating these limitations will be discussed in the next section.

## 3. A Theory of Negotiation Support Systems

THIS PAPER PROPOSES THAT THE USE OF COMPUTER SUPPORT will have much to offer in terms of compensating negotiators with what they lack in conducting rational negotiations, that is, higher information-processing capabilities and capacities. Conceptually, the system consists of decision support systems (DSS) which are networked. Therefore, communications of the parties are enhanced by the addition of an electronic communication channel, whose characteristics will be described later.

To illustrate this position, we present a theory of two-party negotiation support system. In a sense, this theoretical approach is descriptive cum prescriptive. The model to be presented traces in detail the linkage between the introduction of each component of the support system and the negotiation outcomes. Also, the model predicts a fairer as well as better-for-all solution to be realized with the negotiation support aids.

## 3.1. Defining Negotiation Support

In order to lay out a theory of negotiation support systems (NSS), we have first to define what, in our view, constitutes negotiation support. To this end, we shall describe the basic system configuration and the desired functionalities. Two major components are envisioned for supporting negotiations: a decision support system (DSS) for each negotiating party, $^{2}$ and an electronic linkage between the DSSs so that the negotiators may communicate electronically. For this configuration, the negotiators are confined in a face-to-face setting, and verbal communication between them is available.

## 3.1.1. Decision Support Systems

A DSS is a computer-based system used by managers and professionals as an aid to decision making in semistructured decision tasks through direct interaction with data and models. Benbasat, DeSanctis, and Nault [7] illustrate an integrated view of a generic DSS through an examination of their component parts, namely, a data-knowledge component, and modeling-inference component, and an interface-communication component. They adopt and extend a taxonomy proposed by Zachary [39] to show the type of decision-aiding techniques available from a DSS.

Zachary [39] identifies six major kinds of support that decision aids provide to human decision makers: (1) process models (for supporting the prediction of complex processes; e.g., “what-if” capabilities); (2) choice models (for supporting the integration of individual criteria across aspects or alternative choices; e.g., multi-attribute/multi-alternative utility models); (3) information control techniques (for supporting the storage, retrieval, and organization of data, information, and knowledge needed for a decision; e.g., database management systems); (4) representational aids (for supporting the expression and manipulation of a specific representation of a decision problem; e.g., visual, spatial, and matrix data and model representation methods); (5) analysis and reasoning aids (for supporting the performance of problem-specific reasoning processes based on a certain representation of a decision problem; e.g., knowledge-based systems and mathematical programming); and (6) judgment refinement/amplification techniques (for supporting the quantification of heuristic judgment processes which may be prone to bias; e.g., bootstrapping Bayesian analysis). In this taxonomy, items 1, 2, 5, and 6 relate to the modeling-inference component, item 3 relates to the data-knowledge component, and item 4 relates to the interface-communication component of a generic DSS.

## 3.1.3. Functionalities

There are four key elements in any negotiation [22]: interdependence, perceived conflict, opportunistic interaction, and possibility of agreement. Consider the following typical scenario: The marketing manager cannot sell the products unless the production manager produces them. Likewise, the production manager's production schedule is influenced by the marketing manager's sales plans; thus the element of interdependence is necessary in a negotiation situation. However, interdependence alone need not imply bargaining. There must be potential conflict over “dividing the pie.” In this example, the conflict may arise out of different preferences over how much to produce. Opportunistic interaction involves the attempts of the negotiating parties in influencing each other's decisions, and in the process, guarding some information, moving to stake out favorable positions, and seeking to mold perceptions and aspirations. In other words, it refers to strategic maneuvering of some sort. Without this component, the interaction might best be called pure “problem solving” rather than “negotiation.” Finally, the possibility of agreement is essential in bargaining. In some situations, this is achieved because the negotiators look for a joint decision that is better than their unilateral alternatives. In other situations, this agreement is reached because a higher authority demands that the parties achieve it. This is the case for the production and marketing managers, since their joint decision is essential to the functioning of the company as a whole.

Interdependence (or the degree of it) between negotiating managers is defined by the organizational structure, and is therefore determined outside of the negotiating situation. Similarly, as explained above, the reaching of an agreement is in many organizational settings enforced by a higher authority. The remaining two elements (perceived conflict and opportunistic interaction) are the dynamic components of the negotiation which change and evolve during each scenario. They are also the components that define the functionalities of the computer-support system. These functionalities are:

1. The system should support requirements analysis (for the marketing manager, this will mean demand forecasting).

2. It should support strategic analysis, which includes analysis of the other party's needs (and thus may involve some degree of guessing to begin with) as well as the team's joint position with respect to the efficiency frontier. This new functionality could even be utilized to locate the fairest solution (for example, the Nash solution) for the team.

3. It should support the interaction per se. The system should provide a communication channel through which the negotiators are able to refer to some common referents for facilitating the interaction. The common referents may appear in the form of reasonings and justifications for supporting one's arguments.

It can easily be seen that the assistance given by a DSS can more than match the first two required functionalities. Specifically, the probabilistic models (one type of process model as defined by Zachary [39]) address the need of the first functionality, and the closed-form analytical models (another type of process models) can be used to accomplish partially the objective of the second functionality by aiding in the calculation of the Nash solution $^{4}$ and efficiency frontier. $^{5}$ Also, the representational aids, with features such as windowing and graphics, will offer partial support for the third functionality by fulfilling the common referent purpose (i.e., graphics, windowing, etc, can be used to realize the common referents). This support will nevertheless be partial since the common referents cannot be made accessible if the DSSs of the two parties are not somehow linked. Hence, electronic communication between the DSSs is another important element for achieving the third functionality.

## 3.2. Boundary Conditions of the Theory

Negotiations involve many different kinds of settings; the following discusses three important dimensions that differentiate one type of negotiation from another. In light of theory building, they form the boundary conditions of the theory being proposed. First, there is a major difference between conflicts involving two parties and those involving more than two parties. In negotiations involving three or more parties, coalitions of parties may form that act in concert against the other disputants.

Second, in some negotiation settings involving two parties, there are actually three agreements, rather than one, to be made. The reason for this is the nonmonolithic nature of the negotiating parties. Many negotiations that take place within organizations involve monolithic parties. An example is joint decision making by several managers. Even though in this case the managers may represent different divisions, they have the power to conduct the negotiations without having to consult their divisions.

The third important distinction is whether a particular negotiation is integrative or distributive. Distributive negotiations are those that involve single issues. An example is the buying and selling of a used car: the buyer strictly wants the price low and the seller strictly wants it high; the notion of cooperation does not seem to be applicable in such situations except to the extent of forging a deal. In contrast, integrative bargaining involves the solving of multiple issues. The parties are not strict competitors. It is no longer true that if one party gets more, the other necessarily has to get less: they both can get more. They can cooperate in order to enlarge the pie that they eventually will have to divide. The integrative setting (compared to the distributive setting) is probably more commonly found in negotiations that occur within organizations.

This theory is focused in a negotiation setting that involves two monolithic parties, solving multiple issues.

## 3.4. An Information-Processing Perspective for Understanding the Effects of DSS on Negotiations

Rational negotiation involves substantial information-processing activities—in the estimating of parameters, evaluating of strategies, and so on. Much of the prescriptive negotiations presented by the game theory literature cannot be realized without considerable time and/or information-processing capacity. Indeed, this paper holds the position that the reasons for not achieving optimal or near-optimal negotiations lie in the very limitations of humans as information processing systems $[5, 6]$ . Once such processing capability and capacity are improved, self need can be readily analyzed and the rival’s position can also be strategically examined.

One way to come out of the inherent limitation of human information processing is to make use of an external memory and to support processing capacity. Depending on the type of external memory, of course, one gets different degrees of assistance. Decision support systems can be used to expand one's information-processing capacity and capability by a great deal, assuming that a competent model base subsystem is in place. Such increase in information-processing capacity and capability is likely to lead to three specific outcomes.

## 3.4.1. Distance from Efficient Frontier

Efficient frontier refers to the locus of achievable joint evaluations from which no joint gains are possible. This is shown graphically in figure 3. Any settlement reached in the shaded area such as X is falling short of the optimal settlement from a joint-gain perspective.

Figure 3. Utility Graph for Negotiators A and B

Given that each negotiator is equipped with a DSS with the proper modeling tools to achieve the functionalities as prescribed in section 3.1 (i.e., support for requirement analysis and strategic analysis), we expect the negotiating team to be more aware of their potentials. Consequently, the negotiating parties will collectively strive toward the efficient frontier and the distance between the settlement and the frontier will be smaller than otherwise.

Hypothesis 1: The distance from the efficient frontier will be smaller for negotiation dyads supported with DSSs than for dyads not supported.

## 3.4.2. Distance from Nash Solution

The Nash theory strives to maximize the joint gain of two negotiating parties while taking into consideration the notion of fairness. Bishop [9], comparing Nash theory with four other theories dealing with the same subject, points out that it is the most ingenious and plausible. Also, Bartos [4] finds the same theory to be consistent with the rule of distributive justice formulated by sociologist Homans [18]. Essentially, the rule indicates that people view as fair rewards that are proportional to the recipient's contribution to society. The Nash solution (a solution yielded by the theory) is regarded as fair because it gives to each side exactly one-half of the maximum payoff it can rationally expect to get. One should note the difference between such a settlement and a settlement that gives the two sides the same payoff.

Under normal circumstances, it is difficult for the negotiating parties to be aware of where the fair solution lies even if they know the formula to calculate it, for reasons associated with limitations in information-processing capacity and capabilities. As pointed out above, computer support improves the information-processing capacity and capability of negotiators. To the extent that negotiators are better equipped to process information and perform modeling, their settlement will come closer to the Nash solution than otherwise.

Hypothesis 2: The distance from the Nash solution will be smaller for negotiation dyads supported with DSSs than for dyads not supported.

## 3.4.3. Confidence with Solution

In many instances, negotiators settle the negotiation and leave the scene with one of two types of feelings; they feel either like a winner or like a loser. Whether a negotiator ultimately feels like a winner or a loser is very much a consequence of whether or not the negotiator perceives that he or she has been in control during the process. A decision aid, through helping negotiators to be capable of performing rational analyses, gives the negotiators a sense of control over the course and settlement of negotiation. As a result, they will gain confidence with the solution.

Hypothesis 3: Confidence with the solution will be higher for negotiation dyads supported with DSSs than for dyads not supported.

The assertion that the electronic channel is an ideal channel for transmitting task-oriented communication is also supported by the following analysis.

From another perspective, Zigurs [42] identifies four properties of a communication channel: (1) the skill it requires of the communicator; (2) the capacity of information transmitted (i.e., the amount of information communicated); (3) the specific features of the channel; and (4) the impact on the receiver. Whereas the last dimension is important in Zigurs' study, which involves groups of size greater than two, it is not quite relevant in our case. We shall therefore contrast verbal communication with electronic communication along the first three dimensions (see Table 2).

The degree of verbalization skills varies widely among people and, depending on the particular language being spoken, cultures. In fact, the skill is often thought of as an inborn talent. Whether or not this is true, the amount of training required to upgrade verbalization skill remains substantial compared with that required to impart typing skill. The impact of differences in skillfulness will therefore be minimal in electronic communication compared with verbal communication. In fact, when verbal communication is shut off, an enormous amount of information can still flow through the keyboard to the other party. The high transmission capacity of electronic channels not only allows text to be communicated; indeed, graphical information often encountered in negotiation situations involving technical materials can also be communicated.

The nature of verbal communication dictates that only one person speaks at a time for any effective communication to occur. But such restriction is not necessary in electronic communication since concurrent typing of both parties is not a problem. In summary, the additional channel requires no difficult skill to master, allows much higher capacity of information flow, and provides greater flexibility in terms of “concurrent communication.” However, it should also be noted that the new mode of communication is likely to cause a loss in social cues. In this respect, communications that are primarily concerned with task rather than social purpose $[3]$ may be more faithfully transmitted using the new channel. Taking all the above into consideration, it seems clear that the new channel offers an ideal mechanism for transmitting task-oriented communication involving technical data and graphics. This type of information will serve as a common referent for negotiators in their bargaining process. Therefore, the verbal channel will carry mainly the bargaining exchanges and the social-oriented communication, and the electronic channel will transmit the reasoning of the exchanges in the form of common referents. To the extent that such reasoning is used to back one’s argument (or bargaining), the opposing party can be led to perceive the commitment of the first party.

Schelling stresses the importance of commitment in negotiation in “An Essay on Bargaining” [33]. In a culture where “cross my heart” is universally accepted as potent, all that the buyer $^{7}$ has to do is to allege that he will pay no more than 16,000, using this invocation of penalty, and he wins. Nevertheless, many cultures of today are not like that, and commitment has to be made known and believed. A necessary tool to achieve this, as argued above, is to make use of electronic communication to link the decision support systems, so that common referents can be generated as reasonings of arguments.

The increase in perceived commitment of one party by the other and vice versa is likely to build up trust in the negotiation, and will thus speed up the negotiating process. Also, the presence of such trust will bring about higher satisfaction in the negotiation settlement.

## 3.5.1. Time to Settlement

Hypothesis 4: The perceived commitment and consequently the time to settlement will be shorter for negotiation dyads provided with full communication and decision support than for dyads provided only with individual DSSs.

## 3.5.2. Satisfaction

Hypothesis 5: The perceived commitment will be higher and consequently the satisfaction will be higher for negotiation dyads provided with full communication and decision support than for dyads provided only with individual DSSs.

## 4. Concluding Remarks

THE THEORY JUST PRESENTED REPRESENTS AN ATTEMPT TOWARD UNDERSTANDING the role of computer systems in negotiations. Rather than evaluating NSS as one single entity, it distinguishes between the effects of its individual subcomponents, namely the DSS portion and the electronic communication channel. The DSS portion improves the human information-processing capacity and its effects (on Nash solution and the efficient frontier) can best be understood within the reference disciplines of game theory and economic theory. The electronic communication channel improves perceived commitment and its effects can best be understood within the context of social-psychological theories of negotiation. The proposed theory is not intended to be a universal theory as there is no universal setting for negotiations to begin with. Indeed, it is limited by the very boundary conditions it contains, and thus is applicable only to a two-person, monolithic, and multiple-issue setting.

Also, the system configuration outlined in this paper is but a basic one; consequently, the electronic communication channel plays the fundamental and simple role of facilitating communication exchanges. A more advanced system such as a Level 3 system described by DeSanctis and Gallupe [14] will be characterized by machine-induced group communication patterns and can even include expert advice in the selecting and arranging of rules to be applied during the negotiation. Within this type of support, more prominent effects than those hypothesized in this paper can be expected.
