## I. The Challenge

PEN-BASED INTERFACES PROVIDE MANY FEATURES, but one in particular, handwriting recognition, has recently received a great deal of attention in both the academic and the trade press $[2, 10]$ . The appeal is obvious. A user no longer needs to type to make the computer work; the user just writes, and the computer understands. Some think this alone will usher in a new era of user-friendly computing $[5]$ .

The conventional wisdom says that handwriting recognition will persuade many current nonusers to adopt computers because handwriting is more natural than typing. The reasoning is that handwriting should be faster for nontypists, and people who fear computers should find the tablet more friendly and comfortable than the keyboard $[1]$ . Business executives in particular, who are not regular computer users, may be more likely to accept the pen-based machines $[1, 6]$ .

As is often the case, the conventional wisdom is mostly founded on conjecture. There is little empirical evidence to support or refute it. Thus the objective of this study was to investigate the efficiency and user acceptance of pen-based interfaces across a variety of potential applications (e.g., word processing, graphics, and spreadsheets).

## I.1. Pen-based Interfaces

A pen-based computer interface consists of a digitizer, a penlike stylus, and an output screen. On early pen-based systems the screen and digitizer were separate components attached to the computer. More recently, the digitizer, screen, and computer have been integrated into a single box about the size of a thick tablet of paper. The digitizer senses the presence of the stylus (e.g., by a magnetic field, pressure sensitivity, or a weak radio signal) and reports its location to the computer at any given moment.

Pen-based interfaces differ from traditional keyboard and/or mouse-based interfaces in three principal ways: software navigation, position control, and data entry (e.g., handwriting recognition). Software navigation enables the user to move from one function to another within the software. For example, in a document-editing application, the user would use a specific software navigation strategy to move from entering text to printing the document. Menus and function keys are traditional software navigation strategies in keyboard-driven applications. A pen-based interface allows the user to navigate by touching “buttons,” or active areas on the input tablet. The “buttons” on the digitizer might behave like keyboard keys (such as escape, delete, and insert), or they might initiate actions that would normally correspond to function keys and pull-down menus.

Position control refers to the ability to position objects (such as cursors and icons) on the screen. Traditional interfaces provide a mouse or arrow keys for this function. A pen-based interface uses the stylus and digitizer for position control. The computer tracks the motion of the stylus on the digitizer, and responds appropriately.

Data input on the pen-based interface can be accomplished several ways, the most glamorous of which is, of course, handwriting recognition. The user prints characters on the digitizer tablet, and the interface recognizes them and translates them into a computer-readable form. Alternate approaches include allowing the user to tap in characters using a virtual keyboard drawn on the digitizer, or simply capturing the graphic images of stylus inputs without translating them into computer-readable form. Users can also tap on check boxes or make selections from lists with the pen.

Of the three principal differences between pen-based and keyboard/mouse-based interfaces, handwriting recognition is the conspicuous hook that has captured wide interest. Proponents of pen-based interfaces argue that hand-printing is more “natural” than typing. Critics counter that a competent typist can enter large volumes of text more quickly with a keyboard than with a pen-based interface. In 1967, Devoe showed that even beginning typists could type faster than they could do constrained printing [3]. Handwriting recognition vendors, however, claim that the state of the art in handwriting recognition had advanced beyond its early constraints. They say that current technology should enable users to get a 98 to 99 percent recognition rate at speeds as high as 25 words per minute [6], which is much faster than the 10 words per minute of a beginning typist.

The pen-based interface computer is essentially a rebirth of the old light-pen technology, with all of its advantages of faster, more accurate pointing, selecting, and so on, and without the one disadvantage that kept light-pens from becoming widely used: light-pens were tied to a desk-bound screen, and light-pen users tired quickly as they repeatedly lifted their arms to the screen. The new technology promised something you could pick up, hold in your lap, or set on the desk in front of you.

While there has been a great deal of prior empirical research studying pen-based interfaces, virtually all prior research has examined the elementary components of pen-based interfaces separately (cursor movement, software navigation, handwriting recognition) for very elementary subtasks such as pointing, cursor movement, and menu selection. We were unable to find any empirical research studying the efficiency, effectiveness, or user preferences for pen-based interfaces for business applications such as word processing, graphics, or spreadsheets. $^{1}$

## I.2. The Research Questions

This study examined three key issues relating to the successful adoption and use of pen-based interfaces for practical applications in organizations (summarized in Table

1). Our first research question was: For what kinds of applications are pen-based interfaces useful? The pen at its fastest is likely to be much slower than the keyboard for high-volume character entry, so it is less likely to be suitable for the high-volume text entry of word processing [3]. However, the fine motor control and direct object manipulation that are possible with the pen may make it appropriate for tasks such as editing the text in existing documents, and creating graphics. The ability to tap software navigation buttons on a digitizer might make the pen-based interface a viable replacement for tasks requiring a great deal of software navigation, such as interactions with an operating system. We further reasoned that the touch-button navigation features could be used to shorten tasks that consisted of many repeated character sequences, such as spreadsheet formula building. Therefore, we tested the suitability of pen-based interfaces for these four applications: word processing, spreadsheets, disk management, and graphics.

Our second research question concerned the kind of users who were most likely to adopt the pen-based interface. Were nontypist computer novices more likely to favor the technology than experienced keyboard users, as the conventional wisdom suggested? This conjecture seemed sound; it was certainly plausible that some people did not use computers because they did not want to learn to type. However, the reverse was also plausible: some people may not have typed because they did not need computers (or did not see the value of available applications). To resolve these conflicting possibilities, we tested the pen-based interface on users with a wide range of experience.

Our third research question addressed the issue of user acceptance of pen-based technology. What features and functions of a pen-based interface will be important for user acceptance of the technology (e.g., software navigation, position control, handwriting recognition)? To address this question, we designed our experimental tasks so that subjects had to use a broad set of application features, and so that they had to exercise all components of the pen-based interface.

## II. Method

WE CONDUCTED A SERIES OF six experiments across four business applications commonly used in today's office environment: spreadsheets, word processing, graphics, and disk management (Table 2). Four of our experiments studied novice users of each application. The remaining two experiments studied experienced users of spreadsheet and word processing software. The experiments were conducted during the winter of 1990–91.

## II.1. Subjects

Using a repeated measures design, we had 116 nonmanagerial office workers complete tasks using these four applications. Subjects ranged in computer and keyboard experience from complete novice to consummate expert.

## II.2. Tasks

Each novice user completed four tasks on a single application, two with a keyboard/mouse, and two with the pen-based interface. Each expert user completed two different, more complex tasks on a single application, one with each interface. The order of interface use and task execution was crossed to control for order effects. Tasks for the word processing application (implemented using Word Perfect) focused on text editing rather than on the entry of large amounts of text. Tasks included highlighting, moving, copying, and deleting text, underlining, correcting spelling errors, inserting a single word, and inserting a single sentence. Spreadsheet tasks (implemented using Lotus 1–2–3) included entering labels, numbers, and formulas, formatting labels and numbers, copying with relative and absolute addressing, inserting and deleting rows and columns, and creating a graph. Presentation graphics tasks (implemented using Harvard Graphics) included entering labels, selecting, placing, and sizing clip art, drawing boxes, and creating pie and bar charts. Disk management tasks (implemented using DOS) included creating subdirectories, renaming, copying, and deleting files, displaying directory listings, changing logged drives, and running batch files.

## II.3. Measures

Our study examined both performance and user preferences with pen-based and keyboard/mouse interfaces $[4, 9]$ . There is a trade-off between effectiveness and efficiency when measuring user performance. Subjects can save time by producing lower-quality product, or produce a higher-quality product by taking more time. Thus, studying both efficiency and effectiveness in the same experiment can be problematic. In this study, we chose to hold effectiveness constant and to measure efficiency. Subjects were instructed to produce error-free output; that is, they were instructed to fix mistakes as they worked. Postexperimental examination of output determined that subjects had maintained consistent quality across all treatments. Efficiency was measured as the amount of time required to complete each task. The start time and stop time for each task were time-stamped by the computer.

A posttest computerized questionnaire was used to capture subject preferences, and self-reported keyboard experience. The items included an overall assessment of the ease of use of the pen-based interface, perceptions of its efficiency, and perceptions of software navigation, position control, and data input. We also asked subjects whether they would use the interface for more work of the same kind. The specific questionnaire items are presented in an appendix to this paper.

## II.4. Procedures

All subjects received a standard thirty-minute training session with the pen-based interface at the beginning of the session. Novices then received an additional ninety-minute tutorial on the application they were to use. The tutorial included practice with both the keyboard and the tablet performing tasks similar to the experimental tasks. Experienced subjects did not receive a tutorial, but instead performed a warm-up task, similar to experimental tasks, using both interfaces. Subjects then performed the two or four experimental tasks. After completing the tasks, subjects completed the questionnaire investigating their perceptions of the two interfaces.

## II.5. Technology

The pen-based interface was a state-of-the-art prototype at the time the study was conducted. It consisted of a digitizer tablet attached to a PC. The digitizer was overlaid with a plastic template that had “buttons” the user could tap with a stylus to navigate through the software. The templates also had active areas so the stylus could be used to control the cursor position, much as a mouse does. There was also an area on each template for entering hand-printed characters for handwriting recognition (see figure 1 for an example of a digitizer template).

## III.2. User Preferences

Overall, subjects perceived the pen-based interface to be an efficient tool (t[115] = 3.35, p = 0.001). There were no differences in this evaluation by experiment (F[5,115] = 0.84, p = ns). They also gave the pen-based interface a favorable overall rating for ease of use (t[115] = 5.49, p = 0.001). However, this evaluation differed across experiments (F[5,107] = 3.06, p < 0.05). Post-hoc Tukey tests showed that both the novice and expert word-processing users gave the pen-based system a lower rating than did the expert spreadsheet users. There were no other pairwise differences.

Subjects reported software navigation to be easier with the pen-based system than with the keyboard $t[114]=6.59, p<0.001$ . ANOVA revealed statistically significant differences in this measure by experiment $F[5,104]=2.34; p=0.047$ . Post-hoc Tukey tests revealed that expert text-editing users were more favorable towards pen-based software navigation than other users, while novice text-editing users were slightly less satisfied with pen-based software navigation than others.

The pen-based interface emulated a mouse for two applications: Harvard Graphics and WordPerfect (the other two applications were not mouse-sensitive). The subjects preferred the fine motor control of the pen-based system over the mouse interface for positioning the cursor $\mu(35)=3.19, p=0.003$ . ANOVA revealed no difference by application $F[1,36]=1.38, p=\mathrm{ns}$ .

Subjects strongly felt that handwriting recognition was more difficult than the keyboard for character input ( $t[114] = 26.87$ , p = 0.001). ANOVA revealed no statistically significant differences in this evaluation across experiments ( $F[5,113] = 1.21$ , p = ns).

## III.3. Effects of Keyboard Experience

Subjects had keyboard experience ranging from zero hours to more than 1,000 hours. ANOVA analysis found no statistically significant effects on any perceptual measure by experience (Table 4). Subjects with less typing experience had perceptions similar to those of their more experienced counterparts.

## IV. Discussion

THE RESULTS CAN BE QUICKLY SUMMARIZED. All the spreadsheet users, the DOS users, and the novice text-editing users worked faster on the keyboard than on the pen-based interface. There were no performance differences for graphics users or expert users of text editing. In general, subjects preferred the pen-based interface to the traditional interface, except for character input. There were no differences in preference due to prior keyboard experience (Table 5).

The research questions are perhaps most easily addressed in the reverse order that they were posed. Consider first, therefore, which pen-based interface components gained user acceptance.

## IV.1. User Acceptance of Pen-based Components

The users liked the software navigation and position control features of the pen-based interface. The users were even able to keep up with the keyboard for some tasks, despite the limits of the handwriting recognition system. To be blunt, however, once the novelty wore off, most of the users hated the handwriting recognition component of the pen-based interface. Here our findings conflicted with the conventional wisdom.

## IV.1.1. Input Specd

It is often reported in the trade press that the state-of-the-art handwriting recognition system used in our study, and others like it, recognize hand-printed characters with 98–99 percent accuracy at input speeds approaching 25 words per minute [6]. However, our users were only able to achieve an average accuracy of 90 percent. In a postexperimental session we asked ten users to enter a standard text as quickly as they could with handwriting recognition. They achieved an average speed of 7 words per minute if they did not correct the characters that the computer misrecognized. We then asked another group to enter the same text, but to correct the misrecognitions made by the computer. Average speed dropped to 3.5 words per minute. None of the systems we have tested since that time has performed any better.

## IV.1.2. Attention Drain

The cognitive load associated with entering the characters was high compared with the keyboard. The subjects reported that to accomplish as good a recognition rate as they did, they had to devote their full attention to forming the characters with the pen. That left them too distracted to pay attention to the work they were trying to do, and as a result, it took them longer to complete their tasks.

## IV.1.3. How Good Is Good Enough?

Clearly, the handwriting recognition system we used was not sufficiently mature to gain user acceptance (remember that this was, and still is, a system widely touted as excellent). More importantly, however, we were left with the rather interesting question of whether handwriting recognition could ever be good enough to gain user acceptance. To investigate this, we conducted another postexperimental session wherein we reduced the recognition set to ten unambiguous characters, and thus managed to raise the effective recognition rate to the 98 percent level claimed by vendors. The users were more comfortable with this level of recognition, but still reported that they found it inadequate for real work.

At first it may seem counterintuitive that users should demand higher accuracy than 98 percent given that even experienced typists have error rates in the 1–2 percent range [3]. However, it appears that the users are much more willing to tolerate their own errors than errors made by the computer. It is unlikely, for example, that a user would tolerate a keyboard that frequently generated a wrong character when the right key was pressed.

## IV.1.4. Constrained Input

It was possible that the users in our study felt constrained by having to print characters into special boxes on the digitizer, which, as mentioned above, slowed them down significantly compared with simply writing on a piece of paper. The boxes gave the recognition engine a frame of reference for resolving ambiguities when interpreting characters. We were curious whether users would be more comfortable if this constraint were removed, so we configured the system to recognize characters of any size, written anywhere on the digitizer. This created immediate problems with recognition of punctuation. When the user put a small mark on the screen, the system could not tell whether it was a comma, apostrophe, or single quote. We reconfigured the system to work in a line-constrained mode: the users had to write within horizontal lines, but not within boxes. The recognition rate was better than in the unconstrained mode, but not as reliable as the fully gridded mode. Because of the ambiguity intrinsic to the nature of handwriting we believe that it is unlikely that accurate handwriting recognition will ever be possible in a completely unconstrained mode.

Individuals only recognize their own handwriting with a 98–99 percent accuracy, so it is unlikely that computers will ever do significantly better than this. Yet users still find this accuracy unacceptable. Therefore, we conclude that handwriting recognition will never become widely used as a direct substitute for the keyboard for general-purpose computing. It is more likely to find acceptance in niche applications like form-filling. When people fill out forms on paper their handwriting is typically constrained to block lettering, just as it is for handwriting recognition, so the pen-based interface is not adding an additional constraint. Further, on form-filling applications, unlike free text, much is known about the contents of fields before the form is filled out. A field may permit only numerals, for example. Therefore, the recognizer would only have to distinguish among ten symbols for that field. If a field were programmed only to accept the name of a state, then a lookup dictionary could vastly increase the accuracy of input.

## IV.1.5. Alternatives to Handwriting Recognition

The user response to the components of the interface other than handwriting recognition was overwhelmingly positive. Software navigation and position control features are already sufficiently mature to gain user acceptance. Still, the users will need to have some acceptable means of character input. Two plausible alternatives suggest themselves. The first solution might be to have a convenient way to dock the pen-based computer with an auxiliary keyboard. However, to some extent this limits the portability and simplicity of the system that make it so attractive in the first place. The second alternative might be to tap characters into the system with a virtual keyboard. It is possible to display an image of a keyboard on the screen whenever character input is required. The user can tap letters in with the pen (or with the fingers if the screen is touch-sensitive), and the virtual keyboard can disappear when it is not needed. Recent empirical studies at the University of Maryland with touch-screen input show that users comfortably achieve input speeds of 10–30 words per minute, depending on the size of the virtual keyboard [8]. We are currently experimenting to see whether similar results can be achieved with pen-based input. In contrast to handwriting recognition, the virtual keyboard requires little in the way of computer resources, is quite accurate, and places very low cognitive demands on the user.

## IV.2. Who Will Adopt Pen-based Technology?

The conventional wisdom is that current non-computer-users will be quickly drawn to pen-based interfaces because of their handwriting recognition abilities. The reasoning is that many people do not use computers because they do not want to type. The results of our study, however, tend to support the alternative interpretation: people do not type because they do not need computers or are not convinced of the value of the applications.

The novice subjects in our study responded to the technology in almost exactly the same way as the experienced users. They were neither more nor less likely to prefer the pen-based interface than the experienced subjects. This held for handwriting recognition, software navigation, and position control (i.e., sketching, highlighting words) across all the tested applications. From this we infer that users are not likely to adopt pen-based computers just because they are a novel alternative to the keyboard.

Extending this line of reasoning, we believe that there will not be a big movement of current nonusers into computing unless the pen-based systems offer these users new functionality that is not presently available from traditional mouse-and-keyboard systems. Therefore, the strategy for developers who want to tap the nonuser market might be to seek these new and different applications, rather than focusing on adapting pens to the applications that are already well established on keyboard-based machines.

## IV.3. What Applications?

In retrospect, our experimental tasks took the exact form that our subsequent results suggested would be least successful: we adapted keyboard-based applications to the pen-based interface. Nonetheless, we uncovered some clues that point us toward a useful set of pen-based applications.

At the end of our experimental sessions we gave the users a choice: if they were going to do more productive work, did they want the keyboard or the pen-based interface? Nearly two-thirds of the spreadsheet and graphics users chose the pen-based interface. They were willing to tolerate the poor handwriting recognition to gain the benefits of the other features. The text-editing users, on the other hand, rejected the pen-based interface in favor of the keyboard by about the same margin. The disk management users were about evenly split.

At first glance it was tempting to ascribe the preference differences simply to the bad handwriting recognition interface. After all, text editing is word-oriented; perhaps the text-editing users were more bothered by the misspellings the system generated. However, this explanation was not satisfactory when we reexamined the experimental tasks. All the tasks had users entering about the same number of characters, and all made extensive use of software navigation.

A better explanation presented itself when we considered the complexity of user interactions with the software. In short, the pen-based interface substantially simplified user interactions with the graphics and spreadsheet packages. During informal postexperiment interviews, the graphics users told us that they particularly liked the fine motor control and the direct point-and-shoot software control with the pen-based system, which bypassed some awkwardness in the keyboard interface. Spreadsheet users found the virtual number keypad and the short-cut buttons for writing cell formulas especially attractive. The text-editing users also had the benefit of the pen-based position control and point-and-shoot software navigation. However, in the text-editing tasks the pen interface was only a substitution of one device for another, without much simplification. The disk management pen-based interface simplified some interactions, not others.

Thus, where the pen-based system simplified the interface by reducing the number of keystrokes (or more correctly, pen- strokes) or permitting more direct manipulation of objects, subjects preferred the pen. When it did not, they preferred the keyboard.

From this we infer that the pen-based interface is likely to be successful where it can simplify the interaction between the user and the computer. If it offers enough in the way of simplification, users will tolerate slower data input. Because the pen-based interface at its best will never be as fast as the keyboard for character input, it is likely that the ideal pen-based applications will involve a minimum of data input. This suggests that applications wherein the data are relatively stable over time will be more appropriate than applications where the data are constantly changing. High-volume character input will probably be done with a keyboard for some time to come.

## IV.3.1. Advantages of Pen-based Computers

When thinking about the set of applications to which a pen-based interface is well suited, it is helpful to examine its potential contributions (summarized in Table 6). There are no moving parts to wear out or break, so a self-contained system can be more rugged and reliable in the field than a keyboard-based portable. Also, a device with no keyboard should be lighter. A pen-based system permits one-handed operation, which should make it useful for users on the move. A pen-based system permits silent operation, which should make it more acceptable in meetings. Unlike the mouse, which requires movements of the whole arm from the shoulder, a pen-based system gives the user fine-motor control with the fingertips. Further, it permits direct manipulation of objects such as shapes or words. No cursor is required to show where on the screen the action will take place. The action always takes place at the tip of the pen. User/computer interactions for some applications are simpler.

When one considers the characteristics of the pen-based interface, several kinds of applications with low-volume text entry requirements suggest themselves (Table 7). Two of them, form-filling and graphics, are already commercially available. There are a number of ways to enter many kinds of data with a pen-based system. Check boxes, menus, sliders, and dials may all be manipulated directly with a pen (figure 2). A great deal of the information entered with a pen-based interface may never need to be translated to machine-readable form. For example, pen-based interfaces offer an on-line alternative for notetaking in a meeting setting where keyboard noise might not be acceptable. With a pen-based system it is possible to capture a bit-mapped image of anything displayed on a public screen, and then mark it up with hand-made annotations and store it for future reference, or forward it to another user for further action. Pen-based group support will shortly be available in the form of shared diagramming and sketching tools.

## V. Conclusion: Whither the Pen-based Interface?

THE MOST ATTENTION-GETTING FEATURE OF PEN-BASED INTERFACES according to the conventional wisdom, handwriting recognition, is in fact its weakest component. Further, one of the computer industry's fondest hopes—to attract current nonusers into the information age by simply replacing the keyboard on old applications—is unlikely to occur. Rather, new users are likely to be attracted to computing if the new interface engenders new applications that the users perceive as valuable. The question certainly arises as to whether academic and industry researchers should continue to spend time and money on the development of handwriting recognition, given its intrinsic limitations, and given that an effective, low-cost alternative is available in the form of the virtual keyboard.

Despite the flaws of the conventional wisdom, pen-based interfaces have much to offer. For example, it will soon be possible to combine a pen-based computer with wireless LAN and cellular phone, fax, and modem technology [7]. Such a device might offer the user access to the entire telecommunications and computing infrastructure of an organization through a single device with no moving parts. The combination of technologies would permit access to information as executives prefer it: "My time, my place, my way."

There are several hardware developments that might speed the widespread acceptance of pen-based machines: longer battery life, large, high-resolution screens, and a standardized docking station. If a tool is to be the primary information gateway for a user, its power supply must last through at least a working day. Most current vendors offer units with a battery life of three hours or less. Only one or two have achieved battery life in the range of eight hours, and these systems do not include high-resolution displays or large-volume secondary storage.

In our discussions with potential users, they have repeatedly expressed a desire to be able to see a whole page, full size, and half-size pages side-by-side. Perhaps pen-based technology may be the impetus for the computer industry at last to abandon the venerable 80×25 screen format.

Because keyboards are clearly superior to pen-based interfaces for high-volume text input, users may be more willing to use pen-based machines if standardized docking stations were ubiquitous. If the users could count on finding standard docking stations anywhere they went, the sting of slow data input might be much reduced.
