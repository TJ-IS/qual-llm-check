# Structure and Perceived Effectiveness of Software Development Subunits: A Task Contingency Analysis

K. KYU KIM AND NARAYAN S. UMANATH

K. KYU KIM is an Associate Professor of accounting and MIS at Inha University, Korea. He received his Ph.D. in business administration (MIS) from the University of Utah. His undergraduate and graduate degrees are also in business administration. Dr. Kim's present research interests include business re-engineering, strategic use of information technology, and management of IT infrastructure. He has published in Accounting Review, Decision Sciences, Health Services Management Research, Information & Management, Journal of Information Systems, and MIS Quarterly.

NARAYAN S. UMANATII is an Assistant Professor of MIS at the Pennsylvania State University. Entering academia after fourteen years in software development in industry, where he has held positions such as information systems analyst, manager of systems development, and systems consultant, Umanath received his Ph.D. in business administration from the University of Houston in 1987. His undergraduate and graduate degrees are in mechanical engineering and industrial engineering and management, respectively. His present research interests include organizational issues pertaining to information systems, strategic use of IS, agency theory view of IS problems, and data visualization. He has published in Communications of the ACM and Decision Sciences, and has a paper forthcoming in Management Science.

ABSTRACT: The nature of the task to be performed by the information systems (IS) unit as a contextual factor has received little attention in the IS research literature. In addition, prior empirical work examining IS structure lacks theoretical foundation. In this paper, we first clarify the multidimensional nature of an organization's subunit structure. Then, we examine the impact of the structure variables on the perceived effectiveness of a subunit of IS—software development—using “task” as the contextual contingency influencing this relationship. Organizational information processing theory provides the theoretical foundation for this study. Analysis of data from thirty hospitals reveals that a contingency relationship exists between task context and structural dimensions of software development subunits in determining their effectiveness. Task complexity and decision-making structure in interaction exhibit a nonmonotonic effect on the software development subunit's effectiveness. While the interaction of subunit work flow interdependence with both decision-making structure and control structure was significant, the direction of effect was unexpected. Implications of these findings for practicing managers, and IS planners and researchers are discussed.

Acknowledgments: The authors gratefully acknowledge the insightful suggestions of Carol Saunders and the two anonymous reviewers.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: decentralization, IS effectiveness, IS task characteristics, IS unit structure, structural contingency model.

## 1. Introduction

EMPIRICAL WORK REPORTED IN EXTANT LITERATURE in information systems (IS) addressing organizational issues spans three segments: (a) the IS as an intervention, effecting changes in the organizational structure [20, 39]; (b) the organizational context as the determinant of IS performance/success [10, 15, 27]; and (c) the fit between IS subunit structure and organizational context [11, 24, 27].

Prior work in this area has several limitations. First, prior research has invariably focused on decentralization as the principal proxy for IS unit structure. Thus, the multidimensional nature of IS unit structure has so far escaped research attention. Second, the context factors studied in prior research as influencers of IS success and/or IS unit structure implicitly assume that the context of the organization is the same as that of the IS unit. While organizational context can be expected to influence the context of any of its subunits, the two contexts are not one and the same. Third, organizational theorists have repeatedly asserted that subunits within an organization must also be able to deal with work-related uncertainty emerging from the task(s) faced by the subunit $[9, 12, 36]$ . Prior research in IS has practically ignored task contingencies in their research models. Fourth, previous research in IS has mostly viewed the “fit” between organizational context and IS unit structure in terms of correlations between pairs of variables representing the two domains. Such a criterion-free perspective simply assumes that the fit/misfit between organizational context and IS unit structure affects some criterion of interest (e.g., performance); it does not test it. Finally, there has been a dearth of theoretical foundation for the empirical work conducted in this area of IS research. In this paper we attempt to deal with all these limitations.

This paper describes a field study of hospital information systems. The study is part of a larger research project. Our focus is on a subunit of the IS unit, namely, software development. We capture the elements that constitute the two basic structural dimensions of an organizational subunit, that is, the decision-making structure and the control structure, and frame them in the setting of the software development subunit. We then examine the impact of these two structural dimensions on the perceived effectiveness of the software development subunit using “task” as the contextual contingency moderating this relationship. The structural contingency framework and the organizational information-processing model established in the field of organization theory provide the theoretical foundation for our work.

The paper proceeds as follows. In the next section we discuss prior research in IS relevant to our research. Section 3 develops the theory and the hypotheses. The research method is presented in section 4. Section 5 describes the data analysis and is followed by a section reporting the results. The last two sections offer, respectively, a discussion of findings and concluding remarks.

## 2. Background

OLSON AND CHERVANY [24, p. 57] CLASSIFY INFORMATION SERVICES FUNCTION into three separate elements—systems operation, systems development, and systems management—and argue that different sets of criteria form the basis to assess the organization (i.e., structural configuration) of each element. The focus of our research in this paper is on the systems or software-development function.

Olson and Chervany [24] interviewed corporate executives and IS managers in forty-three business organizations seeking to identify organizational characteristics that are associated with the structure of the IS function. They found that the organizational environment does influence the level of decentralization of information services. When the decision-making authority at the organization level was decentralized, the software development subunit, for instance, resorted to the use of “user liaison” to enhance communication with the user community. Also, decentralization, in general, at the organizational level led to decentralization tendencies in the software development subunit. However, few organizational characteristics were found to influence the structure of IS unit consistently across all the organizations.

Ein-Dor and Segev [11], using a sample of fifty-three organizations from a large U.S. metropolitan area, studied the fit between IS unit structure and the organizational context. Their results indicate significant positive correlation between centralization at the organization level with the centralization of the IS development function. Two measures of firm size—size relative to industry and sales—displayed significant negative correlation with the degree of centralization of the software development subunit. A third measure, number of employees in the organization, was not correlated with the degree of centralization (p. 63).

Finally, Raymond [27] examines the Ein-Dor and Segev [10, 11] propositions, but introduces IS sophistication as an indirect proxy for IS structure [27, p. 15]. While IS sophistication is considered an important factor in the immediate IS functional environment, Raymond does not articulate the rationale behind the substitution of IS sophistication for IS structure. Nonetheless, an important contribution of Raymond's work pertains to the attempt to integrate the evaluation of organizational context, IS structure, and IS success. The earlier studies either implicitly [24] or explicitly [11] assume a specific state for IS success and simply evaluate the congruence between the organization context and IS structure via correlation analyses of independent pairs of variables from the two domains. Other prior studies that examine the relationship between organization context and IS function (e.g., success, use, quality, and performance) assume IS structure as invariant. Raymond [27] examines partial correlations, which suggest that the impact of IS sophistication on IS success is contingent upon organization context or vice versa. However, Raymond [27, p. 17] notes that interaction hypotheses can capture contingency relationships more precisely.

The above review exposes an important shortcoming of prior research. No one has studied the characteristics of the task(s) to be performed by the IS unit as a determinant of IS unit structure. Further, little attention has been given to the possibility that the influence of IS unit structure on unit performance may be contingent upon its context.

Sanders and Courtney [28] took the initial step in considering task variables as determinants of IS success. Their focus, however, was on financial and planning analysts, and managers, and their behavior towards DSS software. Nonetheless, their findings do indicate that management science and data processing staff, a small portion of their sample (14 percent), indicated task interdependence to have a positive effect on their overall satisfaction as well as their decision-making satisfaction with DSS. Task difficulty, on the other hand, displayed a negative influence on the overall satisfaction with DSS. Drawing from the structural contingency research [9, 30], Kim [21] measured IS task context in terms of task predictability, problem analyzability, and task interdependence. Specifically, he assessed accounting information system (AIS) structure in terms of coordination modes used in the subunit. Kim's findings indicate that the satisfaction expressed by the users of the AIS was facilitated by different coordination modes contingent upon the level of predictability of the task performed by the AIS subunit. The more predictable the tasks of the AIS subunit, the better the impersonal (administrative) mode of coordination facilitated performance. Likewise, the less predictable the tasks, the better the personal mode of coordination facilitated performance. However, problem analyzability and task interdependence as contingent variables displayed no influence on the effect of the coordination modes on user satisfaction. The principal limitation of this study is that the author examined each contingency relationship independently as if they all occurred in isolation instead of evaluating all of the task variables and coordination modes simultaneously in a single model. In addition, Kim's [21] study did not examine such structural elements as decentralization, discretion, and formalization.

The limiting factor of all these studies is that they do not use a formal theory to establish and examine relationships among the research variables (e.g., task and structure variables) and their impact on IS performance/effectiveness. In another paper from this research project, Umanath and Kim [37] adopted the organizational information processing model [7, 12, 36] as the theoretical foundation to study the relationship between task and structure variables. The limitation of this study is that the congruence perspective used only examines the multivariate relationship between the task and structure variables. This criterion-free perspective simply assumes that the task structure fit/misfit affects some criterion of interest (e.g., performance, effectiveness), but does not test it.

## 3. Theory and Hypotheses

THE THEORETICAL FOUNDATION FOR THIS RESEARCH stems from the structural contingency framework and the information-processing model extensively documented in the organizational design literature $[7, 9, 12, 30, 36]$ . The information-processing model introduces the concept of organizational information processing as an explanation for why context and structure should match for optimum organizational performance. The model $[7, 12, 36]$ posits that since managers need information to cope with the uncertainty and equivocality of work-related tasks, task characteristics dictate the information-processing needs of an organizational unit. Also, all available information to interpret an organizational situation cannot be processed by the manager due to limited individual information capacity [31]. Formal information-processing mechanisms augment the information-processing capacity of a unit. Different structural configurations of the organizational unit provide different capacities for information processing (i.e., volume of data and richness of information). Performance is a function of matching information-processing capacities (unit structures) with information-processing needs (task characteristics). A mismatch entails either excessive costs or delayed and/or inappropriate decisions [12, 36]. The model is depicted in figure 1.

Since a subunit of an organizational unit often engages in a focal set of tasks, it provides a more viable setting for structural design research than a whole organization with a conglomeration of heterogeneous, possibly conflicting task sets $[14]$ . Hence the target of our inquiry—the software development subunit.

In sum, the software development subunit will be more effective when there is a match between the task context that dictates its information-processing requirements and the structural configuration that generates the information-processing capacity. This leads to the general structural contingency hypothesis:

H1: The interaction between the task context and the structure of a software development subunit influences its effectiveness.

While conveying the structural contingency argument properly, the above hypothesis also lacks precision. For instance, it is quite conceivable that different task characteristics are related to different dimensions of the software development subunit's structure and to different degrees in determining the effectiveness of that subunit. The information-processing theory [7, 12, 36] provides the insight to examine structural contingency at this higher level of precision. Before embarking on this investigation, a deeper understanding of the domains, task context, and subunit structure, is in order.

## 3.1. Software Development Task Context

Perrow [25] defined task context in terms of predictability and analyzability. Daft and Lengel [7] associate these two variables with the two theoretical constructs, uncertainty and equivocality, respectively.

Task predictability is the frequency of unexpected and novel events that occur in the work $[25]$ . High workload fluctuations as in the software development support service activities for real-time applications (e.g., law enforcement systems, process control applications) and nonroutineness and/or variety in assignments (e.g., software development consulting activities, end-user software support services) exemplify poor task predictability. Such tasks increase uncertainty in the task context. On the other hand, stable workload as in structured application development projects with prespecified milestones and a timetable, and routine maintenance of batch-oriented data processing applications represent high task predictability. Here, the work-related uncertainty is low.

The degree to which one can analyze the unexpected events defines problem $analyzability$ [25]. When a task is analyzable, one follows an objective procedure to diagnose the problem and select an appropriate method for dealing with it. In other words, there are known procedures that specify the sequence of steps to be followed in performing the task [38]. Such tasks imply low equivocality in the task context (e.g., life-cycle development methodology, transaction processing applications, use of standard development/programming methods like COBOL applications). When the work is not easily analyzable, the analyst/programmer finds it difficult to develop exact rules, procedures, and computational algorithms/routines and relies more on judgment, experience, and heuristic methods (e.g., software prototyping, decision support applications, use of specialized tools like expert systems, and neural network utility software). Here, the equivocality of the task context is high. Together, these two variables—task predictability and task analyzability—convey the complexity of the task context faced by the software development subunit. $^{1}$

Thompson [35] identified another dimension of task context that captures the nature of the work flow among the members of the subunit. Work flow interdependence is the degree to which collaboration among the members of the unit is required to complete the task [23, 35]. Some software development applications are essentially stand-alone systems with little or no interdependence in the task. Others, like strategic system applications, often involve selective integration of decision support tools (e.g., expert systems, analytical packages, neural networks) with conventional transaction processing. The developmental activities of such applications require diverse technical skills. Then, the subunit members work in concert to complete the tasks.

## 3.2. Software Development Subunit Structure

Six attributes are often cited in the literature as components of a subunit's structure: decentralization, discretion, formalization, coordination, professionalism, and specialization [30, 38]. Given the relatively sharp focus of our research setting, that is, software development activities, we concluded that professionalism and specialization, especially when aggregated at the subunit level, may not display much variability, and thus we focused on the other four as the dimensions of the software development subunit structure domain.

Decentralization reflects the locus of authority and decision making and is the extent to which decision-making autonomy is dispersed or concentrated in an organizational unit [26]. It is usually assessed by the degree of participation of a member of the unit in decision making [16]. In the software development scenario, decisions regarding adoption of new software and/or methodology, policies, and even hiring may be centralized or decentralized. Note that the physical distribution of the subunit location is only an operational symptom; decentralization is a broader construct relating to decision making in the subunit.

Discretion is the extent to which unit members are permitted to choose the means for completing the task $[25]$ , such as which particular software to use, what methodology to use for a particular development project, and so on. Formalization, on the other hand, is the extent of job codification and rule observation $[16]$ . It is a device for prescribing how, when, and by whom tasks are to be performed—for example, tight/loose job descriptions, prespecified/open communication channels, or strict/flexible operating procedures. Coordination involves fitting together the various activities of the unit to achieve the unit’s objectives. Personal coordination requires human input (e.g., project coordinator, user liaison) and bureaucratic mode of coordination (work plans and schedules) is impersonal $[38]$ .

Thus, by definition, decentralization and discretion pertain to decision making, and formalization and coordination represent the control mechanism in an organizational subunit. Centralized decision making and minimal opportunity for individual discretion in task execution are hallmarks of a mechanistic decision-making structure. A mechanistic structure is characterized by a high degree of organization, uniform treatment of problems, and a focus on orderliness $[3]$ . On the other hand, when the decision-making autonomy is delegated to levels most sensitive and responsive to problems and to members who are mutually connected to and depend on one another, the decision-making structure is organic $[3]$ . Likewise, the control structure, an aggregated view of formalization and coordination, may be classified in terms of bureaucratic and interpersonal modes. The bureaucratic mode of control is indicated by a blueprintlike approach where the integrating mechanism includes preestablished plans, schedules, and forecasts, formalized rules, policies, and procedures, and standardized information and communication channels $[35]$ . The interpersonal mode of control uses personal coordination methods such as individual or group mechanisms for mutual task adjustments and less formal modes of control for communication and feedback. Some authors $[22]$ have said that mechanistic (organic) structures tend to utilize bureaucratic (interpersonal) modes of control.

## 3.3. Task Context, Decision-making Structure, and Effectiveness

Organic structures are marked by highly decentralized decision making and use of individual discretion. Such highly connected communication networks not only permit efficient use of individuals as problem solvers by means of increased opportunity for feedback and error correction, but are also less sensitive to information overload or saturation since the networks are relatively independent of any one individual $[36, p. 618]$ . Since increase in task complexity (i.e., uncertainty and equivocality) and higher work flow interdependence inflate information requirements—both volume of data and richness of information—at the point of task execution, increased participation in decision making, and greater discretion exercised by the subunit members—that is, an organic structure—will be more effective than a mechanistic structure under this condition $[12]$ .

On the other hand, when task complexity and work flow interdependence are relatively low, the information-processing requirements are low and exceptions can be referred upward in the hierarchy for centralized decision making as in a mechanistic structure without overburdening the structure. The information-processing capability—both volume and richness—yielded by an organic structure in this case is unnecessarily excessive for the simple and weakly interdependent tasks and prove costly in terms of time, effort and control [36, p. 620].

In short, both mechanistic and organic structures are suitable for the software development subunit depending on the operant task conditions. The contingency prediction is about how the task context mediates the relationship between the decision-making structure and the subunit effectiveness or, alternatively, how the decision-making structure mediates the relationship between task context and the subunit effectiveness. In Schoonhoven's [30] terms, empirical interaction is being predicted. The above arguments can be summarized in the following two interaction hypotheses:

112: The interaction between task complexity and decision-making structure of a software development subunit influences its effectiveness.

II3: The interaction between work flow interdependence and decision-making structure of a software development subunit influences its effectiveness.

The contingency argument of Galbraith [12] implicitly assumes a positive monotonic effect of structure on effectiveness [30]. The premise that underlies the assumption of monotonicity is that a task contingency can be better handled by the presence of structure (mechanistic, organic, or some combination thereof) than by the absence of structure. With respect to task complexity, for instance, the monotonic effect argument is that, with an increase in task complexity, the mechanistic structure type is less effective (not necessarily detrimental) than the organic structure type, and vice versa. Theoretically, the implication is that the information-processing capability provided by a mechanistic structure is less (not negative), that is, insufficient to match the information-processing needs of tasks that are highly complex and/or exhibit high work flow interdependence, thus resulting in suboptimal performance. By the same token, the information-processing capability yielded by an organic subunit structure is too much, that is, wasteful and counterproductive for the nominal information-processing needs of low-complexity/less interdependent tasks, leading again to suboptimality. The following two corollaries convey the monotonic interpretation of the interaction hypotheses, H2 and H3.

C2a: The greater the complexity of task faced by a software development subunit, the lesser the positive impact of a mechanistic subunit structure on its effectiveness.

C2b: The greater the complexity of task faced by a software development subunit, the more the positive impact of an organic subunit structure on its effectiveness.

Likewise, H3 can be restated as:

C3a: The higher the work flow interdependence in a software development subunit, the lesser the positive impact of a mechanistic subunit structure on its effectiveness.

C3b: The higher the work flow interdependence in a software development subunit, the more the positive impact of an organic subunit structure on its effectiveness.

Schoonhoven [30, p. 353] asserts that “an assumption of symmetrical effects is hidden in the language of contingency theory.” In other words, arguments suggesting that lower task complexity, when coupled with a more mechanistic structure, enhances effectiveness should automatically imply that higher task complexity, when combined with more mechanistic structure, or lower task complexity, when combined with a more organic structure, undermine effectiveness due to the incongruence between the task and structure variables in the latter case. The nonmonotonicity assumption for the interaction effect thus interprets H2 and H3 as follows:

C2c: When the decision-making structure of the software development subunit is mechanistic, increases in task complexity will negatively influence the subunit's effectiveness.

C2d: When the decision-making structure of the software development subunit is organic, increases in task complexity will positively influence the subunit's effectiveness.

C3c: When the decision-making structure of the software development subunit is mechanistic, increases in workflow interdependence will negatively influence the subunit's effectiveness.

C3d: When the decision-making structure of the software development subunit is organic, increases in work flow interdependence will positively influence the subunit's effectiveness.

Which of the two interpretations of contingency relationships—monotonic or non-monotonic effect—is correct? We do not see an answer to this question in the literature. The two interpretations convey different viewpoints of contingency effect because they are predicated on different underlying assumptions. One may view the non-monotonic effect as a special case of the monotonic effect or vice versa. In this paper, we test H2 and H3. However, we can only explore the viability of the associated corollaries about the form of the interaction effects (monotonic or nonmonotonic) with a view to identify sharper research questions for future inquiry.

## 3.4. Task Context, Control Structure, and Effectiveness

When complexity (uncertainty and equivocality) in the task domain is low, the work tends to be more predictable. Low work flow interdependence reduces the need for costly horizontal communication channels $[36]$ . Thus, the information-processing requirements of the system are low. Task-related rules and procedures that tend to reduce the information-processing capability of the subunit can then be established (bureaucratic mode of control) for the efficient execution of the tasks. An increase in task complexity and/or higher work flow interdependence increases the information-processing requirements of the subunit. Then accurate a priori assessment of numerous combinations of events, and specification of appropriate rules and procedures applicable for each, becomes extremely difficult. To attempt such assessment is likely to result in inflexible application of rules to cases in which standardized procedures are inappropriate, leading to ineffective performance $[30]$ . Less formality, less imposition of rules and regulations, and greater peer involvement (horizontal communication), that is, interpersonal mode of control, can cater to the increased information requirements effectively. Thus we hypothesize:

H4: The interaction between task complexity and control structure of a software development subunit influences its effectiveness.

H5: The interaction between work flow interdependence and control structure of a software development subunit influences its effectiveness.

The monotonic interpretation for the above interaction hypotheses will result in the following corollaries:

C4a: The greater the complexity of task faced by a software development subunit, the lesser the positive impact of a bureaucratic mode of subunit control structure on its effectiveness.

C4b: The greater the complexity of task faced by a software development subunit, the more the positive impact of an interpersonal mode of subunit control structure on its effectiveness.

C5a: The higher the work flow interdependence in a software development subunit, the lesser the positive impact of a bureaucratic mode of subunit control structure on its effectiveness.

C5b: The higher the work flow interdependence in a software development subunit, the more the positive impact of an interpersonal mode of subunit control structure on its effectiveness.

On the other hand, the nonmonotonic interpretation for the interaction hypotheses H4 and H5 will result in the following corollaries.

C4c: With a bureaucratic mode of control structure for the software development subunit, increases in task complexity will negatively influence subunit effectiveness.

C4d: With an interpersonal mode of control structure for the software development subunit, increases in task complexity will positively influence subunit effectiveness.

C5c: With a bureaucratic mode of control structure for the software development subunit, increases in workflow interdependence will negatively influence subunit effectiveness.

C5d: With an interpersonal mode of control structure for the software development subunit, increases in work flow interdependence will positively influence subunit effectiveness.

## 4. Method

THE UNIT OF ANALYSIS IN THIIS STUDY is the organizational subunit—specifically the software development subunit of the IS unit. Responses of individual members were aggregated to yield subunit level values. Since the task at the subunit level is expected to be relatively more homogeneous than at the overall organizational level, the ecological validity of the aggregation of measurements at the subunit level is expected to be less of a problem.

## 4.1. Sample and Procedure

Software development subunits of hospitals served as the research setting for this study. The software development function included analysis, design, and programming of new applications as well as the maintenance of existing applications. Some of the systems applications worked on include admissions and discharge, materials management, patient billing, insurance, accounts receivable/payable, payroll, general ledger, and diagnostic related groups (DRGs). Out of the 73 hospitals that participated, a total of 185 programmer/analysts from 41 hospitals, the head of the software development subunit of each of these 41 hospitals, and the business office managers from 61 hospitals responded. Since we received responses from both the software development staff and the business office manager for only 30 of these hospitals, the sample size for this study is reduced to 30. Ten and 16 programmer/analysts respectively from two large hospitals responded. From 15 of the remaining 39 hospitals, fewer than 4 programmer/analysts per hospital responded. The number of responses from the remaining 24 hospitals ranged from 4 to 7. The average number of responses per hospital was 4.5. The questions associated with task and structure variables were answered by the programmer/analysts. Since all the respondents were nearly at the same staff level within each subunit, we did not see a need for differentially weighting the responses. The responses for each subunit were averaged to yield a subunit level measurement. The respondents returned the questionnaires directly to the researchers thus preserving the anonymity of the responses.

Since the target of our interest was business-oriented applications, and the primary users of the software development service for such applications in the hospitals were the administrative personnel in the business office, we approached business office managers of the hospitals to obtain an evaluation of the information quality of the software development subunits' output. Thus, the business office managers in the hospitals responded to the Jenkins and Ricketts [19] instrument for satisfaction with information quality. Not only were the managers free to seek opinions from their office staff, but we also encouraged them to do so for answering the questionnaire.

We controlled for the subunit environment by limiting the study to not-for-profit hospitals, assuming that subunits with a functional specialization (e.g., Software Development) in the same industry (i.e., hospitals) with a common business policy (i.e., not-for-profit) usually face similar environmental demands. IS researchers have argued that organization size necessitates and/or facilitates IS use $[10]$ . However, empirical findings in this regard have so far been inconclusive $[11, 15, 27]$ . We treated organization size as a method variable and partialled out its effect in our data analysis.

## 4.2. Independent Variables

All measures for the task and software development subunit structure domains were constructed by appropriately modifying instruments already developed and used by other researchers. The measures were calibrated as Likert scales. All but the task interdependence scale were multi-item measures using a 7-point Likert scale. Task interdependence [23] was recorded using a single-item 10-point Likert scale. Cronbach's alpha was computed for all multi-item scales to test the reliability of measurement. The list of variables, the sources of their measures, number of scale items in each measure, and the descriptive statistics and reliability coefficients of the measures appear in Table 1.

The first part of a two-part questionnaire contained twenty-one items representing the four structural elements, namely, decentralization, discretion, formalization, and coordination. Since minor wording changes were made to a few of the adopted questions (see Table 1 for the sources of adoption) in order to customize the instrument to an information systems setting, principal components analysis was performed on the data to ascertain the integrity of the dimensionality sought. Five of the six “decentralization” items loaded together on one factor; all four “discretion” items also loaded on an independent factor. The three “personal coordination” mode items converged on a single factor. However, two of the six “formalization” items loaded with the two designated “impersonal coordination” items. Nonetheless, since we aggregate formalization and coordination into a single factor reflecting control structure, the partial misclassification of items within this group was considered immaterial. The second part of the questionnaire contained eleven items representing the task variables. Factor analysis supported the expected dimensionality of the task domain, that is, task predictability, analyzability, and work flow interdependence.

## 4.3. The Dependent Variable

Hamilton and Chervany [17] distinguish between “goal-centered” and “system resource” views of assessing IS effectiveness. The former is more widely used in behavioral IS literature. Therefore, we used the goal-centered view for measuring IS effectiveness. Effectiveness, defined as the degree of goal attainment, reflects the value judgments of the different stakeholders [14]. Since end users are considered an important constituent of the software development subunit, and user satisfaction with the quality of information generated by the software development subunit is a crucial indicator of the subunit's effectiveness [18, 33], we used user information satisfaction (UIS) as the operational proxy for effectiveness. Srinivasan [33] argues that despite the criticism of Ives et al. [18], the approach used by Jenkins and Ricketts [19] to measure user-perceived effectiveness is superior. "It is one of the few (if not only) approaches that develops an instrument to measure user satisfaction that is well grounded in a widely accepted theoretical model" [33, p. 245]. Therefore we chose the Jenkins and Ricketts [19] instrument for our measurement of user-perceived effectiveness of the software development subunit. This instrument includes seven information attributes to evaluate user satisfaction with information quality: accuracy, information load, format, understandability, and usefulness for identifying, resolving, and selecting from among alternative courses of action.

## 5. Data Analysis

OUR FIRST CONCERN WITH THE DATA PERTAINED to possible response bias in the performance measure, that is, user information satisfaction, since only 30 of the 61 business office managers' responses could be used in the data analysis. $^{3}$ A t-test, however, revealed that there was no significant difference $(t = 1.043, p = 0.30)$ in the subject responses between the two groups $(n_{1} = 30, n_{2} = 31)$ . Similarly, even though 41 software development subunits responded, for 11 of the 41 responses for the performance questionnaire were not received from the corresponding business office managers. Therefore, in this case, we tested for possible sample bias in all eight variables that constituted the task and structure domains. In every case, the null hypothesis of no significant difference between the comparison groups $(n_{1} = 30, n_{2} = 11)$ could not be rejected. In fact, the lowest p value of the eight t-tests was 0.30. Since the group sizes here differed rather substantially, we also tested for homoscedasticity using the folded form F-test. All eight comparisons tested positive. Univariate tests were performed on all the research variables to observe the descriptive statistics and to check if the data in each case came from a normal population. In all but one case (the UIS score) the Shapiro–Wilk statistic (W) ratified normality. A square transformation rectified this problem for the UIS score.

## 5.1. Multiplicative Model

Relationship between two variables predicting a third variable implies statistical interaction $[30, p. 351]$ . This means that the main effects of the constituent variables are insufficient and/or incapable of capturing the full import of the phenomenon under study. Further, the functional form used to express the contingency relationship is not a trivial operational matter; the mathematical function proposed must be theoretically justified. $^{4}$ When, as in our case, the theoretical interpretation implies that perceived effectiveness is most likely when two factors such as task complexity and control structure are both present, but that perceived effectiveness is less likely when either is absent, then the functional form of the interaction is multiplicative [2]. Since a product term is a legitimate way to express interaction in the multiple regression procedure [1], we examined our problem as a multiple regression model of the form:

(1)

$$
\begin{array}{c} {y = b _ {0} + b _ {1} s _ {1} t _ {1} + b _ {2} s _ {1} t _ {2} + b _ {3} s _ {2} t _ {1} + b _ {4} s _ {2} t _ {2}} \\ {+ a _ {1} s _ {1} + a _ {2} s _ {2} + a _ {3} t _ {1} + a _ {4} t _ {2} + a _ {5} z _ {1}} \end{array}
$$

where $s_{1}$ and $s_{2}$ are the two structure variables, $t_{1}$ and $t_{2}$ are the two task variables, $z_{1}$ is the organization size, and y is the response variable (perceived effectiveness).

Rival arguments often contest that the curvilinear properties of the main variables may be obscured by and/or buried in the modeled interaction effects. However, it is important to note that alternate nonlinear functional forms also require a priori theoretical rationale. The objective of the analysis is not finding the functional form that best fits the data, but rather evaluating how well the theorized functional form fits the data. Nonetheless, an arbitrary check for square and square root forms of nonlinearity revealed no significant nonlinear effect (see Table 2).

## 5.2. Evaluation of the Interaction Terms

Raw (nonstandardized) regression coefficients are used to evaluate the results. Raw coefficients are used because the values and significant levels of standardized coefficients will change when the origin points of the model variables are shifted. $^{5}$ Dewar and Werbel [8] argue that a product term in a multiplicative regression model is likely to be correlated with the variables that compose it, leading to multicollinearity. Observe that with appropriate manipulation of the origin points of, say, $s_{1}$ , $s_{2}$ , $t_{1}$ , and $t_{2}$ in equation (1) the multicollinearity with their product terms can be nearly eliminated [13, 32]. Since such manipulations do not affect the raw coefficients of the product terms, raw coefficients do provide a robust test of the interaction effects despite possible multicollinearity.

Merely inspecting the algebraic signs and significance levels of the raw coefficients of the interaction terms in equation (1) is sufficient to test hypotheses H2–H5. However, examination of the magnitudes and signs of the interaction terms, while controlling for the corresponding main effects, is insufficient to evaluate the contingency propositions associated with these hypotheses that suggest monotonic/nonmonotonic effects. An examination of the partial derivatives from the larger regression equation offers insights concerning the monotonic/nonmonotonic properties not readily apparent from the basic results of regression analysis $[13, 30]$ . A brief description of the method used for the test of monotonicity is included in the appendix.

## 7. Discussion

A SUMMARY OF RESULTS APPEARS IN TABLE 5. Task complexity and decision-making structure in interaction exhibit a nonmonotonic effect on perceived effectiveness of the software development subunit, supporting the symmetrical notion of the contingency relationship [30]. That is, as the complexity of tasks faced by the software development subunit increases, the perceived effectiveness of the subunit deteriorates with centralized decision making and minimal individual discretion for the staff programmer/analyst—that is, a mechanistic decision-making structure for the subunit. This trend worsens the more mechanistic the structure gets. On the contrary, decentralized decision making and a high-level individual discretion for the software development staff (i.e., an organic decision-making structure) not only seems to be conducive to the perceived effectiveness of the subunit under higher task complexity, but this trend also strengthens the more organic the subunit structure becomes.

The interaction between task complexity and the control structure was not significant. However, since the main effects due to the task and structure variables are uniformly insignificant, we performed a follow-up regression analysis with a reduced model that excluded these main effects. The results indicate that in addition to all the other interactions indicated in the full model (equation 1), the interaction of task complexity and control structure is also significant (t = -2.934, p = 0.008). Thus, one could argue that the dissipation of model variance over the four nonsignificant main effects obscured this, apparently genuine, interaction effect. While this evidence is supportive of our contingency arguments, the finding is still tentative and requires additional empirical validation.

The monotonic effects reflected by the interaction of work flow interdependence with both decision-making structure and control structure contradict the theorized direction of effect. In other words, as work flow interdependence increases, an organic structure (that is, decentralized decision making and a high level of individual discretionary actions) is less conducive to perceived effectiveness than a mechanistic structure (centralized decision making and minimal individual discretion). Likewise, a bureaucratic control structure appears to facilitate perceived effectiveness, as work flow interdependence increases. Follow-up investigation reveals interesting facts that reconcile the apparent contradiction. The range of observed values for work flow interdependence (measured on a 0–9 scale) in our sample is 0.25 to 4.20 with a mean and median values of 2.055 and 1.8, respectively (see Table 1). An inspection of the frequency distribution of the variable shows that over 83 percent of the observations fall in the range of 1 to 3. This means that our data essentially reflect one category of interdependence, the one that signifies minimal work flow interdependence. In retrospect, one can see from the cross-section of our sample a predominant focus on traditional EDP tasks that are usually modularized and well structured. Software development subunits of this kind can be expected to experience minimal work flow interdependence within their work flow. That centralized decision making, minimal individual discretion, and bureaucratic mode of coordination and control are most conducive to perceived effectiveness when work flow interdependence is minimal conforms completely with the task contingency model and the information-processing theory. Similarly, that a decentralized subunit structure and interpersonal mode of coordination and control do not facilitate perceived effectiveness of the software development subunit when work flow interdependence is minimal is also consistent with the theory. Nonetheless, additional research over the full range of work flow interdependence as well as in the specific levels of work flow interdependence settings (e.g., sequential, reciprocal, and team interdependence [35]) is required to validate or refute our findings.

## 8. Conclusions

WHILE IN PRINCIPLE OUR RESULTS SUPPORT the structural contingency framework, an important next step is to replicate the study in a task context reflecting a wider horizon of uncertainty and equivocality for the software development subunits (e.g., development of decision support and knowledge-based systems in addition to the crucial EDP systems). Then it may be possible to delineate more sharply particular structural patterns for the software development subunits that meet specific task contingencies. Several intervention mechanisms can then be instituted to mitigate the ill effects of existing structure-context mismatches and to reposition the software development subunit in the organic-mechanistic/bureaucratic-interpersonal continua to cope better with the emerging task contingencies (e.g., use a liaison in a centralized software development department, limit software proliferation in decentralized software development subunits).

Unfortunately, the unexpectedly narrow range of observations in our sample precluded us from examining work flow interdependence over its full range. Certainly there are IS departments that face tasks with inherent reciprocal and team interdependence (e.g., integrated decision support applications requiring a diverse set of expertise). The results within the limited range of values for the variable are nonetheless consistent with the theory.

Effectiveness is a multidimensional construct and user information satisfaction (UIS) is but one measure of effectiveness. It is imperative to evaluate the structural design of the software development subunit in terms of other goal-centered (e.g., employee morale) and system resource (e.g., usage pattern, efficiency) measures.

The scope of this study was limited to software development subunits. The structural design of the other two components of IS—namely, IS operations and IS management—also requires research attention. As stated by Olson and Chervany [24, p. 57], the set of evaluation criteria (i.e., task and structure variables) for these IS subunits is different. A recent trend in IS operations has been to consolidate dispersed data centers into one or two supercenters often sustained by a state-of-the-art network management hub [4]. Transaction-intensive data center operations have been the target of such consolidation efforts. Companies like Martin Marietta Corporation with a historical culture of autonomous lines of business, each with its own IS strategies, standards, plans, and controls are discontinuing local systems in favor of commonality in areas such as networking protocols, operating systems, programming languages, and data dictionaries [5]. However, the cost of such centralization is also phenomenal, especially when the consolidation requires integration of existing heterogeneous hardware/software environments [5]. A scientific analysis of the rationale behind such trends and the forces sustaining the process is nonexistent. The generality of our findings is also limited by the focal setting of the study—that is, a small segment of not-for-profit hospitals. Replication in other settings such as banking or manufacturing should shed more light on the issue of structural design of IS subunits.

Even though seventy-three hospitals participated in the study, we received responses from both the software development staff and business office managers from only thirty of them. The reader should consider the relatively small sample size when interpreting the results of this study. In order to aid the reader in this regard, we also computed the value of $R^{2}$ adjusted for the degrees of freedom lost (Table 3). Also, given that the p value of the model (0.009) is small, the instability of the model due to small sample size need not be a serious concern.

How does this paper contribute to the IS literature? The contribution of our work stems from the five limitations in prior research cited in the first section. First, we have expanded the narrow view of the software development subunit structure reported in prior research, namely, decentralization/centralization, by considering the broader decision-making and control structures of the subunit. Next, we have addressed a whole new domain hitherto neglected in IS literature—the task context—as a codeterminant of performance. Third, we have examined a richer alternative to the unilateral search for subunit structure(s) that enhances performance; that is, the task contingency perspective. Fourth, we have remedied the dearth of theoretical foundation for the research in the design of IS structure by adopting a theory from a referent discipline.

This research also offers a crucial contribution to the referent discipline—that is, the organization design/behavior field. Schoonhoven's [30] assertion that the “language” of contingency theory implies nonmonotonicity (or symmetry) is intuitively appealing, but requires theoretical substantiation. Our examination of this phenomenon questions this stand. It is hoped that our empirical analysis will stimulate further theoretical inquiry into the premise that underlies the expectation of monotonic/non-monotonic effects of structural contingency.

What are the implications of our findings for practicing managers? Our findings inform the practitioners that there is no single best structural design for the software development subunit. It is possible, however, to develop a structural configuration that best matches the characteristics of a particular subunit's task portfolio. Both organic and mechanistic decision-making structures can be good and bad; likewise, both bureaucratic and interpersonal control mechanisms can be good and bad. It all depends on the characteristics of the task portfolio of a specific software development subunit.

Centralized decision making, formal rules, and standard operating procedures are preferable and enhance performance when the task portfolio of the subunit is dominated by structured systems development projects requiring minimal reciprocal work flow interaction among the staff. On the contrary, subunits, facing an environment full of ill-structured tasks (e.g., fuzzy requirement specifications, unexpected workload fluctuations) and tasks requiring a diverse set of skills/expertise, will perform better when decision making is decentralized, discretionary action by staff is encouraged, communication channels are informal, and rules and regulations are minimal. The management, in essence, should search for the best “fit” between the task context of its software development subunit and its decision-making and control structures so as to maximize performance.

The role of information systems in corporations, large and small, is changing dramatically; information systems pervade all functional areas of the firm and all levels of management. More and more, the strategy for IS is treated as a part of corporate planning rather than as something that follows corporate planning. Scientifically developed prescriptions for the structural configuration of the subunits of IS are not available to corporate planners. Academic research can contribute significantly to fill this void. This study is a step in that direction.

## APPENDIX

The regression model evaluated in this research is of the form:

$$
\begin{array}{r} y = b _ {0} + b _ {1} s _ {1} t _ {1} + b _ {2} s _ {1} t _ {2} + b _ {3} s _ {2} t _ {1} + b _ {4} s _ {2} t _ {2} \\ + a _ {1} s _ {1} + a _ {2} s _ {2} + a _ {3} t _ {1} + a _ {4} t _ {2} + a _ {5} z _ {1} \end{array}\tag{1}
$$

where $s_{1}$ and $s_{2}$ are the two structure variables, $t_{1}$ and $t_{2}$ are the two task variables, $z_{1}$ is the organization size, and y is the response variable (perceived effectiveness).

In analyzing a particular interaction effect, say $s_{1}t_{1}$ on y in equation (1), one can interpret the relationship either as effect of $s_{1}$ on y over the range of $t_{1}$ or as the effect of $t_{1}$ on y over the range of $s_{1}$ . Mathematically, both are equally valid interpretations of the effect and will yield partials of the forms shown in equations (2) and (3) respectively.

(2)

$$
\begin{array}{l} {y = b _ {1} s _ {1} t _ {1} + a _ {1} s _ {1};} \\ {y = b _ {1} s _ {1} t _ {1} + a _ {3} t _ {1}.} \end{array}\tag{3}
$$

Note that $s_{2}$ and $t_{2}$ are held constant; that is, the partials in equations (2) and (3) are evaluated for any given value of $s_{2}$ and $t_{2}$ . The choice between the two partials is a matter of substantive assumption about the direction of the research interest. Differentiating equation (2) with respect to $s_{1}$ and equating the derivative to zero will yield the value of the contingent variable, $t_{1}$ , at which the effect due to $s_{1}$ on y is zero, that is, the inflection point where the direction of the effect reverses [13, 30].

$$
\delta y / \delta s _ {1} = b _ {1} t _ {1} + a _ {1} = 0;
$$

then, $t_{1}^{*}=-(a_{1}/b_{1})$ gives the inflection point. If the inflection point lies within the observed range of $t_{1}$ in the sample, nonmonotonicity is said to exist in the contingency relationship. Likewise, the inflection point associated with equation (3) can be shown as:

$$
s _ {1} ^ {*} = - (a _ {3} / b _ {1}).
$$

The interaction effect, $s_2t_2$ , can be analyzed in a similar manner.
