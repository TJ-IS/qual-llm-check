# Managing High-Achieving Information Systems Professionals

STANLEY J. SMITS, EPHRAIM R. MCLEAN, AND JOHN R. TANNER

STANLEY J. SMITS received his Ph.D. in counseling psychology from the University of Missouri in 1964. He is currently a Professor in the Department of Management at Georgia State University specializing in organizational behavior and a Licensed Applied Psychologist in Georgia. Prior to 1986, he spent ten years in academic administration where his responsibilities included serving as the Director of Research for the College of Business Administration and Interim Chairman of the Department of Computer Information Systems. His research interests in I/S career progression were stimulated by his participation in the AACSB-sponsored Information Systems Faculty Development Institutes at the University of Minnesota (1984) and Indiana University (1985).

Dr. McLean earned his B.M.E. from Cornell University in 1958 and his master's in 1967 and doctorate in 1970, both from M.I.T.'s Sloan School of Management. Author of over seventy papers published in such journals as Information Systems Research, MIS Quarterly, Communications of the ACM, Information & Management, Harvard Business Review, and Sloan Management Review, his research interests include I/S strategic planning and assessment, management of information systems, end-user computing, decision support systems, and I/S personnel.

JOHN R. TANNER is a Professor of Management and Quantitative Methods at the University of Southwestern Louisiana. Prior to his current position, he was a Professor of Management Information Systems at Western Kentucky University. He received a B.S. in business administration from the University of Southwestern Louisiana in 1967, an M.B.A. from the University of Arkansas in 1968, and a Ph.D. from the University of Arkansas in 1973. His research interests include career patterns of students majoring in management information systems; analyzing relationships between research productivity and teaching effectiveness of faculty; attitudinal analyses of business faculty's perceptions of the abilities of students in their fields; and studying the financing methods and decision rules governing real estate organizations.

PEOPLE ENTER THE WORKPLACE WITH EXPECTATIONS, preferences, personal characteristics, work traits, and an accumulation of life experiences that influence their performance, job satisfaction, and commitment [23]. Performance is often described as a function of ability, motivation, and environment [4]. Managers have considerable influence over the work environment using such things as job design, staffing patterns, and technology to facilitate desired levels of performance. Likewise, they can acquire and sustain acceptable levels of ability through recruitment and selection procedures, job coaching, and staff development activities.

The manager's capacity to influence motivation, however, is a more complex challenge. The typical work environment uses fairly standardized performance appraisal techniques and reward systems, even though there is reasonable evidence that workers are highly individual in terms of their needs and therefore differ regarding their preferences for specific rewards [11].

Nevertheless, in spite of using a common system of hiring practices, job design, equipment purchases, performance standards, and rewards, there may still be situations where some workers outproduce their peers. Hanan and Haigh [10] contend that people who outperform their co-workers react to opportunities and incentives in their environments in ways that enable “them to get the most out of themselves” presumably because “they have to. They leave themselves no choice” [10, p. 1]. They seem to be describing people with a high psychological need for achievement, one of the three needs that McClelland [16] hypothesized are learned by all members of our society.

The present study describes persons entering careers in the information systems (I/S) field with various levels of demonstrated academic achievement. The implications are discussed in terms of managing the work performance and career advancement of I/S professionals who appear to have a high learned need for achievement.

The research reported here is part of an ongoing, longitudinal study of career maturation and progression involving a national sample of I/S professionals [20, 21, 29]. The overall study was initiated in 1987 and will continue for the next several years. The total study integrates two parallel research efforts: a prospective longitudinal study designed to track a sample of graduating I/S majors from thirty-eight colleges and universities into their I/S careers using demographic characteristics of the students and their programs, their academic and vocational behavior, self-described work traits and capabilities, and their job preferences as the starting point; and a retrospective study of successful senior I/S executives examining their career progression, critical success factors, and pivotal career decisions starting from their most recent position and working backward to their entry into their I/S careers. The overall research model [29] is described in figure 1.

The rationale for this long-term research effort is twofold: first, a profession is shaped by those who pioneer its development (the retrospective study) and those who chose to commit themselves to it (the prospective study); and second, longitudinal research, while rare because of the time and effort that is required, is the best way to study career maturation and progression [32].

## 1. Theoretical Base of the Present Study

THE PRESENT STUDY CATEGORIZES RESPONDENTS on the basis of their academic achievement and uses McClelland's theory of learned needs as the principal theoretical foundation [16]. From a psychological perspective, needs energize behavior; that is, an unmet need has the potential to initiate behavior. Successful attempts at fulfilling a need produce satisfaction and therefore reinforce the process undertaken. Over time, repeated attempts to meet a need, followed by repeated success and reinforcement, produce a behavior repertoire—a systematic, perhaps habitual, approach to deriving satisfaction from need fulfillment [3]. McClelland took the principal need theories of his day [15, 24] one step further and postulated that the childrearing practices and early experiences in our society produce three learned needs: the need for achievement, the need for affiliation, and the need for power, which each member possesses but with varying degrees of strength. These needs can be defined as follows:

Achievement: aspires to accomplish difficult tasks; maintains high standards and is willing to work toward distant goals; responds positively to competition; willing to put forth effort to attain excellence.

Affiliation: enjoys being with friends and people in general; accepts people readily; makes efforts to win friendships and maintain associations with people. Power: attempts to control the environment and to influence or direct other people; expresses opinions forcefully; enjoys the role of leader and may assume it spontaneously [22, p. 113].

While early experiences form the basis for learned needs, McClelland's research [17, 19] suggested that an achievement-oriented mentality can be induced in adults through various training efforts. As summarized by Pinder [26], “the need for achievement is learned when opportunities for competing against standards of excellence become associated with positive outcomes” [p. 63].

McClelland's [16] theory has stimulated much research. Achievement has been studied as an outcome [2], as an internal psychological construct [17], and in combinations [12, 13, 18, 31] where either the outcome produced or the psychological construct may serve as the independent or dependent variable. Persons with a high need for achievement (independent variable) have been observed to engage in several common behaviors (dependent variables). The person with a high need for achievement:

Interestingly, there is a potential sex bias in McClelland's early work. Given the social zeitgeist of the 1950s, his research was done on boys and men, so his original theory was limited to males [26]. While more recent research has included females as well as males, any synthesis of research on the learned needs for achievement, affiliation, and power is probably more generalizable to males alone than to males and females.

The linkage between motivation in achievement and career progression was articulated by Atkinson and Raynor in 1974 [1]. They defined careers as “interrelated sets of skill-demanding activities that are engaged in by individuals over time” [1, p. 371]. Successful performance over a sufficiently long period in a defined career requires both ability and motivation. Therefore, the cumulative achievement that defines career success provides evidence of both requisite ability and motivation.

The importance of motivation in achievement stems from its relationship to performance. Industrial and organizational psychologists often express this relationship using the formula, performance = f(ability × motivation). Campbell and Pritchard [4] expanded and refined the role of motivation in this formula by using it as the label for three performance-related activities: first, to initiate effort; second, to expend a certain amount of effort; and third, to persist in expending effort over time. ## 2. Research Methodology

BEGINNING IN 1987, MORE THAN 1,000 GRADUATING I/S MAJORS from 38 colleges and universities have volunteered to participate in a longitudinal study of I/S career maturation and progression. The institutions that accepted the invitation to cooperate in this multiyear research effort are listed in Table 2. These schools were sent a packet of questionnaires to distribute to those students who expressed a willingness to participate in the study. After the initial solicitation of volunteers, no further effort was required on the part of the schools. The questionnaires were administered to the students just prior to graduation. At the time they completed the questionnaires, 58.3 percent had had one or more job interviews and 23.2 percent had already accepted a position. All questionnaires were returned to the researchers for analysis and followup in subsequent years.

## 2.1. Questionnaire Design

The data were collected using a questionnaire that had previously been pilot-tested initially at three institutions in the southeastern United States and subsequently at sixteen colleges and universities in various parts of the country, then revised to eliminate redundant and nondiscriminating items [29]. The original scale consisted of 115 items. The final version of the questionnaire used in this study consisted of fifteen items describing the student's personal characteristics (sex, age, socioeconomic background), academic behavior (number of majors, overall GPA, GPA in major, and degree to be awarded at graduation), and vocational behavior to date (career choice certainty, previous I/S work experience, job preferences by title, number of job interviews, and whether or not they had accepted a permanent position).

The questionnaire used two scales: one, a Personal Characteristics Inventory (PCI) consisting of twenty-five items, was used to assess the respondents' self-descriptions of work-relevant traits and behaviors; and the other, a Job Preference Inventory (JPI) consisting of twenty items, was used to measure the importance of various characteristics they might encounter in an I/S job. The PCI and JPI each consisted of five-point Likert-scale items anchored at the extremes and at the midpoint of each scale.

The PCI has its origins in the performance evaluation materials developed by Szilagyi and Wallace [34], who in turn relied on the work of Cummings and Schwab [8]. The content of the JPI originated in two scales developed at the University of Minnesota (The Minnesota Importance Questionnaire and The Minnesota Satisfaction Questionnaire) [14] and the work of Sims, Szilagyi, and Keller [28]. Items specific to I/S (the importance of state-of-the-art equipment, relations with end-users, promotional opportunities in I/S) were developed by the authors. The Cronbach alpha coefficients for the subjects used in the analyses reported here were 0.89 for the PCI and 0.92 for the JPI.

## 2.2. Subjects

Students were asked to complete the initial questionnaire only if they were willing to participate in the entire multiyear longitudinal research effort. Therefore, subject selection was voluntary and by self-selection. The total subject pool contained both undergraduate and graduate students. Since academic achievement was to serve as the independent variable for several of the analyses reported here, and since grading practices differ substantially for undergraduate as opposed to graduate students, the present analyses were restricted to the 794 undergraduate students for whom complete data were available for the variables of interest. The 794 subjects are described in Table 3 in terms of selected demographic characteristics and their achievement levels in I/S courses. As can be seen, the distribution of subjects by grade point average in their major courses appears to be moderated by the subjects' sex, age, and degree of career certainty.

## 2.3. Statistical Analyses

The JPI and PCI ratings were analyzed to answer two research questions:

1. To what degree do preferred job characteristics and personal attributes and work traits (dependent variables) differ among subjects with low, moderate, and high achievement (independent variable) in I/S courses?

2. To what degree do preferred job characteristics and personal attributes and work traits (dependent variables) differ among female and male subjects with high versus moderate or low achievement in I/S courses (independent variable)?

The mean item scores for each of the dependent variables (the JPI and PCI scale items) were analyzed among the subjects classified according to the independent variables (level of achievement and sex) using ANOVAs. Significant F ratios were subjected to further analysis using Scheffe's test to determine which groups of subjects were significantly different from each other. The Scheffe test is quite conservative and sometimes failed to confirm significant between group differences even though the ANOVA produced an F ratio significantly different from chance at the 0.05 level of confidence.

## 3. Results

THIS STUDY USED GRADE POINT AVERAGE (GPA) AS A PROXY for achievement. Since psychologists contend that past behavior is the best predictor of future behavior, and since only 20.8 percent of the students in this study had any relevant I/S work experience, the GPA in I/S courses is probably the best available behavioral measure of achievement. The use of college grades as a predictor of later job success has been criticized by Bretz [2], whose meta-analytic review found no overall significant relationship between GPA and job success. However, one of the two exceptions to this overall finding of no relationship involved coursework in business, where a moderate relationship was found between GPA and job success (d = 0.60; p < 0.05). This finding of success in career-specific courses relating to subsequent job success provided our justification for the use of GPAs as the independent variable for measuring the subjects' level of achievement.

Once the choice of GPA as the independent variable was made, however, another set of concerns arose. They involved the potential for error in the subjects' self-reporting of their grades and the possible variability in grading standards and practices among the respondents' universities.

To address the first point, no attempt was made to use a ratio or interval scale to measure GPAs. Instead, all subjects were grouped into just three levels of achievement: high (3.51–4.00), moderate (3.00–3.50), and low (2.00–2.99). This more robust ordinal scale thus minimized the problems that often occur in self-reported data.

As for variability of grading practices, it is entirely possible that some schools grade “harder” than others (i.e., have lower average overall GPAs), or that some schools have better students than others because of a higher-quality applicant pool (i.e., a 3.6 earned at university A requires a much higher level of achievement than a 3.6 earned at university B). This circumstance was addressed by the large number of different universities in the study (see Table 2). Any variability, therefore, in either grading practices or overall student ability is normally distributed and thus, it is felt, does not represent a source of bias.

Statistically significant differences among the three different types of achievers (low, moderate, and high) in terms of preferred job characteristics and personal attributes and work traits are presented in Table 4. All four differences in JPI items occur between high achievers (group 3) and subjects with lower levels of achievement in I/S courses. The high-achieving subjects ascribe more importance to I/S jobs that provide high levels of challenge, accomplishment, autonomy, and greater opportunities for promotion within I/S career tracks.

In terms of self-described personal attributes and work traits, the high achievers again standout as the group associated with significant differences. High achievers, in contrast to low achievers, describe themselves as more punctual, intelligent, educated, industrious, organized, insightful, and independent. They also see themselves as more capable of providing leadership to others. Moderate achievers also differ significantly from low achievers in two categories: punctuality and education.

Having established a pattern of significant differences in JPI and PCI items between subjects with high versus moderate or low levels of achievement, the analyses shown in Table 5 focus on differences between female and male subjects. High-achieving females and high-achieving males are compared with each other and with females and males achieving at other levels (moderate or low) in terms of JPI and PCI items. Here the pattern of differences is more complex than in the results reported earlier. On three of the four significant differences (all but task completion) in preferred job characteristics, it is the high-achieving female who is associated with the difference. Highachieving females want more task variety, more task autonomy, and more relaxed supervision than other subjects. It is only in the area of relaxed supervision that the high-achieving females and high-achieving males differ significantly in terms of preferred job characteristics.

## 4. Discussion

WHAT DO THESE RESULTS AND THE LITERATURE tell us about entry-level I/S professionals who presumably have a high learned need for achievement?

\- If gender and age disparities of persons entering careers in I/S are controlled, the high-achieving, entry-level I/S professional is statistically more likely to be female ( $p = 0.05$ ), older ( $p = 0.02$ ), and to have no doubts about having chosen I/S as a career ( $p = 0.005$ ).

\- Females and males who achieved high levels of success in their I/S coursework appear to be more alike than different in terms of the characteristics they prefer in an I/S job and in the way they describe their work-relevant personal attributes and work traits. If one accepts the Scheffe Test results, the only exception to this is in the extent to which females prefer a more relaxed supervision in contrast to typical or close supervision.

\- The types of I/S jobs and work environments preferred by this sample of high achievers are characterized by a desire for creative and challenging work that provides a sense of accomplishment, by task variety, autonomy, and completion, and by work that provides opportunities for advancement within their chosen careers.

\- In terms of work traits, these high-achieving, entry-level I/S professionals see themselves as punctual, industrious, organized, and capable of providing leadership resulting in goal attainment.

\- In terms of personal attributes, these high-achieving entry-level I/S professionals see themselves as intelligent, educated, insightful, and independent.

\- While these high-achieving I/S professionals ascribe to themselves many of the positive characteristics generally found to be true of persons with a high learned need for achievement, the downside may be that the negative characteristics found in the literature may apply to them as well—interpersonal insensitivity, poor people skills, and a preference for working alone.

Although the job characteristic preferences and self-described work traits and personal characteristics are somewhat idealized, the results reviewed here have implications for recruitment, socialization, and commitment, motivation and performance, and career advancement. Each is discussed briefly here.

## 4.1. Recruitment

High achievers entering I/S careers appear to want jobs similar to those described by Hackman and Oldham [9] in their widely accepted Job Characteristics Model (JCM) which later served as the basis for the well-known work of Couger and Zawacki [7]. The results of this study suggest that I/S jobs that are characterized by creativity and challenge, that allow individuals to make their own technical decisions, that are capable of providing a sense of accomplishment, and that allow one to do a variety of tasks and to see them through to completion with reasonable autonomy have strong appeal for high achievers. Add to this from the literature the widely accepted observation that high achievers thrive on feedback and we see a classic fit with the JCM [9]. Unfortunately, few entry-level positions exhibit these characteristics [35]. Therefore, as we have suggested previously [20], it is important for employers to provide realistic job previews to entry-level I/S professionals.

Realistic job previews have been shown to moderate expectations, thereby increasing the likelihood of job satisfaction and organizational commitment [27]. The use of realistic job previews is especially important for high-achieving I/S personnel because of their significantly stronger preferences for work meeting the core dimensions of the JCM. In addition, given what the literature says about the upward mobility of the high achiever, and what our results indicate about their strong preferences for jobs that provide opportunities for promotion within I/S, it is also important to provide a realistic picture of the career paths open to the high-achieving I/S professional at the time of recruitment. Research suggests that the recruitment losses incurred through setting realistic expectations are more than compensated for through reduced subsequent employment turnover [33].

## 4.2. Socialization and Commitment

Our results show that the high-achieving female and male I/S students describe themselves lower than other male and female I/S students on the dimension of Cooperation (“Works well with others, even disliked others, to achieve a common goal or set of goals”), with males in general rating themselves significantly lower than females with moderate or low achievement. Add to this the interpersonal insensitivity, poor people skills, and preference for working alone that Chusmir [5] says is characteristic of people with a high need for achievement (see Table 1), and it suggests that getting high-achieving, entry-level I/S professionals socialized into the organization and work group may be a real challenge for I/S managers. This contention regarding entry-level high achievers is consistent with Couger’s [6, 7] observations of high growth needs and low social needs among I/S professionals.

Mowday, Porter, and Steers [23] emphasized the importance of early organizational experiences in building commitment. Their model, and the research supporting it, suggest that “felt responsibility induces employees to become more behaviorally involved in the job. Greater behavioral involvement should, other factors held constant, lead to greater attitudinal commitment as employees develop attitudes consistent with their behavior” [23, p. 58]. Early opportunities for success and felt responsibility fit very nicely with what is known about persons with a high need for achievement (see Table 1) and therefore should be given serious consideration by I/S managers attempting to integrate, and retain, high-achieving I/S professionals into their work groups.

Commitment to one's chosen profession and to one's employer are not synonymous. As Zawacki, Scott, and Zawacki [37] have reported, not all people make I/S their career choice through an informed process; and therefore they may reevaluate this choice later. Those who make a less-than-informed choice presumably are at greater risk to leave the I/S profession. The high achievers in this study had significantly fewer doubts about their career choice and therefore seem to exhibit long-term commitment to the profession even though they may change employers in an attempt to find a work environment more conducive to meeting their high need for achievement.

## 4.3. Motivation and Performance

The motivational requirements for high achievers entering careers in I/S are quite explicit; that is, according to our results, they are for creative and challenging work that leads to a sense of accomplishment; reasonable autonomy; and, according to the research literature [5, 12, 13, 17, 25], feedback. In brief, they appear to fit Hackman and Oldham's Job Characteristics Model perfectly; and, since their JCM provided the conceptual basis of Couger and Zawacki's [7] study, these high achievers are probably prototypical of their findings. But does the high-achieving I/S professional of the 1970s and 1980s fit the I/S work environment of the 1990s?

When I/S departments functioned in relative isolation from their users and customers, the less interpersonally oriented, high achiever fit the modus operandi of the day. The technical success or failure of the work itself provided much of the feedback, the tasks were technically challenging, and could be completed “in house.” But the nature of the workplace is changing, with networking and self-managed work teams emerging as key elements of success in dynamic organizations. I/S professionals apply their skills in concert with users throughout the organization, and sometimes with customers outside the organization.

As Watson and his associates noted recently [36], business communication and interpersonal skills are becoming important requisite skills for I/S professionals. Assuming they fit the profiles suggested by the literature (see Table 1), can entry-level I/S professionals with demonstrated levels of high achievement in technically oriented I/S coursework meet the demands for successful performance in today's work environment? Probably not unless they are managed in more creative ways so that their strengths can be maximized, while their weaknesses can be offset through the strengths of others.

Early efforts to get high-achieving I/S professionals involved in team projects as a substantial part of their assigned duties may lessen the immediate communication and relationship problems with users and may prevent future problems when their success moves them into managerial positions. Many high achievers attain first-line and middle-management positions based upon their technical skills and corresponding high performance, but their success in middle management positions and their potential for executive-level responsibilities are hampered by their lack of people skills $[18, 31]$ . While individualism is valued in this society $[30]$ , one's interpersonal orientation becomes more important for success as organizational responsibilities increase. The use of team efforts will not only compensate for the high achiever's interpersonal blindspots when working with users, but it can also be a valuable training ground to develop the skills needed for future managerial success.

## 4.4. Career Progression

Career progression is very important to sustain the motivation of success-oriented individuals with a learned need for achievement. As Atkinson and Raynor [1] point out: “immediate performance in a career path bears on the crucial issue (for the individual pursuing the career) of earning the opportunity to continue” [1, p. 372]. So it is very important that entry-level, high-achieving I/S professionals experience early job success. The “catch 22,” however, is the progression from technical success to career success. As Thompson, Baker, and Smallwood’s [35] Four-Stage Career Model indicates (see Table 6), and our preliminary retrospective research on successful I/S executives suggests, high technical skills help the new graduate acquire an entry-level position, and success as a technical expert often propels him or her into Stage 2, or even Stage 3; but success in Stage 3 and progression to Stage 4 require strong people skills. Somewhere in the process of career progression, the high-achieving I/S professional needs to complete a metamorphosis from technical expert to technical and people expert. This may turn out to be an easier task for some of their former classmates who, in their classes, demonstrated less mastery in the technical aspects of the discipline. The success-oriented, high-achieving I/S professional may in fact be disadvantaged by his or her technical success at some later point in his or her career progression.

While employers and persons responsible for academic programs in technical fields should become more actively involved in encouraging a balanced sets of skills, the ultimate responsibility for career management lies with the person attempting to progress. High-achieving I/S technical experts should seek out opportunities, as uncomfortable as they may be initially, to enhance their communication skills and interpersonal sensitivity. This skill-building quest could take many forms: volunteering for committee assignments, speaking to community groups, coaching new employees, participating in academic and staff development programs designed to build specific “people skills,” and so forth. Once the success-oriented high achiever sees the connection between balanced skills and career progression, the motivation to excel learned early in life should be sufficient to get them to undertake such activities.

## 4.5. Assumptions and Limitations

This study has the usual limitations associated with survey research methodologies. It assumes that the respondents understood the items as intended by the researchers and attempted to respond in an honest and forthright manner. Additionally, this study used grade point averages as the proxy for work achievement and assumed that variations in GPAs could be attributed to a meaningful degree to variations in motivation, that is, that a high GPA was not solely the result of ability, institutional grading practices, and/or luck. The likelihood of variation in grading practices among the thirty-eight institutions is also acknowledged, although this variation is assumed to be normally distributed. Finally, the results were interpreted within the conservative framework of the Scheffe Test, thereby increasing the likelihood of Type II errors in contrast to Type I errors.
