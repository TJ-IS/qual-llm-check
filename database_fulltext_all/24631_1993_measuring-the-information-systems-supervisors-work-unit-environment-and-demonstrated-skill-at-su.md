# Measuring the Information Systems Supervisor's Work-Unit Environment and Demonstrated Skill at Supervising

THOMAS W. FERRATT, LARRY E. SHORT, AND RITU AGARWAL

THOMAS W. FERRATT is a Professor in the Department of MIS and Decision Sciences at the University of Dayton. He has also served on the faculties of Indiana University and Drake University. He received his M.B.A. and Ph.D. from The Ohio State University. He has been an adviser to business and government and has also been a computer programmer, systems analyst, and project supervisor. He has been active in ACM's Special Interest Group on Computer Personnel Research (SIGCPR). His current research interests focus on the management of information systems professionals and managerial use of information technology. His research has been published in several outlets, including MIS Quarterly, the Academy of Management Journal, Decision Sciences, and the Journal of Systems Management.

LARRY E. SHORT is Dean of the School of Business at Central Connecticut State University. Prior to joining CCSU in 1986, Dr. Short was at Drake University for fifteen years as a Professor of Management, chair of the Management Department and Director of Graduate Programs in Business. Dr. Short also served in various human resource management positions of the federal government, including director of personnel. He received his bachelor and masters degrees in business from Texas Tech University and his doctorate in business administration from the University of Colorado.

RITU AGARWAL is an Associate Professor in the Department of MIS and Decision Sciences at the University of Dayton. She received her Ph.D. in MIS and her M.S. in computer science from Syracuse University in 1988. Professor Agarwal's publications have appeared and are forthcoming in Journal of Management Information Systems, Information and Management, OMEGA, Decision Support Systems, Knowledge-Based Systems, International Journal of Man-Machine Studies, Knowledge Acquisition, and elsewhere, and she has presented papers at several national and international meetings. Her current research focuses on knowledge-based systems, decision support systems, and management of information systems professionals.

ABSTRACT: Research suggests that the management of information systems professionals makes a difference in the quality and quantity of systems produced. Although technological developments affecting the productivity of systems professionals are relatively easy to isolate, the effects of differences in management are harder to assess. This paper reports the results of a two-stage effort to develop, validate, and score an

instrument for measuring differences in management. In the first stage an instrument and scoring procedures for measuring the work-unit environment were developed and shown to have acceptable reliability and validity. In the second stage the work-unit environment instrument and scoring procedures were adapted to yield a shorter instrument for measuring differences in demonstrated skill at supervising. Based on different assumptions about the desirability of mid-range work-unit environments, alternative scoring algorithms are used with responses to the ten-item survey to yield alternate indices of demonstrated skill at supervising. Although factor and reliability analyses support the reliability and validity of the instrument, the scoring algorithms require further research. In addition, the relationship between the work-unit environment and demonstrated skill at supervising constructs and other related constructs could also be a worthwhile area for further investigation.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: productivity of information systems professionals, skill measurement, supervision of information function.

SEVERAL WRITERS HAVE SUGGESTED THAT THE MANAGEMENT of information systems professionals makes a difference in the quality and quantity of systems produced (e.g., [5, 11, 12, 15]). While technological developments have obviously made it possible to increase the quality and quantity of systems produced over the last fifty years, the effects of differences in management are not as obvious. Technology is only one enabling element in the output of an information systems department; of equal, if not greater, importance are the people responsible for producing systems, that is, IS professionals. The productivity and effectiveness of the IS function in an organization is a key area of concern for academics and practitioners alike. First-line supervisors of information systems professionals (e.g., computer programmers, systems programmers, and systems analysts) are the managers who establish the immediate work environment affecting productivity. While those promoted to supervisory positions in a technical environment may have demonstrated strong technical skills, they may not have had an opportunity to develop the managerial knowledge and skills needed to establish productive work environments [19]. Personal growth and development have been found to be an important nonsalary incentive for systems analysts [20] and should continue to be one as analysts are promoted to managerial positions and seek to develop their managerial knowledge and skills. Those who have an interest in understanding and making differences in the management of information systems professionals need tools for measuring these differences. Such tools would facilitate the study of two key items: (1) the effects of different methods of managing information systems professionals and (2) the results of specific attempts to alter the management of information systems professionals.

This paper focuses on a tool for measuring differences in managing at the work-group level and presents the results of efforts to develop, validate, and score an instrument used initially to measure differences in the work-unit environment (defined below), and adapted subsequently to measure differences in demonstrated skill at supervising. The work reported here builds upon and extends two pieces of prior research that describe the first stage of instrument development [15] and provide the impetus for the subsequent adaptation [14]. The primary focus of these earlier works is not on the instrument per se; instrument development, validation, and scoring were necessary to conduct the reported research. A consolidated description of the work on instrument development, validation, and scoring completed in both stages is presented here with additional description of the conceptual domain and data not included elsewhere. This consolidated description allows other researchers to make more informed judgments about the content and construct validity of the instrument and scoring procedures reported here. (See [40] and [27] for descriptions of different types of validity.) Although considerable work has been completed, this paper is appropriate now to focus attention on the strengths and limitations of the instrument and scoring procedures and to propose additional work that is needed.

First Stage: Development of the Work-Unit Environment Instrument

## The Conceptual Domain

ONE QUESTION MOTIVATING THE DEVELOPMENT OF THE INSTRUMENT reported here is, “How should people working in the information systems arena be managed?” Couger and Zawacki [11] found motivational differences between IS and non-IS people and suggested they should be managed differently. Specifically, they recommended that information systems people should have jobs with high motivating potential, based on Hackman and Oldham’s [22] work in designing jobs. For Couger and Zawacki, differences in management referred to differences in job design.

At about the same time as the Couger and Zawacki work was published, the first two authors were reviewing the general management literature to identify managerial behaviors that had persisted in the literature as determinants of productive work behavior. Table 1 shows the identified set of managerial behaviors. These behaviors defined the domain for generating the original items for the instrument described in this study. A brief review of these behaviors and the literature from which they were extracted follows.

Designing the work has long been recognized as an important factor influencing employee productivity. At one extreme of job design is Taylor [46], who suggests separating the planning and doing of work, and the related work simplification approach of the scientific management movement [3, 18, 39]. At the other extreme is the job enrichment movement [21, 22, 24, 48]. The primary thesis of this movement is that more complex work (e.g., including the planning with the doing) is more stimulating and therefore more productive.

Related to designing the work is designing coworker relations. The scientific management (or industrial engineering) approach is primarily concerned with designing ergonomically efficient work stations, and thus, either ignores coworker relations or purposely designs work stations to isolate workers from each other.

At the other extreme is the sociotechnical systems approach $[30, 42, 47]$ which emphasizes both job technology and employee social needs when designing work stations. Suggestions from this approach range from physically clustering jobs in order to facilitate interpersonal relations to building autonomous work groups.

Extensive research in the leadership area certainly suggests that two leader behaviors are very important. One behavior is work oriented and includes the commonly referred to leader behaviors of (a) showing concern for production and (b) initiating structure (which refers to initiating activity in the work group, organizing it, and defining how the work should be done). The other is more people-oriented and includes the commonly referred to leader behaviors of (a) showing concern for employees and (b) consideration (which refers to the extent to which the leader exhibits concern for the welfare of the other members of the group). The Michigan and Ohio State studies (e.g., [4, 6, 7, 44]) provide support for including these leader behaviors in our initial model. Table 1 refers to these work- and people-oriented leader behaviors, respectively, as paying attention to production and paying attention to interpersonal relations. One popular approach suggests that managers should show high concern for both production and employees to be most effective [8, 9]. Other research suggests that the situation affects how much of one or both dimensions of leadership should be exhibited to obtain high productivity [6, 28].

Other lines of research have generated studies that overlap to some extent with the job design and leadership literature. Goal setting [31, 32, 36] focuses employee attention on performance as does the work-oriented leader behavior described above. Generally, the literature suggests that setting specific goals leads to higher productivity than merely showing a general concern for productivity. Another area of the goal-setting literature is concerned with whether goals are set unilaterally by the superior or participatively with subordinates. Encouraging participation is a managerial behavior that has been extensively studied [35, 37]. It overlaps with the autonomy dimension within the job design literature and the democratic style of leader behavior discussed by Bass [6] and Tannenbaum and Schmidt [45]. One conclusion from the literature on participatory decision making is that it works best when those participating have knowledge to contribute in the decision-making process.

A widely discussed managerial behavior pattern is the use of management by objectives (MBO) [10, 13, 41]. In addition to goal setting and participation, MBO usually includes feeding back results to the employee. Providing feedback to employees has also received attention as a managerial behavior in and of itself $[1, 25]$ , in conjunction with goal setting $[26, 29]$ , and as part of a reinforcement approach to controlling employee behavior $[38]$ . One view in the literature is that feedback will have a positive impact on behavior only if it leads to the setting of a high performance goal $[33]$ . A more recent view by Locke and Latham $[34]$ is that although feedback and goal setting have robust effects on performance, neither is very effective in the absence of the other.

A final managerial behavior that has received some attention in the management literature [43] and considerably more attention in the psychological literature [2] is role modeling. The main suggestion from this literature is that managers should exhibit highly productive work behavior as a model for subordinates to follow.

Although the managerial behaviors of Table 1 provided the conceptual domain for generating the original set of items, Ferratt and Short [15] subsequently sought to combine these behaviors conceptually. As a result, they defined the work-unit environment with six interrelated managerial behaviors as shown in figure 1. The work-unit environment (which the manager is responsible for creating) is depicted as a determinant of employee productivity through its continuing effects on employee behavior. The discussion below more fully describes the work-unit environment, particularly the pairings of managerial behaviors defining it.

Two managerial behaviors, enriching the job and involving the employee, define how managers relate workers to their work and coworkers in both design and practice and together deal with enhancing the work. These work-enhancing behaviors form the central core of the work-unit environment as shown in figure 1 since employee productivity arises from the employee's work. Writings on designing jobs, including both designing the work and designing coworker relations from Table 1, as well as writings on encouraging participation provide the basis for including these managerial behaviors as defining variables for the work-unit environment.

Another two behaviors, attending to production and attending to interpersonal relations, define the task-social orientation of a work unit and its manager and together deal with creating the work climate. These work climate creating behaviors provide a continuing context for the employee's work and are thus depicted in figure 1 as determinants of employee productivity that form a surrounding context for the central core behaviors. Writings on leader behavior, including both paying attention to production and paying attention to interpersonal relations in Table 1, provide the basis for including these managerial behaviors as defining variables for the work-unit environment.

The final two behaviors, targeting work behavior and reinforcing work behavior, serve as pre- and postperformance guides to employee work behavior and together deal with shaping work behavior. These work-shaping behaviors are depicted in figure 1 as determinants of employee productivity that affect employee behavior before and after it is focused on the work. Writings on goal setting, role modeling, and providing feedback form the basis for including these managerial behaviors as defining variables for the work-unit environment.

## Constraints in Instrument Design

The development of the instrument to describe the various managerial behaviors in Table 1, which were subsequently conceptualized as the individual's perceived work-unit environment shown in figure 1, was guided by two related considerations. First, the instrument was designed to be administered in a field setting. Second, a practical constraint guiding the design was that the instrument should be short enough to be administered as part of a battery of instruments in forty-five minutes or less to facilitate the cooperation of employers. A total of thirty-three items, each measured through a seven-point Likert-type scale, were included in the instrument after pilot testing. (See [16] for the complete set of items.) Some items were adopted from standard instruments (e.g., variety and autonomy from Hackman and Oldham's [22] Job Diagnostic Survey), while others were developed for this instrument. The items were designed to elicit descriptions, not affective reactions. An example of an item follows:

To what extent does your supervisor establish and maintain a warm and friendly relationship with you?

My supervisor discourages social interactions and is somewhat cool and aloof.

My supervisor is friendly when approached but neither encourages nor discourages interactions.

My supervisor encourages friendly interactions and is warm and approachable.

Before the instrument was used it was pilot-tested in conjunction with a number of other questions, including demographic data on respondents. The first test utilized two groups of full-time employees taking evening M.B.A. classes, the second test utilized a group of clerical workers, and the third test utilized a group of technical workers. After each test, refinements were made. The final thirty-three items, in conjunction with fifty-one other items measuring demographics and other variables, could be completed within the forty-five-minute time constraint.

## Validation

The description of the conceptual domain presented earlier allows other researchers to make more informed judgments about content validity. The description below of reliability and factor analysis supports the assessment of construct validity, particularly convergence and discriminability of the items used. Furthermore, the reported relationship of the construct to another construct (i.e., occupation) described below contributes to construct validity. Ferratt and Short [15] used the responses of 503 individuals randomly selected from seven independent samples of individuals employed in clerical-operations, technical-professional, and managerial occupations in the insurance industry to validate the instrument as a measure of the work-unit environment. These individuals included those working in information systems and noninformation systems jobs.

Using iterative factor analysis and scale reliability analysis to identify scales having items that showed convergence within and discriminability across scales, Ferratt and Short [15] derived three factors based on twenty of the thirty-three original items. (See [16] for a list of the twenty items.) Factor analysis with oblique rotation was used to allow for nonindependent dimensions as suggested by the literature review. Table 2 shows the final factor pattern matrix. The three factors represent managerial behaviors related to the person-oriented dimension of leadership (see factor 1), job design (see factor 2), and the work-oriented dimension of leadership (see factor 3).

As reported in Ferratt and Short [15], the Cronbach alpha coefficients of reliability with this sample for the items comprising the three dimensions referred to in Table 2 as (1) attending to interpersonal relations, (2) designing the job, and (3) attending to production, respectively, are 0.89, 0.78, and 0.77. These coefficients of reliability are adequate, as suggested by Nunnally [40, p. 245]. Furthermore, in an analysis of construct validity they report that a multivariate analysis of these three scales yields significant differences beyond the 0.000 level across the three occupations—clerical-operations, technical-professional, and managerial—noted in this sample.

Finally, they present tables showing that the factor, scale reliability, and construct validity analyses are substantively replicated with the remaining 502 individuals randomly held out from their total set of seven independent samples. They also use the holdout sample to test various hypotheses in which the work-unit environment is an independent or dependent variable.

Although the factor analysis with the holdout sample shows that all twenty items have factor loadings greater than 0.30 on the same three factors as in the originally selected sample, three items also have loadings with absolute values above 0.30 on other factors. These stray loadings are less than or similar to the loadings on the original three factors. In particular, job specificity has a loading of negative 0.310 on the work-oriented dimension compared with 0.492 on the job design dimension, upward communication has a loading of 0.304 on the job design dimension compared with 0.719 on the person-oriented dimension, and setting performance expectations has a loading of 0.501 on the person-oriented dimension compared with 0.491 on the work-oriented dimension. While these stray loadings do not substantially affect the validity of the instrument since the highest loading items on each factor remain the same, others wishing to use the instrument may find these data valuable.

The validation procedures indicate the instrument is measuring three nonorthogonal dimensions that operationally define the work-unit environment. The instrument does not have separate scales measuring each of the original set of managerial behaviors shown in Table 1 or each of the six managerial behaviors used in the original conceptualization of the work-unit environment shown in figure 1. Instead, the scale analysis procedures identify only three nonorthogonal dimensions that include items from the various interrelated managerial behaviors.

These dimensions are important for understanding the meaning of “differences in managing IS professionals.” As noted above, “differences in management” means differences in job design for Couger and Zawacki [11]. The results of instrument validation suggest that for Ferratt and Short [15] “differences in management” means differences in the work-unit environment. Differences in the work-unit environment, in turn, are defined by different scores on these three related dimensions of managerial behavior: (1) attending to interpersonal relations, (2) designing the job, and (3) attending to production.

## Scoring

Alternative methods exist for using this instrument to derive a score for the work-unit environment. Ferratt and Short [15] used two methods, which are reviewed here, to derive a work-unit environment score from the perspective of an individual employee. Methods could also be developed to derive a score for the work-unit environment at the work group, rather than individual employee, level.

## Individual Level: Mean Response on Each Dimension

An individual employee's work-unit environment is operationally defined by the mean of an individual's responses to the seven-point items comprising each of the three scales identified above. This method of scoring is straightforward. As a result of this scoring method, an individual employee's work-unit environment score may range from a low of 1.0 on each of the three scales to a high of 7.0 on each of the three scales and an infinite variety of scores in between. Ferratt and Short [15] used this method of scoring, for example, to conduct a linear test of the hypothesis that the work-unit environments differ for IS and non-IS employees.

## Individual Level: Classification Algorithm

A second method of scoring builds on the method just described. Instead of the infinite variety of work-unit environments, researchers or practitioners may be interested in identifying a small number of types of work-unit environments. Two obvious types are those that are relatively low on all three scales and those that are relatively high on all three. Ferratt and Short [15] used the three scales as clustering variables in cluster analysis to identify a parsimonious number of types and identified two mid-range types of work-unit environments in addition to the relatively low and high types. Based on the cluster analysis results, they developed scoring algorithms for classifying an individual employee's work-unit environment into one of the four types. (See [15], Table 11, p. 440.)

The scoring algorithm one uses should classify each individual into one and only one type of work-unit environment, that is, it should provide a mutually exclusive and exhaustive classification of individuals. A classification algorithm similar to those used by Ferratt and Short is presented below. The algorithm classifies each individual employee's perception of the work-unit environment into one of the four types of work-unit environments obtained through cluster analysis: relatively low, relatively high, mid-range with attending to production relatively high, and mid-range with attending to production relatively low. Each type of work-unit environment is given a name below for easier reference. The steps in the algorithm are:

1. Obtain the mean response of an individual respondent to the items for each of the three dimensions;

2. Obtain the mean of the means on the three dimensions;

3. If the mean of the means is less than 3.9, the work-unit environment is relatively low, that is, Underdeveloped;

4. If the mean of the means is greater than 5.2, the work-unit environment is relatively high, that is, Highly Developed;

5. If the mean of the means is between 3.9 and 5.2 inclusive, the work-unit environment is mid-range and:

a. if the means for (1) designing the job and (2) attending to interpersonal relations are both greater than or equal to the mean for (3) attending to production, the work-unit environment is mid-range with attending to production relatively low, that is, Moderate with Enhancements;

b. otherwise, the work-unit environment is mid-range with attending to production relatively high, that is, Moderate with Task Orientation.

The justification of a specific classification algorithm could be based on the conceptual identification of different types of work-unit environments. It could be based on the empirical derivation of different types as in the Ferratt and Short [15] study. Or it could be based on a combination of the two.

Scoring the work-unit environment with a classification algorithm results in a nominal variable, where the individual's score on this variable represents one of the four types of work-unit environment: Underdeveloped, Moderate with Task Orientation, Moderate with Enhancements, and Highly Developed. Since it involves the use of a classification algorithm, this second method of scoring the work-unit environment at the individual employee level is more complex than the first. One's goal in analysis may suggest which of these individual scoring methods to use, or it may suggest using a work-group-level scoring method.

## Work-Group Level

Researchers or practitioners may desire to derive a score for the work-unit environment at the level of the work group, rather than of the individual employee. Two scoring approaches are described here. The first scoring method is straightforward and builds on the first individual-level scoring procedure. Specifically, a mean score on each of the three dimensions of the work-unit environment is obtained for the work group by averaging the corresponding scale scores for all individuals in the work group. The second scoring method builds on the more complex individual-level classification method. Once the type of work-unit environment is identified for each individual employee, the work-unit environment at the work-group level is defined by the profile of types, that is, by the percentage of each type of work-unit environment as perceived by the set of individual employees in the work group. A need to analyze the work-unit environment at the work-group level led to the second stage of instrument development described next.

## Second Stage: Development of the Demonstrated Skill at Supervising Instrument

FERRATT AND SHORT [15] FOUND THAT TWO OF THE FOUR work-unit environments identified in their study were associated with higher-producing employees. They measured productivity through a five-item instrument completed by the employees' supervisor. Causality could not be inferred from their study, though. For example, it was not possible to infer whether differences in management as reflected in the different types of work-unit environment led to higher productivity or higher-producing employees led to the differences in management. One way to test the direction of causality would be to induce differences in management experimentally, that is, to induce different types of work-unit environments and observe the results. Such an experiment requires an experimental procedure that will produce differences in management.

Ferratt and Agarwal [14] developed a guided self-development program as a vehicle for inducing differences in management, particularly at the work-group level. Specifically, the program is directed at improving the management of first-line supervisors. To measure improvement in management Ferratt and Agarwal [14] developed an instrument and scoring procedure for demonstrated skill at supervising. Their work builds on the first stage of instrument development described above. The instrument consists of a reduced set of ten items for the three dimensions of the work-unit environment rather than twenty items, and the scoring procedure builds on the second scoring method for the work-group level described above. A fuller explanation follows.

## The Conceptual Domain

Demonstrated skill at supervising refers to the desirability of observed managerial behaviors of an individual responsible for supervising the work of others. The concept builds on the managerial behaviors defining the work-unit environment. Thus, the same set of managerial behaviors reviewed earlier (see Table 1) for the development of the work-unit environment forms the conceptual domain for this instrument. The major difference between measuring the two concepts—work-unit environment and demonstrated skill at supervising—is that the scored measure for the former yields a description of managerial behavior while that for the latter builds on the description through an evaluative scoring procedure to yield an evaluation of the described behavior.

Given the observed behavior as measured by the work-unit environment instrument, the major issue in developing the instrument concerned how to score it to yield an evaluation. Another issue concerned the length of the instrument. Both issues are discussed below.

## Constraints

The instrument was designed to be administered quickly and easily in a field setting. Thus, the number of items in the instrument had to fit on two sides of a single sheet of paper to facilitate administration; in addition, each of the three dimensions had to have at least three items to represent the breadth of the domain. Ten items were selected from the twenty used to measure the work-unit environment. The two criteria used to select items were high factor loading (see $[15]$ and Table 2) and item content that provides breadth of domain representation. (See $[16]$ for the specific items in relation to the twenty.) The ten-item instrument is included in the appendix.

## Validation

Cronbach alpha coefficients of reliability for these three scales were calculated (0.83, 0.76, and 0.75, respectively, for [1] attending to interpersonal relations, [2] designing the job, and [3] attending to production) for 105 complete responses from an independently collected sample of 114 information systems employees in technical-professional positions. Using this same sample of data, the factor pattern matrix resulting from principle components factor analysis with oblique rotation shows the three expected factors emerging clearly with high factor loadings (see Table 3). Only one stray factor loading greater than 0.30 occurred, viz., emphasis on quality also had a loading of 0.57 on the attending to interpersonal relations factor. While these results substantially validate these scales as reliable measures of the three related dimensions of managerial behavior that operationalize the work-unit environment, future research could explore questions raised by the stray loading.

In the second stage this descriptive profile is converted to an evaluation, that is, it is converted to demonstrated skill at supervising.

Converting individual employee descriptions of managerial behaviors into evaluations requires a sound theoretical or empirical basis for the conversion algorithm. Several alternative scoring algorithms are presented below. These algorithms are based on theoretical and empirical work presented above. The reason for alternative scoring algorithms is that the assumptions about the desirability of specific types of described behaviors (i.e., types of work-unit environments) within any one of the scoring algorithms have yet to be found more valid than the assumptions within another.

A starting point for describing the alternative scoring algorithms is found in Ferratt and Short [15]. Although they found no significant differences in the relationship between managerial behavior and productivity for IS and non-IS people at the same occupational level, they did find four different patterns of managing that were differentially related to productivity. These patterns correspond to the four different types of work-unit environments reflected in the classification algorithm described in the first stage of instrument development.

One pattern is relatively high on all three managerial behaviors empirically defining the work-unit environment. Employees who perceived their supervisors as exhibiting this pattern of managerial behavior had relatively high productivity (as perceived by their supervisors). Another pattern is relatively low on the three managerial behaviors and relatively low in productivity (i.e., the employees' supervisor perceived their productivity to be low). These two patterns of managing are consistent with many other studies of the two leadership dimensions $[7, 44]$ and the job design literature $[17, 23]$ . Bass $[7]$ notes that being high on both leadership dimensions (person-oriented and work-oriented) is better than being low on both dimensions. The job design literature suggests that jobs that are more enriched, or complex, have greater motivating potential and therefore lead to higher job performance. Thus, based on prior research and some empirical evidence, it appears that a pattern of management that is relatively high on all three dimensions is a good method of managing, while a pattern that is relatively low on all three dimensions is a weak method of managing.

The other two patterns are in the mid-range of the three managerial behaviors. One is relatively low on the work-oriented dimension compared with the other two dimensions and is relatively high in productivity in the Ferratt and Short [15] study. The other is relatively high on the work-oriented dimension and is relatively low on productivity in that same study. The limited, nonexperimental evidence regarding the desirability of these mid-range patterns suggests the need for alternative scoring algorithms since the effectiveness of the mid-range methods of managing is not well supported.

In general, a scoring algorithm should yield an index representing the desirability of the participant's method of supervising, that is, demonstrated skill at supervising. The following guideline applies to any conversion algorithm: descriptions of managerial behaviors that represent more effective methods of managing should be converted to higher scores, while descriptions of less effective methods should be converted to lower scores. Thus, lower index scores should indicate lower supervisory skill and higher scores should indicate higher skills.

The two extreme methods from the Ferratt and Short [15] study—low on the three dimensions and high on the three dimensions—have research support from other literature cited above for assigning low and high index scores, respectively, to these methods. Given the lack of clear research support regarding the effectiveness of the two mid-range methods, neither a high nor a low score may be clearly assigned to either of them. However, one could make certain clearly stated assumptions about the effectiveness of each of the mid-range methods. For example, one could assume that Moderate with Task Orientation is less effective and assign a low score; or one could assume that it is moderately effective and assign a moderate score; and one could assume that Moderate with Enhancements is more effective and assign a high score, or one could assume that it is moderately effective and assign a moderate score. Thus, a given set of assumptions defines a specific set of scores to assign, and hence, a specific index of demonstrated skill at supervising. Several sets of assumptions are recommended here for measuring demonstrated skill at supervising; these sets of assumptions cover a reasonable range of possibilities for the effectiveness of the mid-range methods of managing. A detailed description of the algorithms and assumptions follows.

## Group-Level Profile: Starting Point for All Algorithms

To begin each of the scoring algorithms, each employee response is classified as indicative of one of four methods of managing by using the classification algorithm presented above in the description of scoring for the work-unit environment. The four methods are (1) Underdeveloped (called Low in [15]), (2) Moderate with Task Orientation (called Mid-range 1), (3) Moderate with Enhancements (called Mid-range 2), and (4) Highly Developed (called High). A group-level profile of each supervisor's behavior is developed by calculating the percentage of the supervisor's employees whose responses are classified into each of the four types of work-unit environments. This descriptive profile is then used with the alternative evaluative scoring algorithms to yield alternate indices of demonstrated skill at supervising.

## Common Assumptions

In all algorithms the assumption is made that the Underdeveloped category is not an effective method of supervising, indicative of low demonstrated skill at supervising, and that the Highly Developed category is a highly effective method of supervising, indicative of high demonstrated skill at supervising. Therefore, these categories are assigned the lowest and highest scores of 50 and 100, respectively. As stated earlier, the differences in the indices are based on different assumptions about the effectiveness, that is, skill, indicated by the mid-range categories, as noted below.

## Moderate Index

This measure is based on the conservative assumption that the mid-range categories (Moderate with Task Orientation and Moderate with Enhancements) are moderately effective methods of supervising, indicative of satisfactory skill at supervising for scores of 80 and 80, respectively. A score of 80 (rather than 75, midway between the lowest and highest score) is used for moderately effective methods to suggest that moderately effective methods are modestly closer to the highly effective methods than to the least effective methods.

## Moderate-High Index

This measure is based on the mixed conservative-liberal assumption that one of the mid-range categories, Moderate with Task Orientation, is not necessarily low in productivity as reported in Ferratt and Short [15], but that the other mid-range category, Moderate with Enhancements, is related to productivity as reported in the same study, that is, it is high in productivity. Thus, the Moderate with Task Orientation category is assigned a score of 80 (for satisfactory demonstrated skill at supervising), while Moderate with Enhancements is assigned a score of 100 (for high demonstrated skill at supervising).

## Low-Moderate Index

This measure is based on the mixed liberal-conservative assumption that one of the mid-range categories, Moderate with Task Orientation, is related to productivity as reported in Ferratt and Short [15], that is, it is low in productivity, while Moderate with Enhancements is not necessarily high in productivity. Thus, the Moderate with Task Orientation category is assigned a score of 50 (for low demonstrated skill at supervising), while Moderate with Enhancements is assigned a score of 80 (for satisfactory demonstrated skill at supervising).

## Low-High Index

This measure is based on the liberal assumption that the mid-range categories are related to productivity and, therefore, demonstrated skill at supervising, as reported in Ferratt and Short [15], that is, Moderate with Task Orientation is low in productivity while Moderate with Enhancements is high in productivity, for scores of 50 and 100, respectively.

## Examples

If 100 percent of employees for a given supervisor perceive the supervisor as exhibiting the highly developed method, all indices would have a value of 100, the highest possible score. If 100 percent perceive the underdeveloped method, the indices would have a value of 50, the lowest possible score. If 50 percent perceive the highly developed method and 50 percent perceive the underdeveloped method, all indices would have a value of 75, indicating an average demonstrated skill at supervising. The indices—Moderate, Moderate-High, Low-Moderate, and Low-High—vary based on the scores used for the mid-range methods.

If the responses of the ten employees of a specific supervisor generate a profile with 10 percent in the Underdeveloped category, 40 percent in the Moderate with Task Orientation category, 30 percent in the Moderate with Enhancements category, and 20 percent in the Highly Developed category, the alternate indices would yield demonstrated skill at supervising scores for that supervisor as indicated in Table 4.

In summary, building any of the indices for a specific supervisor first requires the identification of a specific method of managing as perceived by each subordinate. This initial step underscores the importance of assessing the method of managing found in each superior-subordinate relationship. Acceptable instrument validity and reliability for this critical initial descriptive step have been demonstrated. The next step in building an index for a specific supervisor is to assign an evaluative score for the method of managing perceived by each subordinate based on the assumed goodness of that method. The final step is to calculate the average goodness across subordinates for a specific supervisor, yielding an index between 50 and 100. ## Using the Scoring Algorithms

One use of the scoring algorithms is to obtain pre- and post-indices of demonstrated skill at supervising when supervisors participate in any type of management development activity. Differences in indices from the beginning and end of the activity would be indicative of changes attributable to the activity. An example of such a use of these scoring algorithms is found in Ferratt and Agarwal [14]. They present an exploratory study of the effects of a guided self-development program based on six cases. One of the effects examined is the change in demonstrated skill at supervising. They used the ten-item instrument and scoring algorithms above to measure demonstrated skill at supervising at the beginning of the study, at the end of the thirteen-week self-development program, and at an approximately one-year followup. They found that not all, or even the majority, of the participants improved their demonstrated skill at supervising even though other indicators suggest that the program is sound and implemented well. An explanation consistent with the data is that the participants with lower scores on demonstrated skill at supervising at the beginning of a management education and development program are the ones more likely to show measurable increases in their scores as a result of participating in the program.

When using the indices to compare demonstrated skill at supervising before and after a management development program, all the indices for a given supervisor should show an increase to clearly indicate that the program was effective in improving demonstrated skill at supervising. If the group of indices do not all change in the same direction, the conclusion about the effectiveness of the program is not as obvious. One should then examine changes in the descriptive profile when ambiguous results occur with the indices; however, given that research does not give a complete answer to the question of what methods of managing are more effective, an understanding of the effects of a management education and development program with ambiguous results will be derived from a description of the outcomes rather than from an evaluation of them.

A second use of the scoring algorithms is to perform a cross-sectional comparison of a given group of supervisors. If all the indices show the same relative order for all supervisors (or any subset), the indices provide a clear indication of the supervisors' comparative demonstrated skill at supervising. A similar caveat as indicated above applies to this use of the scoring algorithms, that is, for a reasonable interpretation of the data, differences in demonstrated skill at supervising from one supervisor to another should be consistent across all scoring algorithms.

A final use of the scoring algorithms is to clearly subscribe to a specific set of assumptions about the effectiveness of the two mid-range methods of managing in a particular organizational context and culture. For example, one could assume that Moderate with Task Orientation is not necessarily low in productivity and Moderate with Enhancements is high in productivity (the Moderate-High index). The index yielded by the scoring algorithm could then be used to continuously monitor demonstrated skill at supervising for a given supervisor. The lack of research support for any one set of assumptions makes this use risky.

## Conclusion

## Summary

RESEARCHERS AND PRACTITIONERS NEED AN EFFECTIVE INSTRUMENT to measure differences in the management of information systems professionals. This paper reports the results of a two-stage effort to develop, validate, and score an instrument for measuring differences in management. In the first stage an instrument and scoring procedure for measuring the work-unit environment were developed and shown to have acceptable reliability and validity. In the second stage the work-unit environment instrument and scoring procedure were adapted to yield a ten-item survey instrument for measuring differences in demonstrated skill at supervising. Based on different assumptions about the desirability of mid-range work-unit environments, four alternative scoring algorithms are used with responses to the ten-item survey to yield four alternate indices of demonstrated skill at supervising. Although factor and reliability analyses support the reliability and validity of the instrument, the scoring algorithms require further research.

## Implications for Practice

The instruments described in this paper have practical relevance for those interested in understanding and making differences in the management of information systems professionals. One possible use is to describe the work-unit environment of information systems supervisors as perceived by those they supervise. Although the ten-item instrument was developed for evaluating behavior, it has acceptable reliability and validity for use as a shorter alternative to the twenty-item instrument in developing a profile that describes supervisory behavior. Such descriptions would provide insights into the types of managerial behaviors exhibited by information systems managers and may be useful in assessing the need for management education and development. The instrument could even potentially be used by human resource specialists to assess whether managers of information systems professionals have a greater need for management education and development than other managers in general or managers in other technical areas.

The ten-item instrument with the various scoring procedures would also be useful in conjunction with a management education and development program for managers of information systems professionals. It could provide before and after measures of demonstrated skill at supervising. It could also be used to provide feedback to supervisors during their management education and development program to help them understand their own behavior vis-à-vis those they supervise and suggest areas where education and development would be helpful. Care should be taken to use and explain all four scoring procedures. The explanation could even provide an educational benefit.

## Directions for Future Research

Although considerable, promising work on measuring the work-unit environment and demonstrated skill at supervising has been completed, additional work is needed. Two areas in which this work could focus are suggested here. The first area, examining the scoring algorithms for demonstrated skill at supervising, is the most critical. The second, examining the relationship of the work-unit environment and demonstrated skill at supervising to other constructs, is also important.

## Examining Scoring Algorithms for Demonstrated Skill at Supervising

All four scoring algorithms for demonstrated skill at supervising assign low scores for Underdeveloped work-unit environments and high scores for Highly Developed work-unit environments. These scores are consistent with prior research. The assumptions about the mid-range patterns, however, need further research to identify which assigned scores—low, moderate, or high—represent the desirability, that is, skill level, associated with a specific mid-range work-unit environment.

Beyond this critical research is further refinement of the scoring algorithms. A useful addition to each scoring algorithm could be an indicator of the variation in the work-unit environments comprising the profile for a given work group. Each scoring algorithm uses each employee's responses to categorize individual perceptions as descriptive of a specific type of work-unit environment and then derives an overall index of supervisory behavior based on the mix of types for all individuals in a work group. In addition to the overall index, an indicator of the spread of types for a given work group could also be generated.

## Examining Relationship to Other Constructs

This instrument has been used in a limited number of studies to investigate (a) the existence of differences in the management of information systems professionals, (b) the effect of differences in the management of information systems professionals, and (c) the results of attempts to change the management of information systems professionals. Additional research on the relationship of the work-unit environment and demonstrated skill at supervising to other constructs would add to the limited prior assessment of construct validity. Such research need not necessarily be limited to samples of information systems professionals.

The measures of the work-unit environment and demonstrated skill at supervising permit the examination of leadership and job design dimensions jointly. Although numerous studies have examined the two leadership dimensions and others have focused on job design, the combined study of these dimensions is rare. Furthermore, the examination of leader behavior has focused on extreme combinations of two leader behavior dimensions, such as high or low on both attention to production and attention to interpersonal relations dimensions or high on one and low on the other. The measures presented here allow examination of both extreme and mid-range combinations of the work-unit environment dimensions as perceived by a given subordinate as well as a mix of types of work-unit environments as perceived by a set of subordinates. Thus, conducting research that examines the relationship of the work-unit environment and demonstrated skill at supervising to other constructs should provide an impetus for significant contributions to the literature.
