# The Use of Mental Imagery to Facilitate Information Identification in Requirements Analysis

ROBERT W. ZMUD, WILLIAM P. ANTHONY, AND RALPH M. STAIR, JR.

ROBERT W. ZMUD is Professor and Thomas L. Williams, Jr., Eminent Scholar in Management Information Systems in the Information and Management Sciences Department at the College of Business, Florida State University. His current research interests focus on the impact of information technology in facilitating a variety of organizational behaviors and on organizational efforts involved with planning, managing, and diffusing information technology. Dr. Zmud currently is an editor with two journals, Organization Science and Communications of the ACM, and serves on the boards of three others. Both his Ph.D. (University of Arizona) and his S.M. (MIT) degrees are in management.

WILLIAM P. ANTHONY, Ph.D. (Ohio State University), is Professor of Management at the Florida State University. His primary research interests include the study of cognition in strategy formulation of top management teams. Current research involves the use of visualization and imagery as an information and sense-making process. Dr. Anthony also is involved in strategic processes related to human resources. Finally, he has also published in the area of stress and stress management.

RALPH M. STAIR, JR., is Professor of Information and Management Sciences in the College of Business, Florida State University. His current research is in the areas of technology management, systems analysis and design, and system resource planning. He has written articles and books in computer information systems, programming, and quantitative analysis. His degrees are from the University of Oregon (Ph.D), Tulane University (M.B.A.), and Purdue University (B.S. Ch.E.).

ABSTRACT: Many alternative approaches have been devised for eliciting information systems requirements. The majority of these approaches, however, assume a familiarity with and understanding of the organizational context by those individuals from whom requirements are to be derived. Such approaches often yield unsatisfactory results when the information system being developed involves ill-structured task contexts. The generation of a robust set of information items can be an important first step in the specification of information requirements. Scenario-based approaches to generate information items for requirements analysis appear intuitively promising as a mechanism for promoting the elicitation of information requirements for ill-structured task contexts. However, little empirical evidence exists regarding their potential effectiveness. This paper proposes the use of mental imagery as a scenario-based technique and reports on the results of an exploratory laboratory experiment that compared a mental imagery approach with goal-oriented and critical success factors approaches in the generation of information items. The results of this experiment provide modest evidence that positive and negative mental imagery protocols may both outperform goal-oriented and critical success factors approaches for ill-structured task contexts. Further, the strongest results were observed to favor the positive imagery protocol. The paper concludes with a discussion of the implications of these findings and suggestions for future research.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: critical success factors, mental imagery, requirements analysis.

## 1. Introduction

REQUIREMENTS ANALYSIS CONTINUES TO BE THE MOST CRITICAL and most difficult activity normally undertaken in information system development [38]. If a requirements specification is ambiguous, inconsistent, incomplete, or otherwise incorrect, significant development difficulties can arise. When the organizational context to be supported with an information system is likely to involve future, new, or radically altered activities, requirements analysis can be particularly troublesome. It often proves to be quite difficult for an organization's members to conceptualize and then describe their information needs in the absence of real experience with the target tasks or activities.

As stated by Davis [11, p. 7], identifying the (potential) elements to be included within the information system under development is the first of five general requirements associated with the design of the information system's data structure:

1. Identification of things and events included within the scope of the object system.

2. Relationships among things and events.

3. Attributes of things and events.

4. Validation criteria for data items.

5. Characteristics of use of data structures.

This first step is especially important for ill-structured environments. While many alternative approaches have been devised for eliciting information systems requirements, the majority assume a familiarity with and understanding of the organizational context by those individuals from whom requirements are to be derived $[14, 35]$ . As a result, these approaches often yield unsatisfactory results when the information system being developed involves future, new, or radically altered contexts.

This problem is stated best by Mittermeir, Hsia, and Yeh [25, p. 154]:

[T]here are some even deeper problems in communication among users, buyers (decision makers) and analysts than just lack of a common notation or language. These problems are due to the inability of humans to see beyond their current frame of reference and, in general, lack of foresight. To adjust the frame of reference of users during requirements analysis, when they already focus on a particular technical solution and the analysis is down at the level of data, processes, quantities and real-time constraints, is difficult, if not impossible. Both users and analysts are very soon involved in too much technical detail to recognize that they are traveling very well on a good road, but the road may lead in the wrong direction.

Mittermeir et al. then argue that abstraction and prototyping provide the two main possibilities for resolving such problems, and propose what they refer to as the “scenario technique” as an approach that combines properties of both abstraction and prototyping [25]. Here, the analyst provides users with projected views of the future system. With such scenarios, users are restimulated to think in terms of their future working environment and the problems and opportunities that may arise.

While scenario techniques appear intuitively promising, little empirical evidence exists regarding their effectiveness in enabling users to understand the information environments of ill-structured task contexts. The objective of this paper is twofold. First, mental imagery is introduced as a scenario-based technique for information generation in the requirements analysis process. Second, a laboratory experiment is described that compares subjects' capabilities to enumerate an ill-structured information context via three techniques: mental imagery; a top-down, goal-oriented approach; and critical success factors.

## 2. Mental Imagery

MENTAL IMAGERY IS A MECHANISM FOR ENRICHING AN INDIVIDUAL'S mental models of some context or situation. It is a cognitive process that involves the visual, auditory, tactile, or kinesthetic sensations one “pictures in the mind” [26]. It is often referred to as “seeing with the mind’s” eye, although the process can and often does engage other senses besides sight. Consider the following example, which one of the authors has used in business strategic planning workshops for scenario building with top management teams:

It is exactly five years from today. You have been away from your job on vacation for two weeks. While you were gone, you saw no television, nor read any newspapers or magazines. You instructed your secretary to cut out articles from newspapers and magazines that dealt with the economy, your industry and your organization. You told her to put them in a folder on your desk. You are now back at work after your two week vacation. It is five years from today. You are sitting at your desk looking at the folder on top of the desk. You open the folder and look at the headlines of the first article. What do you see? Your turn that over and look at the next headline. What does this say? You look at the third headline. What does it say? ...

Each participant is then asked to recount the mental list of headlines he or she saw. Such use of imagery has been found to enable strategic planners to see alternative futures more clearly. Participants are able to relate their vision to those of other participants, and the various individual visions are discussed, debated, and integrated in an effort to find a collective vision.

Mental imagery has been successfully used as an intervention technique in many fields: to enhance sports performance via mental rehearsal of tasks [12]; in counselor training programs as a means for enabling counselors to improve their self-confidence in counseling and their ability to assess their performance accurately [17]; in education to help children understand abstract concepts such as mathematics and in language studies [35, 37]; in medicine and therapeutic situations to treat depression [20], phobias [21], sexual dysfunctions [9], and in cancer treatment [31]; in personnel management as the basis of the technique known as realistic job previews; and to enhance divergent thinking, goal setting, and scenario building [18, 28, 34].

The theoretical basis of mental imagery rests in cognitive psychology, specifically social cognition $[24]$ . In particular, social learning theory and cognitive behavioral therapy provide much of the theoretical basis for the technique $[4, 23]$ . With these processes, covert learning (through vicarious means rather than direct experience) and covert processes to mediate behavior are emphasized $[10]$ : mental rehearsal, scenario building, projective imagery (placing oneself in a future situation), problem solving, and memory exercises. For example, consider the following problem-solving exercise:

## How many windows do you have in your home or apartment?

Few of us can immediately remember the number of windows in our homes (unless we just recently washed the windows, built the house, or put up draperies). Instead, we envision ourselves walking from room to room mentally counting the windows. In other words, we use imagery as a tool to solve the problem.

Imagery rests on the concept of schemas and scripts $[1, 9]$ . A schema is a cognitive structure composed of expectations learned from experience and stored in memory $[33]$ . It is a mental template used to organize information. A script is a specialized schema for action $[16]$ . It is a cognitive structure devoted specifically to the retention of context-specific knowledge about events and event sequences and to the guidelines of action on the basis of that knowledge $[2, 15]$ . When faced with a familiar situation, we activate an appropriate script $[16]$ . When faced with a novel situation, we develop a new script or search for a related script that might apply. Imagery techniques use prepared scripts in at least two major ways. First, a “new” script can be used to place individuals in a specific imagined situation. Second, imagery can be applied to induce individuals to retrieve existing scripts, possessing specific attributes, from their long-term memories. As might be expected, the most essential ingredient to the imagery experience is a good script.

## 3. Development of Research Hypothesis

COMMONLY-APPLIED TECHNIQUES FOR THE GENERATION OF INFORMATION items in performing requirements analysis in ill-structured task contexts are the top-down, goal-oriented approach [22, 25] and the critical success factor (CSF) method [29]. Both of these goal-oriented approaches, however, are acknowledged to have weaknesses.

## 3.1. Goal-Oriented Approaches

The top-down, goal-oriented approach represents an orthodox technique to systems design when the organizational context being confronted is ill structured. Participants begin the procedure with a statement of organizational goals [6], rather than trying to unravel and make sense of the poorly understood activities reflected by the phenomenon under investigation. The key feature of such a rational, analytic approach to systems design is the use of abstraction [36]. By focusing on the essence of a phenomenon, and leaving out trivialities and suppressing details, a user is able to construct a reduced representation of the phenomenon. Once this has been achieved, attention shifts to systematically exploring (1) the decisions and activities necessary to achieve these goals and (2) the required information for successfully pursuing these decisions and activities. Smith discusses the importance of problem identification, definition, and structuring in problem formulation [32]. This includes explicit and implicit representations of the problem.

While the top-down, goal-oriented approach seems reasonable, it can be difficult to apply in practice. Arriving at a representative set of goals for a poorly understood organizational activity is a very difficult task whose difficulty increases when participants have little training or experience with rational-analytic problem-solving techniques. Munro and Davis [26] provide modest support for such a perspective through their laboratory experiment contrasting decision-driven versus data-driven requirements analysis techniques. In direct opposition to their expectations, subjects applying a data-driven approach to ill-structured requirements specification tasks outperformed those applying a decision-driven (e.g., goal-oriented) approach.

Boland [6] points out two additional weaknesses of rational-analytical approaches to systems design. First, solutions obtained from applying such approaches tend to reinforce the status quo. Here, the goals (and associated decisions, constraints, actions, etc.) identified tend to reflect an organization's current realities. Second, these approaches assume an objective, external character to the social world. While such a perspective facilitates the development of simplified representations, it does not account for subjective and intersubjective phenomena that make up much of an organization's social reality.

Rockart [30] introduced the use of CSF, those few things that must go well to ensure success for a manager or an organization, as an alternative goal-oriented technique for facilitating requirements analysis efforts. When used in requirements analysis, participants begin by identifying a set of CSFs and then focus on describing requirements for the collection and reporting of information that provides measures for these CSFs.

The primary strength of the CSF approach is that it is intuitively appealing—perhaps deceptively so—to managers while still utilizing abstraction via a top-down approach to system design $[7]$ . Thus, it overcomes a major difficulty associated with goal-oriented approaches to requirements analysis. However, the necessity for abstraction—that is, distilling the essential features of a phenomenon—as well as its focus on the current and objective—that is, measurable—facets of the organizational context suggest that problems similar to those just discussed for the traditional top-down, goal-oriented approach might arise when applying the CSF approach as a requirements analysis tool. This, in fact, was found to be the case by Boynton and Zmud $[7]$ . Such problems might be expected to intensify with novel work contexts.

## 3.2. Scenario-Based Approaches

Applying Weick's [36] notions of organization sense-making (e.g., sense-makers do not know what they have done until they have done it and can retrospectively impose a structure on their equivocal enactments), Boland [6] argues persuasively for the adoption of scenario approaches to the design of organizational systems. In a planning exercise, Boland had participants create a sense-making experience by placing themselves out into the future (via a scenario of this future) and then look backwards in time in an effort to make sense of the future. Instead of defining goals (or critical success factors) to identify necessary decisions and required information, participants raise and then question the assumptions and decisions that apparently led to this future.

## 3.3. Statement of Research Hypothesis

Considerable conceptual and procedural similarity exists between mental imagery and Boland's scenario technique. It is thus quite possible that mental imagery may provide a mechanism for facilitating information requirements analysis in novel work contexts that overcomes certain of the weaknesses of more traditional goal-oriented and CSF approaches. The following research hypothesis is thus proposed:

In ill-structured task contexts, information generation procedures for requirements analysis based on mental imagery will outperform procedures based on either a goal-oriented approach or a CSF approach.

## 4. Research Method

A LABORATORY EXPERIMENT WAS UNDERTAKEN IN THE FALL OF 1988 to test the above research hypothesis. Four treatment groups were used, each of which was led through one of four requirements analysis protocols: a top-down, goals-oriented protocol, a CSF protocol, a positive imagery protocol, and a negative imagery protocol. These protocols are shown as figures 1–4. Four research assistants were each trained in one of the protocols (each research assistant was a second- or third-year Ph.D. student in either management or information systems). Considerable effort was made to caution the collaborators about “leading” subjects and to periodically debrief the collaborators to ensure that an objective protocol was being invoked. Each subject session thus involved a collaborator leading a single subject through one of the four protocols. While such a design certainly raises the threat of an intervention effect, requiring a single collaborator to become skilled in applying all protocols was both unreasonable and might still not eliminate the threat of an intervention effect.

## 4.2. Variables

The study's independent variable was the protocol treatment. The study's dependent variable was the "robustness" of the information items generated through each of the four protocols. Here, robustness was represented by both the number of distinct information items generated by a subject and the dimensionality of this set of items.

Two additional research (doctoral student) collaborators examined the set of information items generated by each subject and indicated which of the items shown on a coding sheet were present. The coding sheet was produced by the authors from research literatures on human resource management and vocational behavior and by having two of the original four collaborators examine the sets of information items generated by subjects for items not mentioned in these literatures. $^{1}$ The dimensionality of this item space reflects a categorical structure for these items designed for us by a human resource management expert. Thus, the number of information items generated was scored by the number of distinct items marked on a coding sheet, and the dimensionality of this item space was scored by the number of categories for which one or more items had been marked. Intercoder reliability was 0.68 for number of items and 0.70 for dimensionality. The scores produced by both coders were averaged to produce the measures used as dependent variables in the analysis. Re-analysis of the data using each coder's independent scores produced the same results as reported here.

A postexperimental questionnaire was used to obtain measures of control variables believed to potentially influence a subject's capability to generate information items. These included demographic variables (work experience, job search history, age, grade point average, and information systems course work) and two personality measures, tolerance of ambiguity [8] and preference for abstract/concrete thinking (developed by the authors and given in figure 5). Work experience and job search history were used to determine the subject's knowledge of the problem space.

## 5. Discussion

THE ABOVE FINDINGS PROVIDE MODEST SUPPORT for the study's research hypothesis. Specifically:

\- A positive mental imagery protocol significantly outperformed a top-down, goal-oriented requirements analysis protocol and approached significance in outperforming a CSF protocol.

\- A negative mental imagery protocol significantly outperformed a top-down, goal-oriented protocol.

In addition, these results suggest that positive imagery, rather than negative imagery, might be a preferred approach in applying mental imagery techniques in the development of information items in requirements analysis. There are at least two possible explanations for such a recommendation. First, when individuals are faced with both high uncertainty and negative cues, such as threats, there is a natural tendency to constrain information search because of threat-rigidity effects $[34]$ . That relatively constrained information searches would result from a negative depiction of the future, as compared with a positive depiction of the future, is thus not unexpected. A second explanation has to do with the optimism of human nature, particularly with youths, which might promote cognitive dissonance $[13]$ with individuals confronted with negative views of their future. Our subjects were young adults about to enter the business world. While we did not directly measure their level of optimism, this population tends to be relatively optimistic about the future. Such individuals tend to have positive expectations and are more able to verbalize these positive expectations rather than negative ones $[27]$ . As a result, the subjects presented with negative imagery may have experienced difficulty with the negative script as it did not fit their positive view of the future.

## 6. Limitations

SEVERAL LIMITATIONS OF THIS RESEARCH DESIGN NEED TO BE ADDRESSED. First, as mentioned earlier, it is possible that observed treatment effects could be attributed to the collaborators as well as the protocols. While the internal validity of the design could be improved through a repeated measures design in which each collaborator applies each protocol, this would impose a rather severe burden on both subjects and collaborators. Second, a richer set of control variables could have been applied. Specifically, the use of instruments measuring subject optimism, for example, from a personality inventory such as the MMPI, and visualization ability, such as those instruments developed by Betts [5], Sheenan [31], and Edstrom et al. [12], would have added to the current design. Third, the use of student subjects raises obvious concerns regarding the generalizability of the results to business contexts. However, given that the task context was tightly matched to these subjects (i.e., the task reflected a current and important episode in the subjects' lives), it is believed that similar results are likely to be observed with business professionals addressing a current important business task context. It is also recognized that environmental and organizational differences may have an impact on the results. Some organizations may have a fully developed set of organizational and/or task goals, while others may not. Finally, the exploratory nature of the research and the dynamics of the requirements analysis process suggest that much further research is needed before the results can be extended to the full requirements analysis task.

## 7. Conclusion

THIS PAPER HAS DESCRIBED A LABORATORY EXPERIMENT that compared two forms of a scenario-based approach—positive and negative mental imagery—with two traditional techniques—that is, top-down, goal-oriented and CSF approaches. As hypothesized, support—though moderate—was observed to favor the scenario-based approaches. Further, positive imagery was found to outperform negative imagery. Possible explanations for such an outcome were provided. These encouraging results lend weight to prior suggestions regarding the potential usefulness of scenario-based techniques in general—and mental imagery in particular—in eliciting information items from users facing ill-structured task contexts.

While the research was exploratory in nature and only represents one aspect of the systems development life cycle, the results are promising. Developing a robust set of information items for requirements analysis is an important first step for most information systems development projects. Using a positive scenario-based approach can be superior to traditional approaches in this regard.

Future research should move in four directions. First, given the moderate support that was observed in this experiment and the fact that the experimental context was somewhat removed from that commonly experienced in information system development efforts, research that replicates the current study is needed. Second, related research is needed that identifies other scenario-based techniques and contrasts their effectiveness with mental imagery as well as with more traditional techniques. For example, a scenario projection approach, where subjects are asked to “project” negative imagery to a friend or colleague, could make negative imagery more useful. Third, researchers should identify ways in which scenario-based techniques might be embedded within existing systems development methodologies. We hope the results described here motivate such efforts. Fourth, research is needed that investigates a methodology that combines the scenario-based approach and a goal-oriented methodology into a unified technique. Such a technique could result in superior performance in generating information items compared with using the scenario-based approach or the goal-oriented methodology alone.
