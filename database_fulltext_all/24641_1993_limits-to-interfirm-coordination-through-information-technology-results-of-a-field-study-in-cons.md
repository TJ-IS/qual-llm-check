ABSTRACT: Researchers have suggested that information technology (IT) can reduce coordination costs, leading to increased coordination and cooperation among buyers and suppliers in an industry. However, improved coordination through IT, and the economic benefits from that coordination, may not be fully realized in practice; this conclusion is suggested by a field study in the consumer packaged goods industry investigating the impact of IT on interactions between manufacturers and retailers. New coordination mechanisms are emerging, driven by checkout scanner systems and IT, that permit more tightly coupled logistics operations in the distribution channel. The potential benefits of this increased coordination, through reduction in inventory and more stable manufacturing, are dramatic. However, we have observed considerable resistance by retailers to these innovations. Analysis of our field study results suggests that this resistance is due to the impact of the new coordination mechanisms on bargaining power; retailers perceive that their bargaining power will be eroded under the new coordination structure, and fear that this will preclude their sharing in the economic benefits.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: bargaining power in marketplace, intercorporate coordination, interorganizational information systems.

## 1. Introduction

A FUNDAMENTAL CHANGE APPEARS TO BE OCCURRING in how organizations interact with each other. It is difficult to pick up an academic or popular business press journal without some article on the strategic and economic potential of increased inter-organizational coordination and cooperation. Just-in-time inventory techniques with key suppliers or customers are reducing channel inventories and improving system responsiveness. Strategic partnerships are reducing design cycle times, facilitating “total quality management,” and helping companies “compete in time” [e.g., 17]. This interest in interfirm coordination is also reflected in research investigating the strategic impacts of information technology. A growing body of literature suggests that IT can have dramatic effects on the way relationships between firms are managed [e.g., 2, 7, 21].

This paper presents the results of a field study investigating the impact of IT on interorganizational relationships. We explore new approaches to coordinating the flow of products within the distribution channel in the U.S. consumer packaged goods industry. We find that IT can have a significant impact on the efficiency of product flow in the distribution channel. Technologies, such as EDI and checkout scanners, along with the emergence of industrywide data and communications standards, such as Universal Product Codes (UPC) and Universal Communications Standards (UCS), support new approaches to managing product flows based on the ideas of just-in-time (JIT) or quick-response logistics management.

However, despite nearly universal agreement on the net economic benefits to the channel as a whole, we find that the necessary technological and organizational changes to improve coordination of product flow are not being widely or rapidly adopted. New coordination approaches are being met with distrust, ambivalence, and, in some cases, open resistance by many players. The ultimate outcomes of these changes are not yet known; however, it appears that for some segments of the channel the potential benefits of cooperation, coordination, and rationalization achievable through IT may not actually be attained.

Analysis of our field study data suggests that this surprising result is due, in part, to the impact of increased coordination on bargaining power. Improving coordination within the distribution channel requires investment in IT, but it also requires significant changes in the organizational mechanisms used to manage the interactions, which we term coordination structure. Conceptually, improved coordination must be viewed not just in terms of players changing their strategies within an existing game, but in terms of changing the structure of the game. Changes in coordination structure can improve overall channel efficiency, but can also affect the bargaining power of the parties. In some cases, one party can find him or herself actually worse off under improved coordination, due to a loss of bargaining power, thus leading him to resist the change; this can be true even if coordination yields a net economic surplus to the channel.

The next section discusses the research questions addressed in this study in the context of previous research in strategic information systems and economics. Section 3 discusses the theory-building case study methodology followed in this research. Section 4 presents the current coordination structure guiding interactions between manufacturers and retailers for promotions and logistics in the consumer packaged goods industry; this section also explores the key structural factors that create conflict and limit coordination, and thereby introduce large inefficiencies into the system. Section 5 explores how IT can improve coordination by supporting changes in coordination structure. We describe new approaches to coordination, enabled by IT, that were identified during the field study and explore the sources and magnitudes of potential benefits. Section 6 presents field study results that indicate that, despite widespread belief in the positive benefits from new coordination structures, there is less than universal enthusiasm for these changes. These results can be understood in terms of how the new coordination structure affects bargaining power. Section 7 summarizes the key findings and suggests directions for future work.

## 2. Research Questions

THIS RESEARCH INVESTIGATES THE IMPACT OF IT on interorganizational interactions. The questions addressed can be summarized as follows:

\- How can IT support changes in coordination structure?

\- How do changes in coordination structure enabled by IT affect channel efficiency?

\- How do changes in coordination structure enabled by IT affect bargaining power?

\- How does the division of surplus influence the evolution of interfirm coordination?

## 2.1. Coordination Structure and Surplus

A proposition that underlies much of the work in the area of strategic information systems is that IT reduces the cost of coordination, leading firms to coordinate more. This idea is extremely attractive intuitively: as Malone and Rockart point out [22], basic economics tells us that if the cost of a factor of production decreases, more will be used, often substituting for other resources. Dramatic efficiency improvements from interorganizational systems have been reported anecdotally [e.g., 5] and in more rigorous case study research [e.g., 6, 28]. Clearly, interorganizational systems, such as McKesson Drug's Economost [6] and American Hospital Supply's ASAP [28], create value by restructuring the way firms interact.

Some work has sought to clarify the link between IT and coordination between firms, most frequently relying on the concept of information flows. Information sharing underlies Barrett and Konsynski's framework of value creation from interorganizational information systems [4] as well as Malone et al.'s electronic hierarchies [21]. Bakos [1, 2] has formalized some aspects of coordination in his work on information links between firms. He has shown through formal modeling that faster transmission of demand information can lead to reduction of inventories in vertical (channel) relationships.

Implicit in this work is the idea that problems in coordination stem from uncertainty due to insufficient information or information processing capacity. According to this view, coordination is improved by increasing the amount or timeliness of information transmitted across firm boundaries; sharing information is linked with, perhaps even equivalent to, coordination. Drawing on Galbraith's [13] work on organizational structure, we refer to this perspective as the information processing view of coordination.

But there is another aspect of coordination that is suggested by agency theory $[25]$ and game theory $[12]$ , which can be called an objective differences view of coordination. Independent firms are likely to maximize their own objectives at the expense of the other party. In some situations, individual profit maximization leads to suboptimal results for the system when viewed as a whole.

One important implication of the objective differences view of coordination is that aspects of the relationship other than information flows can be critical to coordination. For example, Jeuland and Shugan [16] have shown through game theoretic modeling, that pricing structure can have significant impact on coordination between independent channel participants. Their models show that per-unit product prices introduce fundamental differences in incentives that lead to inefficiencies in coordination when compared to centralized control of the channel. More complex pricing structures, such as volume discounts or two-part tariffs can, in some cases, support optimal channel coordination.

A key objective of this study is to explore the relationships among IT, coordination, and coordination structure. $^{1}$ Unlike internal organizational structure, changes in coordination structure must be accepted by the other party to the relationship. IT can enable new coordination structures, and these changes in coordination structure can lead to the creation of an economic surplus by either reducing uncertainty (the information processing view of coordination) or by changing the objectives of the parties to favor more efficient decisions from the perspective of an entire industry (the objective differences view of coordination). Coordination in this sense is an outcome, describing the joint efficiency of the firms under a specific coordination structure.

## 2.2. The Division of Surplus

The issue of how IT can create economic value is somewhat different from the question of who retains that value. Researchers have begun to focus on the role of bargaining power in explaining the division of benefits from strategic applications of IT, hence the creation of business value $[3]$ . For example, Clemons and Weber $[8]$ , in their case study of the London Stock Exchange, demonstrated that the SEAQ automated quotation system generated considerable economic value, but that the initiators of that system, the market makers, were actually made worse off, due to a shift in bargaining power created by the system.

The proposition that IT can lead to changes in bargaining power leads, logically, to the proposition that players' expectations of power shifts should affect their decisions regarding investment in IT and the design of those systems. For example, a customer who anticipates that an IOS offered by his supplier will entail significant switching costs is likely either not to adopt the system or to take other steps to mitigate the exposure to exploitation created by those switching costs. The supplier is likely to anticipate this reaction and either not to invest in the system or to design the system to reduce the switching costs.

There is some theoretical support to this proposition that power implications can influence the level of investment in coordination and IT. Williamson [30] has argued that increasing the efficiency of coordination often involves investment in transaction-specific capital, resources that have little or no salvage value or use outside of the particular relationship for which the investment was undertaken. Such sunk capital introduces fundamental bargaining problems for the investor, particularly in situations of high uncertainty, leading either to underinvestment in coordination or vertical integration of ownership [18, 19, 30].

However, these changes in coordination structure can also affect the bargaining power of the parties, hence the division of the surplus created by the new coordination structure. Participants use their expectations as to the division of benefits to guide the negotiation over new coordination structure.

## 3. Methodology

THE QUESTIONS PRESENTED IN THE PREVIOUS SECTION ARE EXPLORED through a field study in the consumer packaged goods industry. The research falls into the category of theory-building case studies discussed by Eisenhardt [10] and Swamidass [27]. This approach to case studies must be distinguished from hypothesis testing case studies [e.g., 20]. Rather than seeking to test a priori hypotheses, constructs and propositions emerge inductively through the process of field work. An exploratory case study of this sort is appropriate for the research questions posed previously, given the early stage of theory building in this area. According to Eisenhardt [10], inductive theory building from case studies increases the likelihood of generating novel theory. Important factors and relationships that may be abstracted away in analytical reasoning or broad-based empirical studies must be confronted in explaining the observed reality as reflected in qualitative and quantitative data. Moreover, the resultant theory is more likely to be empirically valid since theory building is so intimately tied to the evidence.

Of course, there are weaknesses in this approach. According to Eisenhardt, inductively developed theory can be overly complex; it can be rich in detail, but lacking the simplicity of overall perspective. Also, inductive theory may lack generalizability, being overly narrow and idiosyncratic to the situations studied. There are also potential problems with researcher bias and replicability. With qualitative data and inductively developed constructs, the risk is greater that the interviewer can subtly guide responses or interpret ambiguous responses incorrectly. Steps we have taken to reduce these weaknesses are discussed in the sections below.

## 3.1. Case Selection

The consumer packaged goods industry is undergoing significant structural change, making it an excellent case for study. The universal adoption of Universal Product Codes (UPC) and the widespread adoption of checkout scanner systems over the 1980s have dramatically increased the availability of information in this industry. Applications of IT are emerging to harness this wealth of data, affecting virtually all business functions in the channel, from managing product flow to supporting shelf layout to delivering marketing messages to consumers.

The research described here focuses on manufacturer–retailer relationships in coordinating product flow in the distribution channel. Our primary unit of analysis is the relationship, rather than the technology. A technology focus would limit us to aspects of the relationship that are directly tied to a specific application area, such as EDI. A relationship focus allows us to explore changes that result from the interaction of applications as well as changes that may be only loosely tied to an IT application.

We focus here on the manufacturer-retailer dyad. In many cases, manufacturers interact with retailers through wholesalers and brokers. That additional level in the distribution channel was not addressed in this paper since manufacturers and retailers large enough to have already experimented with IT and with new coordination structures are typically large enough to deal directly with each other. The role of wholesalers will be addressed in future research.

We also focus primarily on the logistics function in the distribution channel, managing the physical flow of product from the manufacturer to the retailer. Other dimensions of manufacturer–retailer relationships will be addressed in future research.

## 3.2. Data Gathering

Information for the study was gathered through extensive interviews with industry participants supported by a review of secondary data sources. Secondary data sources included newspapers and popular press journals (e.g., Fortune, BusinessWeek), trade journals, $^{2}$ and proprietary consulting reports.

Interviews were conducted with thirty senior managers representing six retailers and three manufacturers. In keeping with the methodology described by Eisenhardt, research sites were selected opportunistically as the study progressed, attempting to cover the major strategic approaches to manufacturer-retailer relationships. Retailers were medium to large regional grocery chains. The smallest retailer had sales of \$992 million from 79 supermarkets; the largest had sales of \$20.3 billion from 1,234 supermarkets. Manufacturers were all large, national firms operating in several categories, including food and nonfood products. Small retailers and manufacturers were not included since they are less likely to deal directly with each other, that is, without intermediation by wholesalers, and are less likely to have the resources to invest in IT.

At least two managers were interviewed at most sites. In all cases interviewees were senior managers with direct involvement in information technology, logistics, or promotion management. Interviews were semistructured, lasting from one to two hours. In some cases, shorter follow-up interviews were conducted to clarify responses.

Interview guides evolved over the course of the study as issues and concepts emerged. Questions probed executives' opinions on recent and proposed changes to manufacturer–retailer relationships, including the effects of the changes both on their own organizations and on the other party, what characteristics of the firms involved moderated those effects, the role of information technology, and the future evolution of those changes.

## 3.3. Analysis

Interviews were conducted by two investigators, to facilitate data collection and to provide some control over researcher bias in questioning or interpreting results. In keeping with Eisenhardt's methodology, field study results were compared with and related to existing theoretical and empirical research from IS, economics, and strategic management. The emerging conceptual structure was used to refine questions for future interviews or for follow-up interviews.

The resulting frameworks and concepts (discussed in the next three sections) were reviewed with five senior managers from previous interviews to assure factual accuracy and conceptual plausibility. By conceptual plausibility, we mean that the concepts developed were defined and operationalized in a way that was meaningful to the managers and could be related directly to their experience, and that the relationships and explanations put forth among the concepts were supported by the managers' experience.

## 4. Current Coordination Structure

THE KEY DIMENSIONS OF COORDINATION STRUCTURE THAT EMERGE from our field study are information flows, the nature and timing of information communicated across organizational boundaries, and pricing structure, the pattern of financial incentives that guide decision making and determine the flow of funds between firms. The current pricing structure and pattern of information flows lead to significant inefficiencies in the channel.

## 4.1. Consumer Packaged Goods Industry Overview

Manufacturers, such as Lever Brothers and Procter & Gamble, produce and sell nondurable, branded goods to consumers through a complex distribution system of brokers, wholesalers, and retailers. Products include grocery items such as soup and spaghetti sauce; health and beauty aides such as toothpaste and shampoo; and nonfood items, such as laundry soap. The logistics system underlying this industry is daunting in its complexity.

Retailers may be grocery stores, drug stores, small convenience stores, or mass merchandisers such as Wal-Mart or K-mart. There is tremendous variation among retail outlets. For example, in 1990 there were 178,000 grocery stores, ranging from convenience stores covering 1,900 square feet and carrying 2,300 products, to mammoth hypermarkets covering 174,000 square feet and carrying over 100,000 products [23]. Retail outlets are increasingly part of chains. In 1990 chain grocery stores accounted for 56 percent of non-convenience store sales [26]; this percentage has been steadily increasing over the past twenty years. This increasing importance of chains is also evident for drug stores, convenience stores, and mass merchandisers. Large chains maintain their own distribution centers, buying goods directly from manufacturers, and managing the distribution of goods to individual stores. Independents and smaller chains are serviced by wholesalers.

Manufacturing in this industry is equally complex. Large consumer packaged goods firms, such as Unilever, offer a wide variety of products in several product categories. For example, Lever Brothers sells products as diverse as laundry detergent, bath soap, and cooking oils and butter substitutes. In addition, there are many small, narrow line manufacturers, some operating nationally and some operating only in certain geographical regions. Manufacturers' primary resource is their brands, although each brand may involve many distinct products: different sizes, flavors, and styles. Typically goods must be delivered to retailers' distribution centers, although some goods, such as soft drinks and snack foods, are delivered directly to individual stores.

## 4.2. Promotions and Logistics Coordination

Coordination of product flow in this industry can be described as complex on just about all criteria: number and variety of products, number and variety of levels in the channel, and number and variety of players at each level in the channel. The logistics problem, however, is complicated even further by the system of promotions that has emerged since the 1970s.

Promotions encompass the manufacturers' and retailers' decisions concerning marketing events whose purpose is to influence directly short-term customer behavior. This may involve coupons, temporary price reductions, or special displays. Promotions can be conducted by the manufacturer, as when a coupon is delivered directly to consumers through an insert in the Sunday newspaper, but a large and increasing level of promotional activity is implemented through retailers. Considerable money is at stake in promotions. In 1988, manufacturers spent \$100 billion on sales promotions, two-thirds of which went to the trade (retailers and wholesalers) [15]. $^{3}$ Additionally, according to manufacturers interviewed for this study, many promotional costs are not captured in reported promotion expenditures.

Promotions create considerable volatility and uncertainty in product demand at all levels of the channel. According to our interviews and industry data, a promotion can increase product sales up to 700 percent of unpromoted volume. Much of that volume comes at the expense of other products in the category, or represents future sales realized early but at a deep discount; as one vice president of sales at a major detergent manufacturer put it, “People don’t use more soap just because it’s on sale.” The result is large swings in product demand at the store level, which are translated into large swings in orders facing the manufacturer, but not in changes in annual sales.

The current promotional regime has given rise to two practices that are extremely dysfunctional from the standpoint of channel efficiency: forward buying and diverting. Manufacturers implement promotions through retailers primarily through case allowances, or discounts offered on all cases purchased during the promotional period. This pricing practice creates the opportunity for temporal and geographical arbitrage:

\- Forward buying: Retailers can stock up on goods at the promotional price to cover sales beyond the promotional period. The effect is a reduction of product cost for the retailer with no promotional benefit for the manufacturer.

\- Diverting: Frequently, manufacturers will only run a promotion in selected regions of the country. Retailers can purchase goods at the promotional price in one region and resell them to retailers in another region where the promotion is not in effect. Again, this reduces product cost for the retailers involved without the intended promotional benefit for the manufacturer. Moreover, the costs of shipping the products between retailers serves no economic purpose for the industry.

Collectively, diverting and forward buying are viewed by the trade as investment buying. Diverting and forward buying exacerbate the logistical inefficiencies caused by promotions in general, multiplying the size of the promotional demand spikes and increasing channel inventories. According to one manager, 80 percent of product sales in some categories occur on four days over the year, the last days of promotion periods. Another manufacturer interviewee estimated that over 50 percent of inventory in the distribution channel was held solely for investment purposes. Retailers interviewed confirmed the importance of investment inventory, but declined to quantify its impact. However, the importance of investment buying to retailers is suggested by the fact that all retailers interviewed had information systems in place to support investment buying and to track the economic returns from these activities.

The spikes in demand caused by promotions and exacerbated by investment buying introduce higher logistics costs in the channel. Manufacturers must build inventory in anticipation of a promotional period, often running plants on overtime. During the trough following the promotion, plant utilization decreases. The result is higher channel inventories, higher manufacturing and distribution costs, and lower utilization of plant than would be possible with more stable demand patterns.

Moreover, manufacturers lack sufficient and timely information on product movement to plan adequately for or to react to these swings. Since promotion activity is negotiated with retailers, manufacturers will generally know when their products are being promoted and can plan accordingly. However, the actual promotion dates may vary, and the manufacturer will typically not know what the promotional price will be, or what other promotions will be going on at the store or at the store's competitors; all are factors that can dramatically affect the level and timing of promotional sales. One vice president of manufacturing at a large consumer packaged goods company notes, "At the aggregate, our forecasts for promotions are pretty good, but at the SKU [stock keeping unit] level we can be off by 50 percent either way." Moreover, even when not promoting, product sales are constantly buffeted by promotions in other products. A major promotion by a competing product can lead to a significant and unanticipated drop in demand.

The ability of manufacturers to react to these changes is limited by a lack of timely information. Typically manufacturers only see retailer orders when they are placed by the retailer's distribution center. The result is frequent expensive shocks to the manufacturing and distribution system. During one interview, a plant manager was in the middle of dealing with a last minute change in manufacturing plan. He was told on Thursday that an order for 190,000 cases of one product, due to begin production on Tuesday, should be changed to 225,000 cases of another product. The increase of 35,000 cases was a problem, but that could be handled. The biggest problem was that the bottles for the two products were different, leading to a large inventory of the old bottles and a mad rush to expedite an order for the new bottles from the supplier. The manager assured us this situation was not unusual.

## 4.3. Analysis of Logistics and Promotional Bargaining

Much of the inefficiency in promotions and logistics stems from structural aspects of the current coordination structure for managing logistics and promotion interactions. From our field study results, the two dimensions of coordination structure that appear most important are information flows and pricing structure. The relationship between these dimensions of coordination structure and the resulting inefficiencies can be understood in the context of the two views of coordination identified earlier: the information-processing view and the objective differences view (see figure 2).

## 4.3.1. Objective-based Coordination Problems

Under the objective differences view of coordination, inefficiencies stem from each party attempting to maximize their individual objectives when those objectives are not compatible with total system performance. The most obvious example of objective-based coordination problems is investment buying. Forward buying and diverting are founded on the pricing practice of case allowances: temporary reductions in the prices manufacturers offer to retailers used to compensate retailers for promotional activity. Case allowances are offered based on products purchased by the retailer during the promotional period, not on products actually sold on promotion by the retailer, creating an incentive to arbitrage. The ability to arbitrage is presently enabled by information asymmetries inherent in the current coordination structure. Manufacturers only have access to information on orders placed by retailers' distribution centers. They lack the information on product movement and inventory necessary to determine when products purchased by the retailer on promotion are, in fact, sold to the consumer on promotion, sold later to the consumer at the regular retail price, or sold to other retailers in nonpromoted regions. The monitoring problem faced by manufacturers is evidenced in a story told by one interview subject with experience as a buyer for a large supermarket chain. He described a situation where a major manufacturer had canceled a promotional allowance after having a truckload of diverted goods physically followed. Information flows that allow manufacturers to monitor product movement cheaply and reliably would enable pricing structures or contractual arrangements that would reduce or eliminate this arbitrage activity.

Figure 2. Relationships between the Coordination Structure and Channel Inefficiencies

The current coordination structure for promotions also creates objective-based coordination problems by leading to a bargaining situation that, for manufacturers, has elements of what is referred to in game theory as a prisoners' dilemma: if all manufacturers reduced the level of promotions, they would all be better off, but then a single manufacturer's motivation for promoting would be higher.

Retailers promote to increase store sales; they are less concerned with which products make up those sales. A promotional price on one detergent will generate increased sales of that brand, but often those increased sales will be at the expense of a comparable product, with limited increase in the retailers' total detergent sales. Under the current coordination structure, retailers essentially auction off promotional rights each period, with the dominant factor being which manufacturer in each category offers the highest payment. This is largely a zero-sum game among manufacturers, with the winners of the auction realizing a dramatic increase in sales at the expense of the losers. The retailer gets paid simply to offer the promotion during that period. Since the retailer's payment is unrelated to the effectiveness of the promotion to the manufacturer, to overall product sales for the year, or to any other measure that would tie the retailers' promotional objectives to those of the manufacturer, there is an incentive to overpromote. The retailer may even accept promotions from several brands in a category, thus severely reducing the manufacturer's promotion payoff for each brand.

The prisoners' dilemma facing manufacturers has two effects: manufacturers promote more and they pay more for their promotions. The latter affects the division of industry profits between retailers and manufacturers. This is an issue of power, rather than coordination, and is addressed in section 6. The former is a coordination issue, since reducing inefficient promotions can increase total industry profits. Our interviews support this analysis of promotion bargaining as a prisoner's dilemma. For example, one interviewee observed that the highest promotional period for snack food and soda manufacturers is around holidays such as Christmas and the Fourth of July, times of highest demand. Such behavior seems counterproductive unless one considers the cost of not promoting during these periods when competitors are doing so. Manufacturers we spoke with universally felt that the level of promotion expenditures was too high, but that reducing those levels would be difficult without severe loss of sales.

## 4.3.2. Uncertainty-based Coordination Problems

Under the information processing view of coordination, inefficiencies stem from uncertainty. Galbraith [13] has defined uncertainty as the difference between the information available and the information required for an optimal decision. The current coordination structure increases uncertainty-based coordination problems in two ways. First, volatility in demand, caused by promotions and exacerbated by investment buying and the prisoners' dilemma bargaining faced by manufacturers, increases the information required for efficient decisions. Volatility in demand makes planning more difficult and deviation from plans are more frequent and more severe.

Second, information flows in the current coordination structure reduce the information available to manufacturers for making efficient decisions. As noted earlier, manufacturers lack sufficient information on retailers' promotion plans to plan adequately for promotional swings. Moreover, since manufacturers do not see product movement until an order is placed by the retailer, they have very little lead time for reacting to demand volatility. Orders placed by retailers may not even be a good signal of future needs, since they are distorted by investment buying. One senior vice president of manufacturing explains it as follows: "If we are expecting an order for 1,000 cases and we receive an order for 2,000, we cannot tell how much of that represents the performance of the promotion, possibly requiring adjustment of future production plans, and how much is simply being diverted."

Uncertainty-based coordination problems and objective-based coordination problems are not independent. The objective-based coordination problems, investment buying and the manufacturers' prisoners' dilemma, increase the volatility of demand, thereby increasing uncertainty and the need for information. Moreover, information flows and pricing structure are not completely independent. The availability of information affects the pricing structures that are possible. For example, as we shall see in the next section, increasing information flows can enable new pricing structures.

## 4.4. Summary of Promotion and Logistics Coordination Structure

The pricing structure and information flows in the current coordination structure for logistics and promotions create objective-based and uncertainty-based inefficiencies. Addressing these problems through IT and new coordination structures can generate a substantial economic surplus. The amounts at stake here are significant. It is estimated that 30 to 40 percent of the \$66 billion (in 1988 [15]) manufacturer promotion budget paid to retailers represents pure inefficiencies, while 30 percent is retained as profit by retailers, and only 30 percent is passed on to consumers [9].

## 5. IT and New Coordination Structures

IT CAN CHANGE KEY ASPECTS OF THE INTERACTIONS between manufacturers and retailers. These changes can significantly affect coordination, and thus channel efficiency. This section describes the key innovations in coordination structure observed during the field study. These changes are all related to IT and the information now available in the channel through scanner systems.

## 5.1. Flow of Information

Over the past ten years, IT has led to a dramatic increase in the availability of information on product movement in the distribution channel. Prior to the introduction of UPC and scanner systems, the only sources of information on product movement were manufacturer shipments or warehouse withdrawals. This information was only available at a high level of aggregation and with considerable delay. Retailers had to rely on manufacturers for data on what products were moving. UPC and checkout scanners at the store made it possible for retailers to track product movement themselves, first at the distribution center level, and then, as scanners became widely used, at the individual store level.

Electronic data interchange (EDI) is becoming common in the industry. A set of industrywide EDI standards, called Universal Communications Standards (UCS), have been adopted to facilitate computerized communication. In 1992, 700 to 800 grocery chains were using EDI for purchase orders, and about half of those are also using EDI for invoices [14]. Some firms are experimenting with extending this to include price and promotion information. The short-term benefits from EDI are administrative savings. (One large retailer estimates EDI-generated savings of \$800,000 a year in clerical expenses and inventory reduction [29].)

For the most part, EDI applications simply automate existing information flows and decision processes. A few large firms are going further than simple EDI, seeking to restructure their logistics systems to reduce costs. The most visible and widely reported example of this is the partnership between Wal-Mart and Proctor & Gamble (P&G) [11]. A foundation of this relationship is shared access to information. P&G receives information on store sales directly from Wal-Mart's scanner systems, and they can then plan production and delivery more efficiently. In effect, many logistics functions have been shifted to the manufacturer: P&G monitors sales and inventory, making ordering decisions and even delivering directly to the store, bypassing Wal-Mart's distribution center entirely. Wal-Mart is using the experience of their partnership with P&G and other key vendors to improve coordination with all of their suppliers. Currently, their EDI network for pricing, purchasing, order tracking, and payment covers more than 2,000 vendors. Recently, Wal-Mart has rolled out a system, available to all vendors, that provides access to detailed internal information on sales and inventory [24]. As William R. Fields, Wal-Mart's executive vice president of merchandise and sales, expressed in a letter to vendors [24]: "Shared information is the basis to a true partnership and through this new system we are giving you access to Wal-Mart data as well as your company data that has never been available before."

Other manufacturers and retailers are also experimenting with “just-in-time” or “quick-response” approaches, similar to the Wal-Mart example. These approaches are typically based on closer cooperation between manufacturers and retailers, including improved access to forecasts and detailed product movement information from the store scanners. They also involve customizing the distribution channel to facilitate coordination. Goods may be shipped directly to stores, rather than to warehouses. Shipments may be made directly from the production plant, bypassing the manufacturers' distribution center. The retailer may even pick up a shipment directly from the manufacturer. The distribution approach is adjusted over time to fit changes in demand.

The success of these quick-response or “vendor-managed inventory” approaches depends on reducing the level of inventory in the channel and the wide, unpredictable swings in demand caused by promotions and investment buying. In all cases studied, a move toward tightly coupled logistics was accompanied by changes in the pricing structure that reduced the retailer’s incentives for excessive promotion and investment buying.

## 5.2. New Promotion Payment Structures

Information technology enables new payment approaches to promotions that can radically change incentives and the structure of bargaining. Scanner systems and electronic marketing systems provide information that can be used to implement “pay for performance” arrangements, where payment to the retailer is tied to specific measurable criteria tracked through IT. The objective of such arrangements is to reduce behavior that creates channel inefficiencies, such as forward buying and diverting, and to align retailers’ objectives more closely with those of manufacturers. The industry is experimenting with two basic approaches, which we call “pay for results” and “pay for behavior.” We will describe two cases that illustrate these approaches.

Procter & Gamble has pioneered a marketing fund system based on “pay for results” [e.g., 9]. In this system, periodic promotions through case allowances are eliminated or drastically reduced. Instead, the manufacturer prices the product at a lower, but consistent price, referred to in the industry as “net down” pricing. Retailers receive a set amount of funds for implementing promotions. The level of these marketing funds is determined by sales targets negotiated with the retailer and supported by analysis of scanner data. These funds are increased or decreased over time, based on the retailers’ performance against targets. The retailer has total discretion over actual tactical promotion decisions, although P&G plays an advisory and coordinating role. Payment is based on how well the retailer makes those decisions, that is, on sales, hence the use of the term “pay for results.”

There are two main effects of this program. First, since case allowances during limited promotional periods are replaced by stable, net down prices, incentives for forward buying and diverting are reduced. Reducing promotions and arbitrage lead to more stable demand patterns, which can reduce channel inventories, manufacturing costs, and distribution costs. Second, there is less scope for retailers to exploit period-by-period manufacturer competition, driving up the level and cost of promotions. Previously, retailers' promotion decisions were made period by period, based on how much manufacturers were willing to pay for promoting in each period; manufacturers essentially bid against each other for weekly sales. This bargaining structure increases the manufacturers' prisoners' dilemma, leading to higher promotion levels and higher promotion payments, regardless of whether the promotion was economically profitable for the manufacturer. With P&G no longer bidding on a period-by-period basis, the retailer will only promote when effective toward store sales and P&G product targets. As a result, there should be a decrease in the overall promotion level.

In contrast, “pay for behavior” involves the manufacturer contracting for specific promotional services (e.g., couponing, price, advertisement, or display) from the retailer. The provision of those services could be electronically monitored, enabling both the manufacturer and the retailer to measure the effectiveness of the program. Several manufacturers are experimenting with electronically monitored “pay for behavior” programs with retailers. For example, one manufacturer interviewed is paying retailers only for goods sold on promotion as verified by scanner information. This type of payment structure is well established with third-party marketers offering electronic marketing services. For example, Catalina Marketing sells manufacturers exclusive rights to promote a specific category on its Checkout Coupon system for a specified geographical region and time period. The manufacturer pays based on a formula that incorporates coupons issued and redeemed, so the cost of the promotion is strongly linked to promotional activity, though not necessarily to promotion effectiveness.

“Pay for behavior” has the advantage that manufacturers only pay for specific services they request, giving the manufacturer more direct control over promotions at the retail level when compared to “pay for results.” If properly structured, forward buying and diverting could be reduced or eliminated. However, “pay for behavior” requires a good understanding of how specific promotional behaviors contribute to larger manufacturer promotional objectives. A poorly structured “pay for behavior” arrangement may eliminate some inefficiencies caused by differences in objectives, but may create new problems and inefficiencies in other areas.

## 6. Bargaining Power and Transition to New Coordination Structures

THE INNOVATIONS IN COORDINATION STRUCTURE DISCUSSED ABOVE can increase the efficiency of the distribution channel. These benefits can be considerable. We would expect to see rapid adoption of these new structures, and for some segments of the retail market this increased coordination was observed. Wal-Mart, K-mart, Target, and other large every-day-low-price (EDLP) retailers have actively embraced close coordination with manufacturers; they share demand information with manufacturers, coordinating distribution resources and product movement to reduce total system costs. These retailers compete on the basis of operating efficiencies and economies of scale, so the logistical savings from net down pricing appear to support their basic strategy by increasing their comparative advantage.

However, the field study also found considerable resistance to many of the new approaches, especially among event-driven retailers, whose current strategy is dependent upon promotion. These responses can be explained by looking at the new coordination structure's effects on the bargaining power of those retailers who are resisting.

## 6.1. Resistance to New Coordination Structures

Perhaps the most significant resistance was found in the case of P&G's move to net down pricing. This program sought to reduce promotions and arbitrage through stable product prices to the retailer. Promotional activity was still supported, but was funded through marketing funds tied to sales performance targets, not to actual consumer product purchases. These funds were less than retailers received through case allowances, though not necessarily less than they netted after consideration of the expenses associated with promotion and investment buying. At the same time, P&G reduced list price on products, reducing the scope for promotional pricing at the retail level.

P&G's move to net down pricing generated strong negative reactions from some segments of the trade, particularly among the event-driven retailers. These stores compete with a merchandising strategy based on promotional pricing. Each period, some products are offered on deep discount to attract customers, while most products carry much higher margins. Event-driven retailers typically buy products from deal to deal, making extensive use of forward buying and diverting. A large portion of their profits thus comes from manufacturers' promotion funds. P&G's net down program was viewed, perhaps correctly, as a direct attack on these retailers' profitability. They felt that reducing promotional activity would lead to increased price competition and favor the larger EDLP merchandisers, such as Wal-Mart, that competed primarily on very low margins made possible through high volume and operating efficiencies.

Given P&G's market power and the strength of their brands, retailers could not stop carrying P&G products. However, retailers did have the ability to retaliate. In one case, a major retailer strongly promoted competing brands while reducing shelf space and placement of P&G products. The result was significant erosion in P&G market share in those categories. Two of the chains interviewed reported such erosion. One senior merchandising manager noted, "Net down only works when all the other manufacturers follow suit. Otherwise, your 'every-day-low-price' is always significantly higher than one or two competing brands that are on promotion. You'll get killed every time." According to our interviews, there are recent indications that P&G is backing off from their program, making more money available to retailers for promotion and halting the roll out of the program to other products.

P&G's net down program sought to achieve logistical efficiencies by changing the payment structure alone, removing the incentives for promotion and investment buying and leading to demand-smoothing, reduced inventories, and level and predictable manufacturing scheduling and distribution. P&G and many other manufacturers are also experimenting with changes in other aspects of coordination structure to improve coordination. For example, quick-response approaches are emerging, based on the sharing of demand information and just-in-time inventory techniques. In most cases, these arrangements were developed with and demanded by the large EDLP mass merchandisers such as Wal-Mart and K-mart. But reaction by the rest of the trade to quick-response, particularly smaller promotion-driven retailers, has been mixed.

No retailers interviewed had quick-response arrangements with manufacturers fully operational. And while all of the manufacturers interviewed were involved with quick-response arrangements with their large, EDLP mass merchandiser customers, they had yet to enter into such a program with any event-driven retailers, although discussions were under way for a few pilot programs. There is considerable uncertainty about what these programs mean to the retailer. One senior vice president in charge of quick-response systems at a major food manufacturer observed, “There are as many definitions of quick-response as there are retailers.”

Several retailers we interviewed expressed interest and enthusiasm for these quick-response arrangements, but did not feel that such approaches would or should eliminate promotions and investment buying. They intended to continue investment buying and to resist any efforts to reduce promotional payments from manufacturers. But this stance is somewhat contradictory to the objectives of logistics integration: the benefits from quick-response cannot be fully realized where investment buying is present, leading to higher inventories and distortions of demand patterns facing manufacturers. One manufacturer manager describes the difficulty: "So what if we get detailed store movement information, if the purchase decision is still being made based on investment decisions rather than actual demand. The information is not useful to us unless the retailer changes his way of doing business."

## 6.2. Role of Bargaining Power in Transition

The observed lack of adoption of and even resistance to new coordination structures appears counterintuitive: if there are significant and widely accepted efficiency gains from improved coordination, there should be some way of sharing this economic surplus that makes things better for all parties. This section discusses some factors related to the effects of the changes on bargaining power that could lead to rational resistance to the new coordination structures by retailers.

Operational decisions regarding promotions and product flow can be viewed as a bargaining game between manufacturers and retailers. This game is played under the rules established by the coordination structure and is repeated each period. The observed inefficiencies and the division of industry profits result from decisions that are, in some sense, equilibrium solutions to the existing bargaining game. $^{4}$ Changing coordination structure is not simply a move to a new equilibrium in the existing game; it is a fundamental change to a new bargaining game.

Under this new bargaining game, retailers lose the ability to arbitrage and to exploit the promotional prisoners' dilemma facing manufacturers, thus losing a significant source of profits. Manufacturers may compensate these retailer for their lost profits in other ways, for example by paying the retailer marketing funds or information access fees equivalent to the profits from investment buying and promotion. Over time, however, these side payments, and the retailer's share of industry profits, are likely to erode, due to a fundamental loss of bargaining power.

In any interfirm relationship, profits are shared among participants based on relative bargaining power. In game theory, the bargaining power of a party is determined by its threat point, or the value to them of employing their resources in other ways. Under the current system of promotions, high list prices, high margins, and high levels of relatively unrestricted promotional funds give retailers the ability to damage a brand severely by heavily and consistently promoting competing products; thus, retailers enjoy an attractive threat point. This ability to threaten manufacturers would be reduced under a coordination structure based on information sharing, and smoothed demand patterns. New coordination structures can lead to reduced list prices, margins, and level of promotional funds while reducing the retailer's discretion in spending promotional funds (e.g., through pay for performance). This would give retailers much less discretion in pricing, and thus reduce their ability to credibly threaten a manufacturer's sales. Of course, retailers would still have the power to de-list a manufacturer's product (although this is not a credible threat for strong brands), or to favor competing brands in shelf space and placement. Reduced promotional activity may also accelerate price competition at the retail level, increasing the importance of scale-driven operating efficiencies and leading to an increasing share of industry sales, power, and profits being held by large retailers such as Wal-Mart.

Once manufacturer payments to retailers begin to erode, it may be very difficult to reverse the trend and reestablish the old promotional regime. Once price levels fall, they cannot quickly or easily be raised, due to consumer resistance. Increasing competition by large EDLP players will also limit the ability to restore margins. Moreover, as noted above, reduced price levels and discretionary promotional funds make it more difficult to threaten manufacturers into returning to a promotion-driven situation.

Long-term contracts could potentially be employed to overcome the problems introduced by the erosion of bargaining power, for example by contractually specifying the level of side payments or the formula by which they are computed. However, there are several characteristics of this situation that make such contracts difficult to write and enforce:

\- Repeated game: The coordination structure changes and resulting power shifts affect ongoing operational bargaining decisions such as purchasing, manufacturing, and promotions. This makes it difficult to establish the duration of any contractual arrangement. Moreover, the longer the contract, the more difficult it is to specify all the key contingencies.

\- Uncertainty increasing over time: Changes in products, consumer demand, strategies, and industry structure introduce compounding sources of uncertainties that affect the level of side payments and are difficult to predict and contract for.

\- Reduced bargaining power: The reduction in retailers' bargaining power discussed above also affects the enforceability of any contractual arrangement. Manufacturers will increasingly have incentives to shirk, renegotiate contract terms, or take advantage of unforeseen and unspecified contingencies. Retailers will be disadvantaged in subsequent negotiations. Over time, side payments will increasingly become a matter of ex post bargaining power rather than ex ante contracting.

A shift in bargaining power is only one possible explanation of the observed resistance by some retailers to improved coordination structures. This explanation is consistent with our empirical observations and our conceptual analysis of manufacturer-retailer interactions. However, there are many other factors that may also contribute to retailer resistance. For example, organizational inertia and internal social and political processes may contribute to the lack of adoption. Moreover, our field study results suggest, but are insufficient to prove, that a shift in power has in fact occurred. Much more work is required both conceptually and empirically to make more concrete statements.

## 7. Summary

THE BARGAINING SITUATION BETWEEN MANUFACTURERS AND RETAILERS for promotions and logistics that has emerged in the U.S. consumer packaged goods industry appears to have created a distribution channel that is inefficient and dysfunctional in many respects. There are many indications that promotions have become a prisoners' dilemma for manufacturers, resulting in a high level of promotions that offer manufacturers little competitive or economic benefit. Only a fraction of manufacturers' promotional expenditures actually are used to accomplish their promotional objectives. Moreover, logistics distortions from promotions, multiplied by the retailers' practices of forward buying and diverting, have led to significant inefficiencies and excess costs in manufacturing, transportation, and inventory.

Our field study found that IT can support rationalized promotion and distribution arrangements, eliminating many of the observed sources of inefficiency. These arrangements involve increasing the flow of information between retailers and manufacturers and adopting new promotional pricing structures to reduce the inefficiencies caused by differences in objectives. The potential economic surplus from improved coordination is significant. However, in some cases these changes in coordination structure are being resisted and the benefits of improved coordination are not being realized. These situations can be explained in terms of the relationship between bargaining power and two dimensions of coordination structure: information flows and pricing structure. In particular:

\- Retailers' have superior information on product movement compared with manufacturers. This information asymmetry, combined with the manufacturers' promotional pricing practices, creates an opportunity for retailers to engage in practices that are beneficial to retailers, but that lead to significant inefficiencies in the channel.

\- Improving channel coordination can create an economic surplus; however, retailers that depend heavily on promotions can be made worse off under the change by the elimination of their profitable abuse of the current promotional system.

\- The loss of profits is unlikely to be recouped elsewhere, due to an erosion of bargaining power. Contractual means to compensate retailers for their loss of bargaining power appear difficult to implement and to enforce.

\- Retailers that suffer a loss of bargaining power will rationally resist such changes.

This field study was exploratory, aimed at developing frameworks and propositions drawn from empirical experience. The concepts and relationships presented must be refined and more formally tested through additional empirical and conceptual work. The research in its current form makes several contributions to the field:

\- Role of differences in objectives in coordination: Previous literature has tended to view coordination in terms of uncertainties and delays caused by lack of information. In this view, IT can improve coordination by increasing the flow or timeliness of information. This research suggests that coordination problems can also stem from fundamental differences in objectives that can lead to suboptimization.

\- Role of coordination structure: Previous literature studying the impact of IT on interorganizational relationships has assumed that the benefits of IT are related to the level of investment in IT or to specific IT applications. This research suggests that organizational decisions not directly tied to a specific IT applications can have dramatic impact on the efficiency of the channel. For example, the decision of what information to share and how to structure prices and other financial incentives can affect the level of benefits realized and the division of those benefits.

\- Role of power: This research has shown that power is a critical factor that can reduce the business value achievable through using IT to restructure interorganizational relationships. Changes in interfirm interactions enabled by IT can create economic value, but can also change bargaining power. Parties that are made worse off by the change will resist. The issue of interorganizational power and the division of benefits is only beginning to be addressed in the IT field.

## This research suggests several directions for further work:

\- Continued conceptual framework development: The current paper deals only with two aspects of coordination structure: information flows and pricing structures. Other aspects of coordination structure are likely to emerge that can affect power and the division of economic benefits. The discussion here also focuses only on vertical, bilateral relationships between manufacturers and retailers. The effects of competition at both stages of this relationship, that is, competition among manufacturers and competition among retailers, are likely to be important in determining the form of interfirm coordination.

\- Formal game theoretic modeling. The frameworks presented here draw heavily on the concepts developed in game theory. Formal game theoretic modeling may be a useful tool for quantifying and defining some of the concepts and relationships developed here.

\- Empirical testing: The frameworks presented here are useful for structuring qualitative empirical data and making propositions. More work is needed in operationalizing the concepts presented here to allow more rigorous testing of hypotheses. Broad-based, statistically valid empirical tests may not yet be possible, but rigorous hypothesis-testing case studies as described by Lee [20] would be a beneficial direction for future work.
