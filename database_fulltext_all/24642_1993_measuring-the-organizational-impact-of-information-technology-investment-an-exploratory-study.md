# Measuring the Organizational Impact of Information Technology Investment: An Exploratory Study

MO ADAM MAHMOOD AND GARY J. MANN

MO ADAM MAHMOOD is Associate Professor of Information Systems at the University of Texas, El Paso. He received his Ph.D. in management information systems from Texas Tech University and an M.B.A. from California State University. His research interests include information technology in support of superior organizational strategic and economic performance, the strategic manager's perspectives on information technology, and information technology for national and international competitiveness. Dr. Mahmood has published in the Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Decision Sciences, European Journal of Information Systems, Expert Systems with Applications, INFOR—Canadian Journal of Operation Research and Information Processing, Information and Management, Journal of Computer-Based Instruction, Information Resources Management Journal, Journal of Systems Management, Data Base, International Journal of Policy and Management, and others. He has presented papers, served as both a reviewer and a discussant, and chaired sessions and tracks for several regional, national, and international conferences. He is currently either a referee or a member of the editorial review board of seven technical journals.

GARY J. MANN is Associate Professor of Accounting at the University of Texas at El Paso. He holds a Ph.D. in business administration from Texas Tech University. His primary research interest is management control systems and related areas. He has published articles in Omega, Behavior and Information Technology, Journal of Accountancy, Internal Auditing, Health Care Management Review, and the Controllers Quarterly, and has presented research papers at meetings of the Decision Sciences Institute and American Accounting Association Southwest and Southeast Regions.

ABSTRACT: Organizations are investing ever-increasing amounts in information technology (IT). However, the existing literature provides little evidence of a relationship between IT investment and organizational strategic and economic performance. The exploratory research reported here appears to be the first to relate comprehensive sets of IT investment measures to organizational strategic and economic performance measures. Although the individual IT investment variables were found to be only weakly related to organizational strategic and economic performance, they were significantly related to performance when grouped and analyzed by means of canonical correlation. NOLAN, NORTON, AND COMPANY [27] CLAIMED THAT ABOVE-AVERAGE SPENDING on information technology (IT) can enhance a firm's profitability. Harris and Katz [16] suggested that high-performance firms spend a significantly higher proportion of revenues for IT than do low-performance firms. Computerworld recently revealed that in 1988 leading-edge firms increased their overall IT expenditures by about 9 percent [33]. Roach [30] found that, in the banking industry, expenditures for IT increased by 20 percent per year during the 1980s and by 1989 such spending totaled 45 percent of the entire industry's capital stock. Today up to 50 percent of businesses' total capital expenditures is for IT [14].

While it is tempting to conclude from such literature that higher expenditures for IT will increase a firm's overall performance, it is unlikely that IT expenditures alone will ensure a firm's superior performance [16]. To the contrary, Roach [30] found that, during the 1970s and 1980s, the service sector's productivity did not increase relative to its outlays in IT. Senior managers responsible for determining the level of IT expenditure are in a quandary. They perceive that appropriate IT investment may significantly improve a firm's profit performance, but they do not know how to measure this performance nor do they know precisely how much should be invested in IT. Much of the investment that is made is based on hunch or intuition, on the assumption that real returns will result. Also, managers generally lack an understanding of which IT investments are most appropriate for support of the various types of decisions that must be made by organizational management.

The purpose of this research is to advance understanding of the relationship between IT investment and organizational strategic and economic performance. Because investigation of the IT investment–performance relationship is in its early stages and a theoretical model of such a relationship does not exist, the nature of this research is exploratory rather than hypothesis-testing. Specifically, the research has three purposes: first, to discover (through the use of an existing literature review) what measures may be significant to this relationship; second, to examine and uncover possible relations among such measures. This is accomplished through: (1) the evaluation of descriptive statistics derived from the IT investment and organizational performance measures used in the study; (2) correlation analyses to investigate pairwise (univariate) relationships between IT and organizational performance measures, individual IT investment measures, and individual organizational performance measures; and (3) the utilization of canonical correlation analysis to determine the presence of a relationship between the multiple IT and multiple organizational performance measures, and the magnitude of such multivariate relationships. Third, to develop and present a model that depicts a framework for management evaluation of the IT investment and organizational strategic and economic performance relationship and, in the process, establish the foundation for future testing of hypotheses.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. The next section presents a review of research that has been performed to investigate the organizational-level impact of IT. This is followed by a discussion of the arguments for selecting different IT investment and organizational strategic and economic performance measures, and presentation of the research framework. The subsequent section discusses the research method and is followed by the research findings, discussion of the findings and, subsequently, introduction of an IT investment–organizational performance model. The paper concludes by emphasizing the importance of formulating and collecting IT investment and organizational strategic and economic performance data for future research.

## Previous Studies

THIS SECTION PRESENTS A REVIEW OF STUDIES of the organizational-level impacts of IT that were performed primarily within the last ten years (1982–92), with an emphasis on those that actually attempted to measure these impacts.

Cron and Sobol [11] investigated the effect of computer utilization on the organizational performance of 138 medical wholesalers. Computer utilization was measured in terms of the reported number of computer applications and classified as: no computer use, light computer use, medium computer use, and heavy computer use. Organizational performance was measured using four profitability measures: pretax profits, return on assets, return on net worth, and five-year sales growth rates. Firms with heavy computer usage were found to be either very strong or very weak performers. Limited computer usage was associated with poor or average performance.

Another researcher [34] studied fifty-eight mutual savings banks in order to investigate whether the size of an organization is related to its willingness to use IT and whether organizational performance is related to how an organization applies this technology. No significant relationship was found between relative IT expenditures (measured as the percentage of IT expense to total assets) and organizational size (measured by total bank assets and full-time staff). Also, no significant relationship was found between relative IT investment and organizational performance (measured as the percentage of net income to total assets).

Bender [4] surveyed 132 life insurance companies to investigate the financial impact of IT on firms in this industry. Organizational performance was measured in terms of the ratio of total operating expense to total premium income. The IT impact was represented by the ratio of information-processing expense to total operating expense. Bender concluded that an appropriate level of investment in IT could have a positive impact on total expenses and that the optimal investment in IT for the companies studied was achieved at a level between 20 and 25 percent of total operating expenses.

Breshnihan [6] investigated the effect of the use of mainframe computers on the financial service sector (comprised of banks, brokerage firms, and insurance firms) from 1958 to 1972. Breshnihan's study showed a large "social" gain from computerization, measured as consumers' willingness to pay for the technology (mainframe computers), as inferred from the derived demand curve of the technology. The author conservatively estimated that the benefits of mainframe computers exceeded their costs by a factor of 1.5 to 2.

Studying the Maple Leaf Life Insurance Company, Clement and Gotlieb [9] investigated the relationship between managerial control and on-line information systems. The information systems were found to improve managerial control over operations through increased performance reporting to managers. The information systems were also able to shorten the transaction-processing chain (for issuing new policies), leading to increased productivity and improved processing time.

One researcher [25] used an econometric model to investigate the productivity impact of IT investment in manufacturing firms. IT output was measured as a function of raw materials expenditures, costs for real non-IT purchased services (e.g., travel, insurance), real total labor compensation, real IT capital, and real non-IT capital. No significant positive correlation was found to exist between IT expenditures and the various productivity measures. Banker and Kauffman [2] used a large commercial bank that participated in the “MAC” automatic teller machine (ATM) network to investigate the impact of ATMs on deposit market share in branch banking operations. They found that membership in the MAC network was positively correlated with a branch’s market share of local demand deposits. A similar result was obtained for estimating savings deposit market shares. However, little evidence was found that the presence of a branch ATM had a significant impact on improving the branch’s deposit market shares.

In a four-year investigation of forty insurance companies, Harris and Katz [16] attempted to determine whether certain IT ratios would be useful as predictors of organizational performance. The ratio of IT expense to premium income and the ratio of information technology expense to total operating expense were used to differentiate organizational performance of system technology leaders, as measured by the ratio of total operating expense to premium income. Thus, the study demonstrated that the IT ratios were related to organizational performance.

Using six years of historical data on IT investment and organizational performance, Weill [35] studied thirty-three valve manufacturing companies. IT investment was categorized as strategic, informational, and transactional, and was tested against organizational performance using six performance measures. Although transactional IT investment was found to be strongly related to superior organizational performance, there was no evidence that strategic IT investment, on a long-term basis, will increase or decrease organizational performance. However, the research results implied that strategic IT investment was beneficial to relatively poor performing firms in the short run.

Alpar and Kim [1] used a microeconomic theory-based approach to investigate the impact of IT investment on the performance of a large number of banks. Time deposits, labor, capital, and IT expenses were used as inputs to the model. Demand deposits, installment loans, real estate mortgage loans, and commercial and other loans were used as outputs from the model. Overall, IT investment was found to be cost reducing. More specifically, IT investment has contributed to the reduction in demand deposits, installment loans, and labor, and to the increase of time deposits, commercial loans, and capital.

Weill [35] investigated the return on strategic investment in IT in sixty-eight manufacturing firms for a six-year period (1982–87). A firm's overall performance was defined and measured by three variables: sales growth, return on assets, and the quantity of nonproduction labor per million dollars of sales. The firm's strategic investment in IT (for the explicit purpose of gaining competitive advantage), as identified by CEOs and controllers, was measured as a percentage of sales. The study concluded that heavy investment in IT, in the short term, was related to lower nonproduction labor productivity and sales, but was not significantly related to return on assets. In the long term, heavy investment in IT had no effect on nonproduction labor productivity and sales growth, but had a positive effect on return on assets.

Harris and Katz [17] investigated the relationship between organizational performance and the intensity of integration and coordination of organizational activities through IT investment in the insurance industry. A year-by-year analysis of data indicated that the ratio of IT expense to total operating expense (IT expense ratio) was higher and the ratio of information technology costs to premium income (IT cost efficiency ratio) was lower in the top-performing life insurance organizations relative to the weak-performing insurance organizations. A longitudinal analysis of data also indicated that insurance firms with improved organizational performance showed increased premium income growth, decreased operating costs growth, decreased non-IT costs growth, increased IT expense ratio growth, and decreased IT cost efficiency ratio growth. Some of these studies used surrogates for measuring IT investment and organizational performance [e.g., 6, 11] while others used direct measures [e.g., 4, 16, 17]. It is quite clear that in order to conduct reliable and valid research in this area, measures that directly gauge the relevant aspects of IT investment and organizational performance are more appropriate. Some studies have used only information system cost to measure IT investment [11], while others have used total expenditures for IT [4, 16, 17]. Since we are interested in measuring the impact of total IT investment, the definition of IT should be broadened to encompass all organizationwide IT including communication technology; system performance in isolation is not an appropriate way to measure IT impact [21]. Also, some researchers used only one ratio for measuring organizational performance [4, 16].

Given the complexity of an organization, it is clear that no single measure will be sufficient to capture all factors contributing to an organization's strategic and economic performance and, therefore, a number of measures will be needed [34, 39]. Finally, some past studies did not present a theoretical basis [4, 11, 16] and were primarily explorations of data. The remainder were based on innovation theory [6, 34] or microeconomics [25]. None of these past works was found to have been based on a central theory.

The major problem with these previous studies is that they failed to address two fundamental issues that are essential to this type of research: (1) adoption of a conceptual framework to define IT investment and organizational performance and, (2) identification of relevant and accurate measures to operationalize these concepts.

## Research Framework

IN THIS SECTION WE SEEK TO IDENTIFY USEFUL MEASURES for IT investment and organizational strategic and economic performance. The determination of IT investment is problematic because of the lack of consensus in defining and measuring such investment. Researchers have difficulty in agreeing as to what actually constitutes IT [36]. Some used a narrow definition that includes only an information system [34], while others used a broader definition that also includes data communications. There is also little agreement as to what measures to use. Some used direct measures [16] while others used surrogate measures [11].

The task was further complicated by the fact that there is little agreement on the concept of strategic performance measurement $[7]$ . Profitability measures $[37]$ have been used, as have financial market measures $[28]$ . Yet another researcher used the concept of slack resource management, devising and using alternative variables $[8]$ .

## IT Investment Measures

One of the most difficult tasks involved in this research was determining how to measure IT investment and how to relate it to existing theory. In addition to the problems mentioned above, data collection is difficult: firms are either not tracking their IT investment data, or have not been willing to divulge it.

Previous researchers have used various ratios as measures of IT investment. Bender [4] and Harris and Katz [16] used IT expense as a percentage of total operating expense as a measure. The Diebold Group [13] used IT budget as a percentage of revenue. A similar measure, IT expense to premium income, was also used by Harris and Katz [16]. All of these ratios have advantages and disadvantages. None, however, captures the firm's investment in decentralization due to increasing end-user computing that is sweeping through industry [36]. Measuring IT budget or expense gives researchers an idea as to “who the big spenders are—not the most successful users” [33]. Thus, there is no doubt that we required more definitive measures for this study.

Computerworld, in selecting its “Premier 100” list of most effective information system users [33], used the following measures of IT investment: annual organizational IT budget as a percentage of its revenue, total IT investment compared with competitors, staff and training investments, and total distribution of personal computers and terminals throughout the corporation. The first measure, annual IT budget as a percentage of revenue, shows how much an organization is spending vis-à-vis its competitors. The second measure—IT investment as a percentage of revenue—indicates how current an organization is maintaining its IT. The third and fourth measures, the percentage of IT budget spent for staff and the percentage of IT budget spent on training, indicate the willingness of the organization to properly manage and train its IT personnel and end users. The final measure, the number of PCs and terminals as a percentage of employees, indicates the extent to which users have access to IT.

Benjamin [5] predicted that by 1990 end-user computing expenditures would constitute over 33 percent of total IT investment (compared with 14 percent in 1980). These increased expenditures are expected to be in the form of hardware and software, as well as support for training and consulting. It is, therefore, imperative that investment in PCs and terminals be included as a part of the total investment in IT. The total number of PCs and terminals, and the ratio of PCs and terminals to employees appear to be valid proxy measures for investment in PCs and terminals.

While end users can perform most of their programming activities themselves, they need expert assistance from end-user support specialists for more difficult applications [12]. This support could be provided in the form of training in the use of hardware, software, and data communication systems. End users must also consult experts regarding hardware, software selection, and purchasing. Information system managers not only must hire well-trained staff, but must also provide training on an ongoing basis to maintain the staff's knowledge of current developments in technology. Thus, percentages of revenues and budget used for IT staff and for their training appear to be good indicators of an organization's IT investment as regards IT personnel, including end-user specialists.

To measure an organization's IT investment, based on the foregoing discussion, the present research uses five ratios including IT budget as a percentage of revenue (BUDBYREV); value of an organization's IT as a percentage of revenue (ITVALBYREV); percentage of IT budget spent on staff (BUDBYSTA); percentage of IT budget spent on the training of IT staff (BUDBYTRA); number of PCs and terminals as a percentage of total employees (PCSEMP). The first ratio reveals how much an organization is spending on IT relative to its competitors. The IT value ratio reflects the currency of a firm's technology. It is derived by dividing IT value by total revenue; IT value figures are estimates of the current market value for all IT equipment as if these were purchased today [33]. The staff spending criterion reflects the organization's relative investment in IT staff. Because information system managers must keep their employees well trained, the training ratio is used to measure the relative amount spent by organizations for this purpose. The last ratio measures the extent to which the organization has made IT available to organizational users.

## Measuring Strategic and Economic Performance

Determining organizational performance is extremely difficult and sometimes confusing since many combinations of social and economic measures can be adopted.

Before the concept of organizational performance can be applied, however, a conceptual framework must be adopted and accurate measures that operationalize organizational performance must be identified.

For this study it was decided to use the Systems Resource Approach [38] as the framework for measuring organizational performance. Under this approach, organizational performance is measured using the key internal and external factors upon which the organization depends for survival. Within this framework, performance measures that had been examined in previous research were considered for use in this study.

Woo and Willard [37] found profitability measures such as return on investment, return on sales, and growth in revenues to be important organizational economic performance measures. Peters and Waterman [28] suggested the inclusion of financial market measures such as the market to book value ratio as a determinant of a firm's strategic and economic performance. Chakravarthy [8] argued, however, that profitability or financial market measures alone are incapable of measuring organizational strategic and economic performance because they are concerned primarily with maximization of stockholder's wealth; the interests of other stakeholders are ignored. Based on extensive research, Chakravarthy suggested the inclusion of an additional productivity measure such as sales revenue per employee, and additional variables that measure the effectiveness of an organization's management of slack resources, such as sales to total assets, which can capture a firm's potential future performance.

Based on a review of previous research, six direct measures of organizational strategic and economic performance were selected for use in this study: return on investment (ROI), return on sales (ROS), growth in revenue (GINR), sales by total assets (SBYTA), sales by employee (SBYE), and market to book value (MVTOBV).

## Research Method

## The Sample

ONE HUNDRED FIRMS REPORTED IN THE Computerworld "Premier 100" list for 1989 were initially selected for the study [33]. In order for a firm to be included in Computerworld's Premier 100 group, it "must be publicly traded, U.S. based, and in the top half of its vertical industry group as listed in the annual Fortune Industrial 500 or Fortune Service 500." Information systems-oriented and information products-oriented organizations were not included. These firms were determined by Computerworld to be the most effective information system users in 1989, referring to them as those "that know how to closely integrate information systems with business strategy and culture" [33].

The data-gathering procedure included mail surveys and telephone interviews. In those few cases in which the organization did not supply the data, Computerworld estimated the data based on “market research and other sources” [10]. This could be a problem. Another problem might be that responding firms may not have defined IT expenditures in the same way. Despite these potential drawbacks, however, the authors consider the Computerworld data to be the best that are currently available and as such they were used for this research.

## Data Collection

The data for the IT investment measures were obtained from the Computerworld "Premier 100" list for 1989 [10]. The data for the six organizational performance measures were collected from the Compact DISCLOSURE database, $^{1}$ which provides operating and financial information obtained from SEC-required annual, quarterly, and other reports, for some 12,000 companies. More specifically, it provides two-year comparisons of balance sheets, three-year comparisons of income statements, and a five-year summary of operating income, sales, and earning per share. Since the norms for IT investment and organizational performance data vary from industry to industry, prior to conducting statistical analysis, each ratio was standardized by subtracting its mean from the ratio itself and then dividing the result by its standard deviation. Chakravarthy [8] used the standardization technique to account for interindustry variance among ratios.

## Canonical Correlation Analysis

Canonical correlation analysis was performed as the second step in the data analysis. This method provides a simultaneous analysis of sets of dependent variables and sets of independent variables, and thus will provide a more persuasive case (if there is one)

for the aforementioned relationship, as well as give some idea as to the nature of this relationship. In general, canonical analysis can be used to determine whether two sets of variables are statistically independent of each other in a linear sense or, conversely, to ascertain the magnitude of their multivariate relationships $[19, 20]$ . By simultaneously considering both multiple dependent and multiple independent variables, it is possible to control for suppressor or moderator effects that may exist among the various dependent variables.

The procedure works in the following way [24]. First, it finds a linear composite of the multiple (say p) dependent variables and a linear composite of the multiple (say q) independent variables. The dependent linear composite is then correlated with the independent linear composite to obtain a canonical function. This process continues until a minimum of $(p, q)$ canonical functions are obtained that capture all the correlation between the sets of the dependent and independent variables [26].

Table 7 summarizes the results of the standard canonical analysis through the use of SAS [31]. The results show that the strategic and economic measures, as a group, are significantly related to IT investment measures. The reason for this significant relationship is explained by the fact that canonical correlation considers all dependent and independent variables simultaneously. As such the results provide a combined effect of IT investment measures on organizational performance measures and this effect is usually stronger and more significant than the individual effects.

The SAS program produced five canonical functions from the data. As stated, a minimum of $(p, q)$ canonical functions are obtained where p and q are the number of dependent and independent variables respectively. Since there were only five IT investment measures as opposed to six organizational performance measures, only five canonical functions were derived. These canonical functions capture all the correlation between the sets of the dependent and independent variables [26]. Only two canonical functions, however, are shown and explained in the present study. The reasons for this are presented in the following paragraphs.

The F statistics reveal that only one of the five canonical functions exceeds the critical value at the 0.05 level of significance. However, use of a single criterion for deciding on whether a canonical function should be interpreted or not is too restrictive. Stewart and Love [32], therefore, suggested the use of redundancy analysis in addition to the level of significance in determining which functions should be included in the interpretation.

Redundancy is defined as the ability of a set of independent variables (e.g., IT investment measures), taken as a set, to explain variation in the dependent variables (e.g., organizational performance measures) taken one at a time. It is common practice to interpret only those canonical functions that contribute significantly to redundancy with the dependent variable set (i.e., contribute most in explaining the variability in the dependent set). This is because we are usually interested in knowing how much variability of the dependent set can be explained by the independent set. Table 8 summarizes the redundancy analysis for the dependent variable (i.e., organizational strategic and economic performance) set. The results indicate that the total redundancy is 10.4 percent of the variance in the dependent set. The first canonical function accounts for 47.12 percent and the second explains 38.46 percent of the total redundancy (Table 8, column 5). Since the first and second canonical functions contributed by far the most to the total redundancy, it was decided to interpret these functions. The third, fourth, and fifth functions contributed very little to the total redundancy, and as such these were not interpreted.

## Discussion

ALTHOUGH CORRELATION ANALYSIS INDICATES THAT THE INDIVIDUAL IT investment measures are generally weakly associated with individual organizational strategic and economic performance variables, canonical analysis suggests that, by considering the combined effect of IT investment measures, the prediction level can be increased significantly. As a matter of fact, the latter analysis reveals a significant relationship between the IT investment variables and the organizational strategic and economic performance variables. This supports findings by Harris and Katz [16] and Bender [4].

The canonical results of this study are a fascinating combination of strongly positive and strongly negative findings. For example, according to canonical function 1, IT budget spent on employee training is positively and significantly related to sales by total assets, market value to book value, and return on investment. Intuitively this is to be expected. As more is spent on the training of employees, assuming that the funds are effectively used, the better the employees are expected to be at what they do, thereby pushing the organization to a higher level of performance. This should in turn result in an increase in the net worth of the organization and return on investment to shareholders. This also makes sense from a practical point of view. Training of employees should, therefore, be one of the more important components of IT investment.

The same canonical function also shows that IT budget as a percentage of total revenue is negatively and significantly correlated with return on investment, sales by total assets, and market to book value. It is not quite clear as to why this is the case. Our interpretation is that some of the highly rated companies used in this study may be spending excessive amounts on IT as a percentage of total revenue without a proper business strategy. This may result in negative returns on these investments for these firms. Bender $[4]$ suggests that “there may be an optimum investment in IT at a level between 20 percent to 25 percent of the total general expense.” In addition, factors not considered (such as economic conditions, competitive circumstances, and management performance, for example) could also have played a role in the negative relationships. Barua et al. $[3]$ , through the development of an economic model, demonstrated how some of these variables can affect IT investment. However, this is beyond the scope of this research.

The aforementioned positive and negative findings, when considered together, lead to an interesting conclusion. They seem to suggest that it is important for an organization not only to invest sufficiently in IT (as a matter of fact an organization should find its optimum level of IT investment, perhaps by using relevant industry data as a basis for evaluating its own position with regard to expenditures $[4]$ ), but perhaps even more importantly it must provide its employees adequate IT training in order to reap the benefits of that investment. This is only logical; untrained employees working with sophisticated technology are not particularly productive.

While the first canonical function did not indicate a relationship between number of PCs per employee and sales by employee or between number of PCs per employee and return on sales, the second canonical function shows that the number of PCs per employee, together with training, is highly associated with sales by employee and return on sales. Thus, while the number of PCs per employee in itself is not related to increased sales by employee or return on sales, when combined with training does appear related to sales by employee and return on sales. This makes sense, as an organization should not expect sales by employee and return on sales to increase by merely providing PCs to employees. Employees must be trained on this equipment before the organization can hope to achieve the benefit of such investment $[10]$ .

These same two IT investment measures, number of PCs per employee and IT budget spent on employee training, were negatively related to the organizational performance variable sales by total assets. On the surface this would indicate that a higher number of PCs per employee and increased employee training result in lower sales by total assets—an outcome contrary to the notion that IT is beneficial to organizational performance. However, because increases or decreases in total assets, and thus in the ratio sales by total assets (calculated as sales/total assets), occur independently of the number of PCs per employee and employee training, no direct relationship between the measures, positive or negative, would necessarily be anticipated. Thus, the relationships noted between these measures in this study suggest the need for additional research to determine whether the findings here were peculiar to the sample used or indicative of some unexpected phenomenon.

Finally, the second canonical function shows that an organization's IT value as a percentage of its revenue was positively and significantly correlated with sales by total assets but, at the same time, negatively correlated with sales by employee and return on sales. This result has two implications. First, this implies that organizations with larger amounts of IT investment relative to revenues achieve a higher amount of sales relative to total assets. This supports the idea that total investment in IT is beneficial to organizational performance or, in this case, beneficial specifically to the sales function. Second, any relationship between IT investment and sales by employee would depend to a large extent on the specific nature of the IT; that is, whether the IT is more in the form of mainframes and other larger applications, or whether it is decentralized such as in the form of PCs. Because IT value as a percentage of revenue—the gross measure of IT investment—represents much more in the way of IT than only PCs, it is a less precise measure than PCs per employee in evaluating relationships between IT investment and return on sales and sales by employee. Thus, the negative relationships found between these variables are considered less meaningful in this cross-sectional study of relationships between IT investment measures and organizational performance measures. A longitudinal study is necessary to more thoroughly investigate the full impact of IT value as a percentage of revenues on the organizational performance measures.

We strongly urge that such studies be conducted. In order to conduct multisite, multiperiod research of different types of IT investments researchers will, however, need the assistance of industry and business. We urge industry and business to maintain detailed records of IT investment data and to make the data available to the academic community. We also urge the academic community to guarantee the privacy of such data and make the ensuing study results available to the business community.

It is also important to note that IT investment in itself does not ensure organizational success, but rather requires an appropriate business strategy to guide it [22]. The use of IT without an accompanying strategy and restructuring can result in serious problems within an organization [23]. In many cases failure to realize benefits from IT investment is directly related to an organization's inability to integrate the use and management of IT infrastructure into the mainstream of the organization [18]. Henderson [18] provides a descriptive model for such an integration. The model contains guidelines for building relationships between information systems and the line organizations. Even with a good alignment between IT and business strategy, some organizations may still suffer from mismanagement of its IT investment, resulting in little or no return on such investment.

One organizational performance ratio, growth in revenue, and one IT ratio, percentage of IT budget spent on staff, did not contribute to the multivariate relationship between the IT investment and organizational performance measures. It is very possible that growth in revenue is affected by myriad other performance factors and, therefore, any IT impact is difficult to identify. It is interesting to note that while the value of investment in IT to total revenue and percentage of IT budget spent on training were found to be important, the percentage of IT budget spent on staff was not significant. This indicates the possibility that the size of the IT staff is not important to superior organizational strategic and economic performance. A smaller number of employees with the proper equipment and training can perhaps perform at least as well as a larger number of less well equipped and less well trained personnel.

## A Model for Measuring the Effect of IT Investment on Organizational Strategic and Economic Performance

THE RESEARCH FINDINGS SUGGEST A PRACTICAL APPROACH FOR EVALUATING the effect of IT investment on organizational performance. In this section the implications of the findings are first outlined, followed by presentation of a model that depicts a framework for management evaluation of the relationship between IT investment and organizational strategic and economic performance.

The following implications for corporate management result from the research findings. The reader is, however, cautioned that no distinction is made in this study between the strategic, informational, or transactional systems (for a definition of these systems see $[35]$ ). It is quite possible that these implications may vary from one system to another and that is why it is all the more important to conduct further investigation to substantiate their validity.

1. Steady investment in information technology should be considered for enhancing organizational strategic and economic performance.

2. To improve organizational performance, IT investment must be accompanied by appropriate employee training.

3. When evaluating the impact of IT investment on organizational strategic and economic performance, performance measures such as sales by total assets, market value to book value, return on investment, sales by employee, and return on sales should be considered. We must warn the reader here that the measure market value to book value could be somewhat problematic in a fluctuating economy.

4. In view of the questions raised by the present study on the use of growth in revenue as a valid organizational performance measure, further study must be considered in the areas of transactional, informational, and strategic IT investment. This is imperative before such variables can be further used as organizational performance measures to investigate the impact of IT investment on organizational performance.

5. Measures such as IT budget as a percentage of revenue, percentage of IT budget spent on IT training, number of PCs and terminals as a percentage of total employees, and estimated IT value as a percentage of revenue should be considered for use as measures of IT investment.

6. Percentage of IT budget spent on staff should not be considered as an IT investment measure.

Previous studies have commented on the relationship between organizational performance and IT investment, but none, to our knowledge, has provided guidance for evaluating this relationship. An empirically validated framework for such evaluation is presented in figure 1. We must emphasize here that this is only a partial framework and other studies must be conducted to complete the model. The framework includes, from the IT perspective, budget and equipment dimensions. From the organizational perspective, it includes only the financial and sales dimensions.

The budget dimension includes organizational IT budget as a percentage of revenue (BUDBYREV) and the percentage of this budget spent on training staff (BUDBYTRA). The equipment dimension includes number of PCs and terminals available as a percentage of total employees (PCSEMP) and the value of an organization's IT as a percentage of revenue (ITVALBYREV).

Sales by total assets (SBYTA), market value to book value (MVTOBV), and return on investment (ROI) form the financial dimension of organizational performance, as related to IT investment. Sales by employee (SBYE), sales by total assets (SBYTA), and return on sales (ROS), on the other hand, form the sales dimension. The financial dimension is affected by the overall IT budget and IT employee training (i.e., the IT budget dimension), while the sales dimension is influenced by IT equipment and IT value (i.e., the IT equipment dimension), and also by the percentage of it budget spent on training.

## Conclusion and Limitations

THE FUNDAMENTAL CONCLUSION OF THIS RESEARCH IS THAT IT investment appears to be related to organizational strategic and economic performance. Obviously, it would be naive to suggest that this performance is due solely to investment in IT. Many other factors, including but not limited to economic conditions, competitive circumstances, and managerial astuteness are at least equally, or even more important. On the other hand, however, it can be argued that the relationship of IT investment to organizational strategic and economic performance measures such as return on investment, return on sales, and market to book value was diluted somewhat by the effects of other factors.

The research indicated a weak relationship between the individual IT investment measures and individual organizational performance measures. When grouped, however, the correlation increased significantly. In particular, the organizational performance measures sales by employee, sales by total assets, market value to book value, return on investment, and return on sales were related to the IT investment measures number of PCs and terminals as a percentage of total employees, IT budget as a percentage of revenue, percentage of IT budget spent on IT staff training, and estimated IT value as a percentage of revenue, indicating that these should be considered for measuring IT investment effect. The organizational performance measure growth in revenue and IT investment measure percentage of IT budget spent on staff were not significantly related to other measures and therefore were not indicated to be useful for investigating possible effects of IT investment on organizational strategic and economic performance.

By focusing on relevant measures and their relationships, this study suggests tentative guidelines to managers for making IT investment decisions. Considering the present constraints on the obtaining of all appropriate data, we believe the research made a positive contribution in that direction.

Some cautions should, however, be stated regarding these conclusions. The results should be interpreted carefully because this was a cross-sectional study. A longitudinal study will be necessary in order to derive a meaningful conclusion in situations where IT investment may not necessarily result in immediate benefits. The research was based in part on the data provided by Computerworld and thus represented information on the so-called 100 most effective organizations, a nonrandom sample. Certain of these data were estimated by Computerworld or its sources. Also, the organizations included represented different industries. The study should be replicated using organizations randomly selected within single industries. Finally, some of the data collected may have been influenced by factors not related to IT. These need to be sorted out before more specific relationships can be identified.

Before such research studies can be undertaken, however, organizations must cooperate by tracking their organizationwide IT investment and strategic and economic performance data. Equally important, they must allow researchers access to the data, or perhaps report the data to an appropriate regulatory agency that could then provide the data to researchers after ensuring the necessary confidentiality. Researchers, in turn, can make the results of their studies available to interested organizations.
