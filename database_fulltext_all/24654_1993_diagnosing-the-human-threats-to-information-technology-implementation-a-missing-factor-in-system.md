# Diagnosing the Human Threats to Information Technology Implementation: A Missing Factor in Systems Analysis Illustrated in a Case Study

HAROLD G. LEVINE AND DON ROSSMOORE

HAROLD G. LEVINE is an Associate Professor in the Graduate School of Education, University of California at Los Angeles. Trained as an anthropologist, he has been interested in organizations and management-related issues for nearly twenty years. His current work, with Don Rossmoore, includes the study of the politics of IT implementation.

DON ROSSMOORE is Managing Partner of Rossmoore Associates, Inc., a Los Angeles-based management consulting firm with specialities in strategy formulation, implementation, reorganization, and re-engineering.

ABSTRACT: Information technology (IT) implementation is likely to be a complex and difficult process involving both the technical and social systems of an organization. Much of the theory and practice of organizational IT implementation assumes that organizational decisions—whether technical or social—are consequences of individuals and organizational units objectively collecting, evaluating, and applying information in a rational manner to make choices on behalf of the organization. However, recent data from case studies on IT implementation suggest that rationality may be the exception, rather than the rule. One important limitation, then, of traditional research and systems analysis methods is that they fail to account for the human factors that impact IT implementation. As a result, there is no systematic way to incorporate what we might learn from individual action and practice into the model. A theoretical perspective and research methodology that successfully encompass and expand the normative approach in mainstream science is “action science.” Action science is a theory of action that helps explain how, and why, individuals behave as they do; and how their actions impact their organizations. THE LURE OF NEW INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY (IT) SYSTEMS to enhance organizational competitiveness and productivity remains powerful despite recent admonitions that new systems may only be the price that must be paid to stay in business [6]. There are further cautions that organizations must first carefully identify aspects of their business that would receive added value from new technology [7, 8, 22, 28]. Other data, from academic researcher and practitioner alike, suggest that, whatever its ultimate benefit for an organization's business, IT implementation is likely to be a complex and difficult process involving both the technical and social systems of the organization.

In general, the theory and practice of organizational information systems (IS) design and implementation tends to be prescriptive and normative [e.g., 11, 32]. A key assumption of this work is that organizational decision making—whether related to technical or social systems—can be best conceptualized in terms of the “rational model.” This model holds that organizational decisions are consequences of individuals and organizational units objectively collecting, evaluating, and applying information in a rational manner to make choices on behalf of the organization. However, as one critic of the rational model notes, managers only intend to use “normatively correct decision procedures, [but] they are generally unsuccessful, due to either intellectual or resource constraints. The model,” he continues, “is really only a model; and, in this case, a fragmentary representation of what actually occurs” [15, p. 49].

The disjunction between the rational model and the world of practice occurs for two reasons. First, normative thinking and rationality in the social sciences, at least from the standpoint of IS researchers, is based upon the natural sciences where a positivist philosophy of science predominates. The tenets of positivism define the scientific enterprise in terms of covering law explanations of phenomena, hypothesis testing, empirically falsifiable theories, and the like $[12, 18, 20]$ . Unfortunately, the use of such a research agenda is argued by others to result in a misrepresentation or oversimplification of the complexities of human action-in-context and an inability to incorporate the social meanings by which actors interpret, and act upon, the behaviors of others $[14, 26]$ . Second, normative models are not specifically designed to produce knowledge that can inform individual action and practice. Rather, the audience for such theories is typically the scientific community that in turn leaves the problems of practice to applied social scientists [5].

A theoretical perspective and research methodology that successfully encompass, but also importantly expand, the normative approach in mainstream science is the “action science” of Chris Argyris, Donald Schon, and their colleagues $[3, 4, 5, 29, 30]$ . One fundamental goal of “action science” is the production of knowledge that contributes both to practice and to mainstream science. To do this, Argyris and others have developed a theory of action that helps explain how, and why, individuals behave as they do; and how their actions affect their organizations.

In the action science approach to human behavior, cognitions—referred to as “theories”—precede, and govern, action $[2, 5]$ . There are two distinct types of such theories: espoused theories and theories-in-use. Both types coexist simultaneously within individual actors, although individuals are unaware of the differences between the two. Espoused theories are publicly held, acknowledged, and, often, defended values and beliefs. In organizational contexts, they are typically synonymous with the ideals of the organization. In an IT context, for example, such values are likely to include rational decision making, clear specification, and agreement on the goals and procedures for the system, cooperation, and so forth.

Unfortunately, individuals unconsciously act on the basis of their theories-in-use, not their espoused theories. The difference is that espoused theories are what individuals claim to be true and to be the basis for their action. Theories-in-use, on the other hand, are inferred by the researcher from what individuals actually do. Argyris and others have demonstrated that theories-in-use have a greater impact on behavior than espoused theories $[2, 4, 5]$ . The theories-in-use are motivated in part by the needs of individuals to protect their interests and to minimize risks associated with the conduct of their lives. In professional contexts they often include such values as maximize winning, control disagreement, show loyalty, and appear rational.

The consequence of acting upon theories-in-use rather than espoused theories is the production of action routines that depart from the very organizational ideals that individuals so readily and enthusiastically champion. By unconsciously, but faithfully, following their theories-in-use, individuals typically act masterfully in ways that create error and misunderstandings, what Argyris refers to as “skilled incompetence.” [1].

Examples of such behaviors include discounting oneself as the source of problems or errors while attributing the cause either to others or to uncontrollable external conditions; speaking at high levels of inference while assuming that the message is obvious, unambiguous, and unassailable; advocating a position with inadequate data or in a way that makes disagreement appear disloyal; and so forth [2]. In this way, people and the organizations of which they are a part become repositories of unresolved issues, unsurfaced data that challenge accepted belief, miscommunications, mistrustful and/or protective relationships, and competing points of view that are undiscussed, and undiscussable. Because individuals are often uncomfortable with issues they cannot discuss, or see themselves being at risk for bringing them out into the open since they conflict with espoused norms of “proper” behavior and values, they further camouflage them. As a result, the fact that there are important issues that are both undiscussed and undiscussable is itself undiscussable. As Argyris has shown, however, it is precisely these unsurfaced, and undiscussed, issues and data that organizations must explicitly identify in order to solve their most important problems [5].

Obviously, the consequences of this style of thinking and action can be disastrous for organizations. In the case of IT management they can lead to delayed systems, systems that are avoided or bypassed by key constituencies, systems that are not justified by a satisfactory business strategy, and systems that generate organizational conflict and opposition. What appears to the outsider to be organizational “irrationality” is, in fact, a predictable outcome of individuals whose thoughts and actions have not been satisfactorily understood, surfaced, and managed.

In this paper, our primary goal is to employ an action science perspective to understand how a large financial services firm was inhibited from implementing a major IT effort in a timely, effective, and error-free way because of the thought and action routines of those charged with designing and implementing the system. In particular, we look for disagreements and unproductive action routines among these individuals, and evidence of their inability to surface and discuss them. Before we present our data, however, we review two well-documented studies of IT implementation that are available in the literature. We hope to show that the level of analysis required by action science is implied, but not made explicit in, these studies.

A related, though largely implicit, goal of this study is to supply a framework, based on action science, for designing and managing organizational IT change. That is, we wish to begin to answer the question of what form scientific knowledge must take if it is to help us in reducing unsuccessful implementation efforts. We wish to talk about organizations in ways that will allow the individuals who manage them, and the outsiders who consult with them, to learn the necessary skills to design and manage organizational IT change in theoretically adequate ways.

## Case Studies of IT Implementation: Issues, Examples, and Learnings

AN IMPORTANT METHOD OF CHOICE FOR RESEARCHERS interested in documenting IT implementation has been case study research. That is, using characteristic case study methods such as interviews with key personnel, observations of meetings and work settings, and document analysis [23, 31], researchers have described, often in rich detail, organizational implementation efforts. In addition, a few authors have sought to account for the success or failure of these efforts in terms of more abstract sociopolitical conditions and variables [e.g., 13, 20, 21].

Although case study empirical work on corporate IT systems has only a relatively short history in the IS literature, it appears to be growing as a research method of choice $[9, 10, 16, 17, 19, 20, 24, 27]$ . However, there has been recent debate about how “scientific” such work can be. The debate centers on whether conclusions extracted from observations in uncontrolled single (or multiple) field situations, in the absence of formal, testable hypotheses, where the falsifiability of theory and a theory’s implications and the generalizability of a study’s findings are perhaps differently construed than in the natural sciences, can be considered as valid scientific work.

Although it is not possible here to examine the intricacies of this epistemological debate, researchers in IS seem to have formulated two distinct responses. One group has cast case study methodology in terms of the canons of mainstream, positivist science [e.g., 18, 20], while the second group seems to have taken a more interpretive point of view where understanding and theory building outweigh traditional hypothesis testing [24, 26].

Our own approach in the case that follows parallels that taken by Argyris and his colleagues. That is, our research practice is informed by the need to subject our findings to criteria of falsifiability, to link inference and conclusion closely to data, to seek rival plausible hypotheses for our conclusions, and to contribute to the ongoing development and testing, through empirical work, of a general theory of human action. Our approach differs, somewhat, in the value we place on interpretive insight and analysis, the hallmark of much case study research performed in other social science disciplines such as anthropology.

Whatever the epistemological orientations of the IT field studies, the often rich data collected in these studies provide clues about the ways in which unstated and unresolved conflicts and disagreements drive the IT planning and implementation process. Two examples follow.

Clemons and Weber [10] documented how various disagreements and misunderstandings became part of the history and management of a major IT restructuring at National Westminster Bank (U.K.). For example, they showed how the Business Case Team, which was formed of senior management to provide a business justification to the bank's board for the large technology investment, clearly saw that the IT project in itself would not yield a competitive edge. This was because their data showed that other banks were making the same investments. This was noted in their final report. However, they succumbed to the bank norm which was to justify projects in terms of traditional payback period analysis: could the project be justified in terms of cost savings and/or new or expanded revenue streams? The team made such a case, despite opinion that "you cannot formally justify replacing systems. You cannot stand up and demonstrate hard benefits." [10, p. 5].

As another example from the National Westminster study, a third-party contractor was brought in to determine user requirements and manage the project. When the consultants appeared they discovered what neither they nor National Westminster suspected: everyone, especially the consultants, assumed the bank knew what it wanted. In fact, “when they went to the users they found neither consistency nor a sensible set of requirements” [10, p. 7]. In addition, the consultants’ group was brought in when National Westminster’s business needs and strategic plans were not yet set. As a result, the consultants developed IT strategies somewhat independently of the business needs. As one manager noted, “We required business decisions, not technical decisions as we had originally thought” [10, p. 7]. This account shows that managers disagreed about how to proceed toward development and that unresolved issues regarding the bank’s emerging new business strategies were largely undiscussed and private.

In another study, Markus [20] documented the introduction of a financial information system (FIS) for the “Golden Triangle Corporation” (GTC). This system computerized, automated, and standardized most of the financial reporting function of GTC. Its main thrust, however, was perceived by its users as removing the autonomous financial accounting of individual departments to the corporate accounting department. In her account, Markus details the resistance that ensued.

One particular set of problems emerged from the effort expended by the FIS task force to find a divisional volunteer to try the new system before the others would be required to subscribe. Ten months after the volunteer division began using FIS, one of its accountants noted in a memo that, with the exception of providing more detailed information, “the FIS system has not been beneficial to us” [20, p. 434]. While technical recommendations were made, and implemented slowly over the next year or more, other divisions voluntarily came on board despite the difficulties encountered by the first division.

The later divisions may have been no happier with FIS than the original volunteer. Markus reports that one division, after adopting FIS, continued using its old accounting methods even though doing so required twice the effort. Frequent inconsistencies between the two sets of books ensued, and the divisional accountants attested to the accuracy of the original system and blamed FIS. This persisted for two years. Other divisional accountants “also admitted to slight ‘data fudging’ to circumvent the technical and human factors problems with the system” [20, p. 435].

As a final example, the individual who originally complained about the lack of benefit to his division from the FIS wrote another memo:

After being on FIS for several months, I expressed the opinion that the system was basically of little benefit. After two years and seven months, my opinion has not changed. Even worse, it seems to have become a system that is running people rather than people utilizing the system [20, p. 435].

Although Markus documents additional errors and disagreements, the data cited above are sufficient to reveal how individuals at GTC made heart-felt, but ineffectual, complaints that did not result in timely action, how managers bypassed the new FIS system because it was not perceived as meeting their needs or because it presented additional technical problems, and how blame for discrepancies between the two sets of books was placed on the FIS system. In the last quotation we see a very concerned employee venting his disappointment. However, he does so in a way that involves a high level of inference with no examples given, while assuming that what is being communicated is concrete and obvious. It is also likely that in the thirty-one months of experience with FIS, many of this manager's views were either privately held or ineffectually expressed in ways that could not lead to real resolution. Thus, as with the Clemons and Weber study cited above, there is evidence in this case study for unproductive action routines that stem from privately held theories-in-use and that conflict with the organizationally sanctioned espoused values. There is also evidence from these two studies of the presence of serious management issues related to the IS that influence its implementation. None of the evidence suggests that these issues are either discussed or discussable. As a result, they cannot be managed, and improvements to the IS implementation cannot be made.

We conclude from these, and other IT case studies available in the literature, that “organizationally rational” decision making may be less common in IT development projects than one might expect, and that rationality actually competes both with entrenched organizational norms of how business is done, as well as with other, more powerful human decision-making routines accounted for in the action science model of individual and organizational behavior. To date, these routines and their consequences have not been systematically investigated in IT implementation.

## Methods

THE STUDY OF BREMERTON INC. (a pseudonym) and its IT project known as “Threshold” occurred continuously over a three-month period from late 1991 through the first quarter of 1992. Primary data collection was through a series of interviews (sometimes more than one) with a total of seventy-seven individuals. These included the CEO, COO, the executive vice presidents (EVP), most senior vice presidents (SVP), foreign affiliates of BI, key vice presidents (VP) involved with the Threshold project, and a large number of development staff (managers and technicians) responsible for Threshold and its integration with current Bremerton data processing systems. Functional groups represented in our interview survey included Operations, Human Resources, Marketing, Finance, Product Services, Customer Relations, Development, IT Systems, Technical Services, and Threshold. In addition, we interviewed all members of the Threshold Executive Task Force, which was composed of a cross-section of senior management. Thus, all management levels and functional groups involved with Threshold were represented in our interview sample.

The interview procedures and protocols we used with Bremerton managers stem directly from the action science perspective we have adopted for studying organizational behavior. That is, a theoretically adequate description of behavior depends upon identifying, and distinguishing between, individuals' espoused theories and their theories-in-use. As Argyris and his colleagues have noted $[5]$ , espoused theories are relatively easily elicited. This is because they mirror the organizational ideals that individuals most eagerly and consciously wish to emulate. "Why" questions and other probes intended to produce causal statements by research participants produce these espoused theories with sufficient accuracy. They are the "bread-and-butter" of most traditional organizational diagnostic research.

Unfortunately, these interviewing strategies do not elicit the theories-in-use that have the more powerful effect on individual action. Theories-in-use, we have argued, are less accessible to participants and must be inferred by the researcher from what interviewees say, do, and say they do. The inferences are themselves subject to verification and testing with the person being interviewed. Because of such requirements, action science interviewing must use a different set of procedures than those used as a part of traditional organizational research.

Specifically, our interviews used the following strategies to retrieve the actual reasoning—the theories-in-use—of Bremerton executives, managers, and employees. We began by asking interviewees both general and specific questions about Threshold and their experiences of, and with, the project. Not unexpectedly, we found that all those interviewed made inferences and conclusions about the conduct of Threshold operations and planning that they then generalized to be true. Rather than accept these individual views as necessarily correct, however, we focused the interviewee on the task of making explicit the inferential steps he or she used to progress from data to conclusion(s). Then we actively challenged them with regard to both the data and the conclusion(s). Often we asked them to tell us to whom they had communicated their conclusions, and how; and we elicited their accounts of these directly observable events, that is, the conversations, meetings, memoranda. We did this by encouraging them to “role-play” the event—to talk to the interviewer as if the interviewer were the person they had spoken with in the actual event. Argyris [5] has demonstrated that the same theory-in-use that governs actual conversation governs role-play approximations, since neither are under the conscious control of the individual. The role-play technique helped the interviewees reconstruct their perceptions of the events, their cause-effect chains, attributions, and the like. We then pointed out discrepancies between what they said to us in the privacy of the confidential interview, and what they reported saying—during the role-play—in the actual event. In almost all cases, interviewees were surprised by the discrepancies, evidence of their lack of awareness of the differences between their espoused theories and their theories-in-use. Finally, we asked them to predict what would have happened if they would have publicly stated what they had said during the privacy of our interview. In almost all cases they predicted that they would have provoked an unacceptable response from others. This provided us with further evidence of their theories-in-use.

All of our interviews were conducted under conditions of explicit confidentiality. Bremerton managers were told that any comments made to us would be sanitized before being placed in a report. Data that could not be sanitized to the satisfaction of the interviewee (because, for example, only the interviewee was present with another person who was being discussed) would be omitted. All interviews were conducted by the authors and a third researcher of equal training and seniority. All three researchers actively recorded notes—and often verbatim comments—during the interviews.

The interviews lasted between one-half and one and one-half hours each, and examined basic questions relating to the employee's involvement with, understanding of, and reservations about the Threshold project. We were sensitive in our interview protocol to the issue of interviewer demand, not wanting to create an impression that we were interested merely in controversy and disagreement. In fact, our interview goals were broader. We wanted to understand the thinking of BI's personnel, BI's patterns of communication and decision making, and its characteristic implementation procedures. We asked for instances of cooperation and asked neutral questions with regard to the details of specific working relationships or conduct at meetings or their outcomes. In addition, we recognized that all Bremerton senior executives are forth-right in their manner of speaking and well aware of the political consequences of their decisions and the decision-making process itself. Asking them about such issues was a natural consequence of our research in their organization. Finally, an important guard against an observer effect was the presentation of our findings to Bremerton managers in ways that the findings could be challenged or disconfirmed. The findings presented in this paper incorporate the results of the challenges raised by BI employees.

In addition to these detailed, extensive interviews, one member of the research team participated in a key Threshold meeting. Documents produced by senior staff on the Threshold project were also examined for discrepancies and clues regarding espoused and actual values regarding Threshold and the status of its development. Qualitative analytic procedures, particularly content analysis, were employed to develop themes regarding management decision making that affected the scope of Threshold and its implementation.

## Background

BREMERTON INC. IS AN INTEGRATED FINANCIAL SERVICES COMPANY employing over 1,000 people. Its main operations center is the hub of a sizable electronic communications and data processing network. In fact, electronic communications capabilities form the core of its business. BI uses “off-the-shelf” hardware platforms adapted for its own business environment with systems and communications software applications designed by its Development group.

BI operates in a highly competitive market environment and has experienced an inconsistent pattern of growth in market share. Despite this, morale appears to have remained quite high over the last ten years, with few defections or resignations. Indeed, none of the seventy-seven individuals interviewed as part of this study expressed any disloyalty to the company or its current CEO, nor was there a lack of optimism that Bremerton would continue to grow and outperform its competition. When we inquired about workload, most of those asked reported being happily overutilized in their roles.

Within the last five years, BI's management team convinced the company's board of directors of the need for an IT initiative that would replace its core applications systems. Management argued that both the hardware and software components of its current applications systems were obsolete, preventing the company from expanding into new products and market services that would place Bremerton at a competitive advantage into the next century. The cost for this project, nicknamed “Threshold,” was set by the board of directors at a sum in excess of \$50 million. Along with the task of completing and integrating a new information technology for the company, management also acknowledged its mission of reviewing and, as necessary, redefining its business strategies. These strategies would drive the functional specifications for Threshold.

At its inception, Threshold was promoted as having far-reaching consequences for the conduct of Bremerton's business. Indeed, when asked about the importance of Threshold to the company's future and its strategic position, virtually all of those questioned asserted quite enthusiastically that Threshold was critical to Bremerton's business future. However, well into the third year of planning for Threshold, major disagreements about what the nature of this contribution would be, and how a new system was to be integrated with current company products and services, were still being privately expressed. Developmental milestones were slipped or missed, and serious doubts were being raised about the ability of Bremerton to produce Threshold in a timely manner. Covert bickering, blame, and worry were on the increase.

## Findings

OUR DATA INDICATE THAT BI WAS BESET by two characteristic sets of problems. These included competing definitions of Threshold as either a technology or a business project, and management indecision and decision-making styles that failed to resolve these, and other, disagreements. All of these factors had serious consequences for project development.

We outline our findings in these two areas below.

## Definition of Threshold and its Strategic Significance

Over two years into Threshold one of the SVP's on the Threshold Executive Task Force rhetorically asked, not without irony, whether Threshold was “a hog-caller or a one-speed bicycle.” Some of those at the meeting later reacted with indignation and not a slight sense of having been betrayed. Even so, the comments of others pointed to the same issue of lack of clarity and definition of what Threshold was to be. For example, one of the European Regional Managers privately noted that there were three tasks facing the Executive Task Force: (1) decide what Threshold was to be; (2) create business ownership within Bremerton to force the company to focus on the necessary business issues; and (3) communicate absolutely nothing to the board of directors until the company settled its current confusion.

Some believed they knew what Threshold was, or, at least, ought to be. They expressed genuine dismay when others either disagreed or appeared uncertain of Threshold's proposed capabilities or benefits. Indeed, many of the managers spoke quite convincingly and perceptively of what their business required and how Threshold would address these needs.

Given the clarity with which some individuals saw the goals for Threshold, it is perhaps surprising that they, and the others who were more uncertain, uniformly agreed that a key problem for Threshold was that the technical requirements were not being sufficiently driven by the business requirements. This view was characteristic of the responses of business and financial managers. One financial director, for example, noted privately:

We do have a problem with where we are on a business plan. . . . There is a quote in the notes from last month's meeting: "Threshold is not a systems project with major business implications; it is a business project with major systems implications" . . . We need to get really busy and at least get the key strategic elements of our long-term plan and get them in place—and start to drive Threshold from those business propositions.

Others, while expressing similar sentiments, argued that work on Threshold was premature since the overall strategic vision for Bremerton was still evolving:

There's not enough discussion on what are the business strategies that Threshold is serving, and how. Who will benefit from this, and how? . . . BI went from wanting to do away with the obsolete hardware platforms to rethinking their systems to trying to make the big plan for the next century. Everyone needs to be brought back to the basic question: what are the business strategies that Threshold is supposedly serving? . . . The input the operations group has had been very low in the organization. The organizational strategy is still evolving. When the CEO originally came, there was no direction and they needed to play catch-up. Now, finally, they can consider new strategizing for the future. Threshold, with all of its implicit and explicit design demand characteristics, comes just when the strategic thinking is just starting to take place.

It is important to note that this concern with Threshold's unclear relationship to business strategies and products was felt throughout the organization and, most importantly, among the majority of operations and development managers and staff. One technical manager said:

I don't know that the business people are capable of articulating a terrific business strategy for the technical people to take and figure out how to get there. There is a terrible, crying need for someone competent to come up with what they think what the business strategy for Threshold is for the next, say, 10 years—in simple terms.

A development manager cautioned as follows:

The rest of Threshold should stop until business managers are made accountable to put into place a series of plans that they are liable for performing—that will give the operating managers the information necessary to “spec out” the new specific transaction processes.

Bremerton's sluggishness in defining its own strategic vision was further complicated in three ways. First, at the urging of an outside management consultant brought into Bremerton by the SVP of Development, the CEO laid out his strategic vision for the company at a Threshold Executive Task Force meeting. Subsequent interviews with those in attendance at the meeting revealed the belief that there was a high degree of understanding of, and agreement with, these goals. The goals, which had significant consequences for the design of Threshold, included commitment to a global strategy; several distinct kinds of products; and dependable, flexible, and fast service. Unfortunately, the CEO's comments were made off-the-cuff, were at a high level of abstraction while appearing to be specific, and were not followed by an implementation strategy with built-in accountability criteria. Thus, everyone felt comfortable that they and their colleagues knew, and approved, the same long-range strategy, and assumed that the responsibility to carry it through had already been clearly delegated. These assumptions were never tested publicly.

The second problem for Bremerton was that, despite general agreement on Bremerton's future direction, critical disagreements and questions—which we uncovered in private interviews—remained. These issues significantly affected the design of Threshold: who should be BI's primary customers, and what transactional, reporting, and backup requirements would be satisfied; should the computer and communications networks serving the global financial company envisioned by the CEO be centralized or regionalized; and should responsibility for initiating the design specifics come from marketing, operations, or development?

The uncertainty regarding who would take responsibility for Threshold was the third factor complicating a clear articulation of the CEO's vision. With an entrenched and basically sound system in place, everyone who thought of changing it experienced two risks. Taking responsibility for altering a system that would change the way Bremerton did business might result in harming the company. The first risk was stepping out in front and being explicit about the changes they would make. This exposed them to attack from other factions within the company which, they feared, would undermine their position and future influence. Second, if they survived the first risk and then succeeded in defining the new system, they feared misjudging what new services and products were necessary to compete successfully.

Taking a hard look at their current platform, Bremerton's systems designers recognized the potential of hardware faster and less expensive than that which they had supported for nearly twenty years. They also knew, of course, that new software would have to be developed to take advantage of currently available hardware; and that software of the 1990s offered far more options than earlier packages. What the technologists could not predict is what systems would be necessary to take Bremerton into the next century. The dilemma was summed up by an Operations manager:

Let's define Threshold. I think the [EVP of Operation's] definition is the replacement of all our systems and electronic services over a period of time to replace systems and services which may be obsolescent and non-competitive. Having said that, it is clear that we should continuously upgrade our services, our equipment, and our systems for them to meet our business needs and those of our customers to remain competitive. The key question of Threshold is how can that change be best managed? And does it have to all be done at once in one big bang project? And how can we realistically implement the changes in a manageable way; and how can we identify the benefits for our customers and ourselves so that everything is supportive and it goes ahead and delivers those benefits? . . . Many people are involved in this process [of specifying design specification requirements]. We don't know whether the resulting requirements are what BI should do for the future, or whether it's a whole host of disjointed comments.

Hence, technicians and managers alike found themselves caught in a dilemma: they believed they could design a core platform that would improve upon the transactions they were currently supporting, and they knew that they did not want to limit their ability to produce the future, but as yet unknown, additions they assumed would be specified from elsewhere in the company.

This kind of development dilemma can be seen poignantly in an overhead prepared by the SVP of Development for an Executive Task Force meeting. Under “Threshold’s Strategic Role” the SVP noted, in one bullet: “Threshold represents an enormous opportunity to redefine our core business in ways that are advantageous to us, our customers, and all other participants in our business.” In the next bullet he noted “Alternatively, if Threshold is not informed by the best available understanding of our long-term strategic goals and direction it will constrain our business in unacceptable ways."

For those in Development, there was no discrepancy or conflict between the two goals. In fact, their simultaneous development represented the best possible strategy to keep Bremerton from ultimately losing market share.

Other BI executives, however, whether from a genuine lack of understanding of the Development group's plan, or mistrust of the Development group itself, variously supported one or the other of the two goals while disparaging the second. Thus, one SVP argued for more effort with the current systems:

The vision [of the CEO] is, in some cases, confusing the Threshold people. There's too much desire there to build a system for the way the company might be in the year 2000. As a result, not enough time may be spent on the requirements of the system for today. The CEO needs to sit down and discuss his vision with the marketing people so they can decide what their products will be and so that they can give information to the technical people. Because we have an open and participative style here, we've let in the technical people into vision setting and product development. The technical people want to be in the sexy development area. But there is also a need for them to fix the system for now. I see three tasks. First, replace the current support infrastructure for our current products. This will be seen as maintenance and not sexy. People don't want to do this. The second project is to get [ideas] on new inputs and offramps. Business people need to prioritize for these guys what the new requirements are for the system to be able to handle. The third task is to integrate the first two. We need to prioritize how these new products and requirements might impact the current system. Everyone has focused on the second project.

Earlier in the interview he had noted: "We shouldn't get bogged down in trying to be too flexible, in trying to plan for everything."

Alternatively, others argued that too much work was being expended on current hardware and software, with an inadequate effort in long-term strategizing. Said one marketing SVP:

Threshold is a methodology—a software/hardware configuration which should be used as a platform from which to deliver products and services. Because we have functional separation of duties, there is no accountability by the business side. They have not done what is required to put the necessary business input into it. They/we don't give a damn—we plan only short term. It takes more than the current planning window. The Development people are looking to create a new operating platform as an end in itself, rather than as a means of making the Bremerton business successful. The people who control product profit and loss haven't asked for Threshold. The operations (systems) people want it. We have the classic problem of an answer looking for a problem.

Another SVP, while echoing the theme promoted by the Marketing SVP, sounded an additional note of distrust of the Development group:

I don't think anyone is taking the time to focus on the issues in meaningful terms. Upgrading the systems is an operations thing, and we should let operations worry about it, but it needs help from business. What they need is help in turning to the rest of the company to ask for significant input to help them in their planning, but this is a disaster, because they ask questions in a way that alienates others. They have taken on a

godlike way. “Threshold is bigger than BI.” “Threshold solves everything.” “Go to Threshold for everything.” That is the problem. Threshold is only hardware and software. The right approach is not there. Get the business people involved from the disciplines that need answers. Then they tell systems what they want for the next 10 years.

In summary, Threshold's definition appeared to be a relatively clear and unequivocal rallying cry for executives and line personnel to guide BI into the next generation of products and services. In actuality, this seeming unanimity masked a lack of agreement about the next steps in development and who was accountable for developmental milestones. One might say, further, that there was tacit collusion among the management group to refrain from surfacing the disagreements and lack of clarity in order to appear to be a “team player.”

## Decision Making and the Politics of Implementation

A Threshold Executive Task Force was appointed and assigned responsibility for selecting a new hardware platform and for designing and implementing Bremerton's suite of application programs. Its initial instantiation included the COO, three of the EVPs, nine SVPs, several regional presidents and directors, as well as senior consultants. In all, twenty-one individuals representing all major functional groups of BI (including Finance, Operations, Development, and Marketing) sat on the Executive Task Force. Notably, the CEO for Bremerton chose not to be on the task force and publicly stated that he did not want to be involved with its deliberations.

Eventually, the CEO assigned the task of overseeing the implementation of Threshold to the COO. However, the COO and two of the three EVPs on the Executive Task Force reported directly to the CEO. All three were peers and each represented a different functional perspective on essential problems facing Threshold. Significant disagreements among the three were uncovered in our confidential interviews, including whether the global system should be regionalized or centralized and whether Operations or Marketing should control products and profit centers. These disagreements were never publicly surfaced in the appropriate organizational forums. When the CEO was informed of the existence of these disagreements, he commented: "These are smart, rational professionals who are all interested in the success of the company. It is their responsibility to work out these disagreements among themselves." He added: "If I have to get involved, I have the wrong people working for me."

After initial meetings the Executive Task Force established four functional committees representing the four major task areas that Threshold was intended to encompass. Members of the Executive Task Force participated on these functional groups, but the functional groups themselves delegated the actual work to working teams. The membership of the working teams was taken from the Development group which was headed by the SVP of Development who reported to the EVP of Operations. In addition, senior management also recruited a new VP, who reported to the SVP of Development, to be a public spokesman for Threshold and to lead the design effort within the Development group. Thus, at the time of this study, a VP, SVP, EVP, and the COO all had line responsibility for Threshold, as well as an Executive Task Force and its four functional groups and working teams.

Those working at the development level on Threshold were quite critical of management, often alluding to its lack of hands-on management and lack of strong project coordination:

There are no barriers [to a large project such as Threshold] as long as you have a strong project team with a strong project plan. This is not in place. Basically, you have the senior managers of Threshold (the SVP of Development and the VP) who have no hands-on working relationship with their staff, no central coordination; [and no] communication internally or externally. I've seen meetings where they don't know what the other is talking about. There was one meeting about 3 weeks ago when the EVP was present when 3 of the VP's people couldn't agree on the date for the hardware replacement. These people are awful in setting up meetings, saying who should be there, setting up distribution lists. It's always last minute. No planning or coordination. No advance agenda.

A related issue was the assessment, voiced by several of those interviewed, of a lack of accountability within management. The Chief Financial Officer commented:

I'd like people there who aren't distracted by their own political complications so that the [Steering Committee] meetings are truly productive. To be more effective, get rid of a lot of the trivialities. Get down to the specific requirements and truly start making decisions rather than by default. Need a much smaller group; and sooner or later a small handful need to live or die by this project, to have the accountability. People involved need to be dedicated and commit resources.

Others, such as the following Operations group member, found fault with the Executive Task Force structure:

The purpose of what it's [the Executive Task Force] to give us is there. But when I go back to the task force structure, these individuals do not all work together with each other. When you have a 10-member task force only four or five are actively contributing. The others who are there are protecting certain interests. I think you need the people [who are] working on the issues everyday, and with [the right] experience, to be there rather than the MBA type without the appropriate background.

We observed problems in the actual day-to-day management of Threshold, as well as problems stemming both from the involvement of multiple layers of management charged with overseeing Threshold tasks and from the top-heavy structure of the Executive Task Force. In addition, we observed that activities on Threshold were further hampered by disagreements and lack of clearly drawn responsibilities between the SVP of Development and his direct report, the VP publicly in charge of Threshold. These disagreements spilled over into the rank and file of the Development group, resulting in ambiguous lines of authority between the VP and his boss.

Almost without exception, members of the Development group felt that the VP had little regard for their efforts. They reported being belittled by him because they had never designed a large system such as Threshold before. They also felt hamstrung by the absence of precise functional specifications, and believed the VP was unsympathetic to the corresponding design difficulties they faced. When interviewed, the VP admitted the essential truthfulness of these perceptions. He was particularly vocal in his belief that the Development group feared moving forward without all the functional requirements of the system clearly specified. Rather, he argued quite strongly that it was important for BI to proceed with some design efforts even if this meant altering them later. In his view, deadlines and promises to the board of directors outweighed the more “narrow” concerns expressed by those in the Development group. Both the Development group personnel and the VP had support for their conflicting views from more senior individuals in the company. The inability to surface this fundamental disagreement and manage it in a productive way resulted in an organizational impasse with each side busily making their largely private development plans.

Further interview data with BI officials indicate that this inability to resolve disagreements and promote coordinated decision making was the norm rather than the exception at Bremerton. Many individuals with whom we spoke noted the “turf wars” characteristic of the organization. Others commented on the expectation that senior management would step in and both own and lead the task:

This is a project that can't [be allowed to] fail. As a senior officer of the corporation I don't want to see it fail. I don't want to see senior management look bad because this fails. But I don't think that everyone is committed to it. Maybe some people don't think they need it.

Some individuals, however, especially those lower in the management hierarchy, were aware of the turf wars and of the inability to surface organizational disagreements. Several illustrative examples follow:

(1) Because of the turf wars we won't have a good product. People are afraid to say "this won't work" or "I won't accept it." Whatever this new hardware does may have a recurring problem which never gets fixed because of the pressure of a new release. You have to spend hours proving that it's a problem.

(2) Somehow [the SVP of Development and the SVP of IT Systems] need to sort out a working relationship that doesn't harm the project. [The latter's] representatives to the various team meetings often don't attend; or when they do attend they claim they were never listened to. It makes no sense to design a system when the genuine business owners of the system don't agree or get together. You could also fault [the EVP of the Operations group] who knows full well they don't get along.

(3) I felt uncomfortable as to how to elevate the issues, through [my boss] to [the VP in charge of Threshold], beyond [this VP] to [the SVP of Development]. I didn't want to go around and rat on my bosses. Yet I felt they were important issues. They didn't say it was a non-issue. They just agreed with me, but that was as far as it went.

## Summary

Our findings from the study of IT implementation at Bremerton Inc. found superficial agreement among all those interviewed on the importance of Threshold to the future of the firm. However, and more important, we also uncovered significant disagreements about: (a) the strategic direction Threshold should take; (b) the strategic direction the firm should take; and (c) the authority over, and control of, Threshold at the executive, development, and operations levels. We also found that when individuals had been assigned key responsibilities for completing Threshold, they had not been delegated necessary and sufficient authority to complete their assignments. This mirrored, and resulted from, the ambiguous decision-making structures and power relationships within Bremerton senior management.

Our data further point to the fact that Bremerton's managers, like those studied elsewhere [2, 4, 29], were more likely to act on the basis of their own privately held theories-in-use rather than the publicly espoused values of the organization. Doing so, from their points of view, minimized their own risk, while effectively undermining a broader based, coordinated effort at overall design and implementation. Specifically, Threshold was undermined because the managers employed a set of unproductive interpersonal strategies: they kept disagreements private and undiscussable; bypassed key issues and interfaces both to protect a veneer of cooperation and harmony as well as to accomplish their delegated tasks; assigned blame and malfeasance to others rather than accept their own roles in the creation of problems; and accepted the integrity and correctness of their own views without either testing these rigorously against data or acknowledging the legitimacy of other points of view. The disagreements about the direction and scope of Threshold persisted, in large part, because the individuals involved had little or no awareness of their own roles in the creation of problems, and because the organization had no effective way to bring these issues to light for resolution.

## Conclusions: Understanding and Managing IT Implementation

WITHIN THE FIELD OF MIS IT HAS BEEN RECOGNIZED for some time that it is inherently problematic to adhere to a developmental model for IS without an understanding of the organization in which it occurs. “The fact is that the goals of the company, adding value to materials and ideas and filling a need in society, are accomplished by operational systems consisting of people, machines, and energy” [25, p. 87]. Unfortunately, the study of the “human side” of information systems has lagged behind the technical studies.

Insofar as the management structure for IS has been examined in the literature, individual managers tend to be seen as essentially independent, rational decision makers. This is because rationality is itself the hallmark of our publicly espoused values regarding the conduct of science. It is also because the traditional research methods used to study managers and decision making stem from a normative model of science. In effect, they reveal what managers would like to believe about their organizations, their colleagues, and themselves.

Like Argyris and his colleagues, we argue that a theoretically adequate description of a system implementation requires that the academic researcher or consultant go beyond publicly espoused accounts of the organization and its operations to the “deeper” level of theories-in-use and the actions such theories produce for individuals within the organization. In this way, it is possible to uncover the barriers to IT implementation that result from the discontinuities caused by decision makers unconsciously equating—and not testing—their own interests, decisions, and strategies with the organization’s.

As critical as an understanding of theories-in-use is to IS diagnostics, we argue that a descriptively adequate account of IT also requires explicit attention to authority and power relationships within the organization. Indeed, several published case studies of IT implementation attempt to do just that. However, they seek to instantiate general theories of organizational power and politics. From our point of view, the use of such theories seems limited given that the disagreements, power plays, territorial intentions, ambiguous lines of authority, and other barriers remain largely private, undiscussed, and undiscussable. As a result, rather than being the central part of an organizational analysis, they remain peripheral. Although much further empirical and conceptual work in this area is necessary, we argue that it is the private, covert, and often unintentional personal and interpersonal strategies that are the basis for understanding organizational politics.

For the practitioner/consultant interested in understanding and helping organizations manage IT projects, we believe, with Argyris and others $[1, 2, 3]$ , that successful interventions require that the practitioner be able to diagnose organizational problems using the concepts of action science. Thus, both the language of scientific analysis and theorizing, on the one hand, and the language of intervention, on the other, must be similar.

In our earlier research on the process of intervention $[29]$ , we found that outside consultants can help managers increase their likelihood of success in the design and implementation of business strategy by imparting both the skills and motivation to surface the undiscussable issues. The successful consultant must also facilitate individual managers in managing the dilemmas they perceive and the action routines they typically, and unconsciously, use. Finally, IS consultants must refrain from giving the “right” answer to an IS problem before first understanding the organization well enough to anticipate the dilemmas that will be created by the “right” answer. To do an intervention without meeting these conditions promises to keep disagreements and competing views private and therefore unmanageable, and to keep IT implementation constantly off course.
