# Middle Managers' Contribution to Implemented Information Technology Innovation

TOR J. LARSEN

TOR J. LARSEN is Associate Professor at the Department of Organization and Management, Norwegian School of Management, Oslo, Norway. He received his Ph.D. in management of information systems from Carlson School of Management, University of Minnesota, in 1989. His main research interests are in the areas of managers' use of information technology, innovation, and effects from the deployment of information systems. He has published in international journals, conference proceedings, and books.

ABSTRACT: This research investigated whether implemented information technology (IT) innovation could best be explained by a general tendency among middle managers to create implemented business innovation or by experience with IT. The results indicated that middle managers responsible for implemented business innovation also generated IT innovation while managers' practical (hands-on or delegated) use of IT was not shown to result in more implemented IT innovation. Middle managers who actively used support functions (secretaries, staff, or IT experts) for help and discussion of IT-related issues created and implemented a greater amount of IT innovation. Implemented business and IT innovations related to the variables of attitude toward change and peer recognition for their implemented innovations but not to internal and external networking. Peers recognized their fellow managers for their implemented business innovation but not for their IT-related achievements.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: attitude toward change, business innovation, end-user computing, information technology innovation, middle manager, networking behavior, peer recognition.

CREATING INNOVATIVE ORGANIZATIONS IS A COMPLEX PROCESS and only partially understood [36]. Within the field of management information systems (MIS), researchers have used various approaches to investigate the role information technology (IT) may play in creating business change. On the organizational level, the development and alignment of IT for strategic business impact has achieved widespread attention [18]. Indirect effects to the bottom line through the innovative behaviors of individuals have also been explored. For example, Rogers's [26] model of the diffusion of innovation through a social network has been employed to investigate how the use of spreadsheets has proliferated among users in accounting departments [5]. Creativity and the use of CASE and other IT tools among experts have been investigated [6]. Each of these research streams increases our understanding of innovation in regard to IT.

Two other dimensions will be considered here. First, business needs may drive the development of IT innovation. Researchers have examined how business-based, top-down strategic planning may radically change the way in which IT is deployed, but how is innovation embedded into the ongoing daily life of the organization? It has been argued that nonexperts in the line organization are responsible for the most important innovative ideas $[4, 27]$ . Moreover, Kanter $[16]$ claims that middle managers are a particularly important source of organizational renewal.

Second, IT innovation may be better explained by managers' experience with IT. The purpose of this article is to explore the role middle managers play in creating innovation in general, and in the area of MIS in particular. IT innovation will be viewed here not as an isolated phenomenon but as coexisting with other business innovation issues. In the next section the research issues and theory are developed, followed by the research model specification and hypotheses. Next, design and methodological choices are discussed. Finally, an analysis will lead into the discussion and conclusions.

## Research Issues and Thoery

THIS DISCUSSION OF MIDDLE MANAGEMENT'S IMPACT ON IT and business innovations will be centered around two questions. First, how can the outcome (implemented IT innovation) be explained? Second, are the commonly used concepts of internal and external networking behavior related to implemented innovation? The concept of innovation needs elaboration before either of these dimensions can be discussed.

## The Definition of Innovation

In organizational settings, the most commonly used definition of an innovation is “a new idea, which may be a recombination of old ideas, a scheme that challenges the present order, a formula, or a unique approach which is perceived as new by the individuals involved” [26, 36, 39]. According to this definition, any idea, whether it pertains to products, production processes, administrative procedures, or information technology may be regarded as an innovation as long as people, on an individual basis, are of the opinion that it is something new to them.

Although any phenomenon may be regarded as an innovation, the scope of its impact may vary. Katz and Kahn [17] suggest that this is the difference between change and innovation. Change activities are actions taken to improve an individual job situation. Innovation, on the other hand, will be used by more than one person. According to Kanter [16], an innovation will have an impact beyond the department of the originator. However, as the term “department” may be problematic since labels for organizational units may vary among organizations, it seems reasonable to conclude that an innovation is a new idea that is to be used by more than one person.

Yet another delineation reflecting scope is that of radical versus incremental innovation. A radical innovation implies a totally new direction for the organization $[39]$ . This type of innovation typically requires top management support and is usually a strategic undertaking $[16]$ . An incremental innovation is smaller and may impact a limited set of the organizational population, although the people concerned may view it as quite radical. It is probable that middle managers are mainly responsible for innovations that impact only a segment of the organization. Seen from the viewpoint of the whole organization, their endeavors are more likely to be incremental than radical. Table 1 summarizes the difference between change, incremental, and radical innovation.

This research investigates the amount of innovative activity among middle managers regardless of whether the innovation is radical or incremental. It looks at innovations that are implemented. That is, creativity, idea generation, and change in the narrow sense will not suffice. In the remainder of the article innovation regarding products, production processes, and administrative routines is labeled “implemented business innovation.” MIS innovations are called “implemented IT innovation.”

## Causal Relationships with Implemented IT Innovation

A single article cannot cover every issue of relevance to the creation of implemented IT innovation. This article focuses on two questions. First, do middle managers who generate more implemented business innovation also generate more IT innovation? And second, does increased experience with IT lead to increased IT innovation? Of course, other factors may also explain implemented IT innovation.

## The Relationship between Business and IT Innovation

Are the same middle managers who are responsible for implemented business innovation also creating implemented IT innovation? The literature on innovation suggests that innovative efforts occur in areas the innovators deem to be important $[16, 36]$ . Since computers have become a necessity for efficiently conducting business, managers concerned with business issues may consider computers an element of the innovation process $[14, 18]$ .

It has been established in the literature that people in the line organization forward many innovative ideas in which the use of computers is an element $[4]$ . One would expect to find that managers responsible for generating implemented business change would also contribute to IT innovation.

## The Relationship between Experience and IT Innovation

One would intuitively think that increased experience with IT would result in increased generation of implemented IT innovation. Education and individual use are two ways that may lead to increased knowledge about IT. Education has been found to relate to innovation in the general business domain as well as to the diffusion of information technology [5, 26]. However, it may be that increased experience with IT only leads to more effective and efficient usage patterns for the computer user him or herself. It may not necessarily lead to innovation that benefit other people. This is an area for examination.

Further, tenure (with a company) has been used by many researchers as a proxy for work experience. The conclusion drawn so far is that tenure does not relate to (business) innovation [7]. However, as there may be doubt regarding a possible relationship between the use of IT and tenure, and IT innovation, the intuitive assumption of a positive relationship between use and IT innovation is chosen as the baseline for this investigation.

Managerial use of IT is a complex phenomenon. Managers may use standardized reports and data from transaction systems, decision support systems, and a host of other predefined computer-based services. Organizations establish many computer systems where the individual cannot intervene with the way in which the system is used or with its outputs. While this type of computer support may be of vital importance to the organization, the focus of this research effort is on IT used in cases where the individual has a degree of free choice. This category is often called “end-user computing” or “personal computing” [25].

If we look at managerial use strictly from a technological standpoint, only hands-on use of computers would be considered appropriate. However, from an information viewpoint, managerial tasks require formal and informal information. Formal information processing may involve the use of computers [40]. To obtain this information managers may choose to do the necessary computing themselves or they may deem it more appropriate to delegate the execution of these tasks to secretaries, staff, or other available sources. Managers combine hands-on use and delegation according to their perceptions of what is most appropriate. In both cases the individual manager is the initiator.

When utilizing IT, whether directly or indirectly, users frequently have difficulty determining what to do to make the computer perform according to their needs. In these situations users often seek help from others. In conclusion, managerial use of IT includes the three elements of hands-on use, delegated use, and the use of the social environment for help and discussion $[22]$ .

The question is which of the three elements is the most effective method for acquiring knowledge about the potential of IT. Direct experience through hands-on use may be the best basis for increased innovation or it may be that knowledge about the potential of IT based on what other employees working for the manager do will suffice. Perhaps direct or indirect use of computers does not matter but increased knowledge about IT through the use of support functions is the best predictor.

## Other Factors that May Influence IT Innovation

Factors related to the individual, job, and organizational level may also influence innovation.

On the individual level, education has been found to be positively related to innovation $[5, 20, 21, 26, 33]$ . And while age has often been found not to correlate with innovation $[26, 33]$ , Brancheau and Wetherbe $[5]$ found that early adopters of a new technology were younger than late adopters. This might indicate that age does, in fact, have an impact on innovation in information technology, but not innovation in the general business domain. Work experience may also influence innovation, although prior research results have found that tenure does not seem to relate to innovation $[7, 20, 33]$ . Despite this lack of relationship, tenure was included in this study because implemented business innovation may differ from implemented IT innovation. Inclusion would allow for comparison.

Job characteristics may also influence innovation. Two dimensions are of particular concern: the nature of the job and hierarchical position. Whether the job influences the incumbent or the incumbent the job $[29]$ , researchers in this field have identified task difficulty (analyzability and predictability) and task variability (the number of exceptions encountered in the work) as being among the most influential factors in determining the nature of the job $[21, 37]$ . The underlying concern here is that a more predictable job which also contains a high degree of analytical tasks may have a higher potential for IT support. In a situation where exceptions dominate, the manager may not have time to develop computer support or may not find relevant data readily available $[28]$ .

Degree of task difficulty and variability are not similar for all managers. In particular, it may be expected that the mix of the two vary with hierarchical position—the higher in the hierarchy a manager is located, the less predictable the job and the larger the number of exceptions. It has also been found that managers placed higher in the organizational hierarchy generate more implemented business innovation $[12, 13, 32]$ .

Quite a few variables on the organizational level have been investigated for their possible impact on innovation. Rogers [26] declared that results from studies of organizational factors and innovation are inconclusive. Ettlie, Bridges, and O'Keefe [10] agreed and summed up the status of empirical research in this area by declaring that only extremely large organizational size is a consistent organizational predictor of innovation. However, Damanpour [7] in his meta-analysis concluded that quite a few variables (e.g., specialization, functional differentiation, professionalism, technical knowledge resources, and slack resources) have a positive impact on innovation, while others (e.g., centralization) contribute in a negative direction.

The unit of analysis in this research was the individual manager. The overriding concern was that organizational factors may influence the probability of individual innovative behavior. This dimension would require detailed studies beyond the scope of the present research. The objective was to study differences among managers under equal organizational influence. A critical organizational factor would be that the organization had used IT for some time. Otherwise, managers may not have had enough opportunity to develop knowledge about the need for IT innovation. Moreover, the situation in organizations going through a period of radical change may be quite different than in organizations where business and IT innovations occur at a steady rate. The steady rate innovation situation was the focus here.

## Implemented Innovation, Networking, and Other Variables

The material presented so far has explored variables that may explain why innovation occurs. Researchers have also been concerned with how innovators innovate. Presumably, increased understanding of innovative behavior would allow for organizational and job designs that increase the likelihood of innovation. The objective of this section is to explore some of the most commonly used indicators of innovative behavior and how these relate to implemented innovation.

A central concept in this domain is networking behavior. The research of Allen [2] and Tushman [34] indicated a two-step process: the development of an innovation and the diffusion of that innovation into the larger organization. In each step a different set of individuals play key roles. They are the gatekeepers in their part of the process.

The originators of a new innovation (external gatekeepers) have a strong network with the environment external to the organization. They read professional journals, are members of professional associations, and participate in their meetings. Innovation transition into the organization, it is claimed, is handled by individuals who are well connected with people in other units within the organization (internal gatekeepers). Job time spent in other units, number of internal meetings attended, reports and memos received from other units, and telephone conversations with people in the larger organization are variables commonly used to measure internal networking.

Advice-giving and advice-seeking approaches have been used to measure internal networking behavior $[5, 26]$ . The difficulty with this approach is data collection. The possible contact patterns can become vast. It is doubtful whether a questionnaire could capture the richness of the advice-giving and advice-seeking process. A longitudinal study, combined with snowball interviewing techniques, would be more appropriate. For this reason, the advice-giving and advice-seeking measures were not examined in this research.

The concept of external and internal gatekeepers was developed based on investigations of the processes in Research and Development (R&D) departments. R&D is, in most cases, a formalized structure for product innovation. Innovation in the line organization may be quite different.

Kanter [16] argued that formal communication (reports, memos, telephone conversations) and formal arrangements (meetings) are not adequate measures of networking behavior supporting innovation. Innovation is a complex phenomenon that requires detailed knowledge of people, business opportunities, and the workings of the organization. Such knowledge can only be obtained by experience. That is, effective innovators must have worked in various organizational units within the organization. Diverse experience would foster a holistic understanding of the organization [36], while staying in one department would increase the risk of homogeneity or group think [15] and social enactments [38]. The outcome of limited organizational experience might be that people perceive themselves to be dependent upon the organization for the content of their jobs and what they can do.

Another view is that individual innovativeness is a basic individual attribute, which may be best represented by the construct of attitude toward change. Reported research results show that a positive attitude toward change is related to innovative behavior $[7, 8, 9, 26]$ . People who exhibit a positive attitude toward change are consistently more innovative. However, organizational factors may moderate their innovative behavior.

Other researchers argue that the best way to identify innovators is to focus on implemented innovation. Networking behavior, dependency, and attitude toward change are, at best, imprecise and incomplete representations of innovativeness. Peer identification of individuals responsible for implemented innovation would be the least biased variable, avoiding the pitfalls of subjective self-reporting $[30, 31]$ .

## Research Model and Hypotheses

THE RESEARCH MODEL IS DIVIDED INTO TWO PARTS. The first reflects relationships regarding the outcome: that is, implemented innovation. The position taken here is that implemented IT innovation, as the dependent variable, may be explained by implemented business innovation or experience with IT, represented by the middle managers' own use of the technology, delegation behavior, and the use of support functions. Variables on the individual, job, and organizational levels that are either of particular interest to this research design (for example, tenure) or that previous research has shown to impact innovation (for example, education) are included as control variables. These relationships are represented by solid lines and arrowheads indicating causal relationships in the graphic representation of the research model in figure 1.

The relationship between implemented innovation and variables such as attitude toward change, networking and dependency behavior, and peer recognition are unclear. Based on research regarding the role of gatekeepers, one might expect that a basic characteristic of innovative middle managers would be that they have a strong external (to the organization) network. However, Tushman and Scanlan [35] found that the people most effective in importing information into the organization combined external and internal networking. ## Research Design

THE TWO MAIN DESIGN VARIABLES WERE SAMPLE DEFINITION and operationalization of variables.

## Sample Definition

The investigation used a field study design. Two factors determined the design of the study: the definition of the term manager and the concepts of innovativeness and computer use. The objective was to study innovation and computer use in a managerial context. Naturally, managers working in a staff or specialist capacity may have drastically different innovation and computer use profiles compared with line managers. Staff and experts typically perform a more narrow set of tasks and often deal with tasks in depth rather than breadth. Therefore, the study was limited to managers in line positions. Even within the line management population, managers at different levels might create innovation and use computers differently $[3, 13, 23]$ . For this reason, the study is focused on middle managers.

There is no clear existing definition of middle manager. In the context of this research, managers below the level of divisional executive officer and above the level of foreman were labeled middle managers. The decision rule used was that managers who, in principle, carried out a managerial role rather than the supervision of people on an hourly basis (for example, a foreman on the job floor), would be included in the study.

There was concern that managers who do not create innovation or use computers themselves might be inclined not to participate in a study in which the outcome did not relate directly to them. The danger of overrepresentation of managers who actively participate in innovation and use computers would be greatest in a general survey, since this design alternative gives the respondent no other incentive to participate than interest in the subject area or in research in general. The need for data in this research was extensive and required detailed responses. These additional factors might also contribute to biased reports and low response rates.

Based on these considerations, a division within a major defense contracting company in the Minneapolis–St. Paul area was invited to participate in the study. The division's main tasks were marketing, contract bidding, production planning, production, and distribution of finished goods. Sales in 1987 were approximately \$200 million and sales had tripled since 1984. The division had been experiencing healthy growth. The number of employees had increased at approximately the same rate, and there were employees of every age.

The division began using PCs in 1982 and the number of personal computers had increased steadily until approximately half of the management population had direct access to PCs at the time of the study (1988). Managers used many different computer applications and many support functions were in place. Computer use and support had grown rapidly. Development in the main business areas, as well as in computing, could be characterized as rapid but steady, rather than as revolutionary.

In all, 130 middle managers were identified as candidates for participation. Twenty managers participated in instrument development and the remaining 110 in the final data collection. The average age of the managers was 46.3 years. They had, on average, 24.9 years work experience and had worked 18.2 years for the company, 10.8 years for the division.

Because of the relatively large number of respondents, a questionnaire was developed as the main vehicle for data collection. The instrument was tested in several steps. Following initial scrutiny among fellow researchers, four managers in staff units in the participating division talked themselves through the questionnaire with the researcher present. The twenty managers participating in the pilot responded to the questionnaire as the final subject population would. However, the twenty managers were instructed to give written feedback on unclear or ambiguous questions. The data obtained were used to check that each item in the questionnaire had a meaningful mean and that at least 50 percent of the alternative responses for scales were used. The data obtained in instrument development were discarded after completion of the test procedure and not used in the final analysis. Participants were guaranteed that the information they provided would be treated confidentially and that only aggregated data would be reported.

In the final data collection 99 middle managers returned the completed questionnaire, for a response rate of 90 percent. Reliability checks on instruments were executed on the 99 responses in the final database.

## Operationalization of Variables

Collection of data regarding implemented innovation in a questionnaire format is quite problematic. As discussed earlier, the implemented innovation to be included should have an impact beyond improvements of the job situation of the initiator.

Based on these considerations it was decided that implemented innovation that required the agreement of the manager's supervisor would be considered as an example of innovation. Of course, this approach did not guarantee that every self-reported example of innovation had a wider impact or, for that matter, that the list would be complete and accurate. As a control measure participants were asked to give a short description of their ideas for change that had required such agreement.

The managers were also instructed to limit their responses to change efforts over the last three years, otherwise recall may have become overly problematic. Compared with endeavors of little impact, it is likely that people would remember their responsibility for implemented change having a wide impact over a comparatively long period of time. The question of whether an idea for change was implemented or not was added to increase reliability. Also, managers were asked to list business innovation (product, production process, and general administration) separate from change efforts that included information technology. In order to counteract some of the dangers of misidentifying business for information technology innovation, or vice versa, the two categories were placed 22 pages apart in the questionnaire, business first and information technology innovation later.

In addition, in the section soliciting information regarding information technology innovation respondents were asked to put a reference to examples listed in the business innovation section if they, at that point, discovered that an item was misplaced. The short descriptions were also used to control whether each listed item was in the category of business or information technology innovation. Of course, this format cannot guarantee that items were correctly categorized. However, a questionnaire format has been used previously and been found adequate [1]. Follow-up interviews with four managers did not indicate problems in this respect. The operationalization of the other variables are documented in appendix A.

## Results

FINDINGS REGARDING CAUSAL RELATIONSHIPS WITH IMPLEMENTED IT innovation are presented first, followed by analysis results regarding cluster relationships.

## Causal Relationships with Implemented IT Innovation

The correlations among the variables of implemented business and IT innovations, managers' use of computers, and control variables are presented in Table 2.

The results clearly indicate that there is a positive relationship between implemented business innovation and IT innovation. Middle managers in the line organization innovate in both areas. Among the variables representing experience with IT, only the use of support functions has a significant correlation with implemented innovations. This type of use is equally correlated with implemented business and IT innovations.

Paralleling previous research findings, age and tenure do not seem to correlate with implemented innovation while education does. Hierarchical position correlates only with business innovation. That is, the higher in the hierarchy a manager is located, the greater the number of implemented business innovations. Regarding implemented IT innovation, managers are equally active across the organizational hierarchy.

In order to control for combined effects of implemented IT innovation, H1 (the impact of implemented business innovation) and H2 (the impact of managers' experience with IT) were analyzed simultaneously employing regression. The results are shown in Table 3.

The results support H1. Middle managers responsible for more implemented business innovation are also accountable for more implemented IT innovation. It would seem that being innovative is a general ability among these middle managers.

H2 is only partially supported. According to the results presented here, hands-on use of IT and delegation of IT tasks are not related to implemented IT innovation. Apparently, experience in the form of use does not result in a greater number of innovations that directly benefit other people. Use might enable innovation in more subtle ways as direct knowledge about what IT can do may form a baseline for new ideas. Managers may find more effective and efficient ways of using IT for their own needs and those solutions may over time spread through the social network surrounding them. However, the design employed in this research excluded such activities for two reasons. First, the prerequisite for inclusion was that the supervisors' agreement was needed. Smaller change efforts where the managers had decision autonomy would be excluded. Second, the questionnaire elicited information regarding business innovation first. This might have made the respondents think of larger undertakings which might have queued them to include IT innovation of some size. Smaller IT innovation endeavors would have fallen through the cracks (which, incidentally, was the objective of this operationalization).

The results did support the notion that middle managers who use support functions more actively are responsible for a larger number of implemented IT innovations. The reason for this relationship may be that innovative middle managers need to talk to colleagues as well as IT experts to verify the soundness of their ideas and build alliances that lead to implementation.

Among the hypotheses describing the relationships between control variables and implemented IT innovation, only the notion that the more varied the job the less middle managers create implemented IT innovation (H3D) was supported. Regarding age (H3A) and education (H3C), the results are different from those of Brancheau and Wetherbe [5] who found that early adopters of spreadsheet packages were younger and better educated than later adopters. Managerial idea development and implementation and the adaption of IT technology may be separate phenomena with their own characteristics.

## Cluster Relationships

Implemented business and IT innovations were found to correlate. For the cluster analysis the two variables were collapsed into one representing implemented business and IT innovations. Also, number of journals read, membership in professional associations, and meetings attended loaded in a factor analysis on one factor and were collapsed into one variable labeled external networking. The variables of number of reports and memos received, formal meetings attended, and number of telephone conversations also loaded on one common factor and were combined into one variable labeled volume of communication with the organization outside own department.

After these procedures, the number of variables remaining was eight, which was felt to be the maximum number that could be meaningfully used considering the size of the sample. The variables were submitted to a factor analysis using the varimax rotation technique, which is the recommended procedure to produce orthogonal factors $[19]$ . The analysis produced three factors with an eigenvalue greater than 1. The results are shown in Table 4.

The factor analysis does not support the hypothesis (H4) that implemented business and IT innovations are primarily related to variables representing networking behavior. The results indicate that implemented business and IT innovations have a close relationship with middle managers' attitudes toward change and with the degree of peer recognition.

The explanation may be that a positive attitude toward change is an important prerequisite for innovative action and that peers recognize that outcome. The combination of implemented business and IT innovations and peer recognition to a common factor lends support to the appropriateness of using a self-reporting format for mapping implemented innovation. Although individuals may exaggerate the importance of their own innovative endeavors, their evaluation coincided, to some degree, with the judgment of others. It seems reasonable to conclude that the triangulation of attitude toward change, implemented innovation, and peer recognition is a fairly straightforward way to measure the degree of innovativeness in a management population.

## Discussion

ALTHOUGH THE RESULTS INDICATED QUITE CLEARLY THAT IMPLEMENTED BUSINESS and IT innovations are related, middle managers might not regard them as equally important. Three aspects might illustrate the role of IT innovation. First, the correlation analysis presented in Table 2 shows that hierarchical position is positively related to implemented business innovation while there is no correlation between hierarchical position and implemented IT innovation. The higher in the organizational hierarchy a middle manager is placed, the more likely that he or she will be responsible for implemented business innovation. The level of activity of implemented IT innovation does not vary across the hierarchy. Further research is needed to answer the question of whether this might indicate that middle managers placed higher in the hierarchy are more concerned with business than with IT innovation.

Second, peer recognition may also reflect the difference between the two aspects of innovation. The argument is based on the variables of attitude toward change, implemented business and IT innovations, and peer recognition. In behavioral theory, attitude is quite often regarded as a factor that explains behavior $[11]$ . Consequently, a positive attitude toward change may be regarded as the antecedent of implemented business and IT innovations. Once innovations are implemented, peers develop an opinion of who among their fellow middle managers should be credited with these innovations. If business and IT innovations occur on an equal level, peers should nominate their colleagues as much because of their implemented business innovation as because of their IT innovation. ## Conclusions

THE RESULTS PRESENTED HERE INDICATE THAT MIDDLE MANAGERS who generate implemented business innovation also create implemented IT innovation. However, practical use of computers neither helps nor hinders IT innovation. Only the use of support functions (using secretaries, support staff, IT experts, or others for practical help and discussion partners in IT related matters) was positively related to implemented IT innovation. Also, middle managers with more varied job tasks achieved a smaller number of implemented IT innovations.

The increase in job variation leading to a reduction in IT innovation needs further research. Additional future research should include in-depth studies of what kind of support middle managers want for their hands-on and delegated use of IT and how support combines with idea development and implementation considerations in dialog with people in the line organization and experts.

In the factor analysis, the outcome variable of innovative processes of implemented innovation was grouped with attitude toward change and peer recognition, indicating that a combination of the three variables is a fairly straightforward method for measuring the level of individual innovative activity.

The results also found that IT innovation was not regarded as equally important as business innovation. This may be due to the manufacturing nature of the participating division. Thus, the degree of importance of IT may change if comparative studies were carried out in information-intensive business organizations. Also, as IT is constantly developing, improvements in the field may lead to change the degree of IT innovation compared with business innovation. The path analysis presented in this paper would be a straightforward method for detecting such shifts.

## APPENDIX A: Operationalization of Variables

Implemented business innovation:

A self-reported list of business changes over the last three years that were implemented with the agreement of the respondent's supervisor [16].

Hands-on use of IT:

A measure of the typical number of weekly hours of hands-on use of IT over the last six months.

Delegation of IT tasks:

The average frequency of delegation of IT tasks relative to hands-on use of IT, measured per application included in the questionnaire on a scale from 1 (never delegate) to 6 (always delegate).

Use of support functions:

The number of times over the last six months the respondent contacted people for assistance in IT use or to discuss IT-related matters. Data were obtained per type of support function, for example, people in the near environment, user group, information center, friends and relatives, etc.

Age:

Respondent's age in years.

Tenure:

The number of years the respondent had worked for the division. Education:

A scale from 1 (grade school diploma) to 6 (doctoral degree).

Job task variation:

An instrument containing three items, developed by Van de Ven and Ferry [37]. A factor analysis was used to check the reliability of the instrument. Job task difficulty:

An instrument containing three items, developed by Van de Ven and Ferry

[37]. A factor analysis was used to check the reliability of the instrument. Hierarchical position:

The number of layers of managers between the respondent and the division executive officer.

## Implemented IT innovation:

A self-reported list of computer changes over the last three years that were implemented with the agreement of the respondent's supervisor [16].

## External networking:

The measure was calculated as: number of journals read + number of memberships in professional associations + number of meetings attended in a typical year.

## Part of job time spent in other departments:

Calculated as the average contact frequency with people within the firm but outside the respondent's own unit.

## Number of departments worked for:

Based on Kanter's [16] research, this item measured the number of organizational units within the firm for whom the respondent had worked. It was calculated as the number of units (worked for) divided by tenure with the company.

## Volume of communication with the organization outside own department:

Three items, established by Van de Ven and Ferry [37], were used to measure external communication: (1) written reports and memos received, (2) work-related one-to-one discussion over telephone or face to face, and (3) participation in internal meetings. The final measure was calculated as the sum of the three items.

## Attitude toward change:

A ten-item scale developed by Ettlie and O'Keefe [9] and later Ettlie [8]. The items were submitted to a confirmatory factor analysis; see appendix B.

## Degree of dependency on the organization outside own department:

An item established by Van de Ven and Ferry [37]. It measured the respondent's reliance on people outside his or her own organizational unit to obtain materials and information needed to do his or her job.

## Peer rating of innovative results:

Managers were asked to list the name of other managers they thought had forwarded many ideas for change that were implemented. The measure was a tally of the number of nominations per manager.
