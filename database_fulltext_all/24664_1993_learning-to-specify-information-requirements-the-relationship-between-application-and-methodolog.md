# Learning to Specify Information Requirements: The Relationship between Application and Methodology

IRIS VESSEY AND SUE CONGER

Her research interests focus on the cognitive process underlying the analysis and design of software. Specifically, she is interested in the cognitive fit of representations, methodologies, and techniques to software tasks, the role of the application in the selection of systems development approaches, and the evaluation of new information systems technologies. She has an M.S. and a Ph.D. in information systems from the University of Queensland, Australia. She is an associate editor of Information Systems Research and of MIS Quarterly.

## 1. Introduction

CLEARLY, THE SYSTEMS DEVELOPMENT PROCESS INVOLVES BOTH an application and a problem-solving domain: it consists of modeling the information requirements of the particular application and successively transforming those requirements into a computer-based model of the application. One first identifies a problem in a given application domain (hereafter referred to as the application); then one identifies a method (or set of methods) by which to accomplish systems development in the problem-solving domain.

Since the inception of computers, the attention of the systems development community has focused on the problem-solving domain. Numerous methodologies have been developed to aid the process. By methodology, we mean a systematic approach to the task of systems development $[25]$ . Couger, Coulter, and Knapp $[8]$ provide a review of traditional systems development methodologies. Since that time, disciplined methodologies such as, among others, the structured techniques $[10, 13]$ , Jackson System Development $[18]$ , and object-oriented methods $[7]$ have been widely advocated.

Despite the interest in systems development by both academics and practitioners, there has been little formal recognition of the importance of the application to be developed to the systems development process. This is perhaps surprising since, as Blum [3] states: “much of what we consider to be software development is actually application domain problem solving using a software solution.” More recently, however, there have been calls for researchers to consider the application domain (see [15] for a review).

That systems development is actually application domain problem solving is particularly apparent in the first step of the systems development process, specifying information requirements, a subset of systems analysis. This phase models the real-world system that will, in the final outcome, be modeled by the information system. Specifying information requirements is arguably the key to developing successful information systems. Since this is the first step in the systems development process, it is clear that not doing it effectively will have significant impact on both the effectiveness and the efficiency of the resultant system $[4, 5]$ . Not getting the correct final system requirements initially is largely responsible for the cost and schedule overruns that are still fairly prevalent in information systems development.

As a first step toward understanding the relationship between a methodology and an application, this study examines the interdependence of these two essential elements of systems development. Specifically, the study addresses the influence of the interrelationship of application and methodology knowledge on performance in the information requirements specification process. Assessment of the interrelationship of methodology and application was achieved by means of a repeated-measures design that manipulated both experience with the application and knowledge of the methodology. Application knowledge was varied using a familiar and an unfamiliar application, while methodology knowledge was varied by conducting the study in a learning context.

Practically, the interdependence of application and methodology knowledge is important since it will shed light on the types of skills needed for systems development, that is, whether it is better to train people in systems development methods or in the functional area under investigation. This issue is particularly relevant in view of increasing calls for systems development by end-users (see, for example, [23]). Theoretically, this issue is also important since it highlights the importance of knowledge of the application, an aspect of systems development that has been largely ignored.

The paper proceeds as follows. The next section reviews prior research into systems development. Then, it articulates the relationship between application and methodology in IS development. Section 3 presents the model of the information requirements specification process investigated in this research, together with the research questions addressed. Section 4 presents the methodology used to assess the research questions. Section 5 presents the results of this initial investigation of the validity of the model and related research questions. Finally, section 6 discusses the results of the analyses, the limitations of the study, and the implications of the research for researchers, instructors in information systems, and practitioners.

## 2. Background

IN THIS SECTION, WE FIRST EXAMINE PREVIOUS RESEARCH into the systems development process. We then develop arguments in support of the approach taken in this study.

## 2.1. Prior Research into Systems Development

Despite the significance of specifying information requirements to the successful design of systems and the large body of descriptive and conceptual literature on methodologies, surprisingly few empirical studies have been conducted into the specification process itself. In what may be the only empirical test of methodologies reported in the literature, Yadav, Bravoco, Chatfield, and Rajkumar [38] investigated the effectiveness of two methodologies, the “Data Flow Diagram” [13] and the “Integrated Definition Method” [29], in specifying information requirements. They report that, although data flow diagrams are easier to learn and to use, neither produced significantly better specifications. The authors make no mention of the application to which the methodologies were applied.

Again, few studies have explicitly addressed the application knowledge used in specifying information requirements. Vitalari [36] assessed IS practitioners' usage of certain types of knowledge during an information requirements determination task for an accounts receivable system. Six of Vitalari's 20 most frequently used knowledge categories relate to the application domain. They are: functional requirements, requirements for information, requirements for systems function, organizational responsibility, descriptions of organizational activities, and purpose of the system. These six categories account for 41 percent of references in the top 20 categories and 24 percent of the total knowledge references. Note that Vitalari investigated requirements determination rather than requirements specification. Hence, his study did not investigate the use of a methodology.

Possibly the greatest interest in any aspect of the systems development process (other than programming) has been in the area of design, which follows the analysis phase. The design phase is concerned with transforming the logical requirements of the system derived in the analysis phase into a physical representation of the system to be developed (see, for example, [37]). Such research is typified by the studies of Adelson and Soloway [1], Guindon, Krasner, and Curtis [17], Jeffries et al. [20], and Kant and Newell [21]. Only one of these studies examined the effect of application experience on the systems design process [1].

Adelson and Soloway [1] investigated the design process when designers have varying levels of experience with both the domain of similar applications and the specific application. Their subjects designed systems for an interrupt handler (familiar domain; familiar application), an electronic mail system (familiar domain—communication systems; unfamiliar application), and a library system (unfamiliar domain; unfamiliar application). They found that the key to systems design is the formulation of a mental model of the “design-in-progress,” and mental simulation of the model. Further, they claim that systematic development of the mental model is essential to permit simulation of the model at different levels of detail.

According to Adelson and Soloway, domain experience facilitates the formulation of a detailed mental model that designers simulate at a global level. When designers do not have domain knowledge, the mental model is less detailed and the system is simulated in piecemeal fashion rather than globally. On the other hand, when the application and the domain are both familiar, a highly abstract, though functional, mental model is readily available, and simulation is not necessary since the designer already knows from past experience that it is adequate. Other behaviors that occur with high domain knowledge but to a much lesser extent with low domain knowledge include problem solving by analogy, constraining the model based on the requirements of the physical environment in which the system will operate, and note making, external memory cues to remind the designer of the need to expand a portion of the design at a lower level of abstraction. Note making is unnecessary, however, when the domain and the application are both familiar to the designer.

While the results of the above studies are potentially applicable to certain aspects of the information requirements specification process, there are a number of limitations from an information systems perspective. First, the studies focused on design rather than analysis. In information systems, the crucial step in systems development is to develop a logical model of the system-to-be (i.e., to determine what the system needs to do) [10]. A related issue, here, is that the types of problems examined were fairly well bounded, algorithmically intensive, applications such as the lift control application [17], the convex hull application [21], and the page-keyed indexing application [20]. As noted above, Adelson and Soloway's subjects developed an interrupt handler, an electronic mail system, and a library system. Hence, the complexity of perhaps all of these applications, with the exception of the library system, lies in determining how to develop computer-based solutions to them rather than in modeling what the system should achieve. Second, since specifying information requirements is such a complex task for systems analysts, $^{1}$ a methodology is usually used to aid the human problem solver [22, 37, 39]. Like the information requirements studies presented initially, none of the design studies examined the use of systems development methodologies.

## 2.2. The Issue for Specifying Information Requirements

The applications addressed in many of the studies examined in the previous section, particularly the design studies, require little application (or application domain) knowledge for problem solving and the solution to the application can be readily structured. (See, for comparison, cryptarithmetic [24], Tower of Hanoi [32], and Missionary and Cannibals [19, 34] problems.) To specify the requirements of an information system, on the other hand, large amounts of knowledge are required. Hence, IS development can be regarded as a semantically rich (i.e., knowledge-based) problem-solving domain. Simon [31] characterizes semantically rich domains as those in which approximately 50,000 chunks of knowledge are required for expertise and in which expertise is seldom achieved in less than ten years (p. 106). He states:

When the processor is solving puzzle-like problems, the memory plays a limited role. The structure of the problem rather than the organization of memory steers the problem-solving search. When it is solving problems in semantically rich domains, a large part of the problem-solving search takes place in long-term memory and is guided by information discovered in that memory. Hence, an explanation of problem solving in such domains must rest on an adequate theory of memory. [p. 104]

Since an application in a semantically rich task domain has a large base of potentially relevant knowledge, it appears to be ill-structured [33]. $^{2}$ Such applications are usually handled by decomposing the area under investigation into subproblems for which solutions can be found or generated. The key to successful decomposition lies in reformulating the problem in such a way that the subproblems can be integrated to form the solution for the application as a whole [28].

In the systems development domain, decomposition is usually achieved by applying structured methodologies that are designed to formally address ill-structured systems development tasks. As we saw in section 2.1, no study has examined the interrelationship between an application and a methodology. There have been few empirical studies of systems development methodologies and those that have been reported in the literature have not considered the application for which the methodology was used. Similarly, there have been few empirical studies of applications and those that have been reported have not investigated the use of a methodology.

As a first step in examining the relevance of knowledge of the application (and/or application domain) to the systems development process, this study addresses the influence of the interrelationship between the application and the problem-solving domains on performance in the information requirements specification process.

## 3. A Model of the Information Requirements Specification Process

THIS STUDY TAKES A PRELIMINARY STEP IN EXAMINING the relationship between the application and a methodology used to model the information requirements of a proposed system. Figure 1 presents the model of the information requirements specification process used to guide this research. It is based on the work of Gentner and Stevens [14] on mental models and on that of Perrig and Kintsch [27], which states that problem solvers induce their mental models from sources available to them. The model is characterized by three major interactions: (1) the interaction between knowledge of the application itself and that of the domain of similar applications that results in the formulation of a mental model of the application requirements $^{3}$ ; (2) the interaction between declarative and procedural knowledge of the methodology that results in a mental model of how to apply the methodology to the task of specifying information requirements; and (3) the interaction between the mental model of the application requirements and of how to apply the methodology, which ultimately results in the specifications themselves.

First, the model suggests that a mental model of the application requirements is formulated from two sources: knowledge of the application itself as well as knowledge of the domain of similar applications. Problem solving by reference to previously developed applications (i.e., problem solving by analogy) is well documented in the design studies cited earlier (see, for example, [1, 17, 20]). Solutions to previously developed applications are stored in long-term memory and recalled and modified to help meet the needs of a current application.

Second, the mental model of the methodology is developed from both declarative knowledge of the steps involved in the methodology and procedural knowledge about how to apply the methodology. According to Anderson [2], systems analysts can first be expected to acquire declarative knowledge, followed by procedural knowledge (see also [6, 12, 16]). Further, declarative knowledge per se is insufficient to perform a task; problem solvers must also have procedural knowledge. Hence, it seems likely that problem solvers using procedural methodology knowledge may be more effective in specifying information requirements than those using declarative methodology knowledge.

Figure 1. Model of the Information Requirements Specification Process

Finally, the model suggests that knowledge of both the application requirements and of how to apply the methodology are required for the successful development of a mental model for specifying the information requirements, and therefore the resultant specifications. This interaction is the crux of the association between application and methodology.

As a starting point in this research, we investigated the following research question:

1: Are there synergistic effects of application and methodology knowledge in specifying information requirements?

We further subdivide research question 1 to investigate the effects of knowledge of one major component of the information requirements specification process on knowledge of the other component. The following two specific research questions emerge:

1a: Does knowledge of the application have an effect on ability to apply a methodology?

1b: Does knowledge of the methodology have an effect on ability to formulate a mental model of the application requirements?

Once we have obtained the results of these preliminary investigations, we can use them as a guide to conduct further exploratory investigations.

## 4. Method

THE ROLE OF A METHODOLOGY IN SPECIFYING INFORMATION REQUIREMENTS can, perhaps, be best assessed by observing the behavior of novice systems analysts whose store of alternative methods and analogous solutions is limited. To explore the above research questions, a process-tracing study was conducted in which novice systems analysts specified the information requirements for each of three applications. One of the applications was familiar and one unfamiliar to the novice analysts.

Protocol analysis furnished data for the study since it provides richer insights into processes than alternative process-tracing approaches (see $[26]$ for a comparison of process-tracing methods; and $[11, 30]$ for discussion of the veridicality of protocol data). The problem-solving sessions were videotaped, providing a visual as well as a verbal record of problem-solving behavior.

## 4.1. Design

Participants developed information requirements specifications for three applications using a given structured methodology; that is, we used a repeated-measures design. The order of presentation of two of the three applications was counterbalanced (i.e., half the participants completed the unfamiliar application on the first trial and the unfamiliar application on the third trial, while the remaining half completed the familiar application on the first trial and the unfamiliar application on the third trial). $^{4}$ The remaining application, which was not analyzed in this research, was always completed second. It was not, therefore, part of the experimental design. The data from the first and third trials were analyzed to better differentiate the participants' attempts at learning to apply a methodology.

## 4.2. Task Materials

Two applications were used as stimulus materials in the experiment. The applications were chosen to be of roughly equivalent complexity and to be completed within two hours: the written application statements were one page in length. $^{5}$ A further criterion for choosing the applications was that participants could reasonably be expected to be familiar with one of the applications, but not with the other. The “familiar” application was known as “Vic’s Video,” a video rental information system involving rental and return of videotapes. Participants were expected to be familiar with the basic functions of the system based on their everyday experiences. The “unfamiliar” application was known as “ABC Printing,” an order processing system for a small printing company. Participants were expected to have little knowledge of order processing and the special needs of printing companies.

Standard sets of training materials were developed for each methodology to ensure comparable levels of training. Participants were provided with materials on: (1) what the methodology entailed, which included the steps involved in the methodology, the information recorded at each step, and the format in which it is recorded. We refer to this material as declarative knowledge, that is, it is knowledge about what needs to be accomplished at each step to fulfill the requirements of the methodology; and (2) how to apply the methodology; this material was presented in the form of the solution to a problem using the given methodology. By examining the example solution, participants could see how the methodology was applied in a given instance. We refer to this material as procedural knowledge. The volume of materials on methodologies presented to participants was similar for each methodology with approximately five to six pages of material on each of the declarative and procedural aspects.

## 4.3. Participants

Of necessity, the number of participants in protocol analysis studies is small. Participants were six students enrolled in a course on software engineering in the business school of a large university. Participants received instruction in a number of methodologies as part of their coursework. The students had no prior experience in using any of the methodologies investigated in the study.

Potential participants were screened for their knowledge of the applications to be investigated prior to the study. The conditions that qualified the candidates as participants were that they had rented videotapes and that they had no experience with job shop operations such as those of the ABC Printing company. All of the candidates satisfied the former criterion. One candidate did not satisfy the second criterion; she therefore participated in the pilot study, but not in the experiment proper.

## 4.4. Procedure

The novice analysts received in-class instruction, completed in-class exercises, and a homework exercise in each of the methodologies. At the experimental session, participants reviewed both the standard instructional materials (the declarative aspect of the methodology) and the application presented using the given methodology (the procedural aspect of the methodology) prior to the problem-solving session. They were then videotaped as they specified the information requirements for the three applications using the given methodology. During the study session, they could refer to the class materials on methodology as well as the example solution. Participants were permitted breaks following the completion of each requirements specification. Finally, they were debriefed regarding their performance, the difficulties they had encountered, the ways in which they overcame those difficulties, and their thoughts on the experimental session. The sessions, with requirements specification for three applications and two breaks, lasted from 3.75 to 6.35 hours.

## 4.5. Data Analysis

Two types of formal analyses were conducted on the protocol data. First, the protocol data were coded to reflect the time spent referencing certain documents. The documents examined in the study were: (1) the problem statement (i.e., the application); (2) the notes on the steps that comprise the methodology (declarative methodology knowledge); (3) the example solution specified using the given methodology (procedural methodology knowledge); and (4) the resultant specification or solution. Since the novice systems analysts who participated in this study were in the process of learning the structured methodologies they had been taught, we expected that they would still need to acquire both declarative and procedural knowledge related to those methodologies. Hence, in our analyses, we inferred that the novice analysts applied the knowledge acquired.

Second, the protocols were examined for the breakdowns that occurred during problem solving. Breakdowns are difficulties that occur during the problem-solving process [17]. In this instance, they can be viewed as impediments or obstacles to successfully specifying requirements. Breakdowns were characterized as due to problems in: (1) formulating a mental model of the application; (2) carrying out the steps of the methodology; and (3) applying the methodology to a particular application to reach a solution. In general, a breakdown of any type may be due to one of three factors: lack of knowledge, incomplete knowledge, or incorrect knowledge. Table 1 describes the types of breakdowns that may occur in each of the categories.

Breakdowns result from lack of ability to formulate a mental model in one of the above categories, or the formulation of an incomplete or incorrect mental model. For example, lack of ability to formulate a mental model of the declarative knowledge relating to the methodology means that the analyst does not know what steps to perform; having incomplete declarative methodology knowledge means that the analyst misses a step in applying the methodology; formulating an incorrect mental model of the declarative methodology means that the analyst performs a step of the methodology out of sequence, or performs a step that is not part of the methodology, hence misinterpreting what needs to be done next in applying the methodology. Similarly, lack of ability to formulate a mental model of the procedural knowledge relating to the methodology means that the analyst does not know how to apply the methodology to specify information requirements; having incomplete procedural methodology knowledge means that the analyst does not complete a particular step in the methodology; formulating an incorrect mental model of the procedural methodology means that the analyst performs a step of the methodology incorrectly.

The breakdown results are presented as made up of three components: (1) the number of breakdowns that the participant actually committed; (2) the number of breakdowns from which the participant recovered; and (3) the number of breakdowns from which the participant did not recover. $^{6}$ A recovery occurred when a participant corrected, or in some way overcame, a breakdown committed earlier in that part of the problem-solving session.

## 5.1.1. Factors Influencing Formulation of a Mental Model of Application Requirements

Based on the protocols themselves and on the debriefing sessions, it is apparent that even novice systems analysts, who most likely have only a small store of previous relevant experiences, draw upon that knowledge in specifying the information requirements for a particular application. As expected, most references to analogous systems occurred for Vic's Video.

Many participants called upon their actual experience in renting videotapes in developing a mental model of the application requirements. Subject S3 used specific experience in renting video cassettes to help him understand the information requirements of the system. He stated, “Customer may require multiple video cassettes . . . ,” when this fact was not explicitly stated in the case. Subject S5, on the other hand, shows that analogies may be drawn from the broader domain of relevant applications in comparing Vic’s Video to a library system, “Like a library system . . . in and out,” as well as from previous experience, “I am using Sam’s Software to help with my thought processes here.” Sam’s Software was the application used as an example in the study.

## 5.1.2. Factors Influencing Formulation of a Mental Model of the Methodology

That participants spent time reviewing both the declarative and procedural methodology materials during the problem-solving sessions (see Table 3) suggests that novice analysts use both types of knowledge in specifying information requirements. Overall, participants spent on average 3.3 and 1.6 minutes referencing declarative and procedural methodology knowledge, respectively. Participants also committed 26 declarative and 44 procedural methodology breakdowns, of which 19 and 40 remained unresolved, respectively. The number of procedural compared with declarative methodology breakdowns (a factor of 2.1 [40/19] for unresolved breakdowns) suggests that the procedural aspects of methodologies are more difficult for novice analysts to learn than declarative aspects.

Novice analysts make clear references to both declarative and procedural methodology knowledge in the process of specifying information requirements. They not only use both types of knowledge; they also have very clear concepts of the utility of each. For example, subject S5, knowing that he had to develop a level 1 data flow diagram stated, “Customer info. . . . need to verify customer and verify tape [the rental video]. Level 1 does not discuss actual process. Level 2 does. So I don’t want to deal with process details here . . . a little confused.” He perused the declarative methodology material where level contents are defined and decided, “I can split ‘verifies’ if I want; so I am going to call ‘verify customer info’ and another for ‘verify tape’.” He then proceeded normally.

## 5.2. Factors Influencing Formulation of a Mental Model of Specifying Information Requirements

This section addresses research question 1, whether there are synergistic effects of application and methodology knowledge in specifying information requirements, in terms of its subsets, questions 1a and 1b: whether application knowledge has an effect on the ability to apply a methodology, and vice versa.

## 5.2.1. Effect of Application Knowledge on Ability to Apply a Methodology

To address research question 1a, we examined the time spent referencing the materials on methodology and the numbers of methodology breakdowns for high and low levels of application knowledge. If there is a synergistic effect of application knowledge on methodology knowledge, we would expect less time to be spent examining the materials on methodology and fewer methodology breakdowns to result for the familiar application (Vic's Video) compared with the unfamiliar application (ABC Printing).

The mean time spent referencing the methodology for Vic's Video (5.1 minutes) was more than for ABC Printing (4.5 minutes), contrary to expectations. A similar analysis for the effect of application knowledge on the number of methodology breakdowns showed the expected result, however. The unfamiliar application resulted in larger numbers of methodology breakdowns than the familiar application (41/9/32 for ABC Printing compared with 29/2/27 for Vic's Video), suggesting that applying a methodology to an unfamiliar application results in more problems than for a familiar application. Interestingly, a substantial proportion of breakdowns for the unfamiliar application were eventually resolved. Nonetheless, there were more unresolved breakdowns for the unfamiliar than for the familiar application. As well as providing general support for research question 1, this evidence also supports research question 1a, that knowledge of the application has a positive effect on the ability to apply a methodology.

To address this issue in more detail, we assessed the support for research question 1a by controlling for differences in both application and methodology knowledge. First, we examined how application knowledge influenced both the time spent on methodology and the number of methodology breakdowns as knowledge of the methodology improved (i.e., from trial 1 to trial 3). From the viewpoint of time spent examining the methodology, we assessed whether knowledge of the application resulted in relatively less time being spent on the methodology as knowledge of the methodology improved. Table 3 shows that the time spent on methodology decreased by 76 percent (8.2 to 2.0 minutes) from trial 1 to trial 3 for the familiar application (Vic's Video) compared with 70 percent (6.9 to 2.1 minutes) for the unfamiliar application (ABC Printing). Hence, novice analysts found the methodology somewhat easier to apply to the familiar application suggesting some synergistic or reinforcing effects of application knowledge on the ability to apply a methodology.

Second, from the viewpoint of methodology breakdowns, we examined whether knowledge of the application resulted in relatively fewer methodology breakdowns as knowledge of the methodology improved. Table 4 shows that the total number of unresolved methodology breakdowns for the familiar application (Vic's Video) declined from 16 on trial 1 to 11 on trial 3, a decrease of 31 percent. Corresponding numbers for the unfamiliar application (ABC Printing) were 17 on trial 1 and 15 on trial 3, a decrease of 12 percent. Hence, there is a relatively greater reduction in unresolved methodology breakdowns for the familiar compared to the unfamiliar application as methodology knowledge increases. This result again suggests that there is a synergistic or reinforcing effect of application knowledge on the ability to apply a methodology.

Three of the four relationships examined suggest that knowledge of the application aids in applying a methodology. Hence, there is considerable support for question 1a.

## 5.2.2. Effect of Methodology Knowledge on Formulation of a Mental Model of Application Requirements

We next examine research question 1b, whether knowledge of the methodology affects the formulation of a mental model of the application requirements. We examined the effect of methodology knowledge on both the time spent referencing the application and the numbers of application breakdowns for different levels of methodology knowledge. If there is a synergistic effect of methodology knowledge on application knowledge, we would expect less time to be spent examining the problem statement and fewer application breakdowns to result for high methodology knowledge (trial 3) compared with low methodology knowledge (trial 1).

First, we compared the time spent on developing a mental model of the application as knowledge of the methodology improved. Time spent on the application decreased from 31.0 minutes on trial 1 to 20.1 minutes on trial 3, suggesting that as knowledge of the methodology improved, so did the ability to formulate a mental model of the application. Further, from trial 1 to trial 3, the mean time spent on problem solving decreased from 103.8 to 64.6 minutes, resulting in percentages of application time to total time on the two trials of 30 and 31 percent, respectively. $^{7}$ Hence, for these two applications and for this level of expertise, it appears that there may be a direct association between knowledge of the methodology and ease of developing a mental model of the application requirements, that is, as knowledge of the methodology improves, formulation of a mental model of the application requirements becomes easier, providing evidence of synergism between the two types of knowledge. As well as providing general support for research question 1, this evidence also supports question 1b.

Second, we can examine the effect of methodology knowledge on formulating a mental model of the application requirements by comparing the application breakdowns that occurred with low and high methodology knowledge (i.e., on the first and third trials). If the methodology knowledge had a positive impact on formulating a mental model of the application, the number of application breakdowns would decrease from trial 1 to trial 3. However, 6/5/1 breakdowns occurred on trial 1 compared with 7/2/5 on trial 3. Hence, these data do not support research question 1b.

Again, we can address question 1b in more detail by controlling for differences in application and methodology knowledge. First, we examined how methodology knowledge (trial 1 versus trial 3) influenced both the time spent on the application and the number of application breakdowns. From the viewpoint of time spent examining the application, we assessed whether improved knowledge of the methodology resulted in relatively less time spent understanding the requirements of the familiar application compared with the unfamiliar application. For the familiar application (Vic's Video), the time spent on the application decreased by 33 percent from trial 1 to trial 3 (23.6 minutes compared with 15.9 minutes), while that for the unfamiliar application (ABC Printing) decreased by 37 percent (38.5 minutes compared with 24.3 minutes). This result appears to indicate a compensatory rather than a synergistic effect of application knowledge on the ability to apply a methodology.

Second, from the viewpoint of application breakdowns, we can examine whether knowledge of the methodology resulted in relatively fewer application breakdowns as knowledge of the methodology improved. The application breakdown results for the two applications are unexpected: the only breakdowns for the familiar application occurred in trial 3 (7/2/5) and the only breakdowns for the unfamiliar application occurred in trial 1 (6/5/1).

The results for the influence of knowledge of the methodology on formulating a mental model of the application are problematic, with only one of the four relationships examined supporting research question 1b. A possible explanation for these apparently anomalous results may lie in the fact that, in a number of cases, participants became heavily involved in elaborating the application from their practical experience of video rental systems (and similar systems, such as library systems). Subject S1's statement, quoted in section 5.1.1, for example, substantiates this argument ("I could figure out things not included in the problem"). Although there are similarities among such systems, the systems one might meet in the real world are by no means standard. The participants, therefore, rather than having less difficulty, appeared to have more difficulty in determining the way the system was supposed to function. In the final analysis, they made their own determinations regarding system functioning. In short, the participants did not simply specify the system as presented to them, but considered many alternative ways of doing business. This approach may have led to problems in developing a mental model of the application.

Given the idiosyncratic nature of the response to different applications, we cannot pursue the effect of methodology on ability to formulate a mental model of the application directly. We can, however, further examine the effect of the interrelationship between methodology and application knowledge. For example, there does appear to be a relationship between number of application breakdowns committed and number of methodology breakdowns committed. Perusal of the breakdowns for each application over time permits the following observations. Methodology breakdowns for Vic's Video decreased from 16/0/16 to 13/2/11 from trial 1 to trial 3, while application breakdowns increased from 0/0/0 to 7/2/5. Methodology breakdowns for ABC Printing decreased from 25/8/17 to 16/1/15, while application breakdowns decreased from 6/5/1 to 0/0/0. The decrease of only 18.7 percent in number of methodology breakdowns committed for Vic's Video from trial 1 to trial 3, while the corresponding figure for ABC Printing was 36 percent, or essentially twice as high, suggests an association between the number of application breakdowns committed and the number of methodology breakdowns committed.

## 5.3. Learning to Apply a Methodology

Since the results for the effect of methodology knowledge on formulating a mental model of application requirements are quite consistent, we can further investigate the role of methodology knowledge in specifying information requirements. In the previous section, we examined total numbers of methodology breakdowns. In this section, we first examine how novice systems analysts use the different types of methodology knowledge in the information requirements specification process. Then we examine the effectiveness of each of those types of knowledge in specifying information requirements.

## 5.3.1. Use of Declarative and Procedural Methodology Knowledge in Specifying Information Requirements

The verbal protocols themselves also provide evidence to support the notion that novices acquire declarative knowledge prior to procedural knowledge. The protocol of subject S6 clearly shows that declarative methodology knowledge is insufficient, in certain instances, to apply the steps of a methodology. He said, “What are the attributes of actions?” This question is declarative in nature and, in fact, S6 perused the declarative methodology notes. Then he said, “How do you decide [ . . . what the attributes of actions are]?” This question is procedural in nature. The information can, therefore, best be obtained using the example solution. The participant first looked again at the declarative methodology notes to identify the criteria for making decisions about the attributes of actions; then he used the example solution to see how to apply the criteria. He repeated the same sequence of activities a little later in his problem solving. Hence, the verbal protocol data support the notion that novice problem solvers learn declarative methodology knowledge prior to procedural methodology knowledge.

We can also gain insight into the process of learning to apply a methodology by examining the differences in declarative and procedural methodology breakdowns committed from trial 1 to trial 3. Table 4 shows that declarative methodology breakdowns on trials 1 and 3 were 18/7/11 and 8/0/8, respectively. Comparable figures for procedural methodology breakdowns were 23/1/22 and 21/3/18, respectively. These results suggest that, although the novice analysts became more adept at knowing what steps to take for a certain methodology as the study progressed, their success in applying the methodology improved much less noticeably, supporting the notion that novice analysts become familiar with declarative methodology knowledge before they learn to apply procedural methodology knowledge.

## 5.3.2. Effectiveness of Type of Methodology Knowledge in Specifying Information Requirements

Perusal of the numbers of declarative and procedural methodology breakdowns revealed that procedural breakdowns outnumbered declarative breakdowns by factors of 1.7 (44/26) for total breakdowns and 2.1 (40/19) for unresolved breakdowns. Hence, we investigated whether participants who acquired procedural methodology knowledge during the study were more effective in specifying information requirements than those who acquired declarative methodology knowledge.

We first categorized participants according to whether they sought predominantly declarative or procedural methodology knowledge to aid them during problem solving. Examining the verbal protocols reveals that subjects S1, S4, and S6 referred exclusively to the materials on the steps involved in the methodology (declarative methodology knowledge—a total of 34.9 minutes), while S2, S3, and S5 referred primarily to the example solution (procedural methodology knowledge—17.8 minutes; declarative methodology knowledge—4.7 minutes). We see that participants who used procedural knowledge quite extensively had a total of 24/10/14 methodology breakdowns. On the other hand, those participants who made exclusive use of declarative methodology knowledge had a total of 46/1/45 methodology breakdowns. Table 5 summarizes the results. $^{8}$

Furthermore, the number of methodology breakdowns committed by the declarative methodology group on the first and third trials was 24/0/24 and 22/1/21, respectively. The corresponding figures for the procedural methodology group was 17/8/9 and 7/2/5, respectively. Hence, the use of procedural knowledge is negatively associated with the number of methodology breakdowns committed initially as well as positively associated with the number of breakdowns resolved. Further, ability to specify information requirements over time improved only for those participants who acquired procedural methodology knowledge.

Examination of the different types of methodology breakdowns for the declarative methodology group shows that, on both trials, the majority of breakdowns were procedural in nature, 17/0/17 of 24/0/24 in trial 1 and 16/1/15 of 22/1/21 in trial 3. Corresponding figures for the procedural methodology group were 6/1/5 of 17/8/9 on trial 1 and 5/2/3 of 7/2/5 on trial 3. Interestingly, the procedural methodology group also demonstrated a greater ability to resolve declarative methodology breakdowns over time than the declarative methodology group. The declarative methodology group committed the same number of declarative methodology breakdowns (7/0/7 and 6/0/6 in trials 1 and 3, respectively) as the procedural methodology group (11/7/4 and 2/0/2, respectively), but was much less successful in resolving them. Further, note that the numbers of declarative methodology breakdowns the procedural methodology group committed decreased over time (from 11 to 2), while those of the declarative methodology group were substantially similar (7 to 6). These findings suggest that those novice analysts who sought procedural knowledge did so when they already had a good grasp of the declarative aspects of the methodology.

In summary, the breakdown analyses for participants using a preponderance of either declarative or procedural methodology knowledge strongly suggest that novice analysts' problem solving was more effective when they sought procedural rather than declarative methodology knowledge. These findings support Anderson's learning theory [2].

## 6.1. Findings

Both application and methodology (or process) knowledge are essential to specify information requirements. The verbal protocol data used as the basis for this study clearly illustrate that knowledge of the application may come from direct experience of the particular application or from experience with analogous systems (the domain of similar applications, such as library systems). Further, they illustrate that methodology knowledge is comprised of both what to do (declarative knowledge) and how to do it (procedural knowledge).

The research focused on whether knowledge of the application has synergistic effects on the ability to apply a methodology, and vice versa. This objective necessitated using an investigatory setting in which knowledge of both the application and the methodology could be varied. We found that there were synergistic effects of application knowledge on methodology knowledge, that is, novice analysts were better able to apply a structured methodology when they were more familiar with the application. Results for the effect of knowledge of the methodology on ability to formulate a mental model of the application were quite idiosyncratic, however. It appears that novice analysts' knowledge of similar systems that perform somewhat differently may have led to greater difficulties in developing a mental model of the application.

Novice analysts in this study used both declarative and procedural methodology knowledge in their problem solving. However, they were far more effective in specifying information requirements when they used procedural knowledge about how to apply a given methodology. Acquisition of procedural knowledge was strongly negatively associated with the number of breakdowns committed initially and strongly positively associated with the number of breakdowns resolved. Interestingly, novice analysts who acquired procedural methodology knowledge had fewer problems with declarative methodology knowledge than those who acquired declarative methodology knowledge directly, suggesting that they had, to a large extent, internalized the declarative aspects of the methodology. Further, ability to specify information requirements over time improved only for those participants who acquired procedural methodology knowledge. These findings support Anderson's learning theory [2] that declarative knowledge is acquired before procedural knowledge and that declarative knowledge per se is insufficient for problem solving.

## 6.2. Limitations

The potential limitations of this study center on the small number of participants, on the lack of generalizability of the results to experienced systems analysts, and on the size of the applications investigated in this research. First, a small number of participants is the norm for exploratory studies such as this. For example, studies referred to in section 2.1 by Jeffries et al. [20], Guindon et al. [17], Kant and Newell [21], and Adelson and Soloway [1] collected data from nine, eight, two, and five subjects, respectively. Most of these studies examined only a fraction of the protocols in detail, however, and none of them formally coded their results. In this study, we attempted to overcome the problems associated with small samples in two ways: (1) we used a repeated-measures design; and (2) we analyzed the verbal protocol data in diverse ways. Specifically, the data were coded to reflect the time spent referencing experimental materials and the breakdowns that occurred in problem solving. Further, excerpts from the protocols themselves illustrate the quantitative findings.

Second, the issues addressed in this research necessitated a design that manipulated both knowledge of the application and of a methodology. Hence, we used novice analysts who were learning to specify information requirements as subjects. Our results do not, therefore, generalize to the body of practicing systems analysts. Future studies should investigate the relative importance of application and methodology knowledge to the problem solving of analysts who have a greater range of experience in both the application and the problem-solving domains.

Third, the applications used as stimulus materials in this study did not reflect the size (and therefore the complexity) encountered in the real world. Further studies should address such issues in the context of more realistic systems.

## 6.3. Implications of the Results

What, then, are the implications of the results for researchers, instructors in information systems, and practitioners?

From the viewpoint of researchers, the findings of this study should first be validated using other research methodologies due to the small sample necessitated by process-tracing studies.

Second, this research has important implications for research into the role of the end user in systems development and the types of knowledge that end users must have to perform this role effectively. The results of this study suggest that, for the types of systems investigated in this study, novice analysts specified information requirements more effectively as their ability to apply a methodology improved. The IS community might wish to investigate in greater depth the types of systems that end users might successfully develop and the types of training end users require to develop different types of systems.

Third, the fact that knowledge of the application plays a major role in specifying information requirements, and the rather idiosyncratic observations with respect to the application in this study, suggest that research should be conducted into the nature of applications. Research is needed to determine the characteristics of applications and then to determine whether existing methodologies for requirements specification are adequate or whether new ones need to be developed. As noted in this paper, although a range of methodologies exist, little attention has been paid to characterizing applications such that methodology can be matched to application. Note, however, that the results of this study reveal that the effects of the application are idiosyncratic rather than systematic, rendering the study of applications even more problematic.

From the viewpoint of instructors of systems analysis and design, this study reinforces something that every instructor knows, but that has not, however, previously been formally demonstrated: teaching students the characteristics of a methodology is insufficient to enable them to apply the methodology; they also require procedural knowledge about how to apply the methodology. Students therefore require extensive training in using methodologies to perform effectively (i.e., they require procedural methodology knowledge).

From the viewpoint of practitioners, the fact that training novice analysts to use a methodology has predictable effects supports the notion that persons developing information systems can be trained in the systems development process. The fact that the effects of knowledge of the application are idiosyncratic (i.e., not predictable) suggests that it may be necessary to place more than one application-knowledgable person on a systems development team.

From the viewpoint of the end user, our experience with small applications in this study leads us to the conclusion that it may be more effective to train applications-knowledgable persons to use a methodology than vice versa, since knowledge of the methodology produces more predictable information requirements specifications than knowledge of the application. This observation is, perhaps, not surprising since much effort has been dedicated to formalizing systems development methodologies, while no attempt has been made to formalize knowledge of applications. We must, however, exercise caution since the current observation needs to be investigated in the context of more realistic applications.
