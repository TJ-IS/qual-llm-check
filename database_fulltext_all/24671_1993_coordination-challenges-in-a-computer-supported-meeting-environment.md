# Coordination Challenges in a Computer-Supported Meeting Environment

MARJORIE HORTON AND KEVIN BIOLSI

MARJORIE HORTON is a Senior Research Scientist at the EDS Center for Advanced Research in Ann Arbor, Michigan. She received her Ph.D. in experimental psychology from Stanford University in 1982. Formerly, she was an Assistant Professor in Experimental Psychology at the University of California, Santa Cruz. She has also worked at IBM on human factors engineering and technical marketing. Her research interests include computer-supported cooperative work, organizational behavior, technology transfer and human-computer interaction. She is a member of the Association for Computing Machinery (ACM) and the ACM Special Interest Groups on Office Information Systems (SIGOIS) and Human-Computer Interaction (SIGCHI).

KEVIN BIOLSI is currently a postdoctoral research associate in the Department of Medical Education at the University of Illinois, Chicago. He received his Ph.D. in cognitive psychology from the University of Michigan in 1993. His research interests include decision aiding and decision support, cognitive processes in intuitive prediction, group cognition and decision making, and statistical methods for classification.

ABSTRACT: In this study we observed the coordination activities of groups collaboratively writing in a low-structure computer-supported meeting room. We used video analyses of the sessions to identify well-coordinated and poorly coordinated groups. Through user questionnaires, group members evaluated their work processes and products as well as the computer-supported environment. Writing experts independently rated the quality of the groups' final documents.

We discovered that quality of coordination was strongly tied to groups' evaluations of their work processes, work products, and tools. Well-coordinated groups tended to be more efficient than poorly coordinated groups although we observed no differences in the quality of their documents. We also identify what coordination strategies lead to effective group work and offer suggestions for additional tools to facilitate coordination.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: collaborative writing, computer-supported cooperative work, computer-supported meeting room, coordination, group work.

## Introduction

However, underlying such different approaches are the common goals of using information technology to improve communication and make group work more effective, for example, by reducing the process losses that are often observed when individuals work together in groups [7, 20].

Some of the early computer-supported meeting environments, including both GroupSystems and SAMM, provide specialized tools, often with facilitation, to structure how groups work on a range of tasks. In contrast, Colab did not require facilitation because it was designed for more sophisticated computer users. But it also had incorporated particular sequences of work into its software tools, such as Cognoter. Because such systems impose considerable structure on their users, constraining how a group completes its work, and explicitly or implicitly the group interaction process, we refer to these as “high structure” technologies $[1, 13]$ .

The Capture Lab was originally designed to offer a “low structure” environment, giving users both flexibility and broad functionality without structuring the processes by which they work or requiring a technical facilitator $[9]$ . Meeting participants may work with a shared computer and individual, private workstations, using whatever commercially available software they find most suitable for their purpose.

Such “low structure” support offers new options in how to work. However, this is not without its costs. From observing many groups working in the Capture Lab, we have discovered that such flexibility poses a considerable challenge for groups. Clearly, some basic computer literacy is important for users. Yet the real challenge to groups is to discover how best to work, how to develop strategies for combining and coordinating their use of technology and people skills to accomplish their tasks. We focus here on the coordination efforts, both the successes and the breakdowns, of small collaborative teams working in the Capture Lab. We were interested in determining how groups reacted to this flexibility and in discovering what natural coordination strategies that these groups developed would lead to effective, successful teamwork.

For the task domain, we selected collaborative writing, owing to its prevalence in the workplace $[4, 8]$ and because it has properties characteristic of many different complex collaborative tasks, including group planning, problem solving, negotiation, and decision making. In the workplace currently much of collaborative writing is asynchronous. However, given the right tools, face-to-face collaboration may be more effective, at least for certain kinds of writing (see $[18]$ ) and may lessen some of the coordination difficulties that groups face when working asynchronously, such as delays due to exchanging and cycling work among members of a team and lack of sufficient consensus $[8, 10]$ .

Clearly, when individuals work together as a group, they must coordinate their work efforts. Malone and Crowston [14] have emphasized this in their discussion of coordination theory. Components of coordination include identifying goals, mapping goals to activities, selecting actors, assigning activities to actors, and managing interdependencies. Applying these to a collaborative writing task, coordination would include identifying the group's objectives and audience for their document, determining what tasks each group member should undertake, and managing the communication about, and sharing and integration of, any individually generated work.

In addition, the concept of work structure, which Bendifallah and Scacchi [2] present in their analysis of software specification teamwork, is germane to our analysis of coordination. These authors define a work structure as “the manner in which the components of the task are distributed among team members” (p. 260) and offer a useful taxonomy of work structures for different types of tasks. For example, an integrative work structure involves group members working together continually on a task, with the final product being “a consensual outcome of concerted action” (p. 262). In contrast, a separative work structure involves group members determining how to divide a task into components for individual work, with later integration of the separately produced components.

When a particular work structure, or set of structures, best suits a given task, an important coordination activity early on in a team's collaboration is selecting the appropriate work structure(s). Important components of effective collaboration for both distributed and face-to-face work include making this selection for the particular team and task at hand, and coordinating the actors, activities, and work objects. In a computer-supported meeting room such as the Capture Lab, coordination includes managing the interplay between focused, group work on the shared computer, individual work in parallel on the individual workstations, and teamwork with subgroups sharing the public or individual computers. Groups need to determine when it is most effective to use one work structure versus another, and also what levels of communication and sharing among group members are necessary for smooth coordination. $^{1}$

One way that face-to-face collaborative technology may facilitate coordination is by functioning as a productivity tool, enabling groups to get work done within meetings, in contrast to the conventional approach, where groups meet to plan work, which they then accomplish outside of the meeting context. The emergence of a shared conceptual model and greater consensus among group members may be fostered by the shared workspace, because people can shape the group work product on the public screen, explaining their ideas and responding to questions as they proceed. The shared workspace also provides “a physical medium in which users share domain knowledge,” in a way somewhat analogous to what Nardi and Miller $[16, p. 204]$ discovered when users shared spreadsheets. In this way, team members can “coordinate” both knowledge and understanding.

The multiplicity of options for a collaborative team can in itself pose challenges to effective coordination when working in an environment like the Capture Lab. The individual computers make it possible for group members to retreat from the group in the face of frustration or conflict within the group $[13]$ . Private work by one or more group members, occurring in parallel with work on the shared, public screen, may fragment the group, which in turn can result in duplication of effort or poorly coordinated components of a group work product. During periods of extended private work, inadequate communication may have similar effects. The computers themselves may also lead a group to plan insufficiently at the outset and to move on to the drafting process too quickly $[12]$ .

Through a detailed analysis of their work sessions, we discovered that some groups coordinated their work quite effectively, with initial and ongoing communication about division of labor, allocation of resources, and integration of work, whereas others coordinated their efforts quite poorly. We compare the coordination strategies and work structures of these two types of groups, and then look at the relationship between groups' coordination efforts and how they evaluated the Capture Lab as a work environment, and their own group processes and work products. We also briefly discuss how groups' coordination efforts were related to the quality of their work products and their efficiency.

This study provides the first, in-depth empirical analysis of how groups coordinate their work in a low structure computer-supported meeting room, in the absence of any human facilitation or structured methodology built into the tools to guide their collaboration. As interest in harnessing computers for group tasks continues to grow and computer-supported environments become more common in the workplace (e.g., [3]), it is important to discover how groups can use them effectively.

## Methodology

THE SIX GROUPS WE REPORT ON PARTICIPATED AS PART of a broader study of collaborative writing in the Capture Lab. In this section we describe only those aspects of the larger study relevant to this discussion.

## Environment

All groups met for a two-hour collaborative writing session. Groups were provided with the full capabilities of the Capture Lab for their task. Each group member had a computer (Macintosh II $^{TM}$ ) consisting of a display monitor, keyboard, and mouse, which could be used privately as a workstation. This equipment was positioned around a large conference table, with the monitors recessed into the table. Group members also shared a public Macintosh II $^{TM}$ . Its output was projected on a large monitor at the front of the room. Additionally, groups had use of a laser printer in the room.

All group members had equal access to the public computer. Users accessed the public computer via a toggle key on their keyboards and only one user could control the public computer at any one time. If one user had control of the public computer and another user pressed the toggle key, the first user was reconnected to his or her personal computer and the second user gained control of the public computer. A clipboard transfer function allowed users to move sections of a document between their individual computers and the public computer using the Macintosh $^{™}$ cut and paste functions. No traditional writing tools such as pens or paper were available.

## Subjects

Six groups of M.B.A. students participated to fulfill a class assignment. Groups ranged in size from four to five members.

## Subject Profiles

Initially, all subjects filled out a questionnaire enquiring about their proficiency with computers, typing, and word processing as well as their attitudes towards writing. The proficiency section assessed expertise using a percentage scale, with 0 percent being a totally inexperienced user and 100 percent being an expert user. Subjects rated their own proficiency on items such as using a mouse, using a keyboard, using a Macintosh, and using Microsoft Word on the Macintosh. Seven-point scales, for which the endpoints were 1 = “Not at all true of me” and 7 = “Very true of me,” were used to assess attitudes towards writing. Items included statements such as, “I avoid writing,” “I like to have my writing evaluated,” and “I find writing easy.”

## Writing Tasks

The writing task was one of two short cases that involved groups in a politically sensitive business situation that compelled them to write a persuasive response in memorandum form. Management communication experts designed the two cases to model real business situations that would warrant a group writing effort. (We pilot tested the tasks to verify that groups performed similarly on the two tasks, in terms of time spent on each and assessed document quality.)

## Training

At the start of the session, training and practice pertaining to the collaborative technology were provided. The training included general suggestions about possible ways groups could take advantage of the public and private computers (e.g., taking notes or developing ideas on one's private machine). Not providing more extensive training allowed us to explore the creative strategies and patterns subjects developed on their own.

## Data Collection

We videotaped all sessions, using several cameras hidden unobtrusively behind mirrors. All screen activities on both the public and private computers were also captured with ScreenRecorder™, a program that creates a software “tape” of computer screen activity that can be played back at a later time.

## Questionnaires

All group members completed a questionnaire at the end of the session. The questionnaire included both open-ended and multiple-choice questions on five-point Likert scales and focused on the subjects' evaluation of the writing tools, task performance, effectiveness, idea communication, and members' contributions.

## Findings

## Videotape Analysis: Group Coordination and Work Structures

GROUP COORDINATION STRATEGIES AND EMERGING WORK STRUCTURES were identified via observational, qualitative analysis of the session videotapes. Both authors independently viewed the videotapes for all six groups' work sessions and individually wrote detailed scenarios of the observed work structures and coordination activities. Particular attention was paid to such activities as the nature of the groups' planning, allocation of tasks (e.g., who should write what section, who should act as scribe) and resources (e.g., who should use the public computer) among group members, communication about their ongoing work (e.g., updating others when a section of the memo is finished), and sharing and integration of individual work efforts. Other behaviors of interest included major shifts in work structures, duplication of effort, and explicit consensus reaching (or the lack thereof) about work processes. We developed this set of coordination activities drawing both from our prior observations of groups working in the Capture Lab and from the analyses of coordination offered by Bendifallah and Scacchi [2] and Malone and Crowston [14]. There was a high degree of consistency between the two sets of scenarios, as confirmed by follow-up discussions. $^{2}$ The appendix presents summaries of these scenarios for each of the six groups. The six groups are labeled A-F.

Groups were identified as either well coordinated or poorly coordinated. The well-coordinated groups executed many, if not all, of the coordination activities described above, that is, substantial high-level planning, reaching consensus about work processes, selecting particular work structures to follow, and communicating about and sharing individual efforts. In contrast, poorly coordinated groups engaged in these activities to a lesser extent, if at all, and they experienced difficulties stemming from their insufficient planning, insufficient communication and sharing, and lack of consensus about work processes.

By these characterizations, three of the six groups (A, B, and C) were identified as well coordinated, and three groups (D, E, and F) as poorly coordinated. Table 1 lists the major coordination activities for these two types of groups.

As Table 1 shows, different work structures resulted in effective coordination among the well-coordinated groups. For example, group A used what Bendifallah and Scacchi [2] characterize as an integrative structure, working together as a group for almost all aspects of the task throughout the session. In contrast, groups B and C adopted more separative structures, drafting sections of their documents individually. Several commonalities among the three groups appear important for their successful coordination: (a) all groups discussed their approach to the task up front, prior to extensive drafting, (b) all groups appeared to reach consensus on their approach, both initially and throughout the session, and (c) members of groups B and C communicated substantially about the nature of their individual work, resulting in well-coordinated individual efforts.

It is noteworthy that each of these well-coordinated groups had at least one aspect of their work that would not be considered optimal for smooth, effective coordination. In group A, for example, one member worked on his private computer without informing anyone about the nature of his work, but when other group members inquired about this, he promptly transferred his work to the public screen for public review. In group B, one member drafted a conclusion to the memo privately, not informing the group he was doing so, as the others continued to integrate their individual sections into the public document. He then transferred it to the public screen for integration into the memo. In group C, during initial brainstorming, one member drafted an introduction to the memo privately without informing the group she was doing so and also without responding to inquiries about her activity until she had completed it, at which time she pasted it to the public screen for integration.

None of these activities, however, resulted in what would be considered a major coordination breakdown, defined by Bendifallah and Scacchi $[2]$ as an “emergent disruption in the flow of work” (p. 261). We speculate that this is because in each case, only one member of the group was not well coordinated with the others, and because each willingly shared the results of the private work with the group once it was completed. Moreover, in each case, the member working privately was never working on anything overlapping with other members’ efforts. Finally, the group members who were engaged in this private work also continued to stay tuned in to the ongoing group discussion and knew the status of what the others were doing since the rest of the group was always working on the public computer (during that particular phase of work).

As shown in Table 1, the work structures among the three poorly coordinated groups also varied, for example, in the extent of group versus individual work. Similar to subjects in the well-coordinated groups, groups D and E communicated up front about the approach to the task, that is, by discussing and agreeing on how to plan and draft their memos. However, these two groups had difficulty executing their original plans for the drafting phase and abandoned them, in one case (D) not developing a clear alternative approach, and in the other case (E), choosing to write separately after conflict about how extensively they needed to plan and about differing styles. Members of group F agreed they would brainstorm together for their task, but failed to discuss or agree upon an explicit plan on how to write their memo. Instead, after a preliminary period of analyzing the case and generating ideas, individuals started to write individually with little discussion of who should, or who would, do what.

In all three groups members eventually ended up working extensively on their own, writing and/or editing. Moreover, work was carried out on both the private and public computers simultaneously. Such simultaneity meant that group members often did not attend to each other's work as it was transferred to the public computer, even when the originator tried to introduce and motivate it to the group. The most striking commonality among these groups was insufficient communication for extended periods of time about what each was doing.

Unlike the well-coordinated groups, these three groups experienced coordination breakdowns, as evidenced by their need to change work structures (see $[2]$ ), by the overlapping work or duplication of effort produced by different individuals, by the considerable periods of “dead time” as particular members watched and waited while others worked $^{3}$ and, for group E, by the increasing frustration and confusion over the extended outlining phase, with group members not even clear themselves whether they were writing their memo or still planning it.

This categorization of groups as well coordinated or poorly coordinated stems from a qualitative analysis of their videotaped work sessions. As we show below, our analysis of the groups' questionnaire responses evaluating the Capture Lab and their work sessions provides converging support for this division of groups.

## Questionnaires

The questionnaire items were grouped into five general categories: tools, task performance, group effectiveness, contribution of work, and communication of ideas. Abbreviated forms of all items, grouped by category, are presented in Table 2. The mean responses presented in Table 2 were obtained by first averaging across the responses of group members within a group and then averaging these mean responses across groups within each group type (well versus poorly coordinated). Two-group t-tests were used to compare the mean responses of the well-coordinated groups (A, B, and C) with those of the other three groups (D, E, and F), and the t-values and significance levels are presented in the rightmost columns of Table 2. These results are discussed below in the context of the more general categories.

## Tools

Subjects in the well-coordinated groups tended to evaluate the tools more favorably, in terms of both current and future usefulness. Individuals in these groups also felt they were less distracted by the computer equipment. The tool evaluations, however, do not seem to be due to differential training effects for the two types of groups, as the mean evaluations of the training did not differ significantly.

## Task Performance

Subjects in the three well-coordinated groups were substantially more positive about task performance than those in the poorly coordinated groups. They gave reliably higher ratings for the quality of their planning, composing, and revising. They were also considerably more satisfied with the final written document. Thus, the well-coordinated groups seemed to be happier with the quality of work throughout the session and as manifested in the final product.

## Group Effectiveness

Overall effectiveness and satisfaction with the group work were also rated significantly higher for the well-coordinated groups. Members of these groups felt they were more efficient and more productive than did the poorly coordinated groups. In addition, members of the well-coordinated groups reported greater satisfaction with their group than did members of the poorly coordinated groups.

## Contribution of Work

Members of the well-coordinated groups were also more satisfied with the extent of their contributions and more satisfied that their own ideas were being incorporated into the final document. They also felt that all group members contributed significantly in document preparation to a greater extent than did members of poorly coordinated groups.

## Communication of Ideas

Interestingly, there were not significant differences between well-coordinated and poorly coordinated groups on the questions evaluating communication of ideas. This suggests that both types of groups were equally capable of communicating and understanding each other's ideas and that the coordination difficulties centered more on communication about, and sharing and integration of members' evolving work contributions.

In general, the patterns of responses to the questionnaire items support our identification of groups as relatively good or poor coordinators. Additionally, these results highlight the potentially strong link between coordination effectiveness and group attitudes. Given this link, the success with which a group coordinates its work efforts may have implications for how willing group members are to work together in the future.

## Computer Proficiency/Attitudes towards Writing

Differences in computer or writing skill levels across the groups might account for the differences in overall satisfaction described above. For instance, groups with computer-proficient members may have been more comfortable with the technology and, therefore, were more positive than less proficient groups for whom using the tools might have been problematic. Similarly, subjects with more favorable attitudes towards writing may have felt more comfortable with the given task of collaborative writing.

We examined the subject profiles to determine whether there were any differences between well- and poorly coordinated groups in either computer proficiency or attitudes towards writing. No significant differences were found between the two types of groups for any of the proficiency items. For attitudes towards writing, significant or marginally significant differences were found for only 4 of the 17 items. These are shown in Table 3. Note that these results actually suggest somewhat more negative attitudes towards writing for the well-coordinated groups. Taken with the proficiency results, then, it does not seem that members of the well-coordinated groups are necessarily more skilled with computers or more adept at writing than members of the poorly coordinated groups.

However, it would be premature to conclude that, in general, a group's coordination efforts do not affect quality for this kind of collaborative task. We speculate that we may not have found any relationship because the particular writing task we used was quite directed, well structured, and perhaps too simple for the groups we studied, who were highly motivated M.B.A. students executing a graded assignment.

## Task Completion Times

We might expect that one effect of poor coordination would be an increase in the amount of time to complete a task. Therefore, as a final measure of effectiveness, we looked at overall task completion times. Two of the well-coordinated groups finished slightly before the two-hour time limit while the third filled the entire two hours. In contrast, two of the poorly coordinated groups were unable to complete the task within the allotted time while the third took the full two hours. Although these differences in time to complete the writing task are not dramatic, perhaps due to the experimentally imposed time limit, they do indicate that effective coordination is important for efficient group work and time management. $^{5}$

## Discussion and Conclusions

GROUPS ENCOUNTERING THE FLEXIBLE ENVIRONMENT of the Capture Lab face a range of challenges coordinating their work and use of the technology. Groups can vary substantially in the extent to which they are able to effectively meet these challenges, even when pursuing the relatively directed, short-term “projects” used in the present study. For example, whether or not groups engage in high-level planning, how well they allocate technical and human resources, and the extent to which they attempt to synchronize activities throughout the session are all important for effective teamwork in this environment. In addition, the questionnaire data give supporting evidence for the critical relationship between groups’ processes and their coordination efforts.

Bendifallah and Scacchi [2] found that separative work patterns were associated with improved efficiency, and integrative structures were associated with higher quality of work products. We observed work structures resembling these two patterns among some of our groups; however, we did not find such a clear relationship between these work patterns and outcome. We submit that the ongoing quality of coordination is more critical for efficient, satisfying group work than adopting a separative versus integrative work structure, at least for the kind of task that we studied. Our comparison of the well-coordinated and poorly coordinated groups points to the importance of ongoing, high-level planning for effective coordination. This suggests that groups need to maintain a negotiative work structure [2], which involves negotiation and a consensual outcome among team members regarding the organization of their work, plans, and/or schedules.

While satisfaction may not necessarily be strongly tied to quality of work product for short-lived groups—it was not for the collaborative writing groups in this study—satisfaction may still be of critical importance to group work, particularly for groups that continue to work together over time. The extent to which group members are satisfied with work sessions may set the tone for subsequent meetings and dissatisfaction may lead to a general degradation of teamwork (e.g., poor group dynamics, lack of cooperation). It seems likely that the work product will also suffer in this event.

In light of the difficulties that some groups experienced coordinating their work in our computer-supported meeting room, the question arises, is it effective to work face-to-face to accomplish tasks? Why not adhere to the conventional approach, where groups meet for planning purposes but delegate the work among task members to be completed in a distributed manner, with follow-up communication and meetings, as necessary, for integration? Obviously, for large-scale tasks such delegation of work is needed.

However, several lines of research point to the advantages that face-to-face work offers groups. Rogers and Horton's [18] case study of face-to-face collaborative writing identified several benefits that apply to many other collaborative tasks. For example, through face-to-face interaction, groups evolve a shared understanding and produce documents that possess a unified, group voice. Group awareness and understanding of the ethical issues involved in particular decisions also can be facilitated, for example, as groups evaluate the truth of their words or work to reconcile important differences in perspective. Galegher and Kraut's [11] comparison of face-to-face and computer-mediated collaborative writing groups suggests that computer-mediated communication may be less satisfying and less intellectually beneficial, and result in less commitment among collaborators. Daft and Lengel [5] also argue that the richness of face-to-face communication is critical for the kinds of problem solving and decision making faced by high-level business managers, where uncertainty and equivocality are high.

What do groups need in order to take better advantage of computer-supported environments for face-to-face work? We suggest that maintaining the low structure design of the Capture Lab is important in that it provides groups flexibility and multiple options in how to work. It may also enable groups to work on a broader diversity of tasks than more structured environments. Yet, clearly groups need to be encouraged and taught, perhaps through initial tutorial sessions and ongoing coaching, how to impose structure on their process and on their evolving work products. Providing them with tools specifically to facilitate their coordination of work may also prevent some of the difficulties we observed as groups pursued individual work with insufficient communication.

For example, we have added Aspects $^{™}$ , a commercial application for simultaneous group authoring. Aspects $^{™}$ provides collaborators many options for sharing documents. Timbuktu $^{™}$ is another mechanism available in the lab for screen sharing. Also, using the “Publish and Subscribe” functions of the Macintosh System 7’s software, collaborators can selectively share files or portions of files very easily and unobtrusively, when using many different commercial applications. These tools allow group members to view each other’s ongoing private work without disrupting others’ work. They would need to overcome the initial learning curve involved in learning how and when to use them, and such tools would not preclude the need for planning. An important arena for future research will be to explore whether providing groups with these kinds of tools would stimulate them to impose more structure and coordination on their own work process.

For groups to exploit these tools fully and also to develop strategies for effective coordination and collaboration requires experience, discipline, creativity, and an openness to working in new ways. It is hoped that the returns for this investment will be more effective, personally satisfying team work and ultimately, improved quality as well.

## APPENDIX: Video Scenarios of Groups' Coordination Activities

Here we present summaries of the very detailed scenarios the researchers developed. Groups A and F each had five members; all other groups had four.

## Well-coordinated Groups

A. The group brainstorms together, using the public screen along with some individual note taking, which gets pasted into the public screen. Group members agree to compose as a group and proceed to do so, using the public computer with only occasional, brief work by anyone at their private computers. The group also edits and revises their document using the public, shared computer.

B. The group brainstorms together and then decides to outline together, using the public computer. They agree to divide the task and to work individually on drafting sections. They paste these sections into the public machine and integrate them as one group member simultaneously participates in this and composes the concluding paragraph on his private computer. He then pastes this last section into the public document for integration.

C. Group members decide to generate ideas and an outline together and use the public screen for this. One member writes an introduction to the memo on her private computer, without informing the other group members about the nature of her work. She also contributes to the group discussion. After reading this introduction to the group, she pastes it into the public screen for group review and revision. After more group work on a detailed plan, group members divide the task up and write individually on their private computers, setting a time limit. They use the public screen for group review and editing, pasting the individual sections up.

## Poorly Coordinated Groups

D. Group members brainstorm individually, and then transfer their private notes to the public machine for group creation of an outline. The group decides to have one person write the memo and the others to edit it. The writer has difficulty writing while other group members are idle, talking, so he asks them to edit his writing on the public computer as he generates more text privately. However, the writer joins in the group editing of his text and group members begin to do different things (e.g., simultaneously writing new text and editing each other's work, on both the private and public computers) with minimal communication about what each is doing. Eventually, group members paste their individual work to the public screen and edit together.

E. Group members brainstorm using the public computer but disagree about whether to create an outline next or write the memo's introduction. Early on they have agreed to write as a group. After an extensive period of outlining on the public screen, with several group members also working on their individual computers with minimal or no communication about what each is doing, some group members are confused about whether the group has begun writing the memo or is still creating an outline.

Dissension about how to proceed continues. Eventually group members divide the task and each takes a section to write individually, in parallel. The last part of the session is spent integrating individual contributions, editing and composing the conclusion as a group. The group runs out of time before completing the memo.

F. Group members brainstorm using both the public and occasionally private computers. One member announces that she will write a paragraph on a particular topic, and others proceed to work individually without informing each other about the nature of their work. Questions from group members about who is working on what often go unanswered. The session includes a mix of private and public work, in parallel. Group members at times write individually on the same topics. The group decides to integrate and edit text as a group. The group is confused at times about where particular text is (on public versus private computers). At the end, the group works together to integrate and edit, but runs out of time.
