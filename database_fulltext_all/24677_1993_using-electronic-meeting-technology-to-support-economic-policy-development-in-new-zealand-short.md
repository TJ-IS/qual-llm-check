# Using Electronic Meeting Technology to Support Economic Policy Development in New Zealand: Short-Term Results

JIM SHEFFIELD AND R. BRENT GALLUPE

JIM SHEFFIELD is Senior Lecturer in Information Systems at the University of Auckland. He received his Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Arizona. He teaches management of information systems in the M.B.A. program and database systems in the MIS and computer science programs. His research interests are in electronic meeting systems, particularly in facilitation techniques, and the impact of communication media on negotiation tasks.

R. BRENT GALLUPE is an Associate Professor in the School of Business at Queen's University at Kingston, Canada. His research interests include group support systems, end-user computing, and the evaluation of information systems. His work has been published in Management Science, MIS Quarterly, Academy of Management Journal, and Journal of Applied Psychology.

ABSTRACT: This paper describes an application of group support technology to a series of economic policy-making meetings. The meetings were part of a national study aiming to upgrade New Zealand's competitive position in global markets. The aim of the research is to determine if electronic meeting technology could support an economic development process where participants came from a variety of backgrounds (e.g., business competitors, different ethnic groups) and where meeting urgency and efficiency were of prime importance. A multiple case approach was adopted. The meetings were organized and facilitated by the coordinator of the national study. Research data were gathered by observing the meetings, by questionnaires administered to participants, and by interviews with the coordinator and his staff. Analysis of these data indicates that participants felt that the meetings were both very effective and efficient. Implications for using group support technology for economic policy development are discussed.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: economic development, electronic meeting systems, group support methodology, innovation.

## 1. Introduction

INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY HAS LONG BEEN USED TO SUPPORT economic policy development by governments in industrialized countries. Computer-based economic models and analysis of census data are examples of the use of information technology by national policy makers. However, few studies report on the use of information technology to directly support economic policy development by industry groups.

This article describes an application of group support technology for a series of twelve economic policy meetings. The technology aids teams, each team representing an important economic sector in New Zealand, to generate ideas on how to restructure and rejuvenate the New Zealand economy. This initiative called Advantage Auckland used the GroupSystems group support system to assist teams of 14 to 33 persons to brainstorm and organize their ideas, and to create detailed action plans. The meetings were organized and facilitated by an economic policy expert. This person had considerable experience with leading conventional meetings and was independent of the research team.

The primary aim in the study of these meetings is to determine if electronic meeting technology could support an economic development process where participants came from a variety of backgrounds (e.g., business competitors, different ethnic groups) and where meeting urgency and efficiency were of prime importance. The main research questions were: (1) What was the impact of using electronic meeting technology on the efficiency and effectiveness of these meetings? (2) What impact did the facilitator have on the outcomes of these meetings? And (3) what impact did the structured meeting process have on participants' perceptions of the meetings?

A multiple case study approach was adopted $[1, 7]$ . The role of the research team in the actual meetings was limited to observation and data gathering. Research data were gathered through observation of twelve meetings, questionnaires administered to 200 participants, and interviews with the coordinator and his staff.

Previous published descriptions of the use of electronic meeting technology $[13, 18]$ have focused on groups or multiple groups made up of members from one organization (such as IBM or Boeing) dealing with organization-specific issues. We report here on a series of electronically supported meetings where the participants were from a variety of organizations that had agreed to meet in order to address industrywide issues, not organizational issues.

We begin with a description of the background for this study, including where these meetings fit in the economic policy making process in New Zealand. We then outline the meeting process that was used and the specific application of the meeting support technology. This is followed by a brief description of the case study research approach used. The results of research are described and discussed. Finally, we offer some conclusions and lessons learned.

## 2. Background

## 2.1. The New Zealand Porter Project and Advantage Auckland Meetings

ADVANTAGE AUCKLAND IS PART OF A NATIONAL STUDY, implemented with the assistance of Harvard's Michael Porter, to upgrade New Zealand's competitive position in global markets. The study, while officially entitled “Upgrading the Competitive Advantage of New Zealand,” was generally referred to in New Zealand as The Porter Project. The project started with an analysis of the New Zealand economy and the development of recommendations for improvement. Case studies were completed on 20 economic sectors which in total comprised 85 percent of New Zealand’s exports.

The results of the first phase of the Porter Project were published in an influential book entitled Upgrading New Zealand's Competitive Advantage [3]. The book was intended to serve as a basis for positive action by individuals, companies, unions, industry groups, and government. It sought to explain why New Zealand needed:

\- a new, more comprehensive economic framework;

\- a fundamental re-engineering of attitudes, strategies, and institutions;

\- systematic upgrading of sources of competitive advantage.

In 1991, however, the New Zealand economy was in recession. Most businesses were dependent on the shrinking local market and as a consequence faced severe competition on price and high levels of business failure. Growth in export earnings became the primary goal of government economic policy. Cooperative efforts to upgrade competitive advantage were urgently required—yet were expected to be difficult to arrange.

Advantage Auckland meetings were part of a national series organized and facilitated by Porter Project coordinator Graham Crocombe (see figure 1 for an overview of the Porter Project process). The meetings would include all those industry sectors that the Porter Project had described as high priority such as furniture, software, tourism, and agricultural technology. The expectation was that New Zealand's economy would be enhanced via the implementation of these initiatives.

The meetings had four key objectives:

\- to involve a large number of business leaders with a variety of backgrounds in sector and enterprise planning;

\- to assist those who were business competitors to move beyond price competition in local markets and seek opportunities for joint action to upgrade industry competitiveness in world markets;

\- to develop business opportunities for ethnic groups such as Maori who were suffering from high rates of unemployment;

\- to develop a collaborative action plan containing five initiatives that the meeting participants were committed to implement.

Twelve one-day Advantage Auckland sessions were held with groups ranging in size from 14 to 33 participants (Table 1). Each Advantage Auckland meeting was the middle phase of a three-phase meeting cycle [15]. Premeting, meeting and postmeeting phases made up the Advantage Auckland meeting cycle. The premeeting phase began with activity by Porter Project staff to organize each meeting. Once an invitation to attend was accepted, an agenda for the meeting was sent to that participant along with a copy of Upgrading New Zealand's Competitive Advantage [3] and a worksheet. Each participant was asked to prepare for the upcoming meeting by reading this book and recording on the worksheet ideas that might help the industry. The book and the worksheet strongly reflected the structure of Porter's Diamond Model [17] of industry competitiveness (figure 2). The model is descriptive and, unlike more highly structured analytical models, provides an appropriate set of issues on which to focus group discussion by those responsible for its application [2].

Postmeeting activities focusing on the implementation of the action plans developed during the meeting will be reported in a subsequent paper.

Prior to the research study, Crocombe had facilitated an initial series of meetings in Hawkes Bay, New Zealand. The meeting process—which was an abbreviated version of that designed for the subsequent Advantage Auckland meetings—was supported by flip charts and whiteboards. Observers reported that although most participants responded favorably to the meetings, the meeting design suffered from certain limitations. The most obvious limitation was that imposed on the size of the meeting. To ensure active participation the number of participants (excluding the facilitators) was limited to twelve. Because of the limited technologies for sharing information, larger groups were expected to be unwieldy. However, this restricted the number of ideas available to the group and the resources available for the implementation of the action plans. This was a major limitation in a process designed to build a broad consensus for implementing the recommendations of the Porter Project.

## 2.2. Electronic Meeting Technology

After the Hawkes Bay meetings, the coordinator of the Porter Project decided to stage all Advantage Auckland meetings in a new electronic meeting facility at the University of Auckland. This facility, called the Decision Support Centre (DSC), consists of a large room containing 20 microcomputers set out on an elongated table. In addition, the DSC contains a set of four large, moveable whiteboards for more traditional methods of recording the group's outcomes. The purpose of the computer facilities is to run meeting support software such as Ventana Corporation's GroupSystems.

GroupSystems is a text-based electronic meeting support system. (For further information about GroupSystems and the use of electronic meeting technology, see [9, 12, 13, 14, 16, 18]. GroupSystems consists of a number of software tools that support a variety of meeting processes. For example, the “Voting Support” tool enables each group member individually to rank order a list of items (ideas, issues, or alternatives), automatically accumulates the votes, and shows the result to the entire group.

Another tool is the “Topic Commenter” tool. This was the most widely used tool in the Advantage Auckland meetings. The Topic Commenter is a “windowing” tool that enables each group member to enter items into a number of predefined windows. The process can be controlled to allow all group members to enter items into only one window at one time, or to enable group members to enter items into any of the windows at any time. The tool allows all group members to see the items entered by any other group member. Entry of items can be anonymous or non-anonymous. Each window is labeled with a specific title. Up to 20 windows can be used. The tool's primary purpose is to support the generation and simultaneous organization of ideas, comments, and the like from all the group members.

For the Advantage Auckland meetings, windows would be set up to match the determinants of Porter's Diamond Model. Each window would match one of the topics on the worksheet that was sent to participants along with Upgrading New Zealand's Competitive Advantage [3] before the meeting (figure 3). The use of Porter's Diamond Model to frame the discussion was designed to provide both useful cues for the elicitation of the domain knowledge of participants, and to inform this knowledge with the results of previous research [3, 17].

## 2.3. Overview of an Advantage Auckland Meeting

The process followed in all twelve Advantage Auckland meetings was the same. The process was devised by members of the research team in consultation with the independent facilitator. The role of the research team in the actual meetings was limited to observation and data gathering. Responsibility for implementing the process lay entirely with the independent facilitator and his staff assistants.

The actual meeting process was a combination of facilitated group discussion $[6]$ and the use of the information technology to collect and organize ideas from all group members $[19]$ . The final design reflected the assumptions of the research team and facilitator:

\- that some participants would require assistance in “unfreezing” from their initial viewpoints [8, 19, 20];

\- that anonymous brainstorming on carefully selected topics would build opportunities for collaborative action [14] although brief oral discussions would be required for agreement on key ideas [22];

\- that building commitment to implement the action plans was primarily a social process that could best be supported in a rich communication medium [4, 5, 11].

Each meeting was divided into five stages (Table 2; see appendix A for details of each meeting stage). Each stage had a distinct purpose and each built on the previous stage. In the first three stages, the facilitator and the technology provided a strong organizing structure for the brainstorming entries entered anonymously by individual participants. Brainstorming was conducted at three levels: (1) objectives, (2) review of competitive advantages and disadvantages, (3) actions to upgrade the competitive advantage of the industry. In the last two stages, structure was not imposed—it emerged largely from the direct face-to-face interaction of the participants. In these stages the facilitator served primarily as coach and the electronic support served primarily as a memory aid. In stages four and five, subgroups developed detailed action plans and the commitment to implement them.

A total of 14–20 windows were set up in Topic Commenter to support these five stages. Initial comments were entered before the start of the meeting to provide cues about the scope of the ideas and the key issues to be addressed on each topic. Approximately 25 minutes were spent on each topic in stages one and two. Several different activities occurred during this 25-minute period. First, the meeting facilitator briefly described the topic. Group members then spent about ten minutes generating ideas on the topic. Three to five pages of ideas were collected by Topic Commenter on each topic. Each group member could use the ideas of others as they emerged to elaborate on those ideas (but not actually changing them). Members then spent five minutes reviewing all the ideas on the topic to identify key themes. Each member in turn orally explained one key theme to the facilitator who then wrote key points on one A5-sized vinyl patch on a whiteboard. After a short discussion, members began work on the next topic. This left the facilitator free to group the ideas on the whiteboard and to add them to Topic Commenter where they formed an executive summary of the group's ideas on each topic.

## 3. Research Method

THE RESEARCH METHOD WAS A MULTIPLE CASE STUDY METHOD $[1, 7]$ that used each meeting as an individual case. The results from each case were combined with those from other cases in an attempt to provide a broader picture of the impact of using electronic meeting technology for these types of meetings. This approach seems reasonable since the same technology, site, facilitator, and meeting process were used for each meeting.

The research team consisted of the primary researchers (the authors of this paper) and two research assistants. As previously stated, the facilitator or his assistants were not part of the research team. The role of the research team was primarily one of data gathering and analysis. The data gathering techniques that were used included direct observation, interviews with the facilitator and his staff, interviews with meeting participants, analysis of meeting reports and computer files, and a questionnaire that was administered to participants at the end of their meeting.

This section summarizes the factors affecting the success of the Advantage Auckland meetings and the design of the questionnaire instrument used to measure the perceptions of attendees.

Each meeting was observed by a member of the research team who received a copy of the transcript and the questionnaires completed by participants. Each meeting participant was given a standard questionnaire that asks for perceptions of the efficiency and effectiveness of the meeting (see appendix B). Exactly 200 responses were received and analyzed from the twelve Advantage Auckland sessions.

This rationale and the nature of the factors considered important to success suggest that the effectiveness of the meetings could be measured at three levels (figure 4): (1) overall effectiveness; (2) effectiveness of the resources provided (that is, the facilitator and the technology); (3) effectiveness of the process (that is, reducing barriers to the free flow of ideas, participation, information exchange, and meeting outcomes).

## 4. Short-Term Results of Advantage Auckland Meetings

THE PRIMARY RESULT FOR EACH PARTICIPANT from their meeting was a 50- to 80-page bound transcript that reflected the topics identified in Table 2. In addition, each participant had committed to one or more industry initiatives.

The meeting transcript was one of the pieces of data that was analyzed by the research team. The second major set of data from the meetings was the questionnaire data.

The results of the questionnaire data are summarized below.

## 4.1. Meeting Efficiency

Questionnaire item 1 was, “You spent 8 hours in the Decision Support Centre to achieve today’s result. How many hours would you expect to spend to achieve the same result by conventional means?”

\- Participants felt that if the meeting were held using conventional meeting support it would have taken three times as long.

In other words, the electronic meeting system provided a 67 percent time saving over a conventional meeting using whiteboards and flip charts. (Because the distribution of responses is positively skewed, the median result for each group was determined and averaged over all groups. Participants' responses had a mean of 3.25 days and a standard deviation of 2.3 days.)

Assuming that participants' perceptions were accurate and assuming that a three-day meeting would have been held had the Decision Support Centre not been available, then the use of electronic meeting support saved 4,000 hours of personnel time.

Individual responses ranged from 8 hours to 60 days. No participant in any of the twelve meetings reported that the process was less efficient than a conventional meeting. The results across twelve Advantage Auckland sessions were highly consistent.

## 4.2. Meeting Effectiveness

Scales were constructed from the 21 questionnaire items numbered 4 through 24 to obtain the seven measures of effectiveness illustrated in figure 4. Results are reported on a seven-point scale (1 meaning low effectiveness, 7 meaning high effectiveness).

\- Overall effectiveness (items 4–6) = 6.22, std dev = 0.61. Overall, the participants thought that the workshop was excellent. Participants seemed genuinely to enjoy being members of the group and were well satisfied with their ability, both as individuals and as a group, to be productive.

\- Effectiveness of facilitation (items 7–8) = 6.28, std dev = 0.67. Most participants felt that the way the session was run by the facilitator was excellent and his organizing of material on the whiteboard was highly effective.

\- Effectiveness of technology (items 9–12) = 6.26, std dev = 0.74. This high result probably represents some novelty effect but participants found that the technology was very easy and enjoyable to use. Participants also felt that the technology helped them to focus and refine their ideas.

\- Reducing barriers (items 13–15) = 6.36, std dev = 0.76. This scale was used to determine if participants felt that there were barriers to communication. Participants felt that the barriers were extremely low and that internal politics, personalities, and status did not impede the free flow of ideas.

\- Participation (items 16–18) = 5.94, std dev = 0.77. Participants felt actively involved throughout the day and felt that all group members participated equally. Both as individuals and as a group, participants viewed themselves as creative.

\- Information exchange (items 19–22) = 5.73, std dev = 0.79. Participants expressed both the willingness and the ability to give valuable information to others. Participants also felt that they received both valuable ideas and support on issues of personal significance.

\- Meeting outcomes (items 23–24) = 6.24, std dev = 0.68. Participants felt that the issues that surfaced during the brainstorming (i.e., on objectives, industry review, and action items) were highly relevant and that at least one of the action plans was worthy of active support.

\- Average effectiveness (items 4–24) = 6.12, std dev = 0.48. Participants' perceived effectiveness of the meeting (measured via the average of 21 items) ranged from 4.52 to 7.00. On this measure, no participant in any of the twelve meetings considered the meetings ineffective.

## 4.3. Observational Findings

Some observational findings are also of note. Participants appeared to like using the technology to express their ideas. While entering ideas, people made comments to the facilitator and his assistants which indicated enjoyment with the process. A common comment was that it would not have been possible to hold the meeting without the supporting technology. No one in any of the groups expressed reluctance to use the keyboards to enter his or her ideas. However, some participants were very slow typists and in most groups the facilitator's staff persuaded one or two people to accept assistance. Participants during the sessions commented on how easy the system was to use. The dividing up of the process into brief idea generation episodes seemed to help keep participants productive. The executive summaries at the beginning of each window in Topic Commenter helped prevent information overload.

The portion of the meeting using the whiteboards to organize ideas appeared to be more complicated and cognitively more difficult. This process was driven by the facilitator and relied heavily on his ability to conceptualize and think on his feet. While the GroupSystems software has some capabilities in this area, the low-tech solution using detachable vinyl sheets on large movable whiteboards was chosen because it placed fewer demands on participants.

Interestingly, the relatively small number of negative comments addressed mainly the regular face-to-face meetings of project groups. Some participants felt that their participation was restricted by other dominant individuals. Others felt that their whole group got lost. Significantly, some individuals complained that too many members of their group lacked the motivation or skills actually to implement the plans they had helped to create. In addition some plans were ambitious. Success would require not only great effort but considerable additional resources and the cooperation of a variety of stakeholders who were not represented in the meeting.

To summarize the results, the questionnaire results indicate that participants felt the efficiency of the meeting enabled three days work to be completed in one day—a 67 percent time savings. Participants rated meeting effectiveness as 6.12 on a seven-point scale (1 meaning ineffective and 7 meaning effective). Observational findings provided additional support for these results.

## 5. Discussion

THE SHORT-TERM RESEARCH RESULTS SUGGEST THAT THE MEETINGS have been successful in meeting the four objectives described previously. Approximately 240 business leaders with a variety of backgrounds participated in what they considered a very cost-effective exercise in sector and enterprise planning. Business competitors showed a willingness to talk to each other about possible joint action. Large numbers of ideas were generated and shared in each session. The chairman and other representatives from the Maori Development Corporation commented that the meeting was helpful in developing business opportunities for Maori. Action plans were developed and commitments made. Participants have left the meetings satisfied with the process and the outcomes. Most have expressed interest in followup meetings.

In terms of the research questions that drove our investigation into these meetings, the apparent short-term success of the meetings seems to result from four complementary factors: (1) the composition of the group, (2) the skill of the facilitator, (3) the appropriate use of the electronic technology, and (4) the structure of the process used.

## 5.1. Team Composition

Members of the research team observed the planning sessions held by the facilitator and his staff both before and after most of the Advantage Auckland meeting. Notes from these interviews provide the basis for the following comments about the recruitment of the participants at Advantage Auckland meetings.

A key assumption underlying the organization of the Advantage Auckland meetings was that the meetings would attract key industry stakeholders, each of whom owned at least some of the ideas and/or the resources necessary to implement the action plans. Ultimately the quality of meetings outcomes would depend on the resources, motivation, and commitment of the members of each group.

The meetings were organized by the facilitator and his staff under severe time pressures. Even when the identity of key players was known, it proved impossible to schedule a day when all could attend. Some of those who declined to attend stated that they did not feel comfortable talking about industrywide competitive advantage with their own business competitors.

Meeting participants did, however, represent a broad spectrum of their industry. Most were presidents or senior managers of leading companies, government planners and economists, or leaders of industries (e.g., financial services, education) that had a direct interest in the industry being considered. Most participants were peers and status differences were minimized. Some participants knew each other well and others had never met. Some participants, but not all, were direct competitors. Many participants expressed differing motivations for attending these meetings. Some wanted to influence the way their industry was going. Others only wanted “industry intelligence” or attended because they did not want to be left out. Groups varied in size and in the total amount of resources commanded.

Most of the participants were senior managers and industry leaders and therefore very busy people running their own organizations. A major constraint on their participation was that the one-day meetings must provide a cost-effective use of their time. Most participants felt the meetings were important to longer-term development but that pressing individual company problems could not be neglected. It was imperative to them that the time away from their organizations be well spent.

In order to achieve a mixing of ideas and a consensus on what should be done for each industry, it was necessary to involve as many people as possible. The average meeting size was 22 people (including the facilitator). It is extremely difficult for even small group meetings to ensure everyone contributes and consensus is reached. It is much more difficult for larger groups.

## 5.2. Impact of the Facilitator

The Advantage Auckland meetings were different from many intraorganizational meetings in the sense that roles (as in marketing and production) and status differences (as in bosses and subordinates) among participants were not well defined. The Advantage Auckland meetings brought together people with different perspectives and organizational affiliations who nevertheless expected that common goals would emerge. Some participants were direct competitors, others were industry suppliers or members of related and supporting industries. All were keenly aware of their individual exposure to business failure. Meeting participants did not know whether or not their own objectives were shared by others. They expected the facilitator to create the environment in which common goals could emerge and be invested with energy and resources that otherwise would be spent within the confines of individual organizations.

The research team expected that in this type of meeting the first and most important task of the facilitator was to make the participants feel comfortable and motivate them to participate actively. The essential nature of the Advantage Auckland meetings was the generation and sharing of ideas within the framework of a conceptual model. A key role of the facilitator is to reduce barriers to the free flow of ideas.

The research team expected on the basis of their own experience with electronic meeting technology that another important task for the facilitator was to determine when and when not to use the technology. The facilitator of Advantage Auckland meetings had to be able to manage the switch between communication modes. The research literature suggests that considerable synergy may accrue from balancing a relatively complex series of meeting stages and activities $[9, 11, 12]$ .

The third important task of the facilitator was the grouping of ideas into action areas. This is something that current electronic meeting systems cannot do well. The expertise of the facilitator in grouping ideas and getting agreement on that classification was one of the keys to success of the Advantage Auckland meetings.

The facilitator's role appeared to be one of process leadership. The facilitator added very little content or ideas to these meetings. It was up to the industry members themselves to generate the ideas. The facilitator drove the process that made this happen. At times the facilitator had to enforce time limits and restrict discussion to focus the group on the task. At other times, the facilitator had to allow the group free rein to explore and expand on the ideas generated.

## 5.3. Impact of the Technology

While it was the facilitator who both guided and drove the process, the technology was the vehicle that carried the meeting towards its destination. The problems experienced by the facilitator with the Advantage Hawkes Bay meetings provided preliminary indications that whiteboards and flip charts were an inadequate vehicle for this type of meeting. This section reviews the general nature of the benefits expected from the technology.

It was anticipated that the use of the technology would enable everyone's input to be generated simultaneously. At the same time each group member would be able to see and to build on the ideas of other group members. The use of the Voting tool would allow the group to reach consensus more quickly and remove options that should no longer be considered. The anonymity of idea generation and voting allowed group members with ideas that were novel or that differed from the group average to express them without concern of being criticized. Anonymity was expected to reduce barriers to the free flow of ideas among participants who might otherwise see themselves as competitors and be unwilling to share business-related information.

It was hoped that participants would find the technology flexible and easy to use. The Topic Commenter tool allows new windows to be added as the group required. Labels on windows could be changed to fit the group's interpretation of its needs. The Voting Support tool could be modified to handle different lists of items and different voting procedures. Previous experience of the researchers was that most people become totally proficient in the use of the Topic Commenter or Voting software within two or three minutes. However, keyboarding skill may still inhibit some group members from participating fully. The facilitator's two assistants were to act as scribes and keyboard ideas for those participants who were uncomfortable.

## 5.4. Impact of the Process

Measurement by the research team of the overall success of the Advantage Auckland meetings required a process framework or methodology by which the “fit” between the group, task, facilitator, and technology could be organized. The rationale for the framework adopted by the research team was the cost-effective participation by relatively large and diverse groups in activities ranging from the initial creation of ideas through informed and enthusiastic consensus for project implementation. A task model (the Diamond Model from Porter) was required to guide the content of the information exchanged and the action plans that were a key outcome of the meeting.

The five-stage meeting process devised by the research team was intended to support this rationale. The first three consisted of creative, brainstorming exercises (that is, the parallel and anonymous sharing of individual's ideas on objectives, industry competitiveness, and action opportunities) followed by two convergent phases (first, the removal of uncertainties about project scope and structure; second, the removal of equivocality about participant's willingness to support the actual implementation of the project).

From another perspective, the independent facilitator provided the following statement to summarize what he believed were the major benefits of the electronic technology:

\- Acceleration of the meeting. The use of the electronic technology enabled 33 people to express their ideas at one time and still see the ideas of others. This resulted in large time savings and a reduction in frustration from having to wait to contribute.

\- Focus on the quality of the ideas rather than on the person putting them forward. The technology enabled groups to focus more on the facts and less on the politics of the situation. The result was more positive meetings.

\- Increased participation, information exchange and maximum group size. Even in a group of 33, everyone was able to contribute throughout the meeting.

\- Increased commitment to action. The broad and active participation led to a sense of ownership and appeared to increase the commitment to action.

\- Permanent record. The use of the keyboards automatically documented progress. This documentation helped groups to avoid confusion and repetition during the meeting. It also provided them with explicit and detailed plans to guide subsequent activities.

Members of the research team believe that the independent facilitator played an important role in these meetings. After observing all twelve meetings and noting participants' responses and comments, the researchers believe that the key contributions of the facilitator were:

\- Perceived authority. The stature of the facilitator provided the credibility required to attract people to the meetings.

\- Positive framing. The facilitator modeled the attitudes that participants required to focus on positive action. This created an environment in which participation and cooperation flourished.

\- Management of group processes. The facilitator's expertise in group dynamics greatly assisted in building and maintaining a positive momentum.

\- Time management. The facilitator's sense of timing enabled synergy to be obtained from a relatively complex series of meeting stages and activities.

\- Ability to organize ideas and action items into categories that make sense to the group. Considerable conceptual skills were required to integrate the contributions of individual participants.

\- Domain expertise. The facilitator possessed the knowledge to interpret the contributions of each industry group in terms of a conceptual model—that is, Porter's Diamond model of industry competitiveness.

\- Staff support. Considerable support from staff was essential to recruit large numbers of senior business people.

## 6. Conclusions

THE ADVANTAGE AUCKLAND MEETINGS ARE AN IMPORTANT NATIONAL initiative in New Zealand. Both in the short term and in the long term the meetings may facilitate the upgrading of the New Zealand economy. However, conclusions on the long-term results of the meetings must await the completion of the follow-up study (figure 1). Interviews have been conducted to determine the status of the action plans developed in the Decision Support Centre. Preliminary results indicate that some plans will be more developed than others. Measures of success include the number of action plans implemented and the degree of success. The followup study will also assess participants' perceptions and attitudes to meetings outcomes and processes one to two years after they were held.

It is interesting to speculate whether positive results may be obtained in other countries and cultures. The financial and technical resources required to obtain and maintain the electronic meeting system pose a barrier to entry. An experienced and resourceful facilitator is essential. Assuming these conditions are met, cultural barriers may still remain.

Interestingly, observation of the 22 Maori participants in the Maori Development Corporation meeting provided preliminary data on cultural issues. In the Polynesian culture, affiliation to family groups and tribes are of central importance. Oral rather than written traditions are strong. A meeting or “hui” is likely to be marked by extended oral expressions of social and emotional issues. Can the parallel and anonymous input of ideas at individual workstations support such an agenda?

Informal observation suggests that the Polynesian groups rapidly adapted the technology to their own needs. The process of reflecting, typing, reading others' ideas, and brief oral discussions on key themes appeared intuitive. The 50-page report produced by the Maori Development Corporation served as an adequate vehicle for the expression of memorable social and emotional issues. For example, a suggested objective for Maori development was “kids jumping out of their skins” through excitement of the opportunities that awaited them. After one hour in the Decision Support Centre the chairman remarked, amid laughter, that the process “was better than 10 hui.”

In conclusion, the short-term results suggest that the objectives of the Advantage Auckland meetings are being met. Electronic meeting technology supported an economic development process where participants came from a variety of backgrounds (e.g., business competitors, different ethnic groups) and where meeting urgency and efficiency were of prime importance. The efficiency and effectiveness of the Advantage Auckland economic development meetings are similar to the results reported by other researchers $[13, 18]$ for strategic planning and requirements analysis applications within a single business organization.

## APPENDIX A: Details of Advantage Auckland Meeting Stages

## Stage 1: Objectives

The first stage was designed to assist all group members to participate in a success visualization exercise. This was an important process for the mood at the time was negative and generally debilitating $[10]$ and some participants saw themselves as business competitors. The exercise entailed generating and collecting objectives from each group member and sharing those objectives with the entire group. The Topic Commenter tool from GroupSystems was used to facilitate the anonymous generation and sharing of the objectives. The facilitator grouped the objectives into categories on the whiteboards and a group statement of objectives was developed.

## Stage 2: Competitive Advantages and Disadvantages of the Industry

The second stage of the meeting focused on developing a consensus about the current competitive advantages and disadvantages of the industry. Again, the goal of the process was to allow all group members to participate equally. Through discussion, consensus was reached as to which ones were most important. This part of the meeting process was based on Porter's “Diamond Model” [17].

The second stage concluded with a “straw vote” using the Voting Support tool. Each group member surveyed the 25–35-page report produced from the idea generation session using Topic Commenter. Each group member was asked to rank order the six to nine topics to provide a preliminary indication of the focus of the action plan to be produced. The Voting Support tool immediately tabulated the votes and produced a report on the rankings for each group member to consider [21].

## Stage 3: Actions to Enhance Competitive Advantage

Stage three examined the opportunities to enhance competitive advantage and overcome the obstacles to growth that had been identified. During this stage, each group member analyzed the information produced during stage two and identified those items on which action should be taken. Four to ten pages of actions were collected by Topic Commenter. Each person reviewed these actions and during the brief discussion identified three actions or opportunities that were most important to him or her. Each action was written by the facilitator or an assistant on a magnetic sheet attached to one of the large whiteboards. Since each vinyl sheet represented one action item, the sheets could be rearranged on the whiteboards to graphically illustrate the scope and structure of the major opportunities available. These action items were clustered into opportunities that the group felt could be capitalized upon. The project opportunities were prioritized using the Voting Support tool. The facilitator or an assistant added a new window to the Topic Commenter tool for each of the five to eight high-priority project opportunities.

## Stage 4: Detailed Action Plans

Stage four involved obtaining consensus on detailed action plans that would be implemented by participants. This stage started with a brief review of the scope and structure of the five to eight action opportunities developed in stage three and the choice by each participant of which initiative to support. Participants physically clustered at the location in the Decision Support Centre where the project of their choice was to be discussed. In some meetings all members voted with their feet not to support one or more of the project opportunities. The layout of the Decision Support Centre enabled six project teams each consisting of up to seven members to sit in a group, yet still have ready access to a large whiteboard and one or more microcomputers. Detailed project plans were hammered out by a process of face-to-face discussion supported by whiteboards and access to 35–50 pages of material on objectives, industry review, and project opportunities. After the structure of the plans emerged, group members used one or more adjacent terminals to enter them into the appropriate Topic Commenter window.

## Stage 5: Commitment to Implement Action Plans

The purpose of the final stage was to cement ownership of the project plans and encourage commitment to implementation. During this stage every group member was given the opportunity to see the plans of the other subgroups and add to or modify their own plans. Stage five ended with an oral presentation by each subgroup and the commitment of members to implement the plan they had developed.

The meeting ended after group participants had given written free-form feedback to the facilitator via the final Topic Commenter window. A one-page research questionnaire (see appendix B) was completed by group members to determine their perceptions of the meeting.

Each session produced a report that includes the objectives, industry review, action items, and the action plans that team members agreed to undertake. The facilitator's staff used a desktop publishing package to enhance the presentation of the electronic text produced by the GroupSystems software. The result was then printed and bound. Within two days of the meeting, each participant was in possession of a bound 50–80-page report of their Advantage Auckland meeting.
