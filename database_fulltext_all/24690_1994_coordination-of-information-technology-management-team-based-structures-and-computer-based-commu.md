# Coordination of Information Technology Management: Team-Based Structures and Computer-Based Communication Systems

GERARDINE DESANCTIS AND BRAD M. JACKSON

GERARDINE DESANCTIS received her Ph.D. from Texas Tech University in 1982. She is currently a Professor of Management Information Systems at the University of Minnesota. Her teaching interests are organizational computing, computer supported cooperative work, and management of information systems. Her research interests are collaborative work and decision support systems. She currently serves as Senior Editor for Theory and Research for the MIS Quarterly and as Senior Editor of organization communication for Organization Science.

BRAD M. JACKSON is currently Assistant to the General Manager of the Information Technology Department of Texaco Inc. Prior to this assignment, he created and managed the Group Decision and Collaboration Team at Texaco. He has held positions in marketing, telecommunications, technology research, and refining. He received a B.S. in computer science from the University of Arkansas and an M.S. in computer science from the University of Houston. He has published in such journals as MIS Quarterly and Journal of Organizational Computing. His research interests include architectural planning for information technology and organizational computing.

ABSTRACT: The coordination of information technology (IT) management presents a challenge to firms with dispersed IT practices. Decentralization may bring flexibility and fast response to changing business needs, as well as other benefits, but decentralization also makes systems integration difficult, presents a barrier to standardization, and acts as a disincentive toward achieving economies of scale. As a result, there is a need to balance the decentralization of IT management to business units with some centralized planning for technology, data, and human resources. Here we explore three major mechanisms for facilitating interunit coordination of IT management: structural design approaches, functional coordination modes, and computer-based communication systems. We define these various mechanisms and their interrelationships, and we discuss the relative costs and benefits associated with alternative coordination approaches. To illustrate the cost–benefit tradeoffs of coordination approaches, we present a case study in which computer-based communication systems were used to support team-based coordination of IT management across dispersed business units. Our analysis reveals possibilities for future approaches to IT coordination in large, dispersed organizations.

The authors gratefully acknowledge the helpful comments of Gary W. Dickson, V. Sambamurthy, and the editors of this Special Section.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: computer-based communications, information technology management, technology-based coordination, team-based organization.

MODERN ORGANIZATIONS CONFRONT A TURBULENT ENVIRONMENT. Economic pressures and uncertainty in markets require rapid, flexible response to changing conditions, and organizations are finding that they must develop mechanisms to support flexible response to change. Organizations are shifting from hierarchies to network arrangements in which horizontal coordination and communication take precedence over vertical lines of authority. For many organizations, information technology (IT) is regarded as an integral component of this shift in organization design. Information technology enables new architectural possibilities, or ways of arranging and linking organizational components. In fact, computer-based communication systems are proving vital to the support of the networked organization. Information technology is regarded as a critical enabler of novel ways of organizing, with the result that heavy burdens are being placed on the information technology subunit. No longer is the IT subunit responsible only for computer operations; rather, IT is providing the basic infrastructure to support new organizational designs.

There is little research on how IT subunits can deal with their growing role in organization design. In most organizations, IT is no longer centrally managed, but the process of managing IT within a networked form is not at all clear. We explore here how various coordination mechanisms might be used to support a networked approach to IT management.

## Coordinating IT Management

COORDINATION OF INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY MANAGEMENT across diverse areas of the enterprise presents a major challenge to organizations today. Downsizing of computer hardware, deployment of applications development to functional areas, and business demands to manage resources in creative, flexible ways are all trends that have contributed to the gradual decentralization of IT management and a corresponding shrinking of the central IT function (see [11]). Indeed, recent surveys indicate that more IT organizations are decentralized in their operations than centralized [35].

The tradeoffs between centralized and decentralized IT management are well known: decentralization provides local ownership of resources and greater responsiveness to business unit needs (see $[10]$ ); centralization facilitates standardization, integration, and economies of scale. Between these extremes are hybrid models of organizing in which some resources, such as telecommunications and large-scale computer operations, are managed as central utilities, while other resources, such as office computing and applications development, are managed locally. Such hybrid arrangements have been referred to as “centrally decentralized” IT organizations $[41]$ or the “information economy within a business” $[44]$ . Hybrid forms of organizing would seem to be a reasonable strategy for balancing the relative advantages of centralized and decentralized management. Indeed, simultaneous differentiation and integration of IT efforts has been shown to be effective in organizations that confront a high degree of complexity, change, and uncertainty [3].

Critical to the success of hybrid forms of organizing is a clear understanding on the part of both the central and various distributed IT units of their respective roles in IT planning and ongoing operations. In other words, simply distributing IT management responsibilities across corporate and business unit locations is not sufficient to guarantee successful use of IT resources. Coordinating mechanisms are needed to assure that the various efforts of the IT management units are synchronized, so that IT goals and operations remain aligned with those of the enterprise and so that efficiencies and learning can take place through sharing of knowledge or specific technologies. The greater the degree of differentiation, the more difficult coordination becomes and the more likely are business practices to diverge across units $[21]$ . How should coordination of dispersed IT practices take place? What mechanisms can be used to link planning and other activities that occur across business units? Since some mechanisms may be more effective than others in achieving integration $[25]$ , the range of possible approaches must be considered. For example, establishing reporting requirements among units or holding information-sharing sessions will likely achieve only low levels of integration. On the other hand, mutual IT planning procedures, leading to common objectives and standardized practices, may achieve high levels of integration.

We present here a typology of alternative coordination mechanisms and explore how team-based structures and computer-based communication systems can be used to enable coordination of IT planning across diverse business units. We begin by showing how team-based structures and computer-based communication systems fit into the range of possible mechanisms for coordinating IT planning. Next, we illustrate the use of team-based structures and communication systems in a case study of IT planning practices at Texaco Inc. The case study illustrates how integration of diverse IT business units, within a hybrid organization form, can be facilitated with computerized communication and team-based approaches. Finally, we draw some conclusions about ways in which IT planning may be coordinated in hybrid organizations, and we outline some directions for future research and practice.

## A Typology of IT Coordination Mechanisms

COORDINATION IS THE PROCESS OF LINKING TOGETHER DIFFERENT PARTS of an organization to accomplish tasks collectively [38]. We can think of coordination as taking place in one of two general directions, either vertically (up and down the management hierarchy, that is, within one unit) or horizontally (across the management hierarchy, that is, among units) [3]. In hybrid forms of IT management, horizontal coordination is the major concern: linking together the IT activities of otherwise independent or loosely coupled units, each of which has its own IT function. Two general ways of enabling horizontal coordination are through structural designs and process modes. Structural designs are ways of organizing the reporting relationships between IT units. For example, dispersed IT units may have direct reporting relationships with a central IT unit (which means they are accountable in some way to corporate IT), indirect relationships (also called dotted-line relationships, which means dispersed IT units exchange information with corporate IT but have no obligation to comply with IT directives), or voluntary relationships (which means the dispersed units participate on an ad hoc basis with IT planning efforts but otherwise pursue their own course of action). $^{1}$ Relationships among the IT functions in business units may be managed through reporting requirements, direct contact, liaison roles, or teams. A summary of structural design approaches for horizontal IT coordination is given in Table 1. $^{2}$

Cross-functional teams represent the most comprehensive structural approach to horizontal IT coordination because the team is permanent (in contrast to a task force), includes direct contact and reporting activities, and can take on liaison roles with the various business units. In other words, cross-functional teams can incorporate several horizontal coordination mechanisms. The cross-functional team provides a formal forum of interaction among organizational units, thus creating an ongoing conduit for horizontal communication. In contexts such as IT coordination, cross-functional teams emphasize knowledge sharing rather than skill specialization; the team is designed to facilitate learning among members who represent different organizational units and the areas of expertise associated with those units. Mintzberg [26] and others [10, 27, 28, 43] advocate the use of cross-functional teams as a horizontal coordination mechanism in modern organizations.

Of course, simply creating a cross-functional team or other structural design is not sufficient for coordination. The mere creation of an IT steering committee, for example, does not guarantee productive linking of IT units [16]. How the team members interact with one another, the frequency and content of their interactions, and the timing, location, and function of their communications can have significant impacts on the ultimate success of interunit coordination (see [8, 15]). Coordination modes are the dynamic processes that overlay structural designs and influence how they operate. Interpersonal communication, or the exchange of information among parties, is the essence of all coordination modes (see [3, 5]). Modes can be differentiated along a number of dimensions. For example, the frequency of communication (e.g., how often reports are exchanged or meetings are held) among the parties involved in the structural arrangement can range from occasional contact to regular contact. Timing of communication might be synchronous (parties communicate with one another at the same time) or asynchronous (communication is lagged, with parties sending and receiving information at different points in time). The place of communication may be the same for all parties (e.g., everyone meets in the same facility or room) or the parties may be dispersed across facilities, regions, or time zones. Finally, the function of the communication reflects the coordination mode of a structural design.

Table 2 illustrates the range of possible functional modes in horizontal coordination. The simplest functional mode is information passing (e.g., exchange of notes, reports, or data that are only indirectly relevant to the coordination task at hand). Information passing consists of sending and receiving messages, typically without extensive dialog or followup exchange between the coordinating parties. Examples of information passing within an IT context include exchange of status reports on local area network operations, sending copies of a magazine article on IT management to business unit personnel, or announcing an upcoming demonstration of a new computer product. Coordination increases in complexity when communication deals with matters more closely related to horizontal coordination efforts, such as arrangement of the structural design itself (e.g., agreeing on reporting relationships or the roles and responsibilities of parties involved in the coordination effort) and procedural operations of the structural design (e.g., determining when meetings will be held or what topics should be covered in a required report). Complexity increases further if the function of communication is task accomplishment (e.g., deciding on the content of an interunit PC purchase policy, creating the policy in written form, or overseeing its implementation). Here the amount of communication exchanged between parties increases relative to simpler modes (since parties must work together to gather information and other resources, resolve differences, and complete tasks), and ease of structuring (or routinizing) the communication goes down. Finally, coordination can take the form of issue analysis, that is, generating ideas and exploring issues in depth. This type of communication consists of a rich dialog among the parties and is directed more toward problem analysis (e.g., debating the pros and cons of policy alternatives) than solution development (which is deciding on the best policy). Coordination is most complex in this case, since the amount of communication is high and the ease of structuring is low.

These five functional coordination modes correspond to the five communication functions identified by Poole and Roth [33, 31] for group decision processes. $^{3}$ Note that the categories in Table 2 are not distinct; rather, they represent the range of communication efforts associated with interunit coordination, from simple to complex. Information passing requires relatively low effort on the part of communicators; message sending and receipt can be routinized. Structural and procedural coordination are more difficult to routinize; they require more extensive dialog on the part of the participating parties. Task accomplishment and issue analysis are the most complex communication processes; for these functions, the amount of communication tends to be high and the ease of structuring is low. Organizations may develop some ways of structuring task accomplishment, such as through meeting protocols for group decision making or through project management techniques, such as benchmarking (e.g., see [39]). Issue analysis is the least routinized coordination mode in most organizations; rarely are these dialogs carried out using routine protocols.

The coordination structures and modes just described illustrate the range of options available for design of horizontal IT linkages in organizations. Given the range of coordination options, how should organizations proceed? The selection of specific coordination structures and modes is, of course, dependent on various contingencies—the size of the organization, the degree of integration desired, the types of tasks requiring coordination, and so on. McCann and Ferry [25], Blanton et al. [3], and others (e.g., [10, 26] have considered how coordination options might be matched with organizational needs and environmental contingencies. Boynton and Zmud [7] and Sambamurthy, Venkataraman, and DeSanctis [34] have explored coordination of IT planning processes. But there has not been specific consideration, to date, for the role of computer-based communication systems in horizontal IT planning processes. For the remainder of this discussion we would like to build on prior works to focus on how team-based structures and computer-based communication systems might be used to enable coordination in planning among dispersed IT units. Team-based structures and computer-based communication systems are of particular interest due to increasing movement toward the divisionalized and adhocracy forms of management. $^{4}$ These fluid organizational forms are replacing traditional hierarchies and bureaucratic forms of managing. The result is that IT planning is increasingly dispersed across business units and functions, and more complex structural designs, such as cross-functional teams, are replacing simpler forms of coordination [34]. Further, with the new team-based coordination, there are corresponding pressures for rapid, responsive communication among team members who must work together across time and space boundaries [6, 20, 26]. How can computer-based systems provide support for a range of communication modes and structural design approaches, particularly cross-functional IT planning teams? The next step in our analysis explores this question.

## Enabling Horizontal Coordination of IT Planning

The potential value of using computer-based systems to support organizational structures and processes is widely acknowledged, especially where the goal is to yield competitive use of IT $[2]$ . Several theorists have argued for the use of computer-based systems in horizontal coordination $[9, 10, 18]$ ; and, on a practical level, there is the widespread practice of using computer-based technology to form common telecommunications infrastructure, networks, and data exchange across divisions or business units $[1, 33]$ . Nevertheless, the use of computer-based systems for interunit planning coordination has been not been systematically studied. Since knowledge exchange—as opposed to exchange of materials or skills—will become the primary basis for horizontal coordination in the future $[26, 45]$ , the application of computer-based communication systems is of keen interest, particularly for intellective (as opposed to production) tasks such as planning. Computer-based systems have the potential to reduce coordination costs $[9, 10]$ , enable more rapid, responsive communication across time and space barriers $[15]$ , and bring structure to otherwise unstructured dialog between coordinating parties $[13]$ .

Figure 1 summarizes the range of computer-based communication systems that may be of use in horizontal coordination efforts. The systems range from simple to complex, corresponding to the five major coordination structures and functional modes outlined earlier. At the simplest level, document-sharing systems can support information passing, a primary function in reporting requirements. Document-sharing systems facilitate the sending and receipt of memos, reports, newsletters, and the like, between individuals or departments. Primitive document systems support only text transmission while more sophisticated systems include support for graphics, images, voice, or video within the documents [4]. Electronic mail (two-way message exchange), bulletin board (broadcast messaging), and conferencing systems (multiparty messaging) can support the structural and procedural coordination needs of IT planning groups [32]. For example, announcements of new liaison officers and their roles and responsibilities can be posted on a bulletin board; meeting agendas can be developed via conferencing systems; and discussions about the procedures for completing specific tasks can take place via electronic mail or conferencing systems. Electronic meeting systems, particularly those designed for group decision support, can provide protocols for idea generation, idea evaluation, consensus formation, choice making, and document preparation [12, 13]. Tyran et al. [37] have demonstrated the potential benefits of electronic meeting systems for supporting planning tasks in large, diverse organizational groups. Finally, discussion databases can support issue analysis and the rich dialog associated with debating the pros and cons of alternative views of a problem or its solutions. Discussion databases represent the most comprehensive support for horizontal coordination because they include document-handling capabilities, electronic mail, computer conferencing, and group decision support capabilities. Lotus Notes from Lotus Development Corporation, VAX Notes from Digital Equipment Corporation, and GrapeVine from Office Express, Inc., are example discussion database products. These systems support issue analysis in team interaction that occurs across time and space barriers. Dialogs are structured using a hierarchical database protocol in which topics, subtopics, agreements, and disagreements are catalogued and can be retrieved and shared along a wide range of possible keys (such as topic, subtopic, date, author, pro, con, etc.). Further, the systems are typically integrated or linked to electronic mail, document handling, and related communication systems (such as agenda managers and bulletin boards).

## The Relative Effectiveness of Coordination Approaches

The three-dimensional model of horizontal coordination management shown in figure 1 illustrates the range of design and functional options to horizontal coordination, as well as possibilities for computer-based support of these approaches. The options range, on each axis, from simple to complex, meaning that more resources are required to use the approaches further out on each axis but more potential benefits may accrue from these richer approaches as well. Structural design approaches become more complex as more people are involved in the designs (moving from individuals to team approaches), and so more coordination effort is required. On the other hand, benefits may be higher because the more complex designs include more interpersonal contact and joint problem solving and analysis. Functional modes become more complex as the amount of communication goes up and the ease of structuring goes down. This complexity means that issue analysis requires more effort than, say, information passing or procedural communication. On the other hand, more productive results may occur from higher-level functional modes (as tasks are completed and issues are analyzed). Computer-based communication systems become complex as more sophisticated features and protocols for communication are included in the systems and the systems contain more integration or linkages with other communication systems. The more complex approaches tend to include components of the simpler approaches on each axis. A key assumption of our model is that both horizontal coordination efforts and benefits should increase with the use of more complex coordination structures, modes, and computer-based support systems.

Since horizontal coordination approaches bring about both efforts (costs) and benefits, a critical organizational goal is to maximize coordination benefits relative to costs [23]. In decentralized or hybrid forms of IT management, participation in horizontal coordination efforts is usually voluntary. $^{5}$ So, when organizations select specific coordination approaches, it is important that the approaches be perceived as convenient, easy to access, and providing some benefit to the parties involved (see [19]). Computer-based communication systems have been proposed as mechanisms for reducing coordination costs and increasing benefits [9, 10, 22, 45]. For example, document sharing and electronic mail can improve efficiencies of report and memo distribution, assuring that materials reach the correct individuals rapidly, while at the same time allowing easy storage and retrieval of the materials.

Surveys of users of computer-based communication systems report the potential benefits of these systems for work coordination [17, 26, 37]; but all systems may not be equally appropriate for all coordination contexts. Electronic mail, for example, may provide effective support in direct contact situations and when the functional mode is procedural. On the other hand, for support of decision making and issue analysis in team settings, electronic mail—while helpful—may not provide the sophistication of support required in these coordination contexts; rather, electronic meeting software or discussion databases should be of greater use because they include features to support decision making, policy formulation, and issue analysis. In a similar way, we would not expect systems on the complex end of the computer-based continuum (of figure 1) to bring about greater benefits relative to costs if the functional modes for coordination are largely procedural or structural. It is when the design structure is complex (involving groups of people—teams) and the functional mode is demanding on the participants (task accomplishment or issue analysis) that electronic meeting software and discussion databases will be perceived as most useful. To summarize, we are proposing that complex computer-based communication systems will be perceived as useful when relatively complex coordination structures and modes are being applied. Likewise, simpler computer-based systems will be perceived as useful when relatively simple coordination structures and modes are being applied.

Of course, many theorists and empirical researchers have shown that mere availability of computer-based communication systems does not guarantee their successful use (e.g., [15, 19, 30]). How the systems are used is as important as whether the systems are available and the kinds of capabilities they contain. For example, a discussion database used only for electronic mail cannot be expected to bring about more efficient or effective issue analysis. Similarly, an electronic meeting system used only for information sharing will not necessarily enable successful task completion. As computer systems become more complex, “full use” involves a greater range of possible actions on the part of users. So, to reap the benefits of electronic meeting software and discussion databases, users must actively apply the meeting and database capabilities of the technology; if only the lower-level capabilities of the software are used, then the benefits of the systems are unlikely to outweigh their costs. In other words, we are proposing that to maximize benefit from a computer-based communication system, the system should be used at its highest level of complexity.

## Conclusion

Figure 1 and the preceding discussion offer some general theoretical perspective on the use of horizontal coordination mechanisms and the relationship among structural design, functional coordination modes, and computer-based communication systems. To illustrate these concepts, we next present a case study of one organization's attempts to integrate a decentralized IT planning process using several of the coordination mechanisms shown in figure 1. Following Yin [42], our case is descriptive: it illustrates a real-life context in which various computer-based communication systems were applied to support task forces and cross-functional teams. Our analysis follows a linear-analytic structure [42], first describing the IT planning context and the problem being studied, followed by our research methods, findings, and conclusions.

A Case Study: IT Planning at Texaco Inc

The IT Planning Context

STRATEGIC PLANNING FOR IT AT TEXACO INC. is similar in philosophy, goals, and content areas to IT planning in other large, multinational corporations (see [3, 14, 24]). Planning involves the development of a vision or architecture for acquisition, use, and overall management of information technology in the company. Plans are based on analysis of current and future computing, telecommunications, and human resource environments, and include action steps for achieving desired objectives within specified time periods. Major areas of IT planning include data management, telecommunications architecture, software development and services, office and end-user computing, and human resources. Strategic planning includes development of general policies for various planning areas as well as specification of new directions and migration paths for change.

Texaco has taken an organizational approach to IT planning $[14]$ , which means that planning involves continuous integration between the IT function and the organization at large. The emphasis is on dynamic processes rather than strictly business-directed priority setting or application of a specific planning technique. The organizational approach has been found to be a more effective approach to IT planning than other approaches (such as business-driven, method-driven, administrative, or technological) $[14]$ . The organizational approach relies on coordination mechanisms in which the central IT function works in partnership with the rest of the organization. Planning is not highly formalized. Rather, IT strategies emerge from ongoing dialogs, special initiatives, and experimentation. Planning is viewed as a process of collective learning across the organization.

A critical issue at Texaco, as well as other companies adopting an organizational approach, is determining exactly how to coordinate planning when responsibility for IT management is widely dispersed across the organization. Figure 2 shows the overall management structure of Texaco and the large collection of IT units spread across divisions, departments, and subdivisions. In all, there are 16 distributed IT units across the corporation, employing approximately 1,400 employees. Budgeting for these units is done at the business unit level, so there is no global IT budget for the company. In addition to the 16 IT units, there is a corporate-level information technology department that employs approximately 850 people and has a budget of \$150 million. The purpose of our analysis was to describe the evolution of approaches used by the corporate information technology department to coordinate IT planning across worldwide Texaco during the period 1989–93. We particularly focused on the company's use of team-based structures and computer-based communication systems.

## Research Methods

Our case analysis was based on the following sources of data: corporate documents, such as organizational charts, memos, and written plans; observation of IT conferences, meetings, and other forums in which IT planning matters were discussed; unstructured interviews with IT business unit representatives currently serving on interunit planning teams; and observation of electronic discussion databases devoted to IT planning. Our approach was not quantitative; rather, we used a variety of sources to build a qualitative understanding of how IT coordination mechanisms evolved at Texaco. We included both corporate IT and business unit IT perspectives in our analyses.

## Texaco: Some Background

Texaco is among the ten largest publicly held corporations in the world and the third largest oil company in the United States. Texaco was founded in 1902 and today consists of five major business units: Texaco USA, Texaco Europe, Texaco Latin America/West Africa, Texaco Middle East/Far East, and Texaco Chemical Company. Corporate-level service organizations, including the information technology department, provide service support to the business units. Worldwide, the company employs over 32,000 people. Revenues for 1992 were \$37.7 billion. Texaco's operations are global, spanning six continents.

Like other major corporations, Texaco is facing an increasingly competitive and volatile market environment. Accelerating technological innovation in the petroleum industry, worldwide slow economic growth, and flattening oil prices create continual pressures to update equipment and systems and to use technology for competitive advantage. In recent years Texaco has undergone a transition from authoritative, hierarchical management styles to more participative, decentralized ways of managing. This transition has been facilitated by the influx of new, aggressive people in top-level corporate positions and a widespread commitment to improving quality throughout the company. Major quality initiatives have been undertaken, with each business unit and department implementing its own quality program. The evolution of IT coordination described below should be viewed in light of these broader corporate changes, particularly general management pressures toward participation and decentralization.

## Evolution of IT Coordination Approaches

Texaco was an early user of computer technology, installing IBM mainframes starting in 1957. In fact, an IBM 705 located in Houston in 1957 led to the following news headline: "Texaco installs electronic brain equivalent to 25,000 trained mathematicians!" By 1966 the company established a corporate-level computer services department, and by the 1980s, most large-scale systems development and computing were done in this centralized computer department. End-user computing came early to Texaco and developed along with the corporate computer department. Minicomputers and mainframes were installed in departments for processing oil exploration and producing data and for applications in marketing, refining, transportation, and research and development. But most systems development was done at the corporate level. By the late 1980s there was a push to move application systems development activities out into the user departments because, at that time, the corporation as a whole was gradually moving decision making and control away from the corporate level and into the operating units. Corporate management was concentrating on strategic financial concerns (such as avoiding takeover and bankruptcy), and decentralization of management authority was considered vital to increasing Texaco's competitiveness.

By 1988 most Texaco business units had established their own IT functions, with systems development being the primary activity of these units. Previous end-user computing activities were not always folded into these units, so sometimes older IT groups operated side-by-side with the new IT functions. Personnel for the newly formed IT functions came from the central (now called) information technology department, and they were given the explicit charge to provide systems development and support for the business units they were now serving. To facilitate focus on business needs, rather than IT per se, the new business unit IT functions were given no reporting relationship at all to the central information technology department. Rather, each reported directly to the particular business unit it served. In this way, Texaco moved from a relatively centralized IT management function to a highly dispersed and decentralized federation of IT functions. Throughout this transition the corporate information technology department remained strong; indeed, its overall responsibilities grew as demand for mainframe computing steadily increased (as business expanded), the telecommunications infrastructure grew in complexity, and interests in sophisticated end-user tools, local area networks, and advanced technologies placed the department in a technology service role for the company as a whole. Today the major responsibilities of the corporate information technology department include: the corporate data repository, telecommunications services, technology research, and a human resources function for all information technology professionals throughout the company. IT-related products and services are marketed by the department and managed by contractual arrangements to all of Texaco's business units.

The move from a centralized to a hybrid form of IT management (with both centralized and decentralized IT functions) at Texaco quickly led to a corresponding need for mechanisms to facilitate horizontal coordination among the various IT functions. The coordination problem was complex because not only did the five major business units have unitwide IT departments, but smaller IT groups also were spread throughout the various divisions of each business unit (as an illustration, see Texaco USA in figure 2). Approximately fifteen to twenty IT functions existed across the company during the period 1988–92 (and still do as of this writing). With no required reporting relationships among them, the corporate information technology department instituted an annual conference to promote information exchange among IT professionals across the company. The structural design was one of direct contact, and participation in the annual event was voluntary. The conference was a success, attracting nearly 1,000 participants since its inception in 1989. Although helpful for promoting dialog about IT developments throughout the company, the conference ultimately proved inadequate as a coordination mechanism because the need for more frequent contact and more sophisticated coordination modes began to grow (see Table 3).

## Team-Based Structures

## Problems Resulting from Insufficient Coordination

Driving the need for more complex structural designs to facilitate interunit coordination were a number of problems that arose during the 1988–91 period. Since each IT unit essentially did its own planning and operations management, independent of all other IT units, problems of inconsistent architectures, incompatible systems, and lack of alignment with corporate planning rapidly arose. For example, business units selected different, incompatible desktop application packages (word processing, graphics, spreadsheets, electronic mail) and local area network standards. Sharing of files across units became extremely cumbersome and, more importantly, development of an enterprisewide network was impossible due to the wide variety of standards and support functions operating across the organization. With no incentive to view IT as a companywide resource, accounting and tracking of technologies became infeasible, and each IT unit developed a “stovepipe” mentality—managing technology to suit the business unit or division but not the larger organization. The original goal of decentralization was to bring applications development close to users, but over time the dispersed IT units grew to do much more than applications development. They became responsible for planning their own architectures, selecting hardware and software platforms, hiring and firing personnel, and so on. Concern for technology interdependencies was largely ignored, with the result that local area networks, desktop applications, databases, and electronic mail and communication systems, were incompatible across the company. As one user put it:

My work involves communication with engineers in a variety of Texaco departments. But because IT is so decentralized, we're all on different electronic mail systems. If I want to send the same memo to three people, I may have to send it as many as three times using three different hardware and software platforms because we all use different systems!

## Liaison Forum

In order to encourage greater frequency of interaction among IT units and coordination modes other than purely information passing, the information technology department established an IT “round table” in 1991. The structural design consisted of designated representatives of major IT units who met together on a quarterly basis to share problems and ideas. The representatives were the directors of IT in each of nine major business units and divisions. The hope was that through regular conversation, some learning and common vision for IT at Texaco might emerge. This group of nine people met quarterly for approximately 18 months. Although some progress was made within this forum, horizontal coordination remained inadequate because the primary functional mode of the roundtable remained one of information sharing, without sufficient structure or purpose to conduct mutual planning. Much time was spent on structural and procedural matters, such as trying to define the purpose of the roundtable forum and its operations and objectives. Liaison forums are useful in the short term, as people with weak knowledge of cross-organizational activities achieve some learning through information sharing. But over time the liaison structure is a weak coordination mechanism because it lacks the necessary “glue” to keep the parties involved working together. For this reason, the roundtable disintegrated; the cost of meeting began to outweigh the benefits, and the information technology department sought a more sophisticated coordination structure.

## Cross-Functional Teams

In 1992 a cross-functional team was formed consisting of 16 people from the business unit IT functions, division-level IT groups, and the information technology department. This team, called the information planning group (IPG), was formed with the general goal of achieving synergy among the many IT groups throughout Texaco. Unlike the roundtable forum, the IPG was created as a cross-functional, ongoing team with a task-oriented charge: IT planning for Texaco. A critical design decision in the creation of the IPG team was to use it as a mechanism to spawn other cross-functional teams. First, the IPG created an asset management task force, which was a temporary committee charged with conducting an inventory and valuation of computer and telecommunications hardware and software throughout Texaco. Because IT resources were managed decentrally, there was no common understanding of the value of investment in technology at Texaco. The findings of this task force provided a foundation for the IPG's initial planning efforts. Major areas in need of cross-functional coordination were identified:

\- Application development processes and procedures

• Data processes and procedures

\- IT resource allocation (budgeting)

\- IT asset management

• Human resource management.

Two major goals of IT planning were identified:

\- Implement standards in three key areas (information architecture, data processes and procedures, and applications development)

\- Align IT resources to reflect corporate priorities.

Next, a series of cross-functional subteams were created to address specific planning areas:

\- Architecture (networks, workstations, local area networks, servers, etc.)

\- Systems development (end-user and application development methods and tools)

• Data management (information resources management)

\- Electronic mail (integration and standardization of mail systems throughout the company)

• Executive information systems (development of a common EIS)

• Human resource management of IT professionals.

Like the IPG, the subteams were given a general (planning) task, but each team was responsible for defining its own objectives, strategies, and operating approach. Like the IPG, the subteams were ongoing and worked together on a regular basis. IPG members were business unit and division directors of IT; subteam members included one IPG member, with the rest being a collection of IT professionals from across Texaco. Like the task force, the subteams reported upward, to the IPG, but, as before, there were no formal reporting relationships across IT units; participation in the IPG and subteams was voluntary. Figure 3 summarizes the cross-functional planning team structure.

As of this writing, the IPG and subteams have been in existence for approximately 18 months. This interlocking team approach appears to be more successful than the earlier conference and liaison forums because it is a more comprehensive approach to horizontal coordination. Participation in the teams has remained strong, enhanced (as we shall see in the next section) by the use of computer-based communication technologies. The IPG and its subteams are permanent. Their functional modes include direct contact and reporting activities (in the form of meetings, memos, and reports). They take on liaison roles with the various business units; but they are also goal-oriented, directing their energies toward common tasks.

## Summary

Texaco moved from relatively simple to more complex design structures in order to achieve more effective horizontal coordination. With each addition in complexity, coordination efforts increased: more meetings were held, more people were involved, the number of activities to be done increased, and so on. But the progression also brought increased benefits, in the form of greater mutual understanding of IT issues and priorities and, ultimately, the basis for corporatewide IT planning. Further, the structural designs themselves were insufficient for the coordination benefits to be realized. Coupled with the formation of the technology conference, roundtable, and information planning teams, was a parallel progression in coordination modes. The function of each coordination attempt moved from pure information passing, to structural and procedural communication, and ultimately to task accomplishment and issue analysis. Overall, the evolution of coordination approaches appears consistent with our theoretical view that horizontal coordination efforts and benefits increase with the use of more complex coordination structures, modes, and computer-based support systems.

## Computer-Based Support for Coordination Activities

## Computer-Based Communication Systems

Texaco was an early user of computer-based communication systems. In the late 1970s, electronic mail, bulletin board, and conferencing systems were introduced on a number of mainframes and minicomputers scattered about the company. For example, Digital Equipment Corporation's Vax Notes product was used in several research and development groups within the business units to support computer conferencing. In 1983, Data General's electronic mail software, CEO, was added to many of the Data General minicomputers located in the refineries. Starting in 1986, token ring local area networks were rolled out from the information technology department to the business units—the same year that a beta version of Lotus Corporation's discussion database, Lotus Notes, was installed. IBM's electronic mail and bulletin board system, PROFS, was installed for corporatewide E-mail in 1987. Experimentation with electronic meeting systems began in 1989, with installation of electronic meeting software in a number of facilities, and has grown to include a modern, multimedia decision room that is used for both face-to-face and videoconferenced meetings for business units throughout Texaco.

The most sophisticated coordination mechanisms are electronic meeting and discussion database systems. Four electronic meeting products have been used since 1989, and Ventana Corporation's GroupSystems is a popular product within Texaco. GroupSystems supports electronic brainstorming, issue prioritization, idea organizing, topic commenting, and strategic planning within a face-to-face or videoconferenced environment. Teamkit/VM is available on one of Texaco's large IBM mainframes; it supports only two electronic meeting functions (idea generation and voting), but it can be accessed from any location within Texaco and can accommodate very large numbers of users. With regard to discussion databases, Lotus Notes is the most widely used product. Notes is an information manager implemented using the client/server computing model and interconnects DOS, Windows, OS/2, Unix, and Macintosh workstations on local and wide area networks. Through Notes, groups can collaborate on topics and organize them in group discussion databases. Whereas electronic meeting systems aim to move the group toward decision choice or consensus, usually within one or two meeting sessions, the discussion database is a forum for issue analysis over a long time period (such as several months or perhaps a year). Typical Notes applications at Texaco include: team activities databases, technical discussion databases, and planning, marketing, and quality discussion forums. In addition to discussion databases, Notes includes the lower-level communication capabilities given in figure 1, such as electronic mail, computer conferencing, and document sharing. As of this writing, there are approximately 5,500 Notes users at Texaco interconnected over local and wide area networks spanning the United States, Europe, South America, and Indonesia, with 44 Notes servers in 20 office locations, and nearly 1,000 available discussion databases.

## IT Support for Horizontal Coordination

Table 3 shows the progressive use of computer-based communication systems for the IT planning activities documented in our study. Very little IT support was required for the annual IT conference. Electronic mail and bulletin boards were used to announce conference activities, invite calls for participation, and plan the specifics of conference events. Similar technologies were used to support the information passing and related functions of the IT roundtable; electronic mail and document sharing were used to schedule meetings and plan agendas for face-to-face meetings. Electronic meeting systems were not used in roundtable discussions because there was no function other than information sharing in meetings, and this was accomplished via formal presentations and informal, unstructured conversation.

In one attempt to broaden the scope of the IT roundtable, a “virtual town meeting” was conducted during 1992 using the Teamkit/VM electronic meeting software. The event was technology-driven, in that the availability of the conferencing software was the primary driver for holding the town meeting; and it was held as a one-time event in which any IT professional could contribute ideas or comments for improving IT management at Texaco. In all, over 1,200 comments were collected from three U.S. locations and six international locations. The comments were not explicitly used as inputs to decision making but served an information passing function for the IT round table.

## IT Support for Cross-Functional Teams

With the formation of the IPG and subteams came opportunities for application of more sophisticated communication systems. Approximately 50 percent of team members were unfamiliar with electronic meeting software prior to their participation on these teams, and 30 percent were unfamiliar with discussion databases. Yet, these technologies proved to be integral to the operations of the cross-functional teams. The information technology department provided encouragement and support for the teams to become regular users of electronic meeting (GroupSystems) and discussion database (Lotus Notes) software, and nearly all of the teams adopted these technologies early in their life. Our study of the first year of IPG and subteam operations showed that most teams met face-to-face on a monthly basis, and electronic meeting software was used in these meetings for activities such as clarifying team goals and objectives, developing priorities and action plans, and assigning work tasks to specific team members.

Discussion databases also were used in monthly meetings, to document meeting activities, but their biggest use was in supporting communication outside of face-to-face meetings. Intermeeting support was critical to issue analysis and task accomplishment on these teams because members were widely dispersed in time and space. For example, the 16 members of IPG were located in seven different cities and four different time zones. To organize dispersed teamwork, the IPG established three discussion databases: (1) an IPG briefing book, describing the purpose, structure, and operations of the IPG; (2) an IPG inventory/planning database, reporting the results of the asset management task force and the implications of the task force findings; and (3) the IPG team activities database, which documented progress on team activities but, more importantly, served as a forum for IPG members and observers to read and comment on IPG progress. Each IPG subteam established similar discussion databases to support their work. Although discussion databases could be designated as public or private, most teams (including the IPG) made their activities public; in this way, the database not only facilitated interactions among team members but also enabled people outside of the team to view team discussions and engage in issue analysis along with team members.

## Summary

Simpler computer-based communication systems provided satisfactory support when the coordination structures were relatively simple and the functional mode was primarily information passing. Over time, the progression of IT support mechanisms at Texaco tended to increase in complexity, just as the structural designs and functional modes became more sophisticated. This progression was not without costs: The move to electronic meeting and discussion database systems required some effort for the cross-functional teams, since many members had not been regular users of these technologies (though others in their business units were). Individuals had to learn how to operate the systems, and groups had to identify activities for which the systems might be useful. The increase in effort brought corresponding benefits: Greater progress was made in horizontal IT coordination following the formation of the IPG; goals and objectives were identified, priorities were established, and matters of corporate alignment and moving toward standards in key areas began to be addressed. In sum, Texaco's experiences were consistent with the theoretical view presented earlier in this article. Horizontal coordination efforts and benefits increased with the use of more complex coordination structures, modes, and computer-based support systems; and the more complex computer-based communication systems were perceived as useful once relatively complex coordination structures and modes were applied. Use of the discussion database did not consistently reach a high level of complexity. Members perceived benefit from the database (as evidenced in high use), and it became an integral part of the team's work. On the other hand, information sharing dominated the database communication that we sampled, and some IPG members complained about insufficient use of the “full potential” of the database in their team. We would expect team members to perceive an increase in benefits from the discussion database if they apply it in the future to a greater proportion of issue analysis functions.

## Conclusion

OUR CASE STUDY PROVIDED A DESCRIPTION OF ONE ORGANIZATION'S experiences with applying the horizontal coordination mechanisms shown in figure 1, and it illustrated some of the relationships among use of these mechanisms. The case illustrates several principles. First, horizontal coordination efforts and benefits increase with the use of more complex coordination structures, modes, and computer-based support systems. Second, complex computer-based communication systems are perceived as useful when relatively complex coordination structures and modes are being applied; simpler computer-based systems are perceived as useful when relatively simple coordination structures and modes are being applied. Third, benefits from a computer-based communication system increase as the system is used at higher levels of complexity. Neither our conceptual model nor these principles are proved or disproved by the Texaco case; rather, they are illustrated in a descriptive sense. Further, we must emphasize that our model of coordination mechanisms is not meant to be exhaustive, and our discussion has not fully explored the relationships among the three types of horizontal coordination mechanisms. Clearly, additional theoretical development and empirical study are needed before the structures and processes of horizontal IT coordination—as well as the role of computer-based communication systems—can be fully understood.

Our contribution has been to explain how advanced information technologies, such as electronic meeting systems and discussion databases, can be enablers of coordination when used in conjunction with cross-functional teams and complex functional coordination modes. We believe that conceptualizing the coordination problem along the three dimensions of design structures, functional modes, and IT support mechanisms provides a basis on which to create and evaluate alternative coordination arrangements in organizations. Future research might consider how organizations choose among possible coordination mechanisms, the contingencies under which specific coordination mechanisms are effective or ineffective, and the implementation of structural designs, IT support mechanisms, and coordination modes so as to maximize benefits while minimizing coordination costs. As Victor and Blackburn [40] point out, management of interunit coordination should rest on a strong conceptualization of alternative approaches to coordination. This article has proposed an initial conceptualization for IT coordination on which future research and organizational practice can build.
