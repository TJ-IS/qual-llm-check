# A Strategic Decision Support System at Orell Fussli

SALVATORE BELARDO, PETER DUCHESSI, AND JOHN R. COLEMAN

SALVATORE BELARDO received the B.S.M.E. degree from the Rochester Institute of Technology, the M.B.A. degree from the State University of New York, and M.S. and Ph.D. degrees from rensselaer Polytechnic Institute. He has worked for the Eastman Kodak Company, General Electric, and the Watervliet Arsenal. He presently teaches and does research at the State University of New York at Albany. His current research interests include strategic uses of information resources, multimedia approaches to learning and decision making, and creativity. Dr. Belardo has published in a number of journals, including Management Science, Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Sciences, Interfaces, and IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man and Cybernetics. He is a member of TIMS.

PETER DUCHESSI is Associate Professor of Management Science and Information Systems and Chairman of the Management Science and Information Systems Department, School of Business, State University of New York at Albany. His areas of expertise include internal and external business analyses, development and implementation of advanced decision support technologies such as decision support systems and expert systems, and total quality management. He has published in several journals, including Management Science, Interfaces, and the California Management Review.

JOHN R. COLEMAN was educated in London, England, where he received the Diploma of Technology in printing and design. Mr. Coleman has over twenty-five years international experience in security printing. He holds the Swiss Federal Diploma of Commerce and has completed studies for the M.B.A. in Switzerland as well. Mr. Coleman is Managing Director of Orell Fussli Graphics Arts Lts. and Banknote Printing, Zurich.

ABSTRACT: Strategic decision support systems (strategic DSS) are one means of helping managers understand strategy and its implications. Strategic DSS contain many general decision support system (DSS) features, and are intended for senior managers. This article discusses the ongoing development of a strategic DSS for the Banknotes and Securities Division of Orell Fussli, a Swiss printing company. The system incorporates conceptual models, and provides explanations, cases, and guidance on how to use the models effectively. It helps train managers in strategic concepts and facilitates strategic thinking, and has been used to help develop and validate product/market strategies. We also provide several practical guidelines, including use

STRATEGIC DECISION SUPPORT SYSTEMS (strategic DSS) support executives in their strategic management activities [17, 18, 45]. The systems are intended for top management, yet they are not to be confused with executive information systems (EIS), which primarily perform information access, monitoring, and control functions for executives. A strategic DSS is a specific instance of a DSS and provides comparable functionality, such as business modeling and sensitivity or “what-if” analyses that are absent from EIS.

Although there are a number of implemented and useful DSS, progress toward strategic DSS has been slow. Among the reasons for low deployment of these types of systems are the complexity of strategy and the low credibility of computer-based approaches. With regard to the second item, some executives feel that information systems have little to offer them and have been reluctant to accept the systems in their offices $[21]$ . However, the growing utilization of EIS may dispel this notion and open the door to the development and implementation of strategic DSS. In performing strategic decision-making tasks, senior managers can benefit from strategic DSS that present relevant information and models to help them understand threats, opportunities, internal capabilities, and suitable strategies. These systems can also present the interdependencies among the many internal and external factors that require consideration during strategy development, including manufacturing capability, price competitiveness, and product technology.

We discuss strategic DSS and describe one system that was developed for Orell Fussli, a Swiss printing company. We begin with a discussion of strategic DSS and distinguish them from other types of systems, namely, expert systems (ES) and EIS. After background information on Orell Fussli, we present the current system. Then we present a set of guidelines and principles for designers of strategic DSS; these guidelines were distilled from our experiences in building the system. We finish with a conclusion that presents the implications of our work.

## Strategic DSS

STRATEGY IS A LOOSE AND IMPRECISE TERM. While most can agree on the origin of the term (from the Greek word “strategos,” meaning “general”), there are numerous definitions that reflect different points of view [5, 12, 35]. As a result, we often see different terminology, such as strategic planning [11, 43], strategic management [33, 39] and strategy formulation [3, 35], used interchangeably. Weihrich [43] notes that some authors focus on a definition that describes the ends of strategy (i.e., where does the organization want to go?), while others focus on the means (i.e., how will the organization get there?). In an effort to combine both means and ends, Hofer and Schendel [14] developed a hierarchy of strategies that distinguishes three levels of strategy:

\- Corporate strategy—defines the set of businesses an organization should be in and the extent of resource deployment appropriate for each business;

\- Business strategy—focuses on how to compete in a particular industry or product/market segment;

\- Functional area strategy—focuses on the efficient and effective utilization of resources.

Strategy has also been viewed as consisting of three processes: strategic planning, strategic thinking, and opportunistic decision making $[42]$ . Strategic planning is a comprehensive analysis used to develop a plan of action for the company. Strategic thinking results in creative ways for a company to develop, and opportunistic decision making is anticipating problems and opportunities that will confront the company.

DSS are evolving, and strategic DSS represent one aspect of that evolution. Thus, while strategic DSS inherit several DSS features (e.g., database [19, 36]), they employ additional features (e.g., conceptual models) that are specifically intended for strategic management activities. Table 1, which is based upon the work of Turban [40], lists attributes of ES, EIS, DSS, and strategic DSS for several technical, problem, and user dimensions (e.g., frequency of use). Variations between Turban's conception of a DSS and the one suggested by our DSS appear in bold under the strategic DSS column.

With regard to the technical dimensions, strategic DSS provide more explanatory information than DSS and EIS because they use cases, examples, and analogies to explain concepts, facilitate use of techniques, and stimulate thinking. ES also provide explanatory information, but through a trace of their reasoning process. DSS use quantitative, operations research (OR) models, or statistical models. Strategic DSS may combine conceptual models (e.g., value chain model) with the OR models. ES use rules, facts, and an inferencing mechanism; and EIS employ summary, drill-down and time series capabilities.

Concerning the problem dimension, relative to DSS and ES, strategic DSS rely on both internal and external data for analysis of internal capabilities, identification of threats, and determination of competitive posture. The external data are often limited, especially competitors' data on cost structures, marketing, and new product development. EIS may incorporate external data (e.g., Dow Jones financial figures), but they rely mostly on internal financial and operational data. Strategic DSS problems occur less frequently, have a smaller set of standard procedures, and have longer time horizons and a more significant impact on the business than those problems addressed by DSS, ES, and EIS. For example, compare vehicle routing with product migration strategy formulation. A DSS for vehicle routing incorporates one or more OR models for formulating routes, makes use of internal data (e.g., distances, waiting times, and vehicle weights), and may have its routes implemented on a daily basis. Finally, with respect to user dimensions, the impetus for strategic DSS is the improvement of strategic management effectiveness rather than the improvement of tactical and operational effectiveness, which is the stimulant for DSS, ES, and EIS. Strategic DSS are designed to support executives in their strategic management activities, rather than staff, middle management, and technicians, the users of DSS and ES. They are also used less frequently than DSS, ES, and EIS.

Strategic DSS are just emerging and research describes either theoretical underpinnings for them or specific applications. Theoretically based articles describe modern approaches or frameworks to strategic management that include analysis of threats, opportunities, strengths, and weaknesses; description of strategic situations; generation, evaluation, and selection of alternative strategies; and monitoring of performance. Excellent examples of the modern approach appear in Yoo and Digman [45] and Ward et al. [42]. Other research focuses on human decision-making phases of intelligence, design and choice, and factors that affect strategic decision making, including problem homogeneity, problem knowledge, and problem duration [24, 32].

Examination of the applied work indicates that strategic DSS applications have evolved in two directions: application-specific systems [35] and generic packages [7, 29, 41]. These studies demonstrate that application-specific systems are tailored to a specific organization, product/market, or industry setting. The systems present their models according to the organization's strategic management framework. To remain flexible, the generic packages do not integrate the models with a strategic management framework or offer advice on when and how to use them. The quantitative models include econometric models [18], decision tables and trees [6], and multicriteria decision-making models [16]. Conceptual models are just beginning to emerge as important components. The literature is replete with examples of how conceptual models have helped facilitate strategic thinking [9, 27, 28, 44]. The success stories are partially responsible for the models' popularity as strategic DSS modeling techniques. Some examples of conceptual models are: product life cycle model [20], multifactor portfolio matrix [14], product portfolio matrix [13], value chain model [28], Porter's five forces model [27], threats, opportunities, weaknesses and strengths (TOWS) matrix [43], and strategic position and action evaluation (SPACE) [31]. These models show the interrelationship among critical variables and suggest pertinent strategies. For example, SPACE evaluates a company on four dimensions (financial strength, competitive advantage, industry strength, and environmental stability) and suggests strategies based upon a company's assessment of those dimensions.

We take the view (held by others) that managers' acceptance of quantitative and conceptual models depends to a large extent on their understanding of the models' underlying principles and the quality of their solutions [1, 23, 24, 38]. A simple example serves to illustrate the first point. As part of an awareness building exercise, we asked several Orell Fussli managers to place a particular product on the multifactor portfolio matrix. The matrix, which juxtaposes market attractiveness (market size, growth rate) with relative competitive position (e.g., market share, sales/distribution effectiveness), helps managers assess a company's present position and identifies strategies necessary to maintain the position or move to a more desirable position. The managers, who were all senior-level managers, failed to place the product in exactly the same place on the matrix (see figure 1). Subsequent discussion among the managers revealed notable differences among the criteria they used to evaluate each dimension. Andersen and Rohrbaugh [2] suggest that differences such as these may be the result of individual differences caused by different experiences, perceptions, and preferred outcomes. Ackoff [1] and Little [23] suggest that users need to understand a system's models and must be trained in their use. Little [23] went on to say that online models that are user-instructing contribute to learning by introducing the user to problem- and model-related issues. Thus, strategic DSS require explanations of models, criteria, and examples on how to use them, preferably in a familiar context that can be provided by company-specific cases. Additionally, explanations can facilitate communication among managers, helping to resolve differences.

## Orell Fussli

ORELL FUSSLI IS A FIVE-HUNDRED-YEAR-OLD SWISS COMPANY that began printing books in Zurich approximately fifty years after Gutenberg invented the movable type printing press. Orell Fussli's Banknotes and Securities Division prints banknotes and securities for a number of countries and markets a number of other products including credit cards, debit cards (also referred to as stripe cards which have a black magnetic stripe attached), and Euro-checks. The division has an excellent reputation as a high-quality, state-of-the-art securities printing firm, employing the finest technology and printing expertise. While it has migrated to electronic printing methods, it still views itself as an "Old World" printing company.

Several changes are taking place worldwide that threaten some of the division's most profitable businesses. The beautifully crafted securities that have been one of its primary cash generators have been threatened by a number of events, including a growing trend toward electronic data interchange, the need to alleviate back-office bottlenecks in banks caused by cumbersome physical documents, the demand for larger denominations, and changes in financial markets. The paper currencies produced by the division are considered among the best in the world. It employs numerous techniques designed to thwart counterfeiting, but recent developments in copier technology and a possible trend in some countries toward putting some of their M1 money supply on plastic cards (with an integrated chip) threaten this business as well [25].

Although Orell Fussli has been profitable for years, recently it has experienced financial losses and a change in ownership. The director of Orell Fussli's Banknotes and Securities Division recognized the need for his business to move in a more profitable direction. He decided that the best way to improve profits was to use the division's favorable international reputation and technical competencies to develop new products and/or markets. He commissioned the development of the strategic DSS, and his viewpoint was responsible for the system's focus on strategic thinking rather than strategic planning and/or opportunistic decision making. The situation at Orell Fussli and emphasis on strategic thinking guided our design, including selection of conceptual models for strategic thinking and incorporation of real cases to demonstrate use of those models.

## Orell Fussli's Strategic DSS

THE STRATEGIC DSS DESIGNED FOR ORELL FUSSLI is primarily a training vehicle for strategic concepts, although several managers have used it to validate actual strategies and other managers have used it to formulate, or "craft" strategy. The system is menu-driven and consists of three major sections: Tools, Tutorial, and Application.

## Tools

The Tools section contains conceptual models that are widely discussed and employed in marketing, strategy, and information management, such as product portfolio matrix, product life cycle model, value chain model, multifactor portfolio matrix, and SPACE. We chose these models for several reasons: (1) the models, particularly the life cycle and the value chain, have been researched and discussed extensively, and are universally recognized and accepted [4]; (2) research suggests that these models can be effectively employed by managers in medium-size companies to facilitate strategic thinking; (3) the product and multifactor portfolio matrices are classic examples of two-dimensional matrices for conducting product/business portfolio analysis, and have been used by numerous companies (e.g., General Electric and Shell); and (4) SPACE provides two additional dimensions for decision makers to consider, overcoming the narrower scope of the two-dimensional models [10].

Dhar and Jarke [8] argue that it is important to provide the users with the knowledge that goes into the development of the model. We concur and, in the Tools section, provide functionality that explains each model, provides its underlying criteria, logic, and assumptions, and recommends objectives for sets of conditions. These features (along with those described in the Tutorial section below) help users understand the models and their recommendations and represent a form of extended decision support: support for facilitating and encouraging system use [17]. For example, through a series of menu options, managers can obtain a detailed description of the multifactor portfolio matrix, including definitions of its underlying dimensions (i.e., market attractiveness and relative competitive position) and sources of important information (e.g., A.C. Nielsen and COMPUSTAT). The system provides information sources so managers can inquire about the division's performance vis-à-vis that of the industry. For example, a 10 percent ROI may be unsatisfactory, especially if the industry average is 15 percent. This information facilitates assessment of a model's various dimensions. The models also recommend objectives, or "grand strategies," for various situations. For example, for a specific product, if a manager selects a cell that corresponds to high market attractiveness and strong relative competitive position, then the multifactor portfolio matrix recommends protection as an appropriate objective. Managers can also view various detailed protection strategies by making selections from the submenu.

Currently, the system does not provide access to information electronically via CD-ROM or online databases because of system interface and budgetary constraints as well as uncertainty over the direction of the European Information Services industry. While the industry lags considerably behind that of the United States, there are CD-ROM and online databases available to European managers $[37]$ . European managers prefer CD-ROM over online databases, which may be due to the absence of standards for online technology in Europe. Factors that impede the development of standards include legal, linguistic, and technical differences. For example, the French Minitel system is incompatible with ASCII $[26]$ . While working with a particular model, managers are responsible for obtaining the requisite information. The system describes the information required by a model and provides a reference to it. The division's managers have several directories to help them acquire the information. One directory is a 36-page document that describes CD-ROM and online databases that contain environmental information, including political, economic, social, and specific industry data. The document provides instructions on how to access each database, cost per minute connect time, and the cost of downloading a record. Managers use only a few databases and CD-ROM applications on a routine basis; these are readily available on other systems. For example, for the stripe card business, the division maintains a directory of contacts that contains telephone numbers, key words, expressions, and a narrative about recent conversations. This directory resides on a dBase IV system that is available to all managers throughout the company.

## Tutorial

Schwenk and Thomas [34] suggest that decision makers can benefit from decision aids when they are discussed in relation to familiar problems. During management development programs in which managers were introduced to strategic management concepts, we found that managers preferred to use the models in conjunction with real cases. Thus, the Tutorial section contains three Banknotes and Securities' cases to demonstrate the use of the models. Each case is based upon one of the division's products. The three cases are banknotes, securities, and plastic cards. The system walks managers through what constitutes a management consultant's use of the models with dialog to help them understand the appropriateness of each model and the important relationship between the situation and related strategies. For example, if a manager chooses to view the plastic card case, the system provides an explanation of how the various models could be used to analyze the situation and presents several potential strategies. A part of the plastic cards tutorial contains a discussion of the costs associated with each value-added activity involved in the production and distribution of plastic cards and of the companies that are considered leaders in each of those activities. The system also recommends appropriate strategies that are tied to the value-added analysis, and contains a glossary of terms and sources of relevant information.

## Application

The Application section consists of two stages and six steps that require both system and user participation. The first stage helps managers define an incremental improvement to an existing product and/or a new market opportunity to pursue. It consists of three steps: (1) strategic product/market position, (2) product/market objectives, and (3) strategic thinking via intrinsic value task analysis. The second stage is designed to make managers aware of the gap between the division's current situation (e.g., manufacturing capabilities, markets, technologies) and a more desirable position for making and marketing a new product. It also helps them evaluate specific product/market strategies for accomplishing stage 1's product/market objective(s).

The following sections describe the stages and steps in the context of stripe cards and smart cards. Stripe cards are plastic cards with a black stripe that contains information, such as personal identification number, dates, and charge limitations. Smart cards are plastic cards with nonvolatile memory, security logic, a microprocessor, and the ability to communicate with a reading device. They represent opportunities for traditional plastic and stripe card makers. For each step, we describe generic tasks performed by the system and user, and describe one senior marketing executive's analysis (see Tables 2 and 3).

## Stage 1: Product/Market Analysis

Step 1: Strategic product/market position: In step 1, the system displays the product portfolio matrix and the product life cycle model and requests that managers place an existing product on each diagram. The product portfolio matrix juxtaposes market growth rate with relative market share to distinguish among four product classifications: cash cows (low market growth, high market share), stars (high market growth, high market share), question marks (high market growth, low market share), and dogs (low market growth, low market share). The product life cycle model distinguishes among four product life cycle stages: introduction, growth, maturity, and decline. Each stage has different characteristics, such as sales, costs, and customers, and marketing objectives. This exercise allows managers to describe the product's current product/market position, which the system uses to present product/market objectives in the next step.

During step 1, the marketing manager placed the stripe card in the stars cell of the product portfolio matrix and the growth stage of the product life cycle model. The manager considered stripe cards to be a high growth market and the division had a significant share of the Swiss market.

Step 2: Product/market objectives: In the second step, based on the product's position in the matrix and model, the system presents several objectives for managers to consider—namely, increasing market share for question marks, extending the product's life for cash cows, bundling products and services for stars, and divestiture for dogs. In addition, to help managers understand the objectives and to prepare them for subsequent steps, on the user's command, the system presents several cases and anecdotes that explain the objectives and describes ways for achieving them.

For the stripe card, the system recommended bundling products or services with the card as an objective to pursue. For a stripe card, the manager had several options for achieving the objective: add more security to the existing card (e.g., use laser technology to embed the card holder's image on the card); improve customer service (e.g., maintain card holder's information for the division's customers, such as banks and finance companies that distribute the cards); and build more electronic functionality into the card by embedding a microprocessor. In the next step, the system helped the manager determine the specific nature of these improvements and formulate several product/market strategies.

Step 3: Strategic thinking via intrinsic value task analysis: In step 3, the system poses ten general tasks for managers. We designed these tasks to build awareness of the product's intrinsic value and to help stimulate thinking about the product in terms of nontraditional uses and markets. For example, the Arm and Hammer Baking Soda case illustrates how a cash cow can be revitalized by identifying a product's intrinsic value and using this insight to develop new businesses or uses for the product. The American Hospital Supply Corporation case illustrates how the value chain can help managers identify products or services that can be bundled with an existing product. During these exercises, managers develop specific product/market strategies for accomplishing the objective(s) that the system presented in step 2.

In step 3, the marketing manager interpreted each task with the objective of bundling products or services with the stripe card and, as a result, defined a list of generic, value-added features for a card-size plastic instrument, such as large nonvolatile memory, electronic security logic, microprocessor control, and read/write capabilities that go beyond the capabilities of the division's existing stripe cards. Collectively, the features constituted an advanced smart card. In addition, he identified a list of stakeholders, including potential Swiss and German competitor's local and international producers and global customers. The smart card represented a new product for the division and presented new market opportunities. At the end of this step, the manager also specified the following product/market strategies:

\- Acquire existing competitor with technical knowhow, distribution network and customer base;

\- Develop in-house expertise for production, marketing, and sales;

\- Form a joint venture with existing competitors to make and/or market the cards in new or existing markets.

These strategies are evaluated in stage 2.

## Stage 2: Product/Market Implementation Analysis

Stage 2 prepares managers for addressing implementation issues associated with the new product/market strategies from stage 1 and consists of three remaining steps: (4) strategy evaluation with multifactor portfolio matrix (MPM); (5) sensitivity analysis; and 6) strategy evaluation with SPACE method. MPM and SPACE help managers think about implementation strategies for the new product/market. A description of how the MPM model is used illustrates the final stage of the Application session.

Step 4: Strategy evaluation with MPM: At the beginning of step 4, managers place the new product on the MPM based on their understanding of its market attractiveness and the company's relative competitive position, or business strength. Next, the system presents managers with a set of criteria for both market attractiveness (e.g., market size, value added, environmental impact) and relative competitive position (e.g., market share, price competitiveness, facilities location) to evaluate specific product/market strategies for the new product. Using a five-point rating scale (1 = low, 5 = high), managers assign values to the criteria and weight them to reflect their relative importance. The system defines the criteria in the Tutorial section, and provides sources of pertinent information for comparative purposes (e.g., print, online and CD-ROM sources). Where possible, it provides industry averages in order to help managers complete the exercise. On demand, managers can use the value chain model to understand the value-added activities of the new product and associated costs. The model helps managers assign values and weights to the MPM criteria and provides additional insight into the primary activities associated with producing and marketing the new product. The exercises help managers understand specific product/market strategies for the new product in the context of the company's ability to make and market it successfully.

The system combines the separate scores into overall measures for market attractiveness and relative competitive position and plots the result on the MPM. If the system plots the new product in a cell that corresponds to strong competitive position and high market attractiveness—the most desirable position—it presents appropriate protection strategies along with examples. If the system plots the new product in any other, less desirable cell (e.g., weak competitive position and high market attractiveness), managers may select one of two options, sensitivity or target, to help them evaluate ways to close the gap between the current and most desirable positions. When the manager’s initial product placement is the same as the system’s placement, which occurs after the criteria evaluation process, the manager’s intuition is confirmed. When there is a significant discrepancy, the manager can reevaluate the criteria scores and weights via sensitivity analysis to identify specific weaknesses or strengths that were not intuitively obvious at the beginning of the analysis.

Initially, the marketing manager placed the new product in the high market attractiveness and medium relative competitive position cell of MPM, reflecting his a priori understanding of the division's ability to make and market the new product successfully. After the system displayed MPM criteria, the manager assigned scores and weights to the criteria. He also performed a value chain analysis to enhance his understanding of important manufacturing steps. The manager's value chain analysis for one version of a smart card revealed that the smart card involves several additional manufacturing activities, namely chip fabrication, chip programming, and card personalization (see figure 2). Additionally, this analysis showed that the smart card had a significantly different cost structure than the stripe card because of the additional activities, especially fabrication and programming. Each type of smart card, such as plain memory, memory with security logic, and memory with microprocessor, differs in terms of value-added activities and costs. The smart card analysis helped the manager assess a number of MPM criteria, including experience curve effects, investment utilization, and raw material costs (see figure 3). For example, he reduced his initial rating scale values for these and other criteria as a result of the value chain analysis to better reflect the division's lack of experience in manufacturing smart cards. The manager also organized the list of stakeholders from stage 1 according to value chain activities, facilitating the evaluation of MPM market attractiveness factors, including market share, product line breadth, and sales effectiveness. These analyses allowed him to identify gaps between the desired state for making and selling smart cards and the current state of Orell Fussli's manufacturing and marketing capabilities. After the criteria were evaluated, the system computed measures for market attractiveness and relative competitive position and plotted the results in the medium market attractiveness and low competitive position cell of the MPM. This gap revealed that there were critical weaknesses in the division's ability to market and make the new products, so much so that the strategies of buying an existing competitor or forming a joint venture with an existing competitor were heavily considered in the next step.

Step 5: Sensitivity analysis: In step 5, if a manager chooses sensitivity analysis, the system provides options for changing the ratings and weights of any of the MPM criteria (see figure 3). The system recalculates the overall measures based on the new values and displays the results on the MPM. For example, if the system placed the product in a cell corresponding to high market attractiveness and medium competitive position, the manager could use the sensitivity analysis option to determine how best to move to a more desirable position. In this case the manager would examine the weights and ratings for the criteria that contribute to competitive position (e.g., market share, product line breadth, and sales effectiveness). By examining the weights, managers can focus on those criteria that most affect competitive position. Ratings indicate the extent of additional effort and resources that must be committed in order to close the gap between the current and desired positions. If managers choose to increase market share, the system makes them simultaneously consider other criteria, including capacity and production efficiency.

If a manager chooses the target option, based upon prior ratings and weights, the system automatically identifies the criteria that would have to be improved to achieve a more desirable position. If the system placed the product in a cell corresponding to high market attractiveness and medium competitive position, the target option shows the competitive position criteria that would have to be changed to attain a stronger competitive position. Criteria are examined in order of highest weights first, and criteria with ratings of five are not candidates for improvement.

The marketing manager chose sensitivity analysis to reevaluate the criteria. The sensitivity analysis allowed the manager to examine several of the candidate strategies introduced above. For example, by identifying existing weaknesses, the magnitude of those weaknesses and industry stakeholders, the manager evaluated ways to improve the company's position. The manager considered developing in-house expertise for production, marketing, and sales, either alone or with the participation of another company by acquiring an existing competitor or forming a joint venture with existing competitors. Step 6: Strategy evaluation with SPACE method: In step 6, managers perform the same analysis using SPACE, which presents them with other, related criteria. The SPACE analysis can provide managers with additional perspectives and insights via its different dimensions and criteria. Space is similar to the multifactor portfolio matrix and the TOWS matrix in that the factors included are a function of the specific organization under study. SPACE is a four-quadrant framework that consists of two internal dimensions (financial strength and competitive advantage) and two external dimensions (environmental stability and industry strength). Factors for each dimension (e.g., return on investment for financial strength; control over suppliers and distributors for competitive advantage; price elasticity of demand for environmental stability; and growth potential for industry strength) are selected and ranked. These factors are then used to calculate a directional vector, which is used to identify one of four recommended generic strategies to pursue: aggressive, conservative, defensive, or competitive. For each generic strategy, the system provides several specific strategies. For example, for the aggressive quadrant the system may recommend market penetration, or various integration strategies [31]. The purpose of this analysis is to provide managers with another perspective about the product/market decision under consideration, and to increase their awareness of the actions required to implement a strategy as well as the consequences of those actions.

## Design Guidelines and Principles

THE RESEARCH THAT LED TO THE CURRENT SYSTEM PROVIDES SEVERAL GUIDELINES and principles for designers of strategic DSS. We discuss each of them below.

## Incorporate Multiple Conceptual Models into Strategic DSS

The system employs several conceptual models (e.g., product portfolio matrix, value chain model) in order to overcome the incompleteness of any single model. From our experience, no one model adequately addresses the complexity of strategic thinking. The models present slightly different perspectives by incorporating different dimensions and criteria, providing managers with multiple views of the problem, much in the way DSS employee multiple OR models (e.g., optimal control theory and integer programming for allocation problems). This approach can give managers greater insight into strategic thinking, strategy formulation, and eventual implementation issues.

As Merten [24] points out, conceptual models help decision makers transform their mental models into discussable and transparent formal ones. The variation among managers associated with placing products in different places on a matrix provides ample opportunity for managers to discuss underlying criteria and to resolve differences when considering potential product/market strategies.

## Employ a Strategic Management Framework

The system organizes and sequences models according to a general strategic management framework that fits Orell Fussli and incorporates strategic product/market positioning, strategic thinking, strategy formulation, and implementation. This framework facilitates the selection and orchestration of the many potential models. For example, our specific implementation of this framework at Orell Fussli consists of the following models: the life cycle and product portfolio models for strategic product/market positioning; intrinsic value task analysis for strategic thinking and strategy formulation; and MPM, value chain, SPACE, and sensitivity analysis for strategy implementation. Undoubtedly, another framework and set of models would be required for another company or for other areas of strategy, including corporate strategy, functional area strategy, and opportunistic decision making.

## Promote User Guidance

The system facilitates system use by providing explanations of models, examples, cases, and analogies that describe the application of models in familiar settings. While managers have an intuitive understanding of the system's models, they prefer to preface a session with a brief tutorial which makes it easier for them to define the models' underlying criteria. We found that managers appreciate the tutorials, examples, explanations, and cases, and the assurance such support provides.

In addition, by providing a list of criteria and a method to subjectively weight them, the system allows managers to consider multiple dimensions and criteria simultaneously. An initial listing of the criteria that underlie a model makes it easier for managers to modify the list to model their own internal situation and market conditions, rather than create a totally new list from scratch. This functionality together with the cases, examples, and explanations discussed above represents an implementation of Keen's [17] notion of extended DSS.

## Promote Learning throughout the Development Process

Houdeshel and Watson [15] identified six keys to the successful development of management information and decision support systems. The keys are a committed senior executive, carefully defined system requirements, carefully defined information requirements, a team approach to systems development, an evolutionary development approach, and careful hardware and software selection. We concur: these are critical to the success of any system. We would add a seventh factor to these key success factors, namely, the need for a continuous education of users throughout the development process. Zmud [46] noted that users' attitudes play a critical role in determining whether a system is used and ultimately in whether it is perceived as a success. Educational programs that address preconceived attitudes regarding the role of the system in the decision-making process help encourage user participation and operational use.

## Conclusion

THE SYSTEM REPORTED HERE HELPS MANAGERS learn about strategic concepts, facilitates strategic thinking, and, in some instances, has been used to validate their strategies. Our work suggests that several features—including use of multiple conceptual models, organization of models around a strategic management framework, and provision of explanations, examples, and cases—provide valuable functionality to support strategic thinking. Our research also indicates that the types and number of conceptual models and the strategic management framework that organizes them will be determined by user-researcher interaction and continuous education of users throughout the development process. In identifying the appropriate models and framework for the situation, developers may need to educate users about the most applicable conceptual models and how they can be applied to their company. Although strategic DSS are in their infancy, as were EIS approximately ten years ago, we expect future systems to incorporate features similar to those in our system, and strategic DSS to expand to other areas of strategic management.
