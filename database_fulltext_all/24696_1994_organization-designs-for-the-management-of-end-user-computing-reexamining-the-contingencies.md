# Organization Designs for the Management of End-User Computing: Reexamining the Contingencies

CAROL V. BROWN AND ROBERT P. BOSTROM

CAROL V. BROWN is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Decision and Information Systems at Indiana University's School of Business (Bloomington and Indianapolis). She holds an A.B. from Vassar College, an M.Mus. from Northwestern University, and an M.B.A. and Ph.D. in management information systems from Indiana University. Her prior work experience includes a faculty appointment with the Krannert School of Management at Purdue University, as well as a nine-year tenure as a user manager with information systems liaison responsibilities. Her current research interests focus on IS organization and management issues.

ROBERT P. BOSTROM is an Associate Professor of Management Information Systems in the Department of Management at the University of Georgia. He is also Program Director of the Group Support Systems Research Project. He holds a B.A. and an M.B.A. from Michigan State University, an M.S. in computer science from the State University of New York, Albany, and a Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Minnesota. Besides numerous publications in leading academic and practitioner journals, he has extensive consulting and training experience in the areas of MIS management, MIS design, organizational development, high-performing professionals, and computer support for groups/teams. Dr. Bostrom is president of a training and consulting company that focuses on the effective integration of people and technology, especially within teams. His current research interests focus on high-performing individuals, user-designer relationships, group support systems, and effective design of organizations via integrating human/social and technological dimensions.

ABSTRACT: To date, little research has been directed at the management infrastructure for end-user computing (EUC) from an organization design perspective. This study reports on the testing of a previously published contingency model in which two different structural forms are modeled for four states of EUC implementation: (1) an organic design (characterized by a decentralized, highly participatory form of decision making with loose controls), and (2) a mechanistic design (characterized by centralized decision making with a more tightly controlled form of management). Based on questionnaire data from 63 companies, statistical support is found for only the two states of EUC implementation for which a mechanistic design is modeled. Although only moderate support for this contingency model is found, the results of this study provide additional evidence for the effectiveness of a more controlled approach to EUC management under a low EUC growth objective at an early stage of implemen-

## 1. Introduction

TO DATE, THE MAJORITY OF RESEARCH ON THE EUC management infrastructure has been directed at a centralized unit commonly referred to as an information center. Within this context, the focus has been primarily at what to implement: for example, what support services to offer, what staffing characteristics and support levels to provide, what technology standards to establish, what training methods to offer, and so on. Much less research has been directed at how to design the EUC management infrastructure from an organization design perspective that extends beyond an information center mechanism. In particular, little attention has been given to research questions that address the structural form of the EUC management infrastructure—that is, the locus of authority for EUC management decisions, the allocation of EUC management support roles between IS and user departments, and the extent of rules and guidelines for personnel performing EUC support roles.

Among the EUC management studies that do investigate organization design issues, several conceptual stage models—based on the assumption that the organization's strategy should change over time to match the stage of EUC assimilation—were published in the latter half of the 1980s. Of these, two normative models based on McFarlan and McKenney's innovative information technology assimilation model [29] were independently developed by Henderson and Treacy [25] and Alavi et al. [1]. Both sets of authors warn of the perils of managing EUC with a high-control approach at too early an implementation stage. In contrast, a descriptive model developed at about the same time by Munro and Huff [31, 32] depicts four strategies of EUC management that represent low/high combinations of control and expansion "levers." This model depicts a high-control strategy as an appropriate early strategy when a firm's objective is a slower pace of EUC growth.

More recently, Brown and Bostrom [7] propose a contingency model for predicting EUC management success that takes into account the above discrepancy. They conclude that the above three models and their own field data suggest that two contingent factors may be important. The first contingent factor is the firm's overall growth objectives for EUC. The second contingent factor is the firm's stage of implementation of EUC activities. Brown and Bostrom also conclude that there is a need for a common agreement as to what the key organization design variables for EUC management are. Since the three prior models take the perspective that EUC should be managed as an innovation, they propose that two structural forms—“organic” and “mechanistic”—found to be important in innovation management research [e.g., 9, 13, 22], as well as proposed for the management of emerging information technologies [11], be applied to the domain of EUC management.

Brown and Bostrom propose a four-cell model in which an organic or mechanistic structural form is depicted as the effective design dependent on the organization's growth objectives and implementation stage for EUC. Here, for example, dispersed decision authority, end-user participation in decision making, and high task discretion for EUC support personnel are among the characteristics of the organic form—a form of management infrastructure associated with both control risks and responsiveness. Concentrated decision-making authority, more rules, and less individual employee discretion are among the characteristics of the mechanistic form—a management infrastructure associated with both maintaining control and high bureaucratic costs [39].

Although at the time the authors were developing their contingency model, none of the three earlier EUC management models had been empirically tested, a few validation attempts have now been reported. The choice between a low growth–high control strategy (containment) and a high growth–low control strategy (acceleration) as modeled by Munro and Huff is supported by Saarinen et al. [40] based on case examples from Finnish firms. Brancheau and Amoroso [4] confirm the existence of the same two strategies in a U.S. sample of 18 firms, but also report finding strategies other than the four modeled by Munro and Huff. Brown and Wynne [8] report face validity for the patterns of actions for some of the Alavi et al. stages as well as the expansion and control measures of Munro and Huff, based on a single case example.

The purpose of this article is to report on a validation study of the Brown and Bostrom contingency model $[7]$ . This is the first known study to test any of the EUC management models with a large field sample.

Further, since this contingency model synthesizes elements of several earlier stage models, the findings of this study have important implications for both researchers and practitioners which extend beyond this single model. First, this validation study addresses whether or not a more controlled approach to EUC management is a valid approach at an early implementation stage. Whereas today's largest U.S. organizations are likely to be in a later stage of EUC management, this is still an important question for smaller U.S. organizations, as well as for large organizations in other nations that are reportedly several years behind in EUC activities [23, 35].

Second, since this contingency model (and other EUC management models) is based on the assumption that EUC is an organizational innovation, the findings here also contribute to our understanding about how to implement new information technologies in general. This is important because information technology assimilation is expected to continue to have major impacts on organizations in the 1990s and beyond [e.g., 24, 41], yet our knowledge about what organization design factors are important for implementing innovations in general is still lacking [34, 42].

Third, a situation analogous to that found by Benbasat et al. [2] for the stage model by Gibson and Nolan [19, 33] is beginning to occur: recently published MIS textbooks for core business courses are promulgating one or more of the earlier EUC management models for which to date we have only weak or mixed empirical support. This study is offered as a contribution toward reducing this gap in the EUC management literature, as called for by recent MIS researchers [5, 30].

The article is organized as follows. The contingency model which is the focus of this validation study is introduced in more detail in section 2. Section 3 presents the four hypotheses and our research methodology for testing them. Our findings are discussed in section 4, along with a statement of limitations. These findings and their implications for other EUC management models are discussed in section 5. Based on the results of this study, as well as other findings from this research stream, in section 6 we propose a revised contingency model as well as other recommendations for future research.

## 2. Four-Cell Contingency Model and Hypotheses

BROWN AND BOSTROM'S FOUR-CELL CONTINGENCY MODEL OF EUC management effectiveness is reprinted in figure 1. The four states of EUC implementation are determined by the values of two contingent variables: EUC implementation phase (initial or integrated) and EUC growth objective (high or low). Contingent on which of the four states of EUC implementation an organization currently resides in, a mechanistic or organic design is modeled as the effective design for the EUC management tasks.

The mechanistic and organic forms are multidimensional phenomena that are close to “logical opposites” [21, p. 100]. The definitions for the mechanistic and organic designs are based on Duncan’s “ambidextrous” model for the management of organizational innovations [16], and include three structural characteristics:

Centralization refers to the degree to which decision making is dispersed in terms of (1) the locus of authority for decision making and (2) the hierarchical levels of the organizational employees who participate in the decision process. Low centralization (decentralization) is a characteristic of the “organic” form; high centralization characterizes the “mechanistic” form.

Formalization refers to the extent to which appropriate rules and procedures for performing a job task exist in formal written statements, and the extent to which there is an organizational emphasis on following them. Low formalization is a characteristic of the “organic” form; high formalization characterizes the “mechanistic” form.

Complexity refers to the number of occupational specialties and the degree of differentiation in the task structure. The degree of complexity is measured by the reliance on separate units to perform the tasks, the degree of discretion that personnel have in performance of the tasks, and the degree to which division of labor has occurred. High complexity is a characteristic of the “organic” form; low complexity characterizes the “mechanistic” form. [7, pp. 83–84]

Highly controlled roles and tasks for EUC support personnel will contain learning at the individual and organizational levels. The organization thus avoids the potential risks encountered in an early stage under a high growth, low control approach.

## 3. The Validation Study

## 3.1. Hypotheses

THE SCOPE CHOSEN FOR THIS STUDY IS TO TEST THE VALIDITY of the organic and mechanistic design choices in the Brown and Bostrom [7] contingency model. In order to test the validity of the path an organization takes from an initial to an integrated phase, longitudinal data are required. Since the level of analysis is the organization, the costs of a longitudinal study would be considerably higher than for a cross-sectional study. The decision was therefore made to collect cross-sectional data from a large field sample in order to first investigate the validity of each of the four cells in figure 1, rather than initially invest in the more costly data collection methods required to investigate an organization's progression through the cells.

Four hypotheses, one for each cell of the model, were therefore restated as follows:

For the two high growth cells:

H1a: Among organizations with an objective of high EUC growth that are in an initial implementation phase, those that manage with an organic EUC structure have higher EUC management effectiveness than those that manage EUC with a mechanistic structure.

H1b: Among organizations with an objective of high EUC growth that are in an integrated implementation phase, those that manage EUC with a mechanistic EUC structure have higher EUC management effectiveness than those that manage EUC with an organic structure.

For the two low growth cells:

H2a: Among organizations with an objective of low EUC growth that are in an initial implementation phase, those that manage EUC with a mechanistic EUC structure have higher EUC management effectiveness than those that manage EUC with an organic structure.

H2b: Among organizations with an objective of low EUC growth that are in an integrated implementation phase, those that manage EUC with an organic structure have higher EUC management effectiveness than those that manage EUC with a mechanistic structure.

## 3.2. Design

A cross-sectional, field survey design with questionnaire administration by mail as the primary data collection method was utilized. The target respondent was a single informant per organization with the most knowledge about how EUC is being managed within that organization: an upper-level manager with organizationwide responsibilities for EUC. It was our judgment that such an informant could be expected to respond from an organizational (versus parochial) perspective to questions about the relevant variables: the organization's EUC growth objectives, characteristics of its EUC implementation phase, the organizational design of the EUC management tasks across functional boundaries, and EUC management performance. This design choice also allowed for a large number of organizations to be sampled.

Several procedural methods were also adopted to minimize threats to the validity of a study based on data collected by mailed questionnaire from a single informant per organization. For example, initial mail and telephone contacts were designed to clearly communicate the organizational responsibilities for the target respondent sought in order to appropriately “place” the questionnaire. This ensured that the respondent would be answering from the same position bias. No questionnaires were sent out prior to this step. The wording of the survey instrument also emphasized an organizational versus a more parochial perspective. Finally, during our instrument pretesting phase, we found such organizational participants to be not only knowledgeable but also candid in their assessments of how well EUC was being managed. We think this is partially due to the fact that the typical informant for this study devoted less than a quarter of his or her job time to EUC management responsibilities.

## 3.3. EUC Definition and Scope

Although EUC activities have been defined as including word processing, electronic communication, and other office automation activities, the focus here was a subset of EUC activities frequently termed “user application development” [38]. For this study, the scope was narrowed to the management of business application development by end users, utilizing micro, mini, or mainframe technology—alone or in combination. The term “application” was defined in its most general sense, and included the use of statistical packages and report generators.

The scope of activities involved in EUC management was defined according to the four EUC management infrastructures first identified by Henderson and Treacy [25], as reformulated by Brown and Bostrom [7]:

(1) a people support infrastructure, which includes tasks such as training and education, problem-solving through hotlines, ongoing consulting, and periodic communication mechanisms;

(2) a technology infrastructure, which includes scanning, selection, and acquisition tasks;

(3) a data infrastructure, which includes data structuring and storage, data collection and maintenance, and data access tasks; and

(4) a planning and evaluation infrastructure, which includes establishing EUC goals for the organization, allocating resources, assigning roles and responsibilities, developing policies and procedures, and assessing performance. [7, pp. 79–80]

For the remainder of this article, the term “EUC” therefore refers to the above restricted definition of the term, and the term “EUC management task area” refers to the above listing.

## 3.4. Survey Instrument

The instrument development process was an iterative one, and was designed to address the methodological concerns of content and construct validity. An instrument has content validity when it is constructed with representative items from the domain of items associated with each construct it purports to measure. The primary procedure undertaken to establish content validity was first to develop a survey instrument using prior EUC literature to ground the theoretical variables, and then to undertake an “expert review” process $[14]$ . First, a preliminary version of the instrument was reviewed by four MIS academics familiar with prior EUC research and four IS managers who held positions that included organizationwide responsibilities for EUC. In addition to a cover letter identifying the purpose of the review and the intended use of the questionnaire, the four practitioners were given a list of the research variables and a set of questions to focus their assessment. The four academics were also provided a copy of the research model and a statement of hypotheses, as well as a list of the research constructs. Followup interviews were held with the IS managers, in addition to feedback in written form.

Based on the feedback from this process, the survey instrument was then revised and submitted to a second “expert review.” For the repeat participants, specific questions were used to structure their review and feedback via telephone. A new expert (another IS manager with organizationwide EUC responsibilities) was also asked to provide feedback via telephone within a more open-ended format. The above process led to additional but minor revisions to the questionnaire.

The next step was the pretesting of the instrument under the administration procedures planned for the final study. A convenience sample of 22 potential participants was identified by initial mail contacts and followup telephone calls. A total of 14 surveys (64 percent response rate) were returned. The respondents were also asked to comment on any problems experienced with answering the survey questions. The pretest identified some problems with unclear (or ambiguous) instructions as well as some measurement problems.

Concurrent with the period of instrument development described above, the first author was also engaged in data collection for two case studies on EUC management that entailed in-depth interviews with the target respondent for this study, in addition to in-person interviews with other IS personnel and managers of end-user developers, as well as a paper-and-pencil survey of end-user developers. The version of the instrument used in the pretesting step described above was utilized in these two firms to collect data from key managers, and the researchers were able to compare the data collected via the questionnaire with the data collected via interview methods. Both the data collection and in-person feedback sessions held with IS and user managers in these two firms increased the researchers' confidence in the content and construct validity of the instrument.

A third and final version of the instrument was then administered by mail in a pilot study with a sample of firms $n = 23$ from the final study population. Since no revisions to the measures or administration procedures were identified as a result of this pilot study, the data collected from the pilot sample were included as final study data. In section 3.6 we report on the psychometric properties of the final instrument based on the final sample $n = 105$ .

The research variables needed for the testing of the four primary hypotheses include the dependent variable EUC management effectiveness, and three dichotomous independent variables: initial/integrated EUC implementation phase, high/low EUC growth objective, and organic/mechanistic EUC structure. In addition, four organization-level factors that could potentially confound the relationships between the independent variables and dependent variable served as control variables. The research model for the study is provided in figure 2. A description of each research variable follows.

## 3.4.1. Independent Variables

EUC Implementation Phase: Although the five-stage model of application maturity by Huff et al. [26] was utilized for the field study reported on in Brown and Bostrom [7], practitioners in the “expert review” process for the survey instrument expressed concerns about its historical versus current relevance due to advancements in end-user tools. Given the model’s assumption that EUC is an innovation, we developed eight additional items based on prior management of innovation studies $[21, 44]$ and the EUC literature. Two additional items were based on the conceptualization of an organization’s EUC stage in terms of the “application maturity” of its portfolio of user-developed applications. Six other items measured the organization’s investment in EUC tools and the degree of organizational learning. All nine items are reprinted in the appendix.

EUC Growth Objective: Four types of EUC growth objectives were measured with single items, as operationalized in Brown and Bostrom [7]: growth in (1) the number of user departments doing EUC, (2) the number of user personnel developing applications, (3) the number and range of tools being utilized by user personnel, and (4) the number and range of application types being developed by user personnel. Formalization: RULES, FOLLOW. The extent to which policies/guidelines for task execution existed and the extent to which they were written were measured with one item each (RULES). Two items measured the extent to which the latter were “expected to be” followed, and “are” followed (FOLLOW). Complexity: STAND, PROF. The standardization indicator (STAND) was composed of three items that measured the degree of reliance on separate units or task groups to perform the EUC management tasks (information center, steering committee, and user group), and three items that measured the extent to which standardized roles for the EUC management tasks were implemented outside of the IS department. The professionalization (or occupational complexity) indicator (PROF) measured with four items each the degree of differentiation in the task structure for IS and (if they existed) non-IS department personnel performing EUC management tasks. The survey questions that measured these two complexity indicators are reprinted in the appendix.

## 3.4.2. Dependent Variable

EUC Management Effectiveness: The dependent variable was designed to be measured with a direct referent, as recommended by Cameron and Whetten [10]. Separate, one-item measures of EUC management effectiveness were collected for each of the four EUC management task areas, and an aggregate score was calculated. As in other empirical studies that have investigated a relationship between aspects of a firm's EUC structure and a performance variable [e.g., 38], perceived data only were collected.

The actual survey question for the people support task area is reprinted in the appendix. Similar items were developed for the other task areas.

## 3.4.3. Control Variables

Sector: The sector (private, public, nonprofit) of an organization was identified as an important control variable due to differences in (1) overall objectives and (2) inter-organizational dependencies for firms in different sectors [27]. Only private, for-profit organizations were included in the field study.

Divisional Status: The divisional status of an organization was identified as an important control variable in the interest of ensuring valid, cross-organizational comparisons. The divisional status of a firm was confirmed by phonecall prior to the mailing of a questionnaire. Informants with EUC responsibilities within “autonomous operating divisions” only were included in the field study.

Information Intensity: The information intensity of a company or industry has been recognized as an important factor for MIS management research [43]. Conceptually, it is this type of measure that could be theorized to have a significant impact on the relationships between the independent and dependent variables for this study. The degree to which a company is “reliant on information processing to create and deliver its product” was measured on a seven-point Likert-type scale and used as a covariate in the statistical analysis.

Size: MIS management research has reported significant relationships between organization size and IS department structure [e.g., 17]. Although organization size has not always been controlled for in EUC empirical research, Raymond [37] points out that EUC may be the only form of organizational computing in small firms, and thus a different set of EUC management issues exists for small firms. The most frequently reported size measure, the number of total employees, was collected. For this study, firms with fewer than 100 employees were excluded from the field study. Although other size thresholds can be found in other studies, this cutoff point was selected after a review of related literature in an attempt to exclude all “small business” firms. A normalized size measure was used as a covariate in the statistical analysis.

## 3.5. Sample

A random sample of Data Processing Management Association (DPMA) members with appropriate position titles in private, for-profit companies was drawn from a population of DPMA members with an address state in the Midwest and Southeast United States. An initial communication by mail was followed by a telephone call to the DPMA member. A firm qualified for inclusion if (1) organizationwide EUC management responsibilities had been assigned to one or more persons, (2) the firm was in the private sector and had 100 or more total employees, and (3) the firm was not dependent on an external unit for EUC management (i.e., was an autonomous operating division).

If the organization qualified for inclusion in this study, the identification of the person with organizationwide EUC responsibilities—that is, the target informant—was pursued. For the cases in which the DPMA contact was not the target informant, the researcher usually contacted the referral by phone; in a few cases, the DPMA member had already secured (or wished personally to secure) the commitment to participate on behalf of the researcher. To help ensure that all participants would have the EUC management knowledge sought for, if the target informant was new to the position, or the position had recently been restructured, the firm was excluded by mutual agreement.

Of the initial contact sample of 559 firms, 256 firms were identified as eligible. $^{1}$ Within these, 63 of the potential participants (25 percent) declined to participate; the most common reasons reported were lack of time due to workload pressures. The remaining 193 potential participants were sent a survey form. Of these, 105 usable surveys were received (representing 41 percent of eligible contacts). We partially attribute the high response rate for the returned surveys (54 percent) to the personal commitments received by telephone.

The organizational sample was relatively equally balanced between service (47 percent) and manufacturing (53 percent) industries. As for the two control variables, the median size was 1,100 total employees; only 12 of the 105 companies had 10,000 or more total employees. The median of the perceived information intensity ratings for the companies was above the midpoint (5 on a 7-point scale).

The sample was also equally balanced between informants who had been in their current positions one to three years, and those who had been in their positions four to ten years. About half (56 percent) of the respondents had a reporting location either one or two levels below the president (or other top officer). Half of the informants also devoted 20 percent or less of their time to EUC management responsibilities. Two-thirds of the informants categorized themselves as more of an IS specialist than a generalist in terms of their educational and employment experience.

Two types of potential nonresponse bias in the final sample of 105 were statistically analyzed: (1) bias in geographical representation, and (2) bias in the values of the independent and dependent variables. Based on Mann-Whitney U-tests, no significant biases were detected. Late returned surveys were used as surrogates for non-respondents to test the second type of bias [12].

## 3.6. Data Analysis Procedures

## 3.6.1. Properties of the Survey Instrument

The psychometric properties of the survey instrument were investigated by correlation analysis and factor analysis for the purposes of establishing construct validity. A response: item ratio of 5:1 was the heuristic used for the choice of the level of aggregation at which to perform these analyses for each research variable. First, the number and direction of significant correlations were examined. Next, the loadings of retained items were examined for orthogonally rotated factor solutions.

Two problem areas were identified by these analyses: (1) unexpected negative correlations between items measuring the complexity construct, and (2) unstable measures of the three centralization indicators for the planning/evaluation task area. The decision was therefore made (1) to eliminate the planning/evaluation measures from the calculations of the organizational scores for the centralization indicators, and (2) to eliminate five items measuring the complexity construct and to collapse the remaining nine items into a single indicator (COMPLEX).

Although these problems with construct measurement were disappointing, they were not totally without explanation. The operationalization of the complexity construct was the most problematic of the EUC structure variables over the instrument development period. The initial problems were attributed to a lack of prior research in this specific area. Consultations with practitioner “experts” (as reported in section 3.4) were relied on heavily. Unfortunately, the final data collected for this validation study suggest that operationalizing companywide implementation of formal user department roles (Complexity Q.8–10 in the appendix) as low complexity—a characteristic of the mechanistic form—may not match real-world perceptions. Further research on formal and informal support roles played by non-IS personnel is clearly needed.

The unstable factor loadings for the planning/evaluation task area suggest a different underlying problem. An absence of an overall EUC strategy to guide a firm's planning and evaluation activities has been reported by earlier EUC researchers [e.g., 18, 20]. We attribute our problems with measuring this construct to the fact that the planning and evaluation tasks for EUC management are still the least well defined and executed in practice. Here, too, additional research is needed.

Cronbach alpha reliability coefficients were calculated for the purposes of identifying the internal consistency of the composite scales used to calculate the organizational scores for the dependent variable, the two contingent variables, and the six EUC structure indicators. The single scale for the complexity construct had an alpha of 0.55. All other scales had an alpha of 0.7 or higher. All but one of the reliability coefficients are therefore within the desired range, and the complexity scale appears to lie within the acceptable range for exploratory research.

## 3.6.2. Classification Procedures

The 105 cases were categorized according to the dichotomous values for each of the three independent variables as follows. For the two contingent variables, median values for the sample were used to split the 105 cases into dichotomous groupings.

The use of a median value to split the sample into equal dichotomous groupings of organic and mechanistic cases was considered for the third independent variable, EUC structure, but was rejected. Although such a procedure would have ensured the retention of all 105 cases, the researchers strongly felt that such a procedure was not in keeping with the definition of these structural forms as two endpoints on a continuum. Instead, an alternative, multistep procedure was adopted, as described below.

First, median values for the sample were used to split the 105 cases into high versus low groupings for each EUC structure indicator. A listing of these high/low values was then generated for each case, and two decision rules designed to capture the theoretical underpinnings of the two forms were adopted. (The decision rules are reprinted at the end of the appendix.) More specifically, in order to qualify as an organic or mechanistic form, a minimum of four of the six indicators were required to match the high/low indicator patterns in Table 2. This ensured that each case classified as organic or mechanistic would be exhibiting structural characteristics for a minimum of two of the three variables. These procedures yielded 28 cases that could be confidently classified as organic, 35 cases as mechanistic. The remaining 42 cases were labeled hybrid. $^{2}$

## 3.6.3. Hypothesis Testing

For each of the four hypothesis tests, a one-way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was first performed and the significance levels of the size and information intensity control variables were examined. Before assessing the contributions of this study, several methodological limitations bear mentioning. ## 5. Discussion of Findings

## 5.1 The Brown and Bostrom Contingency Model

SINCE STATISTICAL SUPPORT WAS FOUND FOR THE TWO CELLS in the contingency model in which a mechanistic design was modeled as the effective design (cells 1b and 2a), this validation study provides evidence that a mechanistic design choice is related to perceptions of effective EUC management under two sets of conditions:

1. when an organization has low EUC growth goals and is in an early stage of EUC implementation, and

2. when an organization has high EUC growth goals and is in an integrated stage of EUC implementation.

The lack of support for perceived effectiveness of an organic design choice in organizations with high EUC growth goals in an early EUC implementation phase (H1a) is surprising given the looser, more flexible designs for early implementation stages recommended by other researchers for EUC management [i.e., 1. 25] as well as for managing the introduction of new information technologies in general [i.e., 11, 29]. Several reasons for this unexpected finding can be proposed. First, one could argue that a mechanistic design has been the predominant design of the traditional IS subfunctions in the past. $^{4}$ A more organic (less controlled) approach could be viewed as incompatible and therefore ineffective. Second, a mechanistic design may be perceived by managers as the best way to “pull in the reins” under high task uncertainty—that is, both high uncertainty about how to manage EUC in an early stage and high environmental uncertainty due to increasing rates of change within the computer industry. Third, it could also be proposed that for these senior managers a more organic design may be perceived as “nonmanagement”; that is, the lack of formal guidelines and dispersed decision-making authority with high participation by end-users may be viewed as a lack of management, rather than as part of a proactive management strategy.

In contrast, the lack of support for H2b—an organic form under low EUC growth goals in an integrated implementation phase—seems to require a different explanation. Among the 28 cases classified as organic in this study, only three were assigned to cell 2b (see figure 3). Brown and Bostrom [7] also published no case example to support this cell of the model. The underlying rationale for this cell is that an organic structure in a later implementation phase would be consistent with a low growth goal because progress toward interdepartmental sharing of data and applications would be impeded. The combined evidence, however, suggests that the management approach modeled in cell 2b may not reflect practice.

This validation study also raises concerns about the relevance of applying Duncan's definitions for the organic and mechanistic structural forms to the domain of EUC management. Of the 105 cases in our sample, only 60 percent could be confidently classified as having either an organic or mechanistic design for the EUC management tasks. One could argue that just as firms are continually tinkering with their designs for the IS organization in general, they may be continually adjusting their EUC management designs. That is, the hybrid designs captured with cross-sectional data may not be desired states, but rather transitional states. Unfortunately, in this study we did not collect the data needed to determine whether the 42 hybrid designs were in fact transitional. Historical data as well as data on current EUC strategies and implementation plans would be required in order to address these questions. Therefore, conclusions about the validity of the organic and mechanistic forms in practice will have to await further research (see recommendations in section 6).

## 5.2. Implications for Other Evolutionary Models

Since the Brown and Bostrom [7] model synthesizes portions of three prior EUC management models (see section 1), what do the results of this validation study suggest about these prior evolutionary models?

First, the statistical support for the effectiveness of a mechanistic design for organizations in the high growth/integrated phase cell (1b) also provides support for portions of all three of the prior models. More specifically, the high-control designs for companies in a later EUC implementation stage in the normative models by Henderson and Treacy [25] and Alavi et al. [1] are supported. The high expansion/high control approach (controlled growth) for companies in a later EUC implementation stage in the descriptive model by Munro and Huff [31, 32] is also supported.

Second, the statistical support for the mechanistic design in the low growth/initial phase cell (2a) provides additional evidence that a controlled approach to EUC management at an early stage is a valid EUC management approach when a low EUC growth rate is the organization's goal. As discussed in section 1, the Munro and Huff model is the only prior evolutionary model to endorse such an approach: the model describes a "containment" approach that requires management actions characterized as low expansion–high control. Henderson and Treacy's model, which remains unvalidated, does not account for such an organizational goal. The authors specifically state that organizations frequently imposed a highly controlled and "centralized" operations perspective "too early" in the EUC growth curve [25, p. 13]. We think that this is because the model was based on the assumption that all organizations would choose a high EUC growth objective. The Alavi et al. model, which was conceived a few years later, comes closer to recognizing that some organizations do opt for an early, high control approach: in the latter part of their article, a highly controlled "monopolist" approach is depicted as one of two possible startup (stage 0) positions.

However, apparently based only on theoretical grounds, this stage is characterized as an “unstable state” which may lead to “a highly controlled, dissatisfied user community” [1, p. 44] unless followed by acceleration and marketing phases in which controls are absent.

The combined evidence of this validation study as well as the studies mentioned in section 1 by Saarinen et al. [40] and Brancheau and Amoroso [4] therefore strongly suggest that a mechanistic design approach can be effective in an early stage of EUC implementation if the firm does not seek a high EUC growth objective. In fact, if a highly controlled approach better matches the firm's management style overall, that firm could effectively maintain a mechanistic design throughout EUC implementation by initially adopting low EUC growth goals (i.e., start in cell 2a and move to cell 1b). However, it should be noted that support for a mechanistic approach in an early stage under high EUC growth objectives was not found.

## 6. Recommendations for Future Research

## 6.1. Revised Contingency Model

GIVEN THE DIFFERENT FINDINGS IN THIS VALIDATION STUDY for organizations in different EUC implementation states, a stage model that includes the firm's EUC growth strategy as a contingent variable in an early implementation stage has some validity. However, neither the findings for this study nor the findings reported in earlier published research on the EUC evolutionary models provide convincing support for the pursuit of an organizational design to match a low EUC growth strategy in a late EUC implementation phase (cell 2b) in practice. This suggests that the growth goal objective may only be an important contingent factor in an early stage, when organizations are concerned about the steepness of the S-shaped growth curve that has been associated with the adoption of end-user technologies at the individual level of analysis [e.g., 6].

Based on these findings, we propose that future research on the organization design of the EUC management infrastructure be directed at a revised contingency model of only three EUC implementation states, as shown in figure 4. This revised model has two paths: a high EUC growth path, and a low EUC growth path. However, unlike the original model [7], the recommended design for EUC management is the same when the firm enters the Integrated phase. This convergence occurs because the rate of EUC diffusion is no longer the key strategic concern in this later phase. Rather, the integration of end-user computing with organizational computing is the overriding strategic issue. $^{5}$ This change in strategic emphasis is also supported by recent conceptual studies on application maturity [26] and on the convergence of personal and organizational computing [30].

The organization design choices for the two early EUC implementation states in the original model have been retained in the revised model. The choice for the single Integrated state is the mechanistic form with one structural “adjustment”: the HORIZ indicator for the centralization construct needs to be revised to reflect shared IS and user department authority (a characteristic of the organic form in Table 2). Robey describes this type of structural “adjustment” to a mechanistic or organic form as a proactive design option [39, p. 109]. This recommendation is based on recent studies that have concluded that IS/user partnership relationships for managing EUC are required to integrate end-user and organizational computing [28, 30].

## 6.2. Research Design Recommendations

Three revisions to the research design employed in this validation study are also recommended. First, this study has pointed out problems with the organic and mechanistic forms as defined by Duncan for his ambidextrous model; that is, a large number of firms could not be confidently classified as one of these logical opposites. The organic and mechanistic forms are a set of three related constructs—centralization, formalization, and complexity—and over the past three decades, management researchers have operationalized these constructs differently $[22]$ . Further, Robey $[39]$ characterizes these two forms as conceptual tools for understanding organization design options (p. 110), but points out that hybrid designs can be not only transitional designs, but also effective permanent designs when several dominant issues compete at the same time for management solutions (p. 207). In order to further sort out some of these potential conceptual and measurement problems, it is recommended that research methods that allow for more open-ended inquiries (e.g., on-site, in-depth interviews) be employed.

Second, the problem of common method variance needs to be addressed. As discussed above, in this study we collected both the independent and dependent variable responses from a single target respondent in each organization. One improvement would be to collect dependent variable measures from a full range of stakeholder groups, including user department managers, end-user developers, and other IS managers. Due to the lack of support for the organic design choice in this validation study, it will be particularly useful to investigate if higher effectiveness ratings are reported by participants organizationally closer to end-user developers under organic designs in an early stage for firms on a high EUC growth path.

Third, the validity of the two paths from an initial to an integrated phase in figure 4 needs to be investigated with longitudinal data. The fact that many organizations today are well into an integrated phase should ensure a large sample of EUC implementation experiences. In-person methods of data collection may be required in order to capture data that will help us better understand how an organization effectively transitions to the later EUC implementation phase on the high EUC growth path in particular, since this path requires an “ambidextrous” firm. Daft characterizes such a transition as a “difficult” challenge in practice [15, p. 273].

Our literature review revealed few validation studies for the conceptual stage models for EUC management published in the latter half of the 1980s. This article is offered as but one contribution toward eliminating this current gap in the published literature. While many of today's largest organizations may be well into a late EUC implementation phase, the results of validation studies such as this one can lead to EUC management guidelines for other U.S. firms that are in an earlier stage of EUC implementation, as well as firms in other countries that are reportedly several years behind the typical U.S. firm in EUC implementation. It is also expected that these findings will improve our understanding of the contingent options for organization designs to manage the introduction of EUC to empowered teams on the factory floor, as well as new information technologies of the 1990s in general.
