# A Mathematical Model of Performance of Computer-Mediated Groups during Idea Generation

JOSEPH S. VALACICH AND ALAN R. DENNIS

JOSEPH S. VALACICH is an Assistant Professor of Decision and Information Systems at Indiana University, Bloomington. His research interests include technology diffusion in organizations and group decision behavior and he has recent publications in Academy of Management Journal, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, Management Science, Journal of Applied Psychology, Communication Research, Communications of the ACM, and Small Group Research.

ALAN R. DENNIS is an Assistant Professor of Management Information Systems in the Terry College of Business at the University of Georgia. He received his Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Arizona. He has published articles in a variety of journals, including Management Science, Academy of Management Journal, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, Journal of Applied Psychology, MIS Quarterly, Communications of the ACM and IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics. His current research focuses on the use of information technology to improve group work.

ABSTRACT: As the use of computer-mediated systems for group work grows, the need for a better understanding of the impact of such systems becomes more important. Studies of non-computer-mediated group work have long been guided by mathematical models of group interaction and performance, but recent research suggests that these models do not fit the unique capabilities of computer-mediated systems. This article builds on previous group research to create a mathematical model of computer-mediated group performance which is then tested using data from two prior studies. This testing enabled us to conclude that models of computer-mediated groups run counter to the many guiding models of non-computer-mediated groups. We therefore discuss the implications of these differences in relation to users of this technology and to future developmental and empirical research.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: group interaction modeling, group process gains, group process losses, group support systems, idea generation.

Acknowledgments: We would like to thank Jay Nunamaker of the University of Arizona for providing the computer software and facilities used in this research. We would also like to thank Bill Perkins, C.A.P. Smith, and the anonymous reviewers for particularly helpful comments on earlier drafts.

## Introduction

IN 1953, OSBORN [15] PUBLISHED Applied Imagination, in which he described group brainstorming and presented persuasive examples of how groups using it could generate more ideas than the same number of individuals working separately (i.e., "nominal" groups [see 21]). The fundamental question in the comparison of nominal groups to verbally interacting groups is whether communication among members improves or impairs performance. Communication among group members can introduce factors that increase performance (called process gains) and factors that inhibit performance (called process losses) [10, 20]. When members do not communicate, as in nominal groups, they experience no process gains or losses. Thus, if groups that communicate outperform nominal groups that do not, then the process gains from that communication must outweigh process losses. The reverse is also true: if nominal groups outperform communicating groups, then process losses from communication are greater than process gains.

Taylor, Berry, and Block [21] presented the first test of Osborn's claims that communication produced more gains than losses. They found the opposite: nominal groups generated significantly more ideas than verbally brainstorming groups. Since then, more than 80 studies have made this or some similar comparison. Overall, the findings from this research have been dramatic: nominal groups usually generate many more ideas than communicating groups and these differences increase with increased group size (for reviews of this research, see [7, 10, 11, 13, 25]). In essence, the nominal group method of generating ideas has consistently been found to be the most effective idea generation method [12].

Thus, for brainstorming groups, communication must introduce significant process losses. Many sources of process losses have been proposed (see $[14]$ for a summary), but two have received much attention. The first is evaluation apprehension, the fear of criticism or ridicule of one's ideas, which may cause members to withhold ideas in group settings $[7, 11]$ . The second, production blocking, stems from the fact that only one member can speak at one time: when one person is speaking, all others are blocked from contributing ideas $[7, 11]$ .

Steiner [20] proposed that process losses increase with increased group size at an increasing rate. Production blocking, for example, should increase directly with group size: in larger groups, more people are blocked when one person speaks. This contention, that increased size brings increasingly greater process losses, has led many researchers to conclude that the optimal group size is often quite small, typically five members or less [16].

Over the past few years, researchers have suggested that group work may be enhanced by the use of technology (e.g., [6]). One technology, called electronic brainstorming (EBS), has been the focus of several empirical investigations (see [14]). With EBS, group members work at computer workstations and exchange ideas via computer-mediated communication rather than verbally. EBS enables all members, regardless of the group size, to make contributions at any time (i.e., without blocking) and to make comments without identification of authorship (i.e., anonymously).

Experimental investigations of EBS have found groups brainstorming electronically to generate more ideas than groups brainstorming verbally, particularly for larger groups (e.g., [8]). Further, EBS groups have been found to generate more ideas than nominal groups, especially when the group size is large [4, 22]. This pattern of process gains and losses appears fundamentally different from that of verbal brainstorming.

The purpose of this article is to build and test a mathematical model of the process gains, process losses, and overall performance of EBS groups. From prior research, we suspect that traditional models of group performance developed for verbally interacting groups that hold that process losses increase rapidly with group size (e.g., [2, 20]) may not fit EBS. In this article, we first develop and test a model for individual performance, and then develop and test a group model. We conclude with a discussion of the implications for research and practice.

## A Mathematical Model of Computer-Mediated Idea Generation

## Previous Research

MANY MODELS OF GROUP PERFORMANCE HAVE BEEN PROPOSED for verbally communicating groups. Most models have focused on group size as the key independent variable driving the model. Although many factors potentially affect group performance, group size is arguably one of the more important factors because the skills and resources available to a group depend upon the number and characteristics of its members. Group size is also a factor that clearly lends itself to mathematical modeling.

Early work by Steiner [19] outlined several models of group productivity that theoretically predicted groups' potential or ideal productivity assuming no process losses. Steiner argued that if we pool the contributions of group members for a "complementary" additive task such as idea generation, their total potential contribution is not the simple sum of all individual members' contributions, because some contributions are redundant [19, p. 282]. Thus, in building group models, one must consider the incremental or marginal value added by each member, which gradually decreases with group size in a curvilinear function [20]. Steiner [19] offered several different theoretical models for ideal performance without process losses, but provided no empirical evidence to support them.

A second key milestone in the development of mathematical models was that of Bray, Kerr, and Atkin [2], who built on Steiner's work [19, 20] to develop and empirically test a mathematical model of group performance. They provided some support for Steiner's marginal value approach, but found significant process losses as group size increased to three members, particularly for more complex problems. Based on a series of modeling trials, they concluded that as the size of the group increases, the number of members who actually work on the task (the "functional" group size) does not increase, and may actually decrease. For example, in modeling the actual performance of ten-person groups, they concluded that the most accurate models were those using Steiner's models of ideal performance for two-person groups; that is, process losses were such that the mean marginal contributions of the third through tenth members were close to zero.

Given these findings of substantial process losses and little marginal benefits of adding more than two or three group members, interest in the development of mathematical models for idea generation waned. Attention shifted to the development and testing of general models of decision making (e.g., [9, 17, 26]). The need for such mathematical models has been underscored by past research which has found errors in conclusions of studies when mathematical models have not been used (see [9, 26]).

When building a mathematical model of group idea generation, we must return to our discussion of process gains and losses. Group performance is a function of the expected performance of the group working as individuals (i.e., as a nominal group) plus process gains minus process losses. Nominal groups experience no gains or losses, so nominal group performance has been used as a measure of individual performance of group members. Most prior research has measured the combined effects of process gains and losses (although both elements exist simultaneously, but separately) as deviations from nominal group performance [10]. Here we model the effects of process gains and losses separately, but again test their combined effects. We begin by developing and testing a model for individual performance, and then use this model to develop and test a model for group performance. The data used for the testing of these models was taken from prior studies [5, 22] (see the appendix). The analysis reported here is a new and distinct analysis of these data.

## A Model of Individual Idea Generation Performance

## Model Development

If we pool the ideas of a group of individuals working separately, the total number of ideas is not the simple sum of all ideas because some ideas are redundant. Building on Steiner's approach [19], as group size increases from $x - 1$ to $x$ members, the mean number of ideas generated is the mean from the group of size $x - 1$ , plus the mean number of ideas generated per person $(p)$ , minus the mean number of redundant ideas $(r)$ . In mathematical notation, the mean number of ideas of a nominal group of size $x$ (Nominal $(x)$ ) is:

$$
\text { Nominal } (x) = \text { Nominal } (x - 1) + p - r.
$$

The mean number of redundant ideas, r, is some function of the size of the task's idea space and the number of ideas already generated [19]. For simplicity, let us assume that r can be approximated by a linear function of the number of ideas currently generated. In mathematical terms, the mean number of redundant ideas of a group of size $x(r(x))$ is:

$$
r (x) = c 1 * \text { Nominal } (x - 1) + c 2,
$$

where c1 and c2 are constants. $r(x)$ must have a value between zero (no ideas are redundant) and p, the mean number of ideas generated per person (in the case where all additional ideas are redundant). Clearly, $r(x)$ must equal p at the point where the solution space is exhausted. The value of x at which this point is reached depends upon the nature of the task and the participants.

Conceptually, we can think of c1 as the mean probability of overlap in the ideas generated by the individuals, and c2 as the mean size of the common pool of ideas that all participants produce. The values of both c1 and c2 depend upon the specific task and the participants, but there are some limits to their values. From a conceptual perspective, c2 must be greater than or equal to zero, as the mean size of the common pool of ideas cannot be less than zero. The upper bound for $r(x)$ is p, as the number of redundant ideas cannot exceed p. Therefore, the upper bound on c2 is $p-c1*Nominal(x-1)$ , where x is the point at which the bounds of the solution space have been reached. Likewise, c1 must be greater than or equal to zero, as the mean overlap of ideas cannot be less than zero. The upper bound on c1 is also determined by p; the upper bound on c1 is $(p-c2)/\text{Nominal}(x-1)$ , where x is the point at which the bounds of the solution space have been reached.

If we place this function $r(x)$ into the original equation for the mean number of ideas, we get:

$$
\text { Nominal } (x) = \text { Nominal } (x - 1) + p - (c 1 * \text { Nominal } (x - 1) + c 2).
$$

Collecting the terms, we have the following general form for the mean idea generation performance of nominal groups of individuals working without process gains or losses:

$$
\operatorname{Nominal} (x) = a + b * \operatorname{Nominal} (x - 1),\tag{1}
$$

where $a = p - c2$ and $b = 1 - c1$ .

If this model is appropriate, a will be greater than or equal to zero, because the upper bound on c2 is less than p. However, a cannot exceed p, because c2 cannot be a negative number. Likewise, b must be less than or equal to 1, because c1 cannot be a negative number. Furthermore, b cannot be less than zero because adding an additional participant cannot decrease the total number of ideas; at worst it simply will not increase it. Therefore, $a \geq 0$ and $0 \leq b \leq 1$ .

## Model Evaluation

The model of equation 1 was fit to our nominal group data [22] using linear regression. (See the appendix for a description of the procedures by which these data were collected.) This pool had data on 43 individuals which were formed into as many nominal groups of each size (ranging from 1 to 43 members) as the data permitted. A simple way to estimate the expected performance of a nominal group of size N is to take the pool of M individuals working alone, randomly assign them into as many groups of size N as the pool allows, and compute the performance level of each of these pseudogroups by eliminating redundancies. This estimate can be improved by repeatedly sampling the pool, in the limit estimating nominal group performance as the mean of every possible group of size N that can be formed from a pool of M individuals (see [9, 22, 26]). Within a single experiment, the same pool of M individuals is used repeatedly in estimating nominal group performance for different values of N. Thus, we enumerated all possible combinations of N (1–43) in order to most closely estimate the nominal group performance for each group size with the given data set (for detailed descriptions of this procedure, see [9, 22, 26]).

The resulting model (Nominal(x) = 2.758 + 0.898 \* Nominal(x-1)) had an $R^2$ of 0.997, indicating a good fit. The fitted value for $a$ , 2.758, was, as hypothesized, greater than zero. The fitted value for $b$ , 0.898, was, as hypothesized, between zero and one. Thus, these data appear to fit this general model for individual performance.

## A Model of Group Idea Generation Performance

The role of a group member using EBS has many similarities to that of an individual working alone, but also many differences. The roles are similar if one imagines both as just typing ideas into a computer terminal, as both, regardless of group size, can type in ideas or comments at will, can review earlier input, or can simply sit and think (or, of course, simply sit). They are different in that the individual working alone has only his or her own earlier ideas to review, while the EBS participant has access to the ideas generated by other group members. Thus, group performance is a function of individual performance minus process losses plus process gains. The model in equation (1) presents individual performance. Process gains and losses remain to be modeled.

## Process Losses

In general, process losses in verbally interacting groups increase with group size, either exponentially [20] or linearly [1]. In either case, the number of ideas contributed per person decreases sharply as group size increases. Several dozen process losses have been identified (see [14]), but two have received the most attention: evaluation apprehension and production blocking. Evaluation apprehension may cause members to withhold ideas because they fear the ideas they suggest may be criticized or ridiculed by other members and that this criticism or ridicule will reflect on them personally [7, 11]. Production blocking occurs when only one member can communicate at once, and can have at least three effects [7, 11]. First, members who cannot contribute their ideas as they think of them may forget or suppress them because later they seem less relevant or original. Second, when waiting to contribute an idea, members may focus on remembering it, rather than thinking of new ones. Third, members must constantly listen to others or miss contributions; they cannot easily pause to think about them or to generate new ideas.

In contrast, EBS provides anonymity (the source of ideas is unknown), parallelism (any group member can contribute ideas at any time), and a group memory (all ideas are recorded so members can refer to them later). Prior research has found evidence that per-person contribution rates remain relatively constant as group size increases within EBS [5, 23]. Thus, the primary process losses associated with verbal group idea generation should be reduced and appear to be very much the same regardless of the group size. EBS may also introduce new process losses (e.g., media speed and information overload), yet, within some bounds, interaction should remain the same regardless of group size. Thus, our formulation for process losses in a computer-mediated environment such as EBS is that process losses are constant regardless of the group size: $Losses(x) = c$ . This formulation varies significantly from that proposed by prior researchers for verbal brainstorming. As noted earlier, models for verbal brainstorming suggest that process losses increase sharply with group size.

## Process Gains

One of the most important process gains is the synergy that develops when group members build on the ideas of others to create new ideas. Synergy increases with information exchange and is a function of the group organization. Synergy should be enhanced if all members work as a single group $[15, 16, 20]$ . Synergy is some function of the number of ideas the group has generated, which in turn is influenced by the number of group members. Thus, different group organizations and sizes will lead to different levels of synergy; for a given number of participants, the larger the size of the interacting group, the greater the synergy. Process gains from a group memory can also arise from redundancy avoidance. In nominal groups, members can spend much time and energy generating ideas that other members have already produced, but as they cannot communicate, they are unaware that these ideas are redundant. With EBS, all members can view ideas generated by others so redundancy can be avoided. For simplicity, let us assume that process gains can be approximated as a multiplier of expected individual performance. Thus, one formulation for process gains in a group of size x is $\text{Gains}(x) = g * \text{Nominal}(x)$ , where $g \geq 0$ .

## Model Development

Putting these three components together, group performance is a function of individual performance minus process losses plus process gains. In mathematical terms, the mean number of ideas for an EBS group of size x (Group(x)) is:

$$
\operatorname{Group} (x) = \operatorname{Nominal} (x) - \operatorname{Losses} (x) + \operatorname{Gains} (x).
$$

Substituting the above functions for process gains and losses, we get:

$$
\operatorname{Group} (x) = \operatorname{Nominal} (x) - c + g * \operatorname{Nominal} (x).
$$

Rearranging the terms, we get:

$$
\operatorname{Group} (x) = (1 + g) ^ {*} \text { Nominal } (x) - c,\tag{2}
$$

where $g$ and $c$ are constants, $c$ and $g \geq 0$ .

Within the bounds of this investigation, group performance is modeled as a linear function. However, at some point, group performance may in fact be nonlinear (e.g., as the number of ideas generated reaches the upper bound of the solution space). During the development of this model, we explored several nonlinear models, none of which fit the data set as well as the linear model (highest $R^{2}$ among the nonlinear models was 0.79). Thus, we have chosen the linear representation because of its inherent simplicity and its suitability to model this phenomenon within the range of groups, sizes, and task studied here.

## Model Evaluation

The model of equation (2) was fit to the data from our EBS groups using linear regression. (See the appendix for a description of the procedures by which these data were collected.) The resulting model had an $R^{2}$ of 0.947, indicating a good fit (see Table 1). The fitted value for c (process losses), 0.597, was, as hypothesized, greater than zero. The fitted value for g (process gains), 0.151, was, as hypothesized, greater than zero. Further, the g coefficient (0.151) is significantly greater than zero ( $t(15) = 2.16$ , p < 0.025), indicating that groups experienced process gains resulting in an average of 15.1 percent more ideas than the same number of members working as nominal groups (minus process losses estimated to be 0.597 ideas).

## Discussion

OUR MODEL OF GROUP IDEA GENERATION PERFORMANCE attempts to model the effects of process gains and losses. This model examines the combined effects of gains and losses; we have no direct measures of the individual components of process gains and process losses. Based on the fit of the models to the experimental data, we conclude that these theoretical models seem appropriate for EBS-supported group idea generation. The $R^{2}$ 's for the models (nominal groups = 0.997, EBS groups = 0.947) indicate that these models explain a very significant proportion of the variation in performance of these groups; moreover, the resulting coefficients fit our theoretical model (e.g., negative and constant process losses; process gains as a function of the number of ideas).

Thus, our primary explanation for the superiority of EBS groups over nominal groups in the prior EBS/nominal group research $[22]$ was the holding constant of process losses such as production blocking and evaluation apprehension. In particular, for larger EBS groups, process gains exceeded process losses since they outperformed the nominal groups (which had no losses or gains). For smaller groups, the fixed process losses equalled (or exceeded) the process gains, resulting in performance that was no better and no worse than if the individuals had worked separately.

The practical implication of this is clear. It has long been argued that the use of EBS and other group support technologies introduces an “overhead” cost that makes them less useful for small groups. This model provides some evidence of this overhead in the form of fixed process losses incurred by all groups, large or small. Given the relative size of these fixed process losses (about 0.60 ideas) to the modest process gains for the groups in this study (15 percent), it is no wonder that some small groups feel that the potential gains do not outweigh the fixed overhead cost.

The task we used (identifying who would be affected by a policy requiring all undergraduates to have access to a microcomputer) had a rather small solution space (47 ideas), and almost without exception every subject mentioned the same two groups of people (students and faculty) among their ideas. Our nominal group model suggests a mean marginal contribution of 2.76 ideas per person with a mean overlap of about 10 percent. In mathematical terms, we can say that in equation (1), $a(2.76)=p(4.75)-c2(2)$ , while $b(0.898)=1-c1(0.102)$ . That is, on average, each person after the first brought a marginal contribution of 2.76 ideas to the task, of which only 10 percent overlapped with other members (after discounting the original two overlapping ideas common to all subjects). Had this percentage overlap been higher, we might have seen more gains due to redundancy avoidance in the EBS groups.

Our group model suggests a mean gain of 15 percent of the total ideas minus a loss of about 0.60 ideas. Doing a simple breakeven analysis, we see that the model predicts EBS groups to generate more ideas than nominal groups for groups as small as two members. However, the mean difference is quite small (0.45 ideas). It is not until group size increases to four members that EBS groups are predicted to generate one idea more than nominal groups (on average). By eight members, the mean difference is about two ideas, which may be more noticeable. While this may not sound substantial, one must remember that the solution space is small and that the mean number of ideas generated by an eight-person group is small (16.6 versus 18.5). If we had used a task with a larger solution space, we suspect that gains would have been more noticeable.

We built a similar group model using data from another experiment of six- and twelve-member groups [4] using two tasks (attracting tourists to Tucson, and improving campus security) with much larger solution spaces (more than 800 ideas). Here the group model was estimated as $\text{Group}(x) = 1.85 * \text{Nominal}(x) - 65$ with an $R^2$ of 0.74. Although the model did not fit these data as well, the $R^2$ still indicates a good fit. We again see a similar pattern of multiplicative gains (85 percent of nominal performance), statistically significantly greater than zero ( $t(28) = 3.98, p = 0.001$ ) and fixed losses (65 ideas, significantly less than zero [ $t(28) = 3.26, p = 0.003$ ]). For those groups (same subject pool) and tasks, we see a greater initial overhead, but substantially more gains. The breakeven point is around eight to nine members, with EBS groups producing about 30 more ideas by size 12.

The specific models of group performance developed here apply only to the groups, tasks, and technology used in this research. The amounts of process loss and gain will depend upon the characteristics of the group and task. For example, if a problem is a highly political one (where apprehension to contribute might be high), we would expect the gains from anonymity to be greater and thus the noticeable breakeven point for the use of EBS to be lower (i.e., fewer group members) than if the task were routine.

The breakeven point would also depend upon the aggregate skills of the group members in relation to the task (i.e., the heterogeneity of the group skills in relation to the task—see $[20, pp. 105–130]$ ). A more heterogeneous group, whose members have diverse, nonoverlapping domain skills and task knowledge, would likely experience more process gains than a homogeneous group, whose members have similar skills, knowledge, and training in relation to a task. The student groups used in this research were relatively homogeneous, having been drawn from a rather narrow pool (i.e., junior and senior business majors). We would expect the marginal benefits from adding additional group members to be higher in situations where heterogeneity of skills would be beneficial.

In a study designed to test this speculation, Valacich and colleagues $[24]$ investigated the effects of group size (five and ten members) and group heterogeneity in regard to task demands. Group heterogeneity was manipulated by varying the amount of task-relevant information given to each group member; heterogeneous groups were induced by distributing unique task-relevant information among group members; homogeneous groups were induced by providing all task information to all members. The group size and heterogeneity variables interacted and resulted in greater performance gains for increased group size within heterogeneous groups. Consistent with the arguments of Steiner and past group research, the average contribution per group member decreased with group size. However, the contribution per member diminished at a faster rate within homogeneous groups. In other words, the marginal contribution of each additional member was greater in the heterogeneous groups than in the homogeneous groups. This also suggests that the optimal group size for heterogeneous groups may be larger than that for homogeneous groups.

Thus, computer-mediation may be appropriate for groups of varying sizes, as aspects of the task and group composition will be paramount to determining system effects on group process and performance. Nonetheless, while the coefficients for process gains and process losses will shift, the basic model of fixed process losses and process gains that increase with group size should still hold within some bounds.

At some point, however, process gains must cease, as the total solution space will be reached. And as the group size approaches the limits on the solution space, the gains will likely not remain a constant 15 percent. Process losses, too, cannot remain constant for all group sizes because at some point the volume of information produced by the group must induce additional losses due to information overload.

This study has implications for users, researchers, and developers of this type of group technology. For users of computer-mediated idea generation systems, this study suggests using larger groups whose members provide sufficient marginal benefits to overcome the initial fixed overhead losses of using this technology. Our results indicate that EBS can hold process losses constant within some bounds of group size. Here we studied groups of up to 18 members; other studies suggest that groups as large as 31 members remain effective [14].

For researchers, this model of computer-mediated idea generation provides a foundation in which additional contingencies can be examined (e.g., differences in groups, tasks, motivation, technologies). Group research is a very expensive and time-consuming endeavor that is often hampered by having too little data $[3]$ . Thus, it is virtually impossible to collect data on all interesting possibilities. For example, Stasser $[18]$ has used model representations and simulations to gain a substantial understanding of complex group processes and has found the simulations to be a useful supplement to empirical investigations. In a recent analysis $[18]$ , he simulated the behavior of 1,600 four-person and 1,600 eight-person groups (for a total 19,200 group members). He argued that an empirical study would necessarily use a much less complex research design (due to practical time and subject availability limitations), focusing on a very restricted set of conditions. The development of models of group performance can thus be used “to connect islands of existing results and to [much more easily] explore uncharted territories . . . [and] to guide future research by identifying configurations of factors that are likely to yield interesting and useful results” $[18, p. 158]$ .

For designers of computer-mediated group work environments, the results of our model testing suggest that the key components of the EBS technique (parallel communication, group memory, and anonymity; see $[14]$ ) may be essential to improving group idea generation performance. Our model provided a test of the combined effects of process gains and losses. There is a need for empirical researchers to further dissect these components to better understand the extent to which each influences group processing and performance.

Developmental researchers are encouraged to design systems that can vary aspects of the group environment to support such investigation. Another opportunity is to further dissect aspects of both process gains and losses. In this research we modeled process gains and losses separately but tested them in the aggregate. There is a need to identify all process losses operating within the environment in order to best design solutions for a wide variety of groups, tasks, and situations.

In conclusion, our results show that the relationship between process losses and group size is significantly different in computer-mediated contexts from that for verbally interacting groups. We believe that these results suggest that group idea generation in computer-mediated environments warrants some new conclusions $[22]$ . First, the ideal group organization for idea generation tasks may not be the nominal group (individuals working separately), but an interacting group where all members communicate through computer mediation. Second, the optimal group size for such tasks in computer-mediated environments may not be a small number such as five, but may be as large as the task warrants. Indeed, the perceived ability of this technology to enable large interacting groups to tackle complex tasks has been one motivation for the use of these technologies by organizations $[14]$ .

## Appendix: Data sets used to test the theoretical models

THIS STUDY PRESENTS A COMBINATION AND NEW ANALYSIS of data from two previous experiments that evaluated different research questions $[5, 23]$ . The data for these experiments were collected at the same time using the same subject pool. Dennis et al. $[5]$ examined groups of three, nine, and eighteen members communicating anonymously using the electronic brainstorming (EBS) system. Their central finding was that overall number of ideas generated by a group increased steadily with group size up to the largest groups. Valacich et al. $[23]$ examined the idea generation performance of groups using EBS to an equivalent number of individuals working alone whose ideas were later pooled (i.e., nominal groups). The results of this comparison found nine- and eighteen-member EBS groups to outperform equivalent nominal groups; no performance differences were found between three-member EBS and nominal groups.

## Subjects

Upper division business students satisfying a course requirement for experimental participation served as subjects. Subjects were drawn from large sections of core business classes and were randomly assigned into the experimental conditions. One hundred and ninety-nine subjects consisting of seven groups of size three, five groups of size nine, five groups of size eighteen, and forty-three individuals were studied.

## Task

Subjects were given a one-page typed sheet describing in some detail a (fictitious) proposed policy requiring all business students to own or have access to a personal microcomputer. They were asked to identify all individuals, groups, and organizations that would be affected by such a policy, and to discuss how each would be affected. The task was chosen for its relevance to subjects' background knowledge, and for its similarity to tasks undertaken by groups in field studies and to tasks used in earlier idea generation studies.

## Setting

The experiment was conducted in the University of Arizona electronic meeting facility, a large two-tiered workroom containing 24 individual workstations connected via a local area network (see [15] for a description). The network allows formation of groups of any size up to 24. Individuals in nominal groups used a version of EBS designed for single users in which no file sharing occurred.

## Procedure

Subjects reported to the room in groups of between 5 and 20 (depending upon subject availability and experimental treatment), were assigned their workstations, watched a ten-minute presentation on the operation of the EBS software, and were allowed to practice and ask questions about its operation. (In conducting these experiments, we purposely overbooked subjects to guard against no-shows; excess subjects were placed in the nominal treatment.) The experimenter then read aloud the experimental instructions while the subjects followed in their own copies. The instructions reminded them that there were many possible ideas and urged them to identify and discuss as many as possible. They were told that the results would eventually be reviewed by a panel of experts and assigned an overall score. After working on the task for 30 minutes, subjects completed a brief postexperiment questionnaire and were debriefed and released.

## Coding of Transcripts

Each output file was coded independently by two raters who identified and counted the number of different stakeholders listed in each file. A total of 47 distinct stakeholders were identified by merging all outputs, and grouping specific named individuals into labeled groups (e.g., named faculty members were coded collectively as “business school faculty”). There was initial agreement on 97 percent of these codings, indicating adequate interrater reliability. The few remaining differences were resolved by the authors.
