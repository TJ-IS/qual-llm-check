# Business Computing Research Journals: A Normalized Citation Analysis

CLYDE W. HOLSSAPPLE, LINDA ELLIS JOHNSON, HERMAN MANAKYAN, AND JOHN TANNER

CLYDE W. His books include Foundations of Decision Support Systems, Micro Database Management, Business Expert Systems, and The Information Jungle. His main areas of interest are investments, signaling theory, market efficiency, and business education.

ABSTRACT: Several studies have been conducted that rank the relative importance of journals publishing MIS research. Yet the study of business computing systems has grown to encompass not only management information systems, but other business computing systems as well (e.g., decision support systems and business expert systems). In a recent study, journals publishing business computing system research were ranked using a citation analysis methodology. This article extends that study by taking into account the number of years each journal has been published. This normalized ranking scheme is then compared with the original ranking.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: business computing research; citation analysis; MIS journal ranking.

RECENTLY, AN ENTIRE ISSUE OF The Communications of the ACM was devoted to business computing [3]. Its contents reflect the great expanse of this field, relating the discipline of computer science with various business-oriented disciplines. Its papers deal with the management and evaluation of information technology, organizational computing issues, business expert systems, decision support systems, and computer economics. These are symptomatic of the field's interdisciplinary diversity, which, in turn, makes the identification and assessment of relevant research journals a nontrivial activity.

Publication of research articles and conduct of scholarly discourse in the business computing system (BCS) field has increased notably in the past two decades. Commensurately, the number of journals that publish BCS articles has grown to encompass newer journals that focus exclusively on BCS topics, as well as journals that occasionally publish BCS articles $[12]$ . Similar in motivation to studies in business disciplines such as accounting $[2]$ , finance $[15]$ , and management $[8]$ , several studies have been undertaken in the BCS field to evaluate the relative importance of journals serving it $[7, 9, 11, 13, 16, 17, 18, 20, 22]$ . However, none of these studies have explicitly considered the relative longevity of journals in calculating ranks.

Here we present a normalized ranking of journals that publish articles relevant to BCS researchers. This is an extension of a previously reported study $[11]$ that ranked over 1,000 journals based on nearly 15,000 citations to journal articles. Here, the rankings are normalized to reflect the proposition that journals that have been published over a longer period have a greater opportunity to be cited. Relative to other studies referenced above, our ranking not only has a very large citation base, but is drawn from a fairly recent time frame. Thus, the result is an up-to-date objective ranking that does not penalize journals of more recent vintage.

Examining the relative importance of journals publishing BCS-related research and discourse is largely motivated by the performance evaluation system for faculty in academia, including criteria for tenure and promotion. Other important pragmatic motives include the training of doctoral students with respect to publishing outlets as well as reading lists necessary to stay abreast of current research, moving toward a consensus on the “top” journals, establishing tiers of journals for use in related research topics, informing college deans and administrators of the diversity of forums for publishing BCS-related articles, and assisting researchers in targeting manuscripts to appropriate forums. Philosophical motives for examining research and discourse have primarily revolved around studying the evolution and maturation of the field of BCS [1, 4, 5, 10, 12].

## Related Research

IN ASSESSING THE RELATIVE IMPORTANCE OF JOURNALS to the BCS field, both subjective and objective methodologies have been utilized. Subjective studies have mailed surveys to deans [7], department heads [9], practitioners [21], and/or academics [13] to obtain journal submission preferences and compile rankings of journals based on respondents' perceptions. Researchers employing more objective methodology have relied on various forms of citation analyses for tabulating citations from a set of base journals or a set of selected articles over some time period. However, when creating a ranking, none of these have explicitly taken into account the length of time a journal has been published.

Consider, for example, two journals that have been cited the same number of times. Existing studies use a methodology that gives them the same rank. But what if one of the two has been published twice as long, giving its articles longer exposure and more opportunity to be cited? Clearly, its impact has been less concentrated than that of the former journal. An alternative methodology that normalizes citations based on journal longevity would yield rankings that take such impacts into account. Studies of journals in other business fields have employed normalized ranking schemes. For example, Mabry and Sharplin [15] analyzed citations from four finance journals by defining and calculating three measures—simple effectiveness, article effectiveness, and impact efficiency. Simple effectiveness is a ranking derived by sorting journals by the total number of citations received from a set of base journals. As such, it is the approach used in existing BCS studies. Article effectiveness is “a measure that adjusts for journal size and frequency of issue, by dividing total citations by average number of articles published annually in each journal.” Impact efficiency is a measure derived by “dividing total citations by the number of words published in a typical year and multiplying the result by 10,000” [15].

## Methodology

AS OUR STARTING POINT FOR CREATING a normalized ranking of journals relevant to BCS research, we elect to use data from the most extensive BCS citation analysis reported to date. This analysis also covers a relatively recent time period of citations from 1987 through 1991. In this study, 1,366 different journals were ranked based on the number of total citations received from every reference list in each article appearing in a set of base journals during a five-year period [11]. These base journals, in alphabetical order, were Communications of the ACM, Decision Support Systems, Information and Management, Journal of Management Information Systems, and MIS Quarterly.

The base journals were chosen for study by taking the union of the top two journals from each of three relatively recent ranking schemes. Two of these employed subjective methodologies. Of these, one identified MIS Quarterly and Communications of the ACM as the top two journals [13], and the other identified Journal of Management Information Systems and MIS Quarterly as the top two journals [7]. The third, and most recent, of the ranking schemes used an objective methodology that yielded Decision Support Systems and Information and Management [19]. This number of base journals is consistent with the number used in other disciplines' citation studies (e.g., [15]). The resultant set of five journals appears to cover the BCS spectrum fairly well. In their collective pages we find research that ranges from theoretical to analytical to empirical, from technical to behavioral in orientation, from conceptual commentary to applications. For the period studied, a total of 14,784 journal article citations were tabulated from the base set.

To account for the impact of longevity, it is necessary to normalize the number of citations to each journal and to revise the ranking accordingly. Normalization is accomplished by dividing the raw number of citations by a standard related to the longevity of each journal. We considered several possible options for this denominator. First, we examined the desirability of applying the denominators defined by Mabry and Sharplin [15]. Neither turns out to be workable.

To calculate “article effectiveness,” the number of articles in each journal would need to be tabulated. A major problem in using this measure arises in assessing what is and is not an article. For instance, how many articles appear in an issue of Computerworld, Datamation, or Communications of the ACM? Should reviews, introductions, postscripts, annotated bibliographies, editorials, news items, or “Notes” be counted as articles? Should an article in Management Science or Journal of Applied Psychology whose subject matter is not a BCS topic be counted, keeping in mind that it may or may not be cited by articles in base journals? In order to calculate “impact efficiency,” it is necessary to count the number of words published in each journal per year. However, while this measure is available in the Social Sciences Citation Index (SSCI) for other disciplines, similar data for the gamut of BCS-related journals are not published. Moreover, the result of such a calculation does not appear to account for the impact of longevity.

Second, we considered using the number of volumes as the denominator. But, because some journals have had multiple volumes in some years (e.g., Artificial Intelligence), the result would be nonuniform across journals. Third, we considered using the number of issues as the denominator. Here, too, there are problems. How are issues of different sizes to be compared? Should all issues of a journal be counted? For journals such as MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, and Information and Management, the answer is clearly yes. However, for journals such as Management Science, Harvard Business Review, and Communications of the ACM, the answer is not clear-cut. There have been many issues of these journals that have had no BCS articles, or articles that would seem to be particularly relevant to the business computing field. There have also been many issues with only one or two BCS-related articles. Attempting objectively to reduce the total number of issues in such journals to a BCS-equivalent number of issues is clearly problematic.

Most important, the number of issues does not uniformly account for the impact of longevity. Suppose two journals have the same number of citations and have had the same number of issues, say 48. The “simple effectiveness” measure used by existing BCS studies would give them the same rank, as would a measure that has number of issues in the denominator. But if one of the two had achieved its citation count in four years (i.e., twelve issues per year) while the second took twelve years (i.e., four issues per year), its impact is clearly more potent and concentrated. Accordingly, we would expect it to have a higher normalized rank, especially given the lag time between an article appearing in print, being read, being cited in a new manuscript, review/revision of the manuscript, and its ultimate publication in a base journal.

In the end, we opted to use the years of publication through 1990 as a basis for normalization. This avoids the foregoing problems of article counting ambiguity, lack of data, nonuniform treatment, and ignoring age disparities. By normalizing based on years, we directly account for differences in the life of a pool of articles from which citations may be drawn. We also indirectly and roughly account for the size of the body of literature a journal represents without getting into the problematic tasks of counting articles or words. The resultant measure is a journal's citation rate, which characterizes the potency or concentration of the journal's impact on the BCS literature.

We use relative citation rates, rather than raw citation totals, to produce a ranking that is sensitive to both the frequency of and opportunity for citations. Unlike the “simple efficiency” rankings of the past, we get a ranking less likely to overstate relative impacts of older journals and to understate relative impacts of newer ones.

Because our study's citation counts go through 1991 and it is likely to take at least one year for a published article to begin garnering citations, we use 1990 to cap the years of publication for each journal. Since the bulk of citations may well come after one year, our choice of 1990 (rather than an earlier year) results in a ranking that may still be biased somewhat toward older journals, perhaps still understating the potency of new journals' impacts to some degree. We used the 1990 ending point with three different starting points in calculating the normalization factor for a journal: the beginning year of publication, the year 1977, and the year 1967. Rationales for these are discussed below.

The beginning year of publication can be used as a means for normalizing the citation count by dividing the raw citation total for each journal by the total number of years the journal has been in print through 1990. For example, MIS Quarterly began publication in 1977. Hence, a normalized citation measure is determined by dividing the total number of citations to MIS Quarterly (1,489) by the number of years in print (14). The journals are then ranked based on the normalized citation measures. But there are drawbacks to using this kind of normalization factor.

First, normalizing based on the beginning year of publication may arbitrarily penalize older journals that significantly predate the advent of BCS research (e.g., Harvard Business Review has been published since 1922). Second, it is unlikely that researchers would consistently cite articles that are “old.” In fact, scholars in information science have investigated this issue $[14]$ . Webster and Starbuck have reported that reference lists in MIS journals have an average median age of less than five year $[23]$ . To further illustrate this, consider the reference list at the end of this article. It is conceivable that an older article (1930s or 1940s or 1950s or 1960s) could be cited in the reference list. However, all fall within a shorter time horizon.

Hence, we considered whether there may be other, perhaps more suitable, starting points for determining a journal's normalization factor. One alternative is to use 1977 as the starting point for any journal in publication before that point. This represents a ten-year time period prior to the beginning of the five years of citation collection. The year 1977 is also the first year of publication for Information and Management and MIS Quarterly, two prominent journals devoted to BCS research. With this alternative, the normalization factor's divisor is calculated by subtracting from 1991 the higher of the beginning year of publication or 1977.

A second alternative starting point is 1967, which is frequently cited as a seminal year for the beginning of the formal study of management information systems [6]. Thus, citations to works prior to 1967 could be considered uncommon for articles published in the 1987–91 time frame. For this alternative, the normalization factor's divisor is calculated by subtracting from 1991 the higher of the beginning year of publication or 1967.

In this study, we report the rankings that result for each of the three normalization alternatives noted above. However, we limit the normalization to those journals receiving at least 50 total citations during the study period from the five base journals [11]. That is, our normalized rankings ignore journals not sufficiently established, broad-based, or prominent to garner an average of at least two citations per base journal per year. Thus, there are no “flashes in the pan” in our study. A limitation of our study is that high-quality BCS journals that are relatively new (e.g., Information Systems Research, Journal of Organizational Computing), or narrow in coverage (e.g., Expert Systems, Knowledge Acquisition) do not appear in the normalized rankings. The beginning year of publication was collected for the 41 journals that meet our inclusion criterion. The initial year of publication for this set of journals ranges from 1883 (Science) to 1985 (Decision Support Systems).

## Results

TABLE 1 PRESENTS THE THREE NORMALIZED RANKINGS of journals receiving more than 50 citations in the 1987–91 period, as well as the original “simple effectiveness” ranks. The first column contains the journal name with its raw citation rank in brackets. The second column shows the first year of publication for each journal. The next three columns hold journal ranks under each of the normalized ranking schemes. The journals are sorted on the 1967 ranking, which can be regarded not only as reflecting the advent of the field, but also as involving a compromise between the other two normalization factors. The final column shows the differential between the “simple effectiveness” rank and the rank determined by the compromise (1967-based) normalization factor.

Examining this table yields several interesting observations.

1. MIS Quarterly and Communications of the ACM, which were originally ranked 1 and 2 in “simple effectiveness” terms, retain the same ranks after normalization. However, there is considerable shuffling among the other journals’ ranks and all of these other journals’ ranks are at least somewhat sensitive to which normalization factor is employed.

2. More journals made “large” moves up (eight had at least a +5 differential) than made “large” moves down (three had at least a -6 differential). The largest moves up tended to be of greater magnitude than the largest moves down. Four of the five journals that began publication after 1977 had differentials of +10 or more. In other words, those journals with the largest moves up were the newest journals. The fact that fewer journals had large moves down is consistent with the observation that more journals moved down than moved up (see point 7 below).

3. Coupling the largest positive differential (+20 for IEEE Software) and the largest negative differential (-7 for Journal of the ACM), the greatest relative shift in ranks between any pair of journals was 27 places.

4. When rankings are normalized, general business journals tend to decline in relative importance as compared with their original ranks. For example, Harvard Business Review, Sloan Management Review, Decision Sciences, Administrative Science Quarterly, and Academy of Management Journal all had lower rankings after normalization. The one exception is a +4 differential for the Academy of Management Review, which is much more recent than any of the other general business journals. The decline in ranks coincides with the rise of BCS as an academic discipline and the appearance of more focused journals for that discipline.

5. Newer journals that emphasize computing move up in the rankings, reflecting more potent impacts than their unnormalized ranks might suggest. These include Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, ACM Transactions on Office Information Systems, ACM Transactions on Database Systems, and IEEE Software.

6. Four of the five base journals are among the top five in Table 1, compared with only two of the five for the unnormalized ranking. This further substantiates our choice of base journals as being among the most prominent in the field.

7. About one-fourth of the journals have a higher normalized rank. About one-tenth have unchanged ranks. The great majority have lower normalized ranks.

8. Normalizing does not substantially influence the relative ranks of the oldest journals in the list (Business Week, Science, Journal of Applied Psychology, and Psychological Bulletin).

## Conclusions

THERE HAS BEEN A CONSIDERABLE INTEREST in and debate about ranking journals publishing BCS-related research, as evidenced by the number of recently published studies addressing this issue. Additionally, there have been questions and commentaries regarding the progress and maturation of the field of BCS research. Although several studies have identified and objectively ranked journals publishing BCS research, they have not explicitly recognized the role of journal longevity in devising ranking schemes. This article extends previous research by providing a normalized citation-based ranking approach for BCS journals. Accounting for the length of time a journal has been published, this approach yields rankings that reflect the relative potency of journals' impacts.

The results indicate that previous studies may have overstated the relative importance of general business journals, while the relative importance of specific BCS journals may have been understated. This is an important finding, as the relative ranks of journals often play a significant role in tenure and promotion processes at academic institutions. In the future, researchers who seek to devise rankings of BCS journals should consider using the methodology introduced here to report normalized rankings. The availability of a normalized ranking of BCS-related journals can also help researchers (especially new ones) to better budget their efforts in staying abreast of current research, by recognizing the relative impact potencies of specific journals. Finally, the analyses presented here may assist researchers in targeting manuscripts to appropriate journal forums.
