# Influence of Experience on Personal Computer Utilization: Testing a Conceptual Model

RONALD L. THOMPSON, CHRISTOPHER A. HIGGINS, AND JANE M. HOWELL

RONALD L. THOMPSON is an Assistant Professor within the University of Vermont's School of Business Administration. He completed his Ph.D. in business administration at the University of Western Ontario and formerly taught management information systems at the University of Calgary's Faculty of Management. Dr. Thompson also has several years experience in ranching and banking. He has presented at various national and international conferences, and has published in outlets such as MIS Quarterly, Information & Management, and the Journal of Creative Behaviour. Current research interests include information technology and organizational strategy, the impact of technology on individual and organizational performance, and the management of information systems projects in the public sector.

CHRISTOPHER A. HIGGINS is an Associate Professor in the Management Science and Information Systems group of the School of Business Administration, the University of Western Ontario. He holds a Ph.D. and master's of mathematics from the University of Waterloo and has published articles in the Information Systems Research, Communications of the ACM, MIS Quarterly, Administrative Science Quarterly, Organizational Dynamics, Journal of Applied Psychology, Sloan Management Review, ACM Transactions on Office Information Systems, and Office Technology and People. The primary focus of his research is the impact of technology on individuals and organizations. Specific research interests include computerized monitoring, organizational champions, and the impact of personal computers on work and family life.

JANE M. HOWELL is an Assistant Professor in the Organization Behaviour group of the School of Business Administration at the University of Western Ontario. Following four years of human resource management experience, she earned her master's degree in psychology at The University of Western Ontario and then went on to complete a Ph.D. in business administration at the University of British Columbia. She has published articles in Administrative Science Quarterly, Organizational Dynamics, Business Quarterly, and MIS Quarterly. Her current research focuses on champions and innovation, transformational leadership, and managerial use of personal computers.

ABSTRACT: The influence of prior experience on personal computer utilization was examined through an extension of a conceptual model developed and tested previously. Respondents were classified on the basis of their self-reported skill level and length of time having used personal computers. Three competing ways of modeling the influence of experience were tested: (1) a direct influence, (2) an indirect influence through six distinct attitude and belief components, and (3) a moderating influence on the relations between the attitude/belief components and utilization. The results suggested that experience influenced utilization directly, that indirect influences were present but less pronounced, and that the moderating influence of experience on the relations between five of six antecedent constructs and utilization was generally quite strong. For researchers, the implications are that prior experience with an information technology (IT) is an important factor to include when developing, testing, or applying models of IT adoption and use. For practitioners, the results highlight the importance of emphasizing applicability of the information technology to the current job and professional development early in the adoption process, with more emphasis on future benefits as experience is gained.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: adoption of new technology, end-user computing, personal computer utilization.

ORGANIZATIONS CONTINUE TO INVEST HEAVILY IN THE ACQUISITION of personal computers (PCs) and related technology. The reasons for this investment vary, but the overriding belief is that knowledge workers will use the technology to become more productive (in terms of efficiency, effectiveness, and/or quality). The availability of the technology, however, does not guarantee increased utilization or job performance. As Lee [14] notes, when use is optional, the patterns of usage vary substantially.

To ensure that the use of PCs (and other information technology) is managed effectively, it is important for us to understand factors that influence utilization. Earlier studies described PC use $[14]$ or sought to derive theories of information system utilization by drawing on observed results $[16, 24]$ . More recently, researchers have adopted theories of attitudes and behavior from referent disciplines to the context of system use $[3, 4, 17, 27]$ . The research framework proposed by Triandis $[29]$ is one such theory that attempts to explain behavior.

The objective of adopting such theories is to gain the benefit of using well-established streams of research. If it can be shown that attitudes influence PC utilization, then it follows that influencing those attitudes will have an influence on utilization. A positive relationship between information system utilization and performance is generally assumed, and some studies have been able to demonstrate such a relationship under certain circumstances $[16]$ . From this it follows that performance can be influenced, at least indirectly, through attitudes.

The primary contribution of this research is the examination of the influence of prior experience on the utilization of personal computers. The conceptual model developed and tested previously by Thompson, Higgins, and Howell [27] is used as the underlying framework, which builds upon Triandis's [28, 29] theory of behavior. Prior experience with a behavior (in this case, use of a personal computer) is explicitly modeled and tested in two ways: (1) as an antecedent to intervening factors hypothesized to influence behavior (with both direct and indirect effects), and (2) as a moderating variable for relations between these same factors and utilization. The results from empirical tests of these approaches provide valuable insights into the role of experience within this context.

## Literature Review

NUMEROUS INFORMATION SYSTEMS (IS) RESEARCHERS HAVE TESTED the relationship between attitudes and the utilization of information technology [16, 21, 24, 26], with differing results. Although some studies found statistically significant results, the key issue is the substantiveness of the relationship. More specifically, a relation may be statistically significant within a given research study, but may still not be strong enough to have any practical implications. One very important issue is therefore not whether a relation exists, but how important it is within a given context.

When IS researchers began looking at the relationship between attitudes and the utilization of information technology, they generally did not draw on other disciplines for their work. In fact, much of the implementation and attitude research has been criticized for a lack of a theoretical base $[10]$ and insufficient attention to methodological and measurement issues $[25]$ . IS researchers have argued that these shortcomings may explain the conflicting results that have been obtained $[10, 26]$ .

More recent efforts have drawn directly from referent disciplines in the development and testing of theoretical models. A great deal of research in cognitive psychology has addressed the prediction and explanation of human behavior, with contexts ranging from losing weight, to acts of racial prejudice, to voting for political office $[5, 6, 7, 29]$ . IS researchers have not taken complete advantage of these well-developed streams of research, but are beginning to see the advantages of applying them to the context of IT adoption and use.

## Attitudes

Before briefly reviewing the conceptual development for this study, it is important to clarify the concept of attitudes. An attitude is an individual's disposition to respond favorably or unfavorably to an object, person, institution, or event, or to any other discriminable aspect of the individual's world [1, p. 241]. Rosenberg and Hovland [23] proposed a hierarchical model of attitudes that has served as the starting point for many attitude-behavior theories. Their model includes cognition (beliefs), affect (feelings), and conation (intentions) as first-order factors and attitude as a single second-order factor.

Since it is generally acknowledged that attitude is a latent variable, it cannot be observed directly and must be inferred from measurable verbal and nonverbal responses. On the verbal side, cognition may be measured by expressions of beliefs about the attitude object: affect through expressions of feelings toward the attitude object, and conation through expressions of behavioral intentions. The model of behavior proposed by Triandis [29] is consistent with Rosenberg and Hovland's [23] model of attitudes.

## Attitudes and Behavior

Triandis [29] believed that much of the work in psychology was becoming fragmented, lacking a theoretical framework for guiding future research. To address this perceived need, he developed a comprehensive model synthesizing relations among attitudes, values, and other acquired behavioral dispositions to action or behavior. Triandis [29] does not propose a causal link between the cognitive component of attitudes (beliefs, which he terms perceived consequences) with the affective component. Rather, affect and perceived consequences are viewed as independent (but related) factors that influence behavior indirectly through intentions.

The affective component of attitudes has a like/dislike connotation. Affective responses with respect to the medical profession, for example, could be expressions of admiration for physicians or expressions of disgust with the state of health care. Perceived consequences, on the other hand, incorporate the basic premise that individuals evaluate their actions in terms of potential rewards and base their choices of behavior on the desirability of the rewards. According to Triandis [29], each act is perceived as having potential consequences that have value, together with a probability of occurrence.

Social factors include the individual's internalization of individual norms, roles, and values. These entities influence the individual's conceptions of behaviors that are appropriate, desirable, and morally correct [29, p. 210]. Triandis defines social factors as a general construct that reflects individual norms, roles, and values, which are in turn influenced by subjective culture variables (including the norms of groups and referent others). The norms depend on messages received from others, and reflect what individuals think they should do.

Facilitating conditions are objective factors that make an act easy (or difficult) to do. For example, if a person did not have easy access to a personal computer, use would be much more difficult. Triandis [29] argued that the person's perceptions that the act is easy are "internal" factors that would be included in the perceived consequences of the behavior.

Although his model includes other constructs (such as genetic biological factors), the major components of interest to this context are that social factors, affect, and perceived consequences influence behavior. In addition, behavior may not occur if the facilitating conditions make the behavior difficult.

Most theoretical models have limitations, and the one proposed by Triandis [29] is no exception. For one, the model is very complex and it would be difficult to employ it in its entirety. This is not a severe limitation, however, since the model was intended to serve as a framework for research and not as the model of human behavior. A second limitation is that some of the constructs are related in the sense that it may be difficult to operationalize the constructs without introducing some redundancy in the measures.

Although the Triandis theory [29] has not been as widely accepted as alternative models such as the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) [7], it has been tested and used in other contexts. With respect to empirical findings, Valois et al. [30] conducted a study to compare the Triandis [29] model with TRA in the prediction of exercise intention and behavior. They found the Triandis theory to be at least as powerful as TRA in terms of prediction, and superior to TRA in other respects. Bergeron et al. [3] used the Triandis model as the base for their investigation of the adoption of executive information systems, and found it provided a good explanatory model.

Attitudes, Behavior, and IT Utilization

The model previously developed and tested by Thompson et al. [27] was used as the starting point for this study. Although this model was based on Triandis's [29] framework, it does not purport to represent the full Triandis model. Rather, Thompson et al. [27] selected constructs from the model that they believed could be important within the context of IT utilization.

Their model included six factors that were hypothesized to influence the utilization of personal computers (see figure 1). These were: (1) social factors (norms) influencing PC use, (2) affect toward PC use, (3) complexity of PC use, (4) job fit with PC use, (5) long-term consequences of PC use, and (6) facilitating conditions for PC use. Complexity, job fit, and long-term consequences were modeled as three distinct components of perceived consequences (beliefs).

For a detailed description of the constructs and justification for the paths, refer to Thompson, Higgins, and Howell [27]. The primary focus of this study is the way in which experience could be incorporated into their model.

Conceptual Model

## Alternative Influences of Experience

THE RELATION BETWEEN EXPERIENCE AND BEHAVIOR is not straightforward; there are different ways in which such a relation could occur. One possibility is that experience exerts a direct influence on behavior. In the context of personal computer use by knowledge workers, it would be surprising if a strong correlation was not observed between experience with personal computers (past behavior) and current utilization.

A second possibility is that experience exerts an indirect influence on behavior, through intervening variables such as beliefs. Fazio and Zanna [6] suggest that attitudes based on direct experience predict behaviors better than attitudes formed in other manners because direct experience makes more information about the attitude object available to the individual.

This is consistent with the model proposed by Triandis [29]. He argues that behavior has objective consequences (for example, use of a spreadsheet reduces the actual time required to complete a budget). These objective consequences are interpreted (internalized) by the individual, which leads to reinforcement. Reinforcement affects the perceived consequences (beliefs) in two ways: it changes the perceived probabilities that the behavior will have particular consequences, and it changes the value of these consequences. This conceptualization of experience allows for a feedback loop in the model.

A third possibility is that experience could moderate the effect of antecedent factors (including belief components) on intentions and behavior. This is the position advocated by Fazio and Zanna [6] in their work on direct experience and attitude-behavior consistency. By adding experience as a moderating variable, we would hypothesize that the strength of the relation between a given cognitive factor and behavior will increase or decrease, depending on the individual's level of experience with personal computers.

## Modeling the Influence of Experience

To understand the influence of experience within this model more fully, the direct, indirect, and moderating effects of experience were examined. Since it is not possible to test each of these simultaneously, a multistage process was necessary. To model direct and indirect influences, experience was placed as an antecedent to the six constructs previously hypothesized to influence utilization (see figure 1). Paths were drawn from experience to each of the other six constructs (for the indirect effects on use), as well as from experience to utilization (for the direct effects).

Generating a priori hypotheses concerning the influence of experience is a difficult task. In many cases theoretical arguments could be raised that support both positive and negative influences on antecedent variables, as well as positive and negative moderating influences on the relations between the antecedent variables and utilization. Rather than examine each of the thirteen potential hypotheses (one direct, six indirect, six moderating) in detail, we discuss each of the antecedent constructs and present a rationale for indirect and/or moderating influences. The rationale for a direct relation from experience to utilization was presented earlier. All possible relations (hypotheses) were tested, however.

Turning first to the influence of experience with personal computers on the relation between social factors (norms) and utilization, Triandis [28] argued that social norms will play a greater role when the behavior is new. As the behavior becomes more routinized for the individual, habits begin to exert a stronger influence while the influence of social norms weakens. This would suggest that experience will exert a moderating influence, rather than influence utilization indirectly through social norms.

The effect of experience on the relationship between affect and utilization is hypothesized to be more pronounced among inexperienced users. Triandis [28] argued that the influence of the affective component will decrease as the user's experience increases. Rogers and Shoemaker [22] coined the term innovative dissonance to refer to situations where use (or nonuse) of an innovation is inconsistent with the individual's attitude towards the innovation. For example, a new user may, for various reasons, have negative attitudes towards PC usage but adopt the innovation because of peer pressure. This would create a situation of innovation dissonance, and individuals would tend to modify their behavior or attitudes to reduce the dissonance [22]. Thus, experienced users will likely have reduced the extent to which negative (or positive) attitudes affected their usage pattern resulting in a weaker link between affect and usage. Once more this argues more for a moderating rather than indirect influence.

Turning next to the components of perceived consequences (beliefs), it would be consistent with Triandis's [29] theory to hypothesize that experience would influence utilization indirectly through perceived consequences. With respect to complexity, we argue that the negative relationship between complexity and use should be more pronounced for inexperienced users. As individuals become more experienced with PCs, their self-confidence will increase to the extent that they perceive they can handle the complexity of PC use.

Individuals with little experience will have their beliefs about job fit influenced by advertising, opinions expressed by peers, and current practices in the organization. Those with greater experience will have their beliefs influenced more by their personal experience. An inexperienced user may hold unrealistic beliefs concerning the application of a PC to their job, influenced by advertisements, and other secondary sources. As he or she gains experience, job fit beliefs are adjusted to a more realistic level, which means the values obtained from a belief measurement scale could increase or decrease, depending on how realistic the original beliefs were. A priori, we anticipate that experience will exert a positive influence on utilization through job fit.

Long-term consequences of use should provide a stronger influence for those with more direct experience. The argument is that experienced users will see more opportunity for positive consequences of use, such as improved opportunities for more meaningful work, and improved opportunities for preferred future job assignments.

Once more, however, counterarguments (similar to those for job fit) could be presented. As with job fit, beliefs about future consequences of use held by inexperienced users could be unrealistically positive or unrealistically negative. As experience with the technology grows, these beliefs could be modified in a positive or negative direction (on a measurement scale), depending on the initial position. Without additional information to guide our a priori position, however, we propose that experience will exert a positive influence on utilization through long-term consequences of use (consistent with Triandis's [29] conceptualization).

For facilitating conditions (in this study, support for PC use), the expectation is that the influence would be weaker for experienced users. Keeping in mind that the operationalization of facilitating conditions adopted here relates to the level of technical support offered, the rationale is that experienced users require less assistance and have become more independent from the technical support staff. This supports a moderating influence by experience on the relation between facilitating conditions and utilization.

To summarize, the conceptual model developed and tested by Thompson, Higgins, and Howell [27] was used as the base for this research. The extension to this earlier work was the explicit consideration of experience with personal computers. Experience was modeled with direct, indirect, and moderating influences.

It was also believed that the measurement of the dependent variable would be improved by adding a fourth dimension, variety of applications used. The variety of applications was calculated by counting the applications (text reports, budgeting, letters, and memos, etc.) for which the response for extent of use was “to some extent” or greater (3, 4, or 5 on a five-point scale).

## Experience and Expertise

Experience and expertise (or skill) are closely related, but separate, concepts. An individual could use a personal computer as a dumb terminal to access electronic mail for several years, and gain little or no information to influence their beliefs about the usefulness of a PC for their key job tasks. Another individual could undergo an intensive training program on the use of PCs and PC software over a period of a few months, and gain substantial knowledge that could influence their beliefs. Hence, the length of time the individual has used a PC may be correlated with their expertise, but it is the expertise component that will provide more information (for interpretations and reinforcement).

On the other hand, using a self-reported measure of skill may have some limitations. An individual who has used a PC with a spreadsheet program for a very short period of time and has mastered rudimentary skills may believe he or she has mastered PC use, and rate his or her skill level quite high. For that reason we argue that within the context of information technology use, expertise is the most relevant component of experience, but both skill level and length of use should be measured. For experience in this study, these two measures (adopted from Pavri [19]) were employed.

## Procedure

The research instrument (questionnaire) was administered to respondents in eight organizations, which included two telecommunications companies, one heavy manufacturing firm, one aerospace research and development organization, a software development company, one mining firm, and two government offices. The organizations were not chosen at random, but rather selected to represent a variety of settings.

In each organization a senior executive was contacted and asked to support the study. A company representative was then identified to help administer the questionnaire. After selecting a specific business unit (or units) with the help of the senior executive, the company representative was asked to identify all knowledge workers within the unit who used personal computers to any extent. The researchers were then provided with a list of the prospective respondents and their departments.

After receipt of the sampling list, a packet was prepared for each respondent and delivered to the representative for distribution through the company's internal mail system. The packet contained the research questionnaire, a letter from the researchers describing the study, a letter from the senior executive endorsing the study, and a stamped return envelope. The completed questionnaires were returned directly to the researchers through the public postal system. The questionnaires were coded with numbers to protect the confidentiality of the respondents, and the participating organization received only summary results. One followup letter was sent to nonrespondents.

The questionnaires were not of the paper-and-pencil variety, but rather employed the DISKQ technique developed by Higgins, Dimnik, and Greenwood [12]. With this method, an interactive questionnaire was presented on a personal computer, and responses were stored on the questionnaire diskette.

## Sample

A total of 325 completed questionnaires were returned to the researchers, for a gross response rate of 80 percent. The response rates for the eight individual organizations ranged from a low of 62 percent to a high of 100 percent. A verification question revealed that nine of the respondents did not use a personal computer for job-related work, and an additional 62 did not complete all of the questions required to test the research hypotheses. Of these 62, four were returned to the sample by using within-scale mean substitution (if the respondent only completed three of four questions for a construct, the mean of the three was substituted for the omitted question). This left 258 usable questionnaires. Finally, thirty-nine of the respondents indicated that their jobs were clerical or secretarial in nature, where use of a personal computer was mandatory. Since the study's focus was on optional use, these respondents were also removed. The final sample size for analysis was 219 which gives a usable response rate of 54 percent.

Seventy percent of the sample was male, with over 65 percent holding undergraduate or graduate degrees. Over half (54 percent) were between 30 and 50 years of age, with 31 percent reporting ages less than 30 and 15 percent over 50. The distribution of job classifications was about 35 percent staff specialist, 33 percent engineer/professional, 30 percent managerial/supervisory, and 2 percent executive. Descriptive statistics showing the respondents' utilization of PCs are shown in Table 1.

## Data Analysis

The data analysis technique chosen for the study was partial least squares (PLS). PLS $[2, 15, 31]$ is one of several techniques designed specifically for analyzing causal models involving multiple constructs with multiple measures. To test the statistical significance of path coefficients, the jackknifing routine developed by Fornell and Barclay $[8]$ was employed. Jackknifing is a nonparametric technique that is robust in the sense that it is not as affected by violations of the usual assumptions of normality associated with regression analysis.

## Results

## Validity and Reliability

THE METHODS USED HERE TO TEST INTERNAL CONSISTENCY have been employed previously in MIS studies using PLS analysis $[11, 19, 20]$ . These include examining the factor loadings of the indicators on their corresponding constructs, as well as calculating Fornell and Larcker's $[9]$ measure of internal consistency (this measure is analogous to Cronbach's alpha, and the 0.7 standards suggested by Nunnally $[18]$ can be adopted). The tests for internal consistency were satisfactory, with all internal consistency measures exceeding 0.7. Some of the factor loadings were lower than would be desired for more stringent theory testing.

The tests of discriminant validity also followed those previously used in MIS studies employing PLS $[11, 20]$ . The two primary tests involved verifying that each indicator loaded more highly on its associated construct than on any other construct, and that each construct shared more variance with its measures than with other constructs.

The first column in Table 4 shows the results of this analysis. The next step was to add paths linking experience to each of the antecedent factors, and a seventh path linking experience directly to utilization. These results are displayed in column 2 of Table 4.

To test the moderating influence of experience, the respondents were first separated into two subsamples; experienced and inexperienced. The PLS analysis was conducted for each of the subsamples, and the path coefficients compared. These results are shown in columns 3 and 4 in Table 4. Where a significant difference is observed in the magnitude of the path coefficients, it indicates that experience did in fact exert a moderating influence on the relation.

## Interpretation of Results

THE DIRECT INFLUENCE OF EXPERIENCE WAS BOTH statistically and substantively significant (b = 0.23). As suggested earlier, it would have been surprising if a strong path had not been observed. Past use of a personal computer would be expected to influence current use directly.

The combined indirect effect of experience on utilization was 0.22, dispersed as relatively small effects through the intervening variables. This provided little support for a strong indirect influence through any specific variable, but some support for indirect effects in general. It appears that, within this context, the indirect effect of experience through any given belief component is much smaller than the direct effect. This implies that indirect effects may be added to provide additional understanding, but may provide little information with respect to additional prediction of behavior.

The observed results comparing the two groups (columns 3 and 4 of Table 4) provide a much richer understanding of the moderating influence of utilization on the paths in the conceptual model. It is clear from these results that experience does have a very strong moderating influence on most of the paths in the model, which is consistent with previous findings [4].

The lack of a significant difference for the influence of beliefs about job fit on utilization between inexperienced $b = 0.36$ and experienced respondents $b = 0.36$ was not anticipated. We expected that the direct experience gained by using a personal computer would enable experienced users to perceive more numerous ways of incorporating PC use into their jobs, resulting in a stronger influence. There may be a reasonable explanation for the observed results, however. It is possible that inexperienced users also perceived personal computers could provide substantial benefits for their work, even though their source(s) of information would be different. The inexperienced users would have their beliefs influenced more by information obtained from colleagues, advertising, vendor representatives, and so on. The results suggest that although the source of information may be different, the influence of job fit was similar.

For long-term consequences, the influence was much stronger for experienced users $b = 0.18$ than for inexperienced users $b = 0.02$ . The path coefficient for inexperienced users was not statistically significant, suggesting that long-term consequences did not influence utilization for them. This may help explain the results for job fit. It is possible that as personal computer users gain experience, their beliefs about how a PC could assist them for current job tasks remained stable, while their beliefs of how it could help them in the future increase.

It is interesting also to look at the relationship between social factors (norms) and utilization at the same time. As predicted, the influence of norms was greater for inexperienced users $b = 0.27$ than for experienced users $b = 0.17$ . From this, one might suggest that there is some type of inverse relationship between the influence of long-term consequences and social norms. Initially, norms exert a very strong influence, much stronger than beliefs about the long-term consequences of using a personal computer. As experience is gained with a personal computer, however, the internal factor (long-term consequences) begins to exert a stronger influence and the external factor (social norms) plays a lesser role.

The difference in the magnitude of the path coefficients for inexperienced users $b = 0.13$ and experienced users $b = -0.04$ for the relationship between affect and utilization was in the direction hypothesized, and the difference was large enough to provide strong support for the proposed influence. This suggests that, similar to long-term consequences, affect (feelings toward using a personal computer) decreases in importance as users gain experience. The result that requires some examination was for the hypothesis that the influence of beliefs about technical support (facilitating conditions) would be less for experienced users than for inexperienced ones. The relation for both experienced and inexperienced users was negative $b = -0.26$ and -0.14, respectively). These results may suggest that none of the respondents were satisfied with the technical support available to them, but individuals with personal computer experience were even less satisfied than inexperienced users.

Perhaps nonusers and inexperienced users attribute the inevitable frustration experienced when learning to use a PC to insufficient support. Similarly, more experienced users may find their needs are very specific, and surpass the expertise of in-house support staff. Another possibility is that poor facilitating conditions inhibit initial use, but once someone has decided to make the investment and learn how to use a PC, this becomes less of an issue.

## Discussion and Implications

THE PRIMARY OBJECTIVE OF THIS RESEARCH was to investigate the influence of experience on the utilization of personal computers, through an extension of the conceptual model developed and tested by Thompson et al. [27]. The results indicate that experience influenced utilization directly, that indirect effects were present but less pronounced, and that the moderating influence of experience on the relations between five antecedent constructs and utilization was generally quite strong.

## Strengths and Limitations of the Research

This study built on previous research, addressing Keen's [13] concern that MIS researchers are not working towards building a cumulative tradition of research. The results provided support for the findings obtained in previous research [27] and also increased the generalizability of the results by using a sample from multiple organizations. Furthermore, the original conceptual model was based on the theory of attitudes and behavior developed by Triandis [28, 29] in the social psychology discipline. This addresses concerns raised by Keen [13] and Goodhue [10] that MIS researchers often fail to build on relevant research from other disciplines. The study employed measures that had been developed and tested previously, and also tested them rigorously in this study. This addresses Straub's [25] concern that MIS researchers pay inadequate attention to the psychometric properties of measures employed in their research.

The test of direct, indirect, and moderating effects provides a much better understanding of the role of experience in the adoption and use of information technology. The results of this study clearly indicate that experience is an important construct in attitude/behavior research, and that its total effect is not captured by the antecedent variables employed in conceptual models such as the Theory of Reasoned Action $[7]$ . Future model development should therefore explicitly include experience, or a related construct such as expertise or habit.

This research has limitations. First, the affect construct was operationalized using a two-item scale. Clearly, more items are needed to adequately measure users feelings of like/dislike towards PC usage; this was a missed opportunity. A second limitation of the research is the relatively small sample size. While the analysis technique (PLS) can handle small samples, we would have more statistical conclusion validity with larger numbers. Also, the data used to test the hypotheses were cross-sectional. A preferred approach would be to take measurements of individuals as they began using PCs and then take subsequent measures as they become more experienced. Such a study, given a reasonable sample size, could provide insights into the adoption and use of technology.

One final limitation relates to the use of subjective (self-report) measures of PC utilization. Although a strong correlation between subjective and objective measures of behavior is often assumed, there is little evidence to support this assumption within this context. Future studies could employ both objective and subjective measures, examining the correspondence between them (or lack thereof).

## Implications of the Results and Future Research Opportunities

For practitioners, the results highlight the need to take a proactive approach in both (1) adopting new information technology, and (2) supporting the use of technology (such as personal computers). For the introduction of new IT, the results suggest stressing (in order):

1. applicability to current job (job fit);

2. professional development (peer use, social norms);

3. ease of use (complexity);

4. positive feelings toward the technology (affect).

As experience with the new technology grows, (both individually and organizationally), the emphasis should shift to:

1. applicability to current job (job fit);

2. future benefits (long-term consequences);

3. professional development (peer use, social norms).

If ease of use is still a factor after experience has been gained, then, unless the perceived benefits are very strong, the implementation will likely meet with limited success.

For researchers, the opportunity exists to expand the model tested in this study by adding further constructs proposed by Triandis [29], including habit and relevant arousal (motivation). Another way to build on this research would be to adapt the model and approach to different contexts. For example, it would not be difficult to adapt the measures for the context of IS developers using CASE tools. It would be interesting to see if the relative strength of the paths held, and if experience has a similar influence. The conceptual model and approach could be used as a basis for investigating the adoption and use of new information technologies in a variety of contexts.
