# Interdependent Benefits from Interorganizational Systems: Opportunities for Business Partner Reengineering

FREDERICK J. RIGGINS AND TRIDAS MUKHOPADHYAY

## 1. Introduction

THE ADOPTION OF INTERORGANIZATIONAL SYSTEMS (IOS) continues to grow at a significant rate. One type of IOS that has seen significant adoption in recent years is electronic data interchange (EDI). It has been estimated that by 1997 the sales of EDI-related products will reach one billion dollars annually [16]. Managing the growth of IOS networks and the adoption patterns of trading partners have become major business concerns for network managers. The following quote from a manager of an EDI pilot test illustrates the frustration experienced by one manager concerning his inability to control the actions of another trading partner:

We didn't know the readiness of the partner in terms of their experience with EDI. When we were finally ready to conduct the pilot they called to tell us they weren't ready, and didn't know when they would be! Their parent company wanted them to get on their networking system, which was different than ours. In the mean time our management was wanting to know how the pilot was coming [29].

Because IOS cross company boundaries, they impose a reduction in the manager's span of control which results in several limitations on how firms control costs and realize benefits from these systems. In particular, certain firms, often buyers, act as initiators of these systems by strongly encouraging their trading partners, often suppliers, to adopt the technology if they are to be considered as long-term trading partners [22]. Suppliers that adopt the technology due to pressure from trading partners are called followers. Followers that implement IOS simply to meet an initiator's requirement to exchange information electronically often avoid implementing the technology in a sophisticated way. This may hinder not only their own ability to gain benefits, but also the initiator's ability to realize many of the originally anticipated benefits. For this reason there is an increased level of risk associated with IOS as compared with traditional information systems projects. If the initiator had used traditional approaches to estimate costs and benefits of the proposed information system during the feasibility phase of the project, it may find itself with fewer benefits than expected at a higher cost than was originally forecasted.

We examine the existence of interdependent benefits that can cause unique problems for initiators of IOS. The organization of the paper is as follows. In section 2, we consider several decisions that must be made by the implementing organization of an EDI system. We will see that it is relatively straightforward to assume that the way in which a firm's trading partners implement and use the system can have a direct impact on how the initiating firm realizes benefits from the system. According to Kekre and Mukhopadhyay [23], decreased time to process data and improved data accuracy are two important benefits of EDI. In sections 3 and 4, we report the results of two empirical studies designed to study these benefits in more detail, and in particular the issue of interdependent benefits. In both cases, a buyer-initiated modification at the site of the trading partner results in specific benefits for the buyer, but not necessarily for the supplier. We discuss the consequences of the results in section 5. In particular, we propose that the current emphasis in the literature on business process reengineering be extended to include business partner reengineering. Firms seeking to increase their benefits from IOS should consider how to encourage their business partners to reengineer their internal processes in a way beneficial to the original firm. We conclude the paper in section 6 with suggestions for future research.

## 2. Interdependent Benefits

WHEN WE CONSIDER THE LOSS OF CONTROL that an initiator experiences due to the interorganizational aspect of IOS, we refer to the firm's reduced influence on both the decision of trading partners to adopt the technology and how adopting trading partners will implement it internally. Because IOS are shared between separate firms, it is unlikely that the firms will realize identical benefits from the system. Early uses of EDI by companies such as American Hospital Supply Corporation [1] and the McKesson Drug Company [10] were supplier-initiated systems where much of the benefit from the IOS linkage accrued to those suppliers. Even though the recent trend has been toward more buyer-initiated systems, the benefits from the electronic relationship still seem to favor the initiator. Stern and Kaufmann [34] found that the majority of EDI users they surveyed believed their trading partner benefited more from the system than themselves. Hwang et al. [22] found that initiators typically invested more in their EDI systems than followers, resulting in more benefits for the initiators. In the absence of a well-defined mechanism for distribution of the benefits of IOS, unequal benefits have been shown to affect the initial adoption decision. In a field study of the consumer packaged goods industry, Clemons and Row [12] show that even though it is widely believed that industrywide acceptance of EDI would increase the economic welfare of the industry as a whole, many retailers have declined adoption. This is because it was not clear that their firm would receive any of the surplus. In fact, many retailers believe that the technology will result in an overall weaker bargaining position and therefore lower profits.

In addition to these strategic reasons, many small suppliers may not have the internal infrastructure needed to adopt the IOS. While there are several EDI products that allow firms to adopt some form of EDI, even with minimal computer technology, other types of IOS may be infeasible to many smaller firms. For example, an interorganizational CAD/CAM document exchange system may be infeasible for many small suppliers.

While firms may choose not to adopt the technology either for strategic or technical reasons, Bakos and Treacy $[4]$ point out that adopting an IOS can create a new way of achieving vertical integration apart from merging the firms. The result is that the IOS can reduce the transaction costs between the buyer and seller $[8, 11]$ . When this occurs, the technology can be the catalyst to alter the optimal size of the firm, allowing it to focus on what it does best without concern about high transaction costs with trading partners [see also 2, 19, 24].

In many cases where the initiating firm is a buyer with substantial leverage in the relationship, the issue of adoption may be circumvented by coercing suppliers to adopt the technology or face the loss of business of an important customer. Emmelhainz [18] notes a 1988 study that found that the percentage of firms who responded positively to the statement, “Some of our large customers have required us to develop EDI systems . . .” ranged from 12 percent to 45 percent depending upon the industry. For example, retailers such as Sears, Wal-Mart, and K-Mart have threatened to stop doing business with any supplier that does not begin allowing the transmission of purchase orders using EDI within a certain established time frame [13, 14]. J.C. Penney has begun an aggressive effort to increase its supplier participation rate from 80 percent to 100 percent compliance [16]. After achieving cooperation with one another through the Automotive Industry Action Group, the big three automotive manufacturers mandated supplier adoption of the X.12 standard as a prerequisite for continuing to do business in that industry. Recently, large buyers have extended the use of this leverage to include formal management of the customer’s inventory [15].

Often a buyer mandate for adoption is coupled with a subsidy from the initiator to encourage adoption of the technology by followers. For example, large buyers often provide software free of charge to suppliers who otherwise may not be able to justify the investment. Analytical models by Meier and Chismar [25], Riggins, Kriebel, and Mukhopadhyay [30], and Seidmann and Wang [31] examine the issues of subsidizing and coercing the initial adoption of the IOS technology by followers..

While not specifically requiring that suppliers adopt the EDI technology, the Boeing Company considers suppliers that adopt EDI committed to building a long-term trading partner relationship. The aerospace manufacturer counts establishment of electronic communications as one of several mechanisms used in an attempt to reduce the number of suppliers [29]. Bakos and Brynjolfsson [3] and Clemons, Reddi, and Row [9] both discuss the trend toward more outsourcing of procured goods but with fewer total suppliers. The drive toward total quality management has created a situation where manufacturers and retailers must develop closer relationships with suppliers and stop viewing suppliers as adversaries. The outdated view that the supplier/customer relationship is characterized by a zero-sum price negotiation is being replaced with a tightly coupled trading partner relationship view. This change has been brought about due to intense foreign competition that resulted in shrinking market share and a need to increase quality/price performance or have both sides of the adversarial relationship go out of business. Adoption of electronic communications between the trading partners is a major part of the establishment of this highly coupled relationship.

Although a buyer may convince its suppliers to adopt the EDI technology, either by subsidy or coercion, the firm then faces a problem in that it may not be able to dictate how adopting trading partners actually implement the IOS. One factor influencing the decision of how to implement EDI is the fact that the cost of implementation often turns out to be greater than expected. A cost analysis of a proposed EDI system for a large manufacturer would have linked the manufacturer to its 12 plants and 120 trading partners at a cost of \$23.5 million [27]. Given the substantial cost of implementing a full-scale EDI system, many followers choose to implement what has been called “door-to-door” EDI. Here the firm implements relatively few applications on a front-end system that is not integrated to its internal transaction processing systems [18]. This reluctant attitude may be related to the fact that many firms are finding that they are not achieving the benefits they expected. Benjamin, DeLong, and Scott-Morton [6] found that eight different EDI systems they studied had relatively little impact on firm performance, largely because management had deliberately attempted to keep change at a minimum. The authors state that only by changing the way the company does business will it achieve real cost savings from EDI.

By reengineering the internal business processes to complement the new technology, the adopter can make full use of the technology $[17, 20]$ . Hammer and Champy define reengineering as “the fundamental rethinking and radical redesign of business processes to achieve dramatic improvements in critical, contemporary measures of performance, such as cost, quality, service, and speed” $[21, p. 32]$ . In a study of order processing systems, Pokorney, Kekre, and Mukhopadhyay $[28]$ found that combining EDI with business process reengineering can lead to faster recovery of payments from customers as well as fewer errors in order processing.

Therefore, the way in which a firm implements the system internally will affect its level of benefit from the technology. However, because the system is interorganizational, these benefits may very well be interdependent. That is, one firm's benefits may depend on the way in which its trading partners implement and use the technology. Thus, an initiator may not realize some expected benefits that otherwise might occur due to the need to coordinate the implementation of the system with external, and potentially recalcitrant, trading partners.

In an early work on IOS, Barrett and Konsynski [5] recognized that the decision to participate on the network is essentially based on a cost/benefit analysis and that specific costs and benefits are based on how the firm participates and to what degree it is committed to the technology. Commitment can be divided into two areas: the volume of business communications for which the firm uses EDI and the degree to which the firm becomes immersed in EDI as a new way of doing business. The first of these is dependent upon the firm's trading partners, while the second area is dependent upon the results of a series of implementation decisions that the network manager must make.

Three different levels of EDI usage have been proposed to categorize a firm's commitment to the technology [18]. Level-one users transmit only one or two transaction sets (documents) to a limited number of trading partners. Often the system is handled by one department employing the “door-to-door” approach. Level-two users communicate with a large number of trading partners across several industries. The system may be coordinated among several departments and be used to transmit multiple transaction sets. A level-three user is completely immersed in the technology where it is not seen as a new way of transmitting documents, but rather as a whole new way of doing business. These firms use the technology as a catalyst to reengineer the internal business processes to take full advantage of the technology. Within this framework, it is assumed that a firm can improve its EDI usage by investing more in the technology, allowing it to move from one level to another. Barrett and Konsynski [5] also point out that there are different levels of usage, depending upon how much the firm controls the system, how many of the firm’s transactions are implemented, and how many trading partners participate.

This literature suggests that a firm gradually becomes more committed and immersed in the technology by continually adding trading partners, transaction sets, upgrades to the system, and altering their internal processes. Briefly, some of these upgrades and alterations include:

\- Whether to use a value-added network service;

\- Whether to move beyond “door-to-door” EDI and integrate the external IOS with the firm’s internal system;

\- The extent to which data validation checks are used;

\- Whether to bridge internal applications in order to utilize internal EDI;

\- Whether to use EDI as a criterion for reducing the number of trading partners by mandating adoption;

\- Using EDI to shift responsibilities to trading partners such as supplier-managed customer inventories;

\- Whether to use EDI as a catalyst to reengineer business processes internally.

Considering these decisions to be made by the implementing organization, it is relatively straightforward to see that the way in which a firm's trading partners choose to implement and use the system can have a direct impact on how the initiating firm realizes benefits from the system. For example:

\- Due to the volume of traffic, one firm may find it cost-effective to implement a direct line with a particular trading partner rather than utilize a value-added network service preferred by the trading partner.

\- A firm whose trading partner chooses not to integrate its internal system with the external system may not achieve improved data integrity for incoming communications due to manual rekeying of data.

\- A buyer may desire to use the electronic linkage as a window into the operations of its trading partners in order to analyze inventory flows, but may not be able to do so if the trading partner has not bridged the EDI system with other internal applications.

\- A firm may be forced to implement the system internally in a suboptimal manner in order to fulfill the wishes of a powerful buyer that threatens elimination of the firm's business.

These are just a few examples of the interdependency of benefits from IOS. It may be argued that IOS by definition result in interdependent benefits, which is what distinguishes them from traditional internal information systems.

In the following sections, we report the results of two empirical studies designed to examine these issues. For EDI systems, the adopting firm anticipates a reduction in the time and cost required to conduct the business transaction, resulting in improved productivity for buying and receiving personnel. Although fax transmissions can approach EDI in terms of the timeliness of delivery of the document, Kekre and Mukhopadhyay [23] point out that two important elements of the potential gains from using EDI are faster response time to process the document and increased data integrity, both of which result in an overall reduction in the cost of performing the transaction. We examine these two potential benefits in the context of interdependent benefits.

## 3. Impact of Automated Ordering on Order Cycle Time

IN THE FIRST STUDY, WE EXAMINE A LARGE ELECTRONICS COMPANY to study the issue of response time to determine if interdependent benefits exist in terms of realizing reduced cycle time to generate and process a request for purchase (RP) sent from the electronics company to its suppliers. Specifically, the EDI technology has enabled the electronics company to reengineer the process of generating the RP using an integrated automatic vending system. Using the automatic system, certain RPs are automatically routed to the supplier due to a previous quantity/price agreement or option with the supplier. By bypassing the management authorization process and the buyer's vendor selection process, the electronics company stands to reap significant benefits in terms of faster cycle time and reduced personnel costs in generating a new RP. We examine whether the supplier also experiences a reduction in time savings in processing its end of the RP when the electronic document is routed via the automatic system. If the supplier does not reap benefits from the automated process, it will be less likely to participate in this new mode of procurement by placing additional options or agreements with the buyer. Therefore, the way in which the supplier uses the technology will directly affect the electronics company's benefits from the automatic vending system.

## 3.1. The Electronics Company's System

The electronics company has been a leader in the usage of EDI and has been involved in EDI standards formulation for several years. While much of the firm's success in EDI has been based on linkages with its customers, efforts to entice its suppliers to conduct business via EDI have not fared well. For the most part, the suppliers are small companies that are not sophisticated in the usage of information technology. To encourage its suppliers, the company provides a personal computer product free of charge that allows the exchange of purchase orders, requests for purchase (RPs), request for quote, quotes, and other similar electronic documents. This product is one part of a four-module procurementwide system designed to reduce the company's ordering costs. A second module allows requisitioners to prepare and deliver RPs quickly and easily. In addition, the company's buyers utilize an expert system enhanced buyer work station to aid in the process of vendor selection and other buyer-related functions. Finally, an automatic routing system routes the RP through the appropriate management hierarchy to obtain necessary management purchasing authority.

An important part of the overall system is the automatic vending function. When the requisitioner first generates an RP, a database is checked to see if the item to be purchased currently has an open agreement or option with a supplier. If it has, then the RP is automatically sent to the supplier by the automatic vending system to trigger the exercise of the option. The goal is to dramatically improve buyer productivity by bypassing the buyer completely for certain items, thereby allowing the buyer to spend more time on more important procurement management tasks such as negotiations, vendor rating, and vendor selection. In addition, upper management is bypassed, thereby not taking up valuable management time in order to authorize items that already have options in existence. In addition to existing open options, an item may be sent by the automatic vending system for two other reasons. If a supplier has demonstrated itself to be a reliable trading partner, based on price, quality, and turnaround performance, it may qualify as an “automatic vending supplier” such that parts previously procured from that company will continue to be automatically procured from that supplier. Also, if a supplier has demonstrated a historical consistency in terms of the price it quotes for a particular item, that part will continue to be procured via the automatic vending system.

## 3.2. Model Specifications

Clearly, the electronics company would like to have as many items as possible being ordered by the automatic vending system. The goal at this company is to automate as much of the parts-ordering process as allowed by the expert system and EDI technologies. However, in order to increase the number of items vended automatically, the supplier must either be a quality supplier, as defined by the electronics company, or place as many options with them as possible. Based on the description of the order entry system for generating RPs, we expect the automatic vending function to dramatically decrease the time required to process the RP internally (barring exceptions, the automatic vended items should take essentially zero time to process internally). However, if the supplier does not benefit from the item being procured via the automatic vending feature, then it has no incentive to incur additional risk by placing items with the electronics company as options or agreements at prices that may not be appropriate when the option is exercised.

We measure the time required to process the RP internally by the electronics company as the sum of the number of days spent within the internal system before being sent to the suppliers. The system automatically tracks the number of days the RP spends in the buyer's queue, the management queue, and the requisitioner's queue. In addition, the system records the number of days the supplier takes to respond once the RP is sent to the supplier. This is recorded as the number of days the RP is held in the supplier's queue. We hypothesize that the benefits of the automatic vending feature are different for the electronics company and the suppliers. In particular, if the suppliers do not alter their own internal processes to take advantage of the automatic vending function, there may be no reduction in supplier processing time when the electronics company uses the feature. In that case, the suppliers may have little incentive to incur additional risk by accepting additional option contracts with the electronics company, such that the electronics company may not be able to receive the full benefit of the automatic vending capabilities.

Data were collected from one division of the electronics company's procurement department for one month. A total of 2,075 RPs were sent out during the month when the study took place. The first dependent variable of interest is the total number of days the RP spent internally before being sent to the supplier. Total internal processing time was labeled as TOTINT, which is the sum of the days spent in the buyer's queue (BUYQ), the manager's queue (MGRQ), and the requisitioner's queue (REQQ). Note that the variable REQQ was usually zero and exhibited little variance. Another dependent variable in our model is the number of days the RP spent in the supplier's queue, which was labeled as SUPQ. The system rounds off to the nearest whole day, so that it may be possible for an item to spend zero days with the supplier if the supplier responds fast enough. Using this background, we can state the main hypothesis for the cycle time study:

H.1: Items sent by the automatic vending feature to suppliers require less processing time internally, but no difference in the supplier's queue when compared with nonautomatic vended items.

In terms of independent variables, we are interested in which RPs were automatically sent to the suppliers and the reason why they qualified as being sent automatically. Of the 2,075 RPs generated, 150 were sent automatically (AUTO = 7.2 percent). Of these 150 items, 123 were due to existing options exercised by the electronics company, where OPTION is the indicator variable, and 27 were due to historical price data or suppliers being on the automatic vendor list, where OTHER is the indicator variable.

In addition to these indicator variables, other factors about the order itself could affect the length of time needed to process an RP. In particular, the size of the order in terms of number of items ordered and the relative price of the item procured would affect the time spent generating and responding to the RP. For example, within the electronics company, the dollar value of the order would affect the time required by the buyer for vendor selection analysis and management procurement authorization. For the supplier, the total value of the order will affect the time spent generating a realistic quote to be sent back to the electronics company. To account for this, data were collected for the total price of each order. Because this was highly skewed to the right, the log transformation was applied, resulting in the independent variable labeled LNPRICE. In addition, in order to avoid comparing radically different items, care was taken to separate out items with different commodity codes as recorded by the electronics company. The 19 most frequently occurring commodity codes were given indicator variables to allow for the possibility that certain codes would naturally take longer to process. These 19 codes were given indicator variables labeled CCODE1 through CCODE19 and accounted for 71 percent of the 2,075 items ordered. ## 3.3. Results of Cycle Time Study

The effect on the electronics company was as expected. Regardless of the reason why the item was automatically vended to the supplier, the automated feature of the system greatly reduced the amount of time required internally to generate an RP ( $\beta_{1}$ and $\beta_{2}$ in equation [1], significant at p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively). In particular, note that the automatic vending feature frees up the buyer to spend more time on other value-added activities. In addition, the more expensive the item is expected to be, the more time the buyer spends searching for the appropriate vendor and possibly conducting preliminary negotiations via the telephone before sending the RP. The automatic vending feature also frees up management from spending unnecessary time authorizing the purchase of items that essentially have already been approved. Interestingly, it seems that the manager does at times review items that are automatically sent to the supplier for historical reasons ( $\beta_{2}$ in equation [2], not significant). However, the majority of automatically vended items were due to options, and these do bypass the management hierarchy. As shown in Table 1, the dollar value of the order does make a difference in the management review process. In addition, the coefficient for the constant term, $\alpha_{0}$ in equation (2), is negative. This is due to the fact that there is a minimum dollar value below which management authorization is not required.

In terms of the automatic vending feature's impact on the supplier, our hypothesis held. Whether or not the RP was sent automatically due to the supplier having an option with the customer was insignificant in reducing the supplier's response time. On the other hand, if the item was automatically sent because of historical reasons, then the supplier's time to process the order was reduced significantly. The explanation for the second observation is related to causality, and it would seem to us that the supplier had been placed on the automatic vendor list because it had proven itself a reliable supplier in the past, which would include timely response to the electronics company's RPs. As to why having an option exercised would not result in a reduction in time to return the RP, we conjecture that most of the suppliers have not altered their internal processes in order to take advantage of this automatic vending feature. When an RP is sent to the supplier, most suppliers apparently send it through the same routing and approval process regardless of whether it is a new order or an option being exercised. In any case, if suppliers are not willing to reengineer their internal business processes to take advantage of the automated vending capability, they would have little or no incentive to change the way they do business with the electronics company and aggressively attempt to place additional options with them. This is in spite of the fact that the electronics company stands to benefit significantly from that change in procedure. This becomes even more apparent when we consider that in order to place an item with an option for future purchase, the supplier commits to a future price and therefore additional risk, due to the uncertainty of the future business climate.

## 4. Impact of Supplier Integration on Error Generation

ANOTHER POTENTIAL BENEFIT THAT HAS YET TO BE REALIZED by the majority of EDI users is a significant reduction in the cost of data entry. Many industry experts estimate that as much as 85 percent of all EDI messages must be keyed into the system more than once, illustrating the low level of integration of many firms [27]. In order to take full advantage of EDI, it is necessary for firms to integrate their internal information systems with the external system. However, for the majority of firms, the excessive cost of integration is prohibitive. It has been estimated that the typical cost of integrating a single application or transaction set (purchase orders, invoices, etc.) is \$134,000 [27]. Indeed, we would not expect an organization to realize substantial productivity gains if it implements the system in such a way that when it receives a document from its trading partner, it must first print out a hard copy of the document and then key the information into its internal system. Interestingly, the majority of suppliers of the two buyers in our studies were not integrated and used EDI simply as a teletype machine to avoid the postal service and meet the buyer's requirement of using EDI. An important question that has not been addressed in previous EDI research is what impact the trading partner's level of integration has on the initiating firm's realization of improved data integrity from the system. Although we expect integration to impact the firm's benefits from EDI positively, improved data integrity may not be realized by the firm at the other end of the electronic linkage when the trading partner implements the system in such a way that human intervention is required to rekey data into the external system.

In our second empirical study, we examine the impact of the level of integration of the supplier on its ability to successfully transmit error-free advanced shipping notices (ASNs) to a major automotive manufacturer. If integration reduces the number of errors in the data received at the manufacturer's facility, considerably less effort would be required to resolve any discrepancies. Because several of the suppliers provide material on a just-in-time (JIT) basis, minimizing the errors generated from ASNs is a significant concern to the manufacturer.

## 4.1. The Automotive Manufacturer's System

Due in part to foreign competition, the automotive industry has been a leader in utilizing electronic communications with suppliers in order to improve materials management practices. Mukhopadhyay, Kekre, and Kalathur [26] used a longitudinal data set to estimate the total savings from EDI to an automotive manufacturer to be about \$50 per vehicle after controlling for operational complexities. The benefits from EDI in this industry have been several decades in the making. Use of EDI within the automotive industry dates back to 1969 when automotive manufacturers began requiring certain suppliers to transmit information via proprietary formats. Most suppliers found it cumbersome to use more than one standard as they conducted business with multiple customers. As the ANSI X.12 committee developed the X.12 EDI standard, leaders in the automotive industry recognized that the entire industry stood to gain through cooperation and authorized the Automotive Industry Action Group to begin developing guidelines for adopting the X.12 standard within the automotive industry. Driven by efforts of the Big Three manufacturers, virtually all EDI transactions within the industry have migrated to the X.12 standard (see [33] for another empirical study of the impacts of EDI within the automotive industry).

Among the Big Three manufacturers, the manufacturer studied here has been a leader in this migration to the X.12 standard. During 1989, the manufacturer's assembly plants increased the number of assembly suppliers using EDI from 700 to 1,700. By the middle of 1990 virtually all assembly suppliers were on line with the company's EDI system. The goal has been to eliminate all paper transferred between a supplier and the manufacturer and make it 100 percent electronic. In addition, all nonassembly suppliers were to be moved to EDI during the 1990s. Because of its leadership role, this manufacturer was chosen as the research site to investigate the impacts of supplier integration on the manufacturer's data integrity and materials management process.

Using the manufacturer's system, a supplier transmits an advanced shipping notice (ASN) when it prepares to actually ship a part to the manufacturer's facility. The ASN may be transmitted over the manufacturer's own proprietary network or via a selected third-party network and may be transmitted directly from the supplier's facility or using a service bureau. The ASN is received by the manufacturer and is automatically checked for syntactic and content correctness. If an error is detected during the error-checking process, the manufacturer's system will attempt to correct the error itself—for example, inserting a missing data element such as the date. If the error cannot be corrected automatically, it must be corrected manually. Since this can be a very time-consuming activity, the manufacturer desires to have as few errors as possible. One of the main purposes of the manufacturer's system is to improve data integrity in support of its just-in-time (JIT) inventory policy.

In order to improve the integrity of the data sent from the supplier to the manufacturer, suppliers may wish to move beyond the “door-to-door” approach of EDI to an integrated system. In such a system, the ASN would be generated by the supplier’s internal production control system and sent directly to the manufacturer without human intervention. By reducing the number of times the data are keyed into the system, data integrity should be improved. However, the expense of such an integration prohibits the majority of the manufacturer’s suppliers from integrating their systems. As we will see, of the suppliers participating in the study, only a third claimed to have integrated systems.

## 4.2. Model Specifications

Our objective is to predict the frequencies with which ASNs sent to the manufacturer contain errors that result in time spent correcting the errors. Data were collected at the manufacturer's facility from their internal records for information on the 146 assembly suppliers participating in the study. Specifically, the data collected included the number of ASNs sent to the manufacturer and the number of error messages for each supplier recorded over a period of several days. To ensure consistency, we required that the supplier provide an average of at least one ASN per day to the manufacturer, thereby focusing on regular assembly suppliers. With this information, we were able to calculate the number of error messages generated against the ASNs, which were normalized as errors per ASN. This ratio, ERRMSG, is the dependent variable in our model.

Suppliers that chose to participate in the study returned survey information indicating the degree to which their internal systems were integrated. For the purposes of this study, suppliers that indicated that “The incoming/outgoing data is translated and mapped directly into/out of our internal business applications” were considered integrated. An indicator variable, LVLINTGT, was used for this independent variable. Of the 146 suppliers involved in the study, 49, or 33.6 percent, indicated having systems matching the above description. The main hypothesis for the integration study can now be stated.

H.2: ASNs sent from suppliers that have integrated their internal transaction processing system with the external EDI system will have a lower rate of error messages per ASN.

In order to tease out the effects of supplier integration on error generation, several variables related to the technology itself and specific business factors were included as independent variables in the model.

## 4.2.1. Technology-Specific Variables

Clearly, the experience of the supplier will affect the number of errors generated by the supplier. Therefore, we took into account the date the supplier went on line with the manufacturer for the ASN transaction set. This was converted to experience with the system as of the time data were collected and assigned the number of MONTHS of experience with the technology.

Suppliers could use an older or newer version of the X.12 standard to communicate with the manufacturer. In order to take into account the possible effect the version of the standard could have on error generation, each supplier was assigned an indicator variable zero or one distinguishing which version it used. This was coded as VRSN. Because the newer version had only recently been released, we expected that use of the newer version of the X.12 standard would result in increased errors due to learning and adjustment reasons.

## 4.2.2. Business-Specific Variables

Because the automotive manufacturers have all stated that EDI competence is a requirement for continuing as a supplier in the industry, the degree to which a supplier is dependent upon the automotive industry should affect their motivation to use EDI effectively. Suppliers were asked to indicate the percentage of their sales to the automotive industry. The variable, PCTAUTO, was appropriately assigned to each supplier. The higher the percentage of business with the automotive industry, the greater the need for providing quality service. Therefore, we expect that the higher this percentage, the lower the error rate.

Certain suppliers receive the manufacturer's JIT schedule and are designated as "quick response" suppliers. This indicates a close trading partner relationship with the supplier and requires that those suppliers provide a higher level of service to the manufacturer. An indicator variable, QCK, was used to signify these suppliers. We conjectured that QCK suppliers were more likely to have higher error rates since they have greater time constraints to meet deadlines.

Due to the complexity of their business environment, larger trading partners may have higher error rates due to the number of customers and high-volume orders. To take this into account, we asked suppliers to provide data concerning their most recent annual sales figures. Because this was highly skewed to the right, we used the log transformation, which was coded as LNSLS.

The model outlined above can be summarized as:

$$
\begin{array}{r} \mathrm{ERRMSG} = \alpha_ {0} + \beta_ {1} \text {MONTHS} + \beta_ {2} \text {VRSN} + \beta_ {3} \text {PCTAUTO} \\ + \beta_ {4} \text {QCK} + \beta_ {5} \text {LNSLS} + \beta_ {6} \text {LVLINTGT} + \varepsilon . \end{array}\tag{5}
$$

A multiple regression analysis was conducted; the results are shown in Table 2. Special care was taken to ensure that there was not a high degree of correlation among the independent variables. Of the variables in the study, only two correlation coefficients were over 0.23 (these two were around 0.32).

## 4.3. Results of Integration Study

Several significant variables appear to impact the generation of error messages. A move to reduce error messages will result in substantial reduction in the need for personnel to intervene in the system and correct the ASN once it reaches the manufacturer. For our purposes, note that the level of integration is significant at the p < 0.1 level and, as expected, suppliers that are integrated tend to have fewer error messages. This finding supports our hypothesis that the method in which a trading partner or follower implements the IOS has a direct effect on the expected benefits at the initiating firm in terms of improved data integrity. While the benefits to the manufacturer were as expected, the benefit of integration for the supplier is unclear. An analysis conducted by Srinivasan, Kekre, and Mukhopadhyay [33] indicate that supplier integration does have an impact on the supplier's shipment and parts rejection performance. In that case, the supplier's level of integration will reflect on the supplier's overall quality rating, which in turn will impact its opportunity for future business with the customer. While the level of benefits from having the supplier integrate its internal system with the external IOS may be unclear, this study illustrates the existence of the interdependency of benefits that exist with IOS. Clearly, in an effort to improve its level of data integrity, the automotive manufacturer would desire to have as many suppliers integrated as possible; however, the cost of such a system would be prohibitive for many suppliers. This seems to be the case, considering two-thirds of the suppliers operate “door-to-door” EDI systems.

## 5. Implications of Interdependent Benefits

ALTHOUGH THE TWO SYSTEMS STUDIED WERE BOTH USED to exchange multiple documents, the majority of usage was directed at the two processes studied: the transmission of RPs and suppliers' responses to the electronics company and the transmission of ASNs to the automotive manufacturer. In both cases, suppliers were requested to make optional buyer-initiated modifications to their system or process that were to result in specific benefits to the buyer. In both cases, the buyer realized benefits from the modification when implemented; how the suppliers benefited from the change, however, was not clear.

Because the incentives for the suppliers to make these changes to their internal systems and processes seem quite low, suppliers will be hesitant to invest in these changes, thereby limiting the benefit buyers may receive from these interorganizational systems. We have pointed out that powerful buyers often mandate that their suppliers adopt the EDI technology as a prerequisite for continuing to do business with that buyer. These buyers often use the requirement that suppliers adopt EDI as one way of reducing the overall number of suppliers $[3, 9]$ . While the two buyers studied in this research did not require that their suppliers adopt the internal changes the buyer desired, we believe that this is a natural next step for powerful buyers with substantial leverage over their suppliers. By mandating how their trading partners use the technology internally, these buyers stand to reap the full benefits of the technology while continuing to develop strong long-term relationships with those suppliers who are willing to proceed with the internal upgrading of their systems. We call this business partner reengineering, which is the radical redesign of the trading partner's internal business processes and technology implementation in order to achieve dramatic reductions in transaction and product costs while improving services and product quality.

Venkatraman [35] points out that more extensive business transformation should result in a greater level of benefit from the design effort. He proposes five levels of business transformation:

• Level 1—Localized exploitation;

• Level 2—Internal integration;

• Level 3—Business process redesign;

• Level 4—Business network redesign;

• Level 5—Business scope redefinition.

Business partner reengineering as we define it would correspond to a level 4 transformation. According to Venkatraman,

Level four is business network redesign, concerned with the reconfiguration of the scope and tasks of the business network involved in the creation and delivery of products and services. This includes the business tasks both within and outside the formal boundaries of a focal organization and the consequent redesign of this “virtual business network” through IT capabilities. Thus, electronic integration across key partners in the changed business network becomes the dominant strategic management challenge [35, p. 128].

Within the framework of structured analysis and design, we typically model suppliers as external entities that are “external sources or destination of data, which . . . interact with the system but are outside its boundary” [32, p. 157]. Within the context of interorganizational systems, it becomes necessary to redefine what we consider to be “outside” the firm boundary and therefore beyond our control. For large buyers with substantial leverage over their suppliers, mandating changes to suppliers internal systems and processes is now becoming possible. By formally modeling the trading partner’s internal processes, possibly with the use of an enhanced CASE (computer-aided software engineering) tool, buyers will be in a better position to recognize opportunities for potential domains of change at the trading partner’s facility. If buyers possess substantial market leverage over their suppliers, they may be in a position to mandate change (with or without a subsidy) at the supplier’s facility. In cases where the buyer does not possess adequate leverage, suppliers may view the reengineering effort as a source of revenue should the buyer be interested in at least partially funding the redesign. By formally modeling the processes at the “external” entity, areas of change resulting in mutual benefit may be more easily recognized.

In the studies presented here we raise the issue that interdependent benefits can significantly impact the benefits firms receive from IOS. These studies have several limitations that should be mentioned. First, although we demonstrate the interdependency of benefits, we do not actually show that unequal benefits exist. Instead, it is assumed that firms do not gain the same benefit. Second, because the studies examine only two types of interdependency and two types of documents exchanged, it cannot be directly inferred that the interdependency exists in the other areas suggested in section 2. In addition, it is not clear what form these interdependencies would take in settings outside the United States where trading partner relationships may be more highly coupled (in Japan, for example). Finally, the statistical power of these models is relatively low. Additional empirical research is needed to validate these results.

## 6. Directions For Future Research

These studies illustrate the potential impact of interdependent benefits on the buyer's ability to reap expected benefits from the buyer-initiated system. If interdependent benefits are not taken into account during the feasibility phase of the project, it is possible that buyers will not gain the benefits originally anticipated. We recommend that during the initial analysis conducted for the feasibility phase of the project, the initiator should consider the costs associated with being the initiator of these systems. Specific models have been developed that discuss the potential subsidy costs initiators often face as they seek to obtain full trading partner participation in IOS [see 25, 30, 31]. However, there has been no analytical modeling to date relating to the second issue of how to give followers incentives to implement the technology in a way that is most beneficial to the initiator. As Clemons and Row [12] point out, game theoretic modeling should be conducted to arrive at efficient mechanisms to distribute the surplus generated from IOS.

Because initiators may attempt to gain compliance with external, potentially recalcitrant trading partners, IOS possess inherently more risk to initiating firms than more traditional internal information systems projects. The relationship between initiators and followers suggests that it is the initiator who must take the responsibility for much of the additional risk from these systems. Analytically building in this risk factor into the cost/benefit sharing mechanism should also be pursued. In addition, identifying factors that allow the initiator to pass on part of this risk to its trading partners should also be addressed.

Little research has been done to estimate the risk associated with specific types of information systems projects. Cash et al. [7] have proposed that there are three main factors to consider when estimating the risk of an information systems project: relative size of the proposed system, familiarity with the technology, and the degree to which the application domain is structured as opposed to unstructured. In a similar way, research needs to be done to derive specific IOS risk factors. While exploratory in nature, this research suggests that two of these factors would be the initial cooperativeness of the trading partners (their willingness to alter their own internal processes when adopting the technology) and the relative leverage the initiator has in the trading partner relationship (whether or not the initiator can make a credible threat to force the trading partner to implement the system in a particular way). Future IOS theory building should consider what additional factors contribute to the risk incurred by firms initiating IOS. As an increasing number of firms continue to link electronically with their trading partners, the increased risk associated with IOS is a topic that deserves further study. If a firm uses traditional approaches to estimate costs and benefits of the proposed information system without consideration of specific IOS risk factors, the firm may find itself with fewer benefits than expected at a higher cost than was originally forecasted. In this case, the result likely will be a misallocation of information systems funds.
