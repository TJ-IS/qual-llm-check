# An Examination of Information Technology's Impact on the Value of Information and Expertise: Implications for Organizational Change

JEFFREY L. SAMPLER AND JAMES E. SHORT

JEFFREY L. SAMPLER is Assistant Professor of Information Management at London Business School. He received his Ph.D. in 1992 from the University of Pittsburgh. His research interests include the relationship of information technology to cognitive decision theory and implications for strategy and organization design. His work has appeared in such journals as Journal of Management Information Systems, and Accounting, Management and Information Technology.

JAMES E. SHORT is Assistant Professor of Information Management at London Business School. He received his Ph.D. in 1985 from Massachusetts Institute of Technology. His research interests include the impact of electronic integration on industry structure and the relationship between information technology and business design. His work has appeared in such journals as Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Journal of Management Studies, and Sloan Management Review. He received the 1991 Richard Beckhard Prize from Sloan Management Review for his work in information technology and business process redesign.

ABSTRACT: This paper is organized around two research questions and three issue areas in business reengineering. We address: (1) To what extent does environmental volatility affect the rate of accumulation or decay of critical firm assets? and (2) How can expertise and information related assets be operationalized in the context of process-based ("radical") organizational restructuring? With respect to business reengineering, we address: (1) How can differing needs for process reengineering projects be characterized? (2) To what extent does organizational resistance to reengineering projects differ by categories of need? and (3) To what extent are failure rates experienced by reengineering practitioners traceable to lack of alignment between project needs, planning agendas, and dominant modes of organizational resistance? We develop an explanatory framework based on two constructs, expertise half-life and information half-life, and apply these to help analyze current practices in business reengineering. We posit that under certain conditions high project failure rates are associated with weak coupling between reengineering project objectives and the firm's general business and information systems planning agendas. Under other conditions, this weak coupling is associated with successful projects. We conclude with recommendations for methodological approaches and suggested research extensions.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: business reengineering, core competencies, strategic assets, time-based competition, value of expertise.

## 1. Introduction

THIS PAPER IS ORGANIZED AROUND TWO CENTRAL RESEARCH QUESTIONS and three related practice-based issue areas in business reengineering. We address the questions: (1) To what extent does environmental volatility affect the rate of accumulation or decay of critical firm assets? and (2) How can expertise and information related assets be operationalized in the context of process-based ("radical") organizational restructuring? With respect to business reengineering practice, we focus on three issue areas: (1) How can differing needs for process reengineering projects be characterized? (2) To what extent does organizational resistance to reengineering projects differ by categories of need? and (3) To what extent are failure rates experienced by reengineering practitioners traceable to lack of alignment between project needs, planning agendas, and dominant modes of organizational resistance?

Following a background section in which we briefly summarize relevant literatures, we develop our explanatory framework, then elaborate it with examples drawn from fieldwork in business strategic planning and business process redesign (BPR). We introduce two constructs, expertise criticality, derived from strategy research, and time criticality, derived from research on organizational processes and time-based competitiveness, and apply these constructs to help analyze current practices in business reengineering. While our constructs and research questions are theoretically derived, and a central goal is to contribute to work-in-progress on organizational processes, at the outset we were both puzzled and intrigued by reports from practitioners of very high project failure rates for reengineering projects. Numbers on the order of 70 percent and higher are reported widely in practitioner circles, irrespective of industry, firm size, or—surprisingly—prior experience [27, pp. 25, 200]. Based on prior work in strategic information systems (SIS) planning and BPR, our working hypothesis was that the observed high failure rates of reengineering projects were most likely associated with weak coupling between project objectives and the firm’s general business and SIS planning agendas. This conjecture, however, was based unscientifically on our familiarity with planning and process research and related fieldwork in BPR, and we elected here to address the question and our tentative hypothesis more systematically.

Our fieldwork was conducted as part of a larger research effort investigating organizational processes. Over the last four years, the authors have conducted both structured and semistructured interviews in 14 U.S.- and Europe-based companies involved with one or more significant process reengineering projects (see appendix). In all firms, at least four stakeholders were interviewed: project (reengineering) sponsor, project manager (operational), major customer, and business planner. On projects involving a clearly defined information systems (IS) support role, the IS project manager or systems development manager was interviewed. While this article draws only on our interpretations of semistructured interviews and individual case analyses, detailed, structured instruments were administered in three of the fourteen companies and results from these studies will be reported in later work. Of course, no attempt is made here to argue the scientific validity of our proposed explanatory framework under traditional norms of hypothesis-testing research. Rather, consistent with the norms of midrange theory development, our purpose here is to suggest new variables and a logic of inquiry from which opportunities for normal science research will open up and become necessary.

## 2. Background

STRATEGY THINKING AND STRATEGIC MANAGEMENT RESEARCH have gradually shifted in recent years from a focus on tangible assets as the source of competitive advantage to an emphasis on the accumulation of both tangible and intangible assets, the latter including knowledge [51], core competencies [43], learning [44, 46], and “invisible assets” such as corporate culture or brand image [33]. These arguments extend both to the accumulation of multiple competencies, which are seen to be more difficult to identify and imitate than a single skill [3, 47, 49], and to the rate of accumulation of specialized skills, which in one sense, although simplified, can be thought of as a key aspect of the internal rate of learning within the firm.

With regard to the desirability of acquiring multiple competencies, Dierickx and Cool [15] posit that the imitability of organizational capabilities is related to the processes by which they are acquired. For example, time compression diseconomies (the difficulty of acquiring capabilities quickly), asset mass efficiency (whereby the initial level of capability influences the rate of accumulation), interconnectedness (where the rate of accumulation is influenced by the level of other asset stocks), and causal ambiguity (lack of clarity about how a capability works) all influence the degree to which organizational capabilities bestow competitive advantage.

All other things being equal, firms that are able to accumulate more complex resources should be able to execute more complex strategies and thereby sustain competitive advantage over those firms lacking these skills $[3, 28]$ . Moreover, those firms that are able to accumulate complex resources more quickly should in principle be able to execute complex strategies faster $[45]$ . Prahalad and Hamel $[43]$ summarize much of this work in noting that the logic underlying many of these ideas involves “the collective learning in the organization, especially how to coordinate diverse production skills and integrate multiple streams of technology” (p. 82), all with a view toward accomplishing this quickly.

Echoing shifts in strategy research, organization research itself has been shifting gradually away from a focus on hierarchical organization and bureaucratic structures that provide control over activities divided into small parts, and toward an emphasis on flexible, learning organizations, where the organizational “image” is one of a continuously changing organization solving problems through interdependent self-organizing processes $[12, 17]$ . Research dealing with time criticality and/or speed in organizational settings has been focused broadly in two streams of work: research on the timeliness of organizational activities, such as decision-making speed $[6, 19]$ , attitudes and behaviors regarding time horizons in planning and/or other organizational activities $[23]$ , and time synchronization and/or compression in task dependencies and work group settings $[39, 45]$ . A second body of work, financially oriented, is concerned with the valuation of tangible and intangible assets held by firms, in particular strategies for managing asset valuation as a function of many factors, including time $[2, 15, 16]$ .

We are concerned here with the former, which has focused on technologies and procedures for increasing timeliness and speed in organizational settings. Technologies include those related to increasing information processing and/or communications flow (for example, computers, telecommunications, and high-speed machinery). Procedures include both individual and work group processes, such as productivity tools, just-in-time inventory (JIT), or cycle time compression across functions, such as product development and engineering, through parallel rather than sequential work task organization and information flow $[8]$ .

Few writers, however, have examined how time-related behaviors, attitudes, and beliefs existing in organizations or in the organization's environment interact to affect how the firm approaches decision making and strategic planning [48]. At the work-group level, Lawrence and Lorsch [36] argue that different functional departments in firms are characterized not only by differing degrees of specialized knowledge, but also by differences in work attitudes and behaviors, including those related to time, specifically in attitudes regarding the distance into the future over which work is projected. Sales departments, for example, operate on the shortest time horizon; research groups the longest. The variety of time-related attitudes and behaviors and the division of labor causes organizations to operate within a “plurality” of time horizons in individual work settings and planning—that is, organizations face potentially conflicting short-term and long-term goals [9, 13]. Such pluralities affect coordination costs and directly influence the choice of coordination mechanisms used to synchronize task dependencies [39].

The dilemma, of course, is that while organizational literatures on planning, decision making, and time-based competition are suggestive of general research questions on decision-making speed, organization “response times,” and the like, they do not address directly the question of how environmental volatility affects the rate of accumulation or decay of a firm’s critical assets or how the accumulation or decay of these assets is influenced by organizational change strategies such as reengineering. We turn now to defining our time-related constructs and framework, which we offer as a preliminary exploration in this direction.

## 3. Expertise Half-Life and Information Half-Life: An Exploratory Framework

OUR CENTRAL PREMISE IS THAT INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY (IT) influences the rate of decay of expertise and information through (1) altering the rate at which other firms can acquire these intangible assets, and (2) making irrelevant existing stocks of these intangible assets because of fundamental shifts in production paradigms. We focus on IT's ability to affect expertise and information differentially, because of the importance of these factors in the firm's competitive position.

Most existing work in reengineering focuses primarily on rethinking the flow of physical goods, and underemphasizes flows of intangible resources. In this section, we develop an exploratory framework in order to describe reengineering projects where expertise and information are critical factors.

We first define expertise and information. In contrast to previous definitions, we distinguish between individual and organizational expertise. Traditionally, an expert has been defined as an individual who (1) possesses large amounts of information, (2) has this information organized around principles, and (3) can retrieve this information efficiently via problem-solving heuristics $[7, 35]$ . Organizational expertise has received much less attention. In general, the concept is operationalized as competencies or skills shared by large numbers of people engaged in similar work activities $[14, 50]$ . In short, organizational expertise has been grounded in “patterns” of information use within a firm.

Information is defined as data located within an organizational context that has value through actions taken. Context is the reference frame for interpreting data, and may be operationalized at individual, work-unit, or organizational levels.

Figure 1 illustrates the proposed framework using the two constructs. The Y axis, “expertise half-life,” represents the decay rate of the firm’s expertise resulting from the speed with which the firm’s environment and industry factors are changing. The X axis, “information half-life,” represents the decay rate of information value to the firm. In the following sections, we use the framework (1) to examine the impact of IT on information and expertise, (2) to identify different characteristics of reengineering projects across the four quadrants, and (3) to describe likely patterns of organizational resistance to reengineering projects.

## 3.1. Long Expertise Half-Life and Long Information Half-Life

In this quadrant, the rates of expertise and information decay are low, and both expertise and information retain their value over time. For example, as a rule, back office support processes in firms do not utilize highly volatile or rapidly changing expertise, or use or produce information that is, in strategic terms, highly time-critical. In accounts receivable processing, for example, it may be important that the vice president of sales know that a key account is 60 days outstanding (in paying its invoices), but once that information has been produced, there is no time decay in its utility until the invoices are finally settled.

Figure 1. Exploratory Framework Contrasting Expertise and Information Half-Life

Shared financial services organizations, such as those operating in companies like General Electric, Baxter International, and Digital Equipment Corporation, are organized on the principle that similar back office financial functions, such as cash application, general ledger, accounts payable, and accounts receivable, are necessary but not high-value—hence the consolidation into “shared services” organizations of these functions to improve processing efficiencies and to reduce costs $[29, 30, 41]$ . Of course, it is important that these functions are performed in a timely manner, but the information they use and produce is not, in and of itself, of high strategic potential. The impact of IT in this quadrant is simply the substitution of information technology for manual activity.

This cell describes the primary nature of activities undertaken by most of the popular, easily understood reengineering “success stories.” For example, Ford’s reengineered accounts payable system $[26]$ falls in this cell. At Ford, the goal of reengineering was to introduce radical efficiency improvements through streamlining work flows and removing redundancies. Process maps, whereby redundant steps and/or obvious inefficiencies are revealed and therefore subject to redesign, are standard tools, with the specified end result increases in throughput, time compression, and often fewer employees involved $[27]$ .

Altering work flows, eliminating waste on a large scale, and streamlining information flows in an organization are, of course, never easy $[4, 22, 34]$ . Resistance is generally the strongest from those directly affected, and for long-studied reasons they will oppose perceived reductions in their individual resource control and/or work focus and scope. In this quadrant, therefore, resistance comes from those who are directly affected. Executive management, personally unaffected by and distant from the immediacy of the change process, view project initiatives in this quadrant as candidates for benchmarking against competitors, or for outsourcing if the firm does not meet industry standards for efficiency.

As these examples suggest, reengineering projects in this quadrant need only loose coupling with corporate strategy, most likely through traditional coordination mechanisms such as generic business planning processes and/or lateral communications through teams, hierarchy, or direct contact $[22]$ . Projects here optimize local operations, and changes necessary to areas of the firm outside the reengineering process will be subject to only minor modifications at interfaces or points of contact with other parts of the organization. Ford's reengineered accounts payable department has no effect on a car body design engineer; a loan officer in a bank is not directly affected by the bank now sorting its checks using automated check sorting machines; Baxter's reengineered cash application process has little impact on a product manager in the company's diagnostics division. Reengineering activities in this quadrant can be characterized as single-loop learning $[1]$ —that is, improvements within an existing operational paradigm. Perhaps because projects in this quadrant do not directly affect many areas of the business other than where the project was initiated, and therefore are not subject to the same degree of interfunctional and/or other forms of organizational resistance, this is the easiest quadrant in which to act and thus most of the popular “success stories” are here.

## Long Expertise Half-Life and Short Information Half-Life

In this quadrant, short information half-life denotes information that is time-sensitive—that is, the value or strategic potential of information erodes quickly. It follows that the shorter the information half-life—that is, the more quickly the value of information decays—then the shorter the window of opportunity for the firm to act preferentially upon it. Long expertise half-life denotes circumstances where the rate of expertise decay is slow. Typically, this would characterize circumstances of industry stability and the sustainability of existing expertise related advantages.

IT is a major force in shortening the half-life of information value in firms. This occurs through (1) the capability of IT to process large amounts of information quickly, and (2) the capability of advanced telecommunications to transfer information across geographically dispersed areas instantaneously. The net result of the dissemination of these IT capabilities has been to shift many businesses that depended on preferential access to information from the lower left quadrant (long expertise half-life and long information half-life) to this quadrant.

For example, the toy chain, Toys R Us, is widely cited for its ability to respond to information quickly and thus derive competitive advantage $[45]$ . At Toys R Us, daily sales from each of its over 350 stores is quickly consolidated and analyzed at corporate headquarters. Management can tell which products are selling, which products are not, and where. This allows the company to monitor closely the sales of newly introduced products, as well as existing stock items. In one interesting case, a new product—“kids-scooters”—was introduced in a trial product launch of 10,000 units. When the initial lot sold in two days, senior management knew this quickly and was alerted to the market potential of a new product trend. The following season, with adequate inventory on hand, Toys R Us sold over one million kids-scooters $[20]$ .

In financial services, the preferential access to time-sensitive information characterizes the livelihood of many firms, including Wall Street arbitrageurs, stock exchange specialists, and other broker-type intermediaries. The impact of IT in this industry has greatly increased the potential for faster execution of trades and for very rapid dissemination of information on completed transactions. In doing so, IT has decreased the time window of opportunity for profitably trading on information. For example, one Tokyo firm recently spent \$35 million on a supercomputer in order to gain a two-second advantage in arbitraging stock futures [18]. Technology investments of this sort will continue to decrease the half-life of information in this industry as IT allows arbitrageurs to locate and take advantage of price asymmetries in financial markets more quickly.

Similarly, stock trading firms have made large investments in IT to improve their ability to execute faster trades. An example is BZW's TRADE system, an automatic order-entry system for retail brokerage transactions $[10, 11]$ . TRADE automatically routes orders to BZW's traders, then executes orders at the prevailing best prices on, in this case, the London Stock Exchange. System benefits include price guarantees and shortened trade execution for customers. On the other hand, implementation was initially resisted by market-makers at the firm, who feared that their role would be reduced.

Unlike in the previous quadrant, where reengineering focused primarily on workflow redesign, reengineering in this cell assumes a wholly different character in that its principle focus is on redesigning information flows. Here the emphasis is on optimizing information flows throughout the organization, reflecting a focus of critical strategic importance to the firm. Delays in information availability to different parts of the organization, as well as delays in the collection of information, are targets for radical time compression. Generally, the emphasis is on minimizing the time from occurrence of an event (for example, “kids-scooters” sales) until the information provides the basis for strategic decision making $[6]$ .

Other authors have asserted that in addition to redesigning information flows, organization decision points may also need to be relocated $[32]$ . Our example of Toys R Us describes this situation. Here, nationwide store information is consolidated centrally each day, to reduce time delays between information production, information analysis, and decisions made by senior management. Decision points have been relocated from individual store managers to executive management.

It should be noted that although these changes to information flows and decision points may radically alter the character of decision making in firms, companies are still in the same core business. For example, BZW still trades stocks, even though the roles of their customers' brokers and of their own market-makers may have changed. Toys R Us still sells toys, even if store managers make different decisions than before. In other words, IT can differentially affect expertise and information half-lives without changing business scope.

In this quadrant, resistance to change is dominated again by those directly affected by change initiatives. However, the scope for organizational resistance is much greater as the strategic potential of activities under scrutiny is higher. All other things being equal, stakeholders in this quadrant possess greater organizational autonomy and decision rights, but the effect of IT has the potential to destroy such positionally derived authority. In the case of stock traders, for example, BZW's TRADE system, in principle, can destroy much of what the customers' brokers do.

A traditional vehicle for minimizing resistance to organizational change is to focus explicitly on planning and implementation routines that emphasize common goals and coordinated task completion. In circumstances where the focus of reengineering is on information flows, not task activity work flows, traditional planning routines may be incomplete. In this case, the planning progression would involve an examination of how IT devalues existing roles and competencies derived from preferential information access, an exploration of new roles and strategies to secure them, redesign of supporting information based processes and/or new operations, and design of new IT capability. New roles and strategies must be developed; alternatively, the firm may have to exit lines of business, downsize, or fold entirely.

## 3.3. Short Expertise Half-Life and Long Information Half-Life

In contrast to our previous discussion, this quadrant denotes circumstances where the rate of expertise decay is rapid, but the decay rate of information is slow. The subtle distinction here lies in the ability to separate competencies from information attributes. Recalling our shared services example from the first quadrant discussed, we argued that the information used in back office support is necessary but not of high strategic value to the firm. In this quadrant, the information generated by support processes is largely unaffected by shifts in competencies.

More specifically, we are arguing that the information used does not change, but the methods for categorizing it and/or the heuristics for retrieving it have changed. Perhaps the easiest way to visualize this is to note the distinction between science and engineering. Scientific knowledge builds slowly from the accumulation of facts. Engineering, however, which is concerned with the application of scientific knowledge, may change the context or requirements for using scientific knowledge.

For example, Zuboff's study [53] of the Tiger Creek paper mill highlighted how operators had to interpret information in new ways in order to build expertise to operate a new production control process. In the old production system, operators made adjustments to machine production parameters based on qualitative information (for example, sight, sound, and smell). However, when a new computer-based process control system was installed, operators were relocated from the production floor to a central control room. Here they made adjustments to production parameters based on quantitative information displayed on dials, gauges, and computer display screens, rather than qualitative information obtained via senses and observation on the production floor. The “science” underlying paper production remained the same, but the “engineering” of the production process was altered in the way operators and technology interacted.

Tiger Creek provides an example where new IT destroyed the value of existing expertise, without radically devaluing the underlying information needed in paper production. However, in most circumstances, new IT simultaneously destroys the value both of expertise and of existing information. This destruction highlights the risk to strategies based upon intangible assets such as expertise and information.

For example, Pitney-Bowes is at risk from word processing, fax-modems, and voice mail. Dictaphone technology and its associated work skills and information content are being displaced by new office information technologies. Federal Express, UPS, and other overnight delivery services are at risk from electronic document transfer and facsimile machines. Music recording companies and book publishers are at risk from the development of high bandwidth public “information superhighways” that can be used to distribute music or books directly to end consumers [21].

In situations described by short expertise half-life and long information half-life, again, it is not simple work flows that are the focus for reengineering. The fundamental skills or expertise of the firm are at risk of obsolescence. Thus, firms that are reengineering must seek new methods of employing existing expertise or developing new skills. Planning for reengineering, however, is difficult in this quadrant, as the firm is seeking a new operating paradigm $[1]$ . Project selection is driven primarily by questions of strategic fit and change leadership. IT leadership would invariably be high-risk in this quadrant—any competencies destroyed would not be in the IT organization, and resistance to technology-driven organizational change would follow known problems with large-scale systems implementation projects $[38, 40]$ . The most likely planning progression would be similar to that of the previous quadrant: examination of the potential ways for IT to devalue existing competencies and advantages, examination of new strategies and selection among them, redesign of processes or design of new operations to support them, and design of IT support.

The type of strategic options enabled, however, and the scope of process redesign will differ significantly. In particular, in the lower right quadrant of figure 1 (long expertise half-life and short information half-life), there may be few attractive options other than exit the markets for those services that no longer provide profitable opportunities. Firms in this cell, as in the case of Wall Street arbitrageurs, face increasingly sizeable IT investments to gain smaller and smaller time-related information-processing advantages. Conversely, in the upper left-hand quadrant (short expertise half-life and long information half-life), it may be possible to exit portions of the value chain where the firm's expertise has been eroded, and acquire strategic partners or other alliances to perform these functions.

## Short Expertise Half-Life and Short Information Half-Life

This quadrant denotes activities with rapidly decaying expertise and highly time-sensitive information. Firms in this quadrant are what Eccles and Crane [17] define as self-designing organizations—that is, organizations that devolve a high degree of autonomy throughout the firm, allowing individuals to structure their own work unit and the work unit's relationships with others. Eccles and Crane go on to posit that networked organizational structures are the most likely organizational form in conditions requiring constant redefinition of tasks and high levels of interaction among individuals in the firm (p. 135).

Business reengineering in this context loses much of its applicability. The logic of reengineering is to redefine structural inflexibility through attention to horizontal work flows. Faced with rapidly changing environmental conditions, firms in this quadrant “hustle” to adapt to continually changing conditions $[5, 31]$ . In the absence of a formal (bureaucratic) organizational structure, processes themselves can be considered temporal and subject to the same task-specific design criteria used to structure work group interactions in networked organizations.

It should be noted, however, that reengineering in this quadrant advances into largely uncharted territory. There is no theory base from which to derive general research questions regarding organizational networks and business processes $[12]$ . There are, however, cases of innovative firms that provide some direction to researchers using inductive approaches. For example, 3M's policy of allowing research scientists to spend time on projects of their own selection has lead to the development of many new products outside of the firm's traditional product market scope $[42]$ . Motorola has accomplished a difficult, if not dramatic, shift of its core business model from semiconductor chip manufacture to cellular phones and mobile communications devices. Investigation of the dynamics of continual innovation processes is beyond the scope of this paper, but represents a rich area of future research inquiry.

## 5. Conclusions and Future Research Directions

WE BEGAN THIS PAPER BY ASKING TWO RESEARCH QUESTIONS: (1) To what extent does environmental volatility affect the rate of accumulation or decay of critical firm assets? and (2) How can expertise- and information-related assets be operationalized in the context of process-based ("radical") organizational restructuring? Our hope was to inform both researchers and practitioners involved in business reengineering. By conceptualizing IT's capability to destroy both tangible and intangible organizational assets in our exploratory framework, we arrived at two central observations regarding strategic assets, IT, and process reengineering: first, IT's capability to destroy tangible as well as intangible assets, such as expertise, information, or organizational knowledge, suggests a far more dramatic and complex role for IT in the development of core competencies in the firm. Second, reengineering efforts that do not acknowledge the important difference between restructuring physical assets (tasks, work flows) and rethinking the flow or characteristics of intangible assets (expertise, information) increasingly are incomplete. We posited that high reengineering project failure rates may be associated with this lack of acknowledgment in reengineering practice.

In elaborating the framework, we found that most of the popular reengineering “success stories” fell into one quadrant, long expertise half-life and long information half-life, and projects in this cell had only loose coupling with the firm’s broader strategic and information systems planning agendas. We suggested that successful

BPR projects tended to fall in this quadrant precisely because they focused principally on restructuring physical assets, and, with this emphasis, tight coupling with strategy and IS planning agendas was not required. Conversely, the “self-designing” organizations that tended to populate the upper right-hand quadrant, short expertise half-life and short information half-life, had little need for reengineering. Here the extreme volatility of the firm’s environment and its internal allocation of tasks and work flows negated much of the logic behind reengineering. The upper left-hand quadrant, short expertise half-life and long information half-life, and its diagonal opposite, long expertise half-life and short information half-life, exhibited different roles for IT. In the upper right-hand quadrant, Zuboff [53] suggested that because information technology could be designed with different intentions (to “automate” or “informate” work), it had very different implications for the workers at Tiger Creek (controlling and deskilling or empowering and upskilling, respectively). Conversely, in the example of the impact of the Toys R Us daily sales tracking system, the motive behind much of the size of IT investment was to fuel time-based competition—in short, reacting to customer buying patterns more quickly, which should translate into additional sales. Both cells, however, were seen to have similar challenges for reengineering activities.

We have highlighted a number of theoretical and methodological issues in acquiring and retaining strategic assets in the firm and the role of information technology in shaping them. There are two key problem areas for theory development. The first relates to the model of inquiry in use. Given the novelty of studying IT as a means of destroying tangible and intangible assets of the firm, and the difficulty in defining and validating constructs measuring intangibles such as knowledge or expertise, systematic research cases and cross-case analyses $[19, 52]$ are especially promising. Specific propositions derived from empirical cases can then be tested across multiple settings using variance techniques to build a cumulative body of knowledge. However, because of the potential difficulties in obtaining valid organizational data due to the long observation period necessary, strategic modeling, with its ability to model time-related activities and decay rates, appears to have high potential for exploring many of the ideas developed here.

The second problem area involves implementation of the case study method. Our ideas and framework were based on semistructured interviews and archival data in fourteen firms. Multiple cases augment external validity and help guard against observer bias. Yin [52] argues that the underlying logic of multiple cases is similar to that guiding multiple experiments in that each case should be selected so that it “either (a) predicts similar results (a literal replication), or (b) produces contrary results but for predictable reasons (a theoretical replication)” [52, pp. 48–49]. Since the objective of our research is to produce constructs and, ultimately, theory that will be relevant to different managerial situations and capable of describing conditions of success and failure, a methodology including both literal and theoretical replication is required [37]. The development of such practice-oriented methodologies will require further refinement of existing field research methodologies and, very likely, the development of new ones.

## Appendix: Research Methodology

OUR CONSTRUCT DEVELOPMENT AND EXPLORATORY FRAMEWORK are based on ongoing research on organization processes and process redesign at the Centre for Research in Information Management (CRIM) at London Business School.

The goal of the project is to examine various aspects of process thinking and organization design using contrasting research models and methods. This paper develops ideas drawn from semistructured interviews in a mixed sample of 14 U.S.- and Europe-based firms. The unit of analysis was the reengineering project, defined as a project of at least four months' duration, incorporating dedicated personnel with the objective of using process-based concepts and tools to analyze, make recommendations upon, and change an existing business process, and that resulted in some specified and measurable outcome. A minimum of four project stakeholders were interviewed: project (reengineering) sponsor, project manager (operational), major customer and business planner. On projects involving a clearly defined IS support role, the IS project manager or systems development manager was interviewed. The instrument included items in five areas: actors (roles) involved; project tasks (activities, tasks sequences); time (synchronization of activities; event criticality); resources (process-based methods and tools, IT support); and planning integration (relationship of project planning to business strategic and IS planning). Interviews averaged an hour to an hour and a half, and were conducted from 1992 to 1993. In some firms, more than one project was surveyed. The 14 companies included: insurance and financial services (4 firms); process manufacturing (3 firms); medical products and services (2 firms); pharmaceutical (1 firm); petroleum companies (1 firm); government agencies (2 organizations); marketing and distribution (1 firm). Copies of the instrument are available from the authors.
