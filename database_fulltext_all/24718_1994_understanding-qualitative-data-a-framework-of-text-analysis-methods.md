# Understanding Qualitative Data: A Framework of Text Analysis Methods

MARY C. LACITY AND MARIUS A. JANSON

MARY LACITY is Visiting Research Fellow at Templeton College and Assistant Professor of Management Information Systems at the University of Missouri, St. Louis. Her research interests focus on issues relevant to chief information officers such as IS outsourcing, IS insourcing, benchmarking, chargeback systems, and IS metrics. She has published, with Rudy Hirschheim, Information Systems Outsourcing: Myths, Metaphors, and Realities, and has also published in Sloan Management Review, Database, Journal of Information Systems, and the Journal of General Management. Prior to receiving her Ph.D. in business administration at the University of Houston in 1992, she worked as outsourcing consultant for Technology Partners and as systems analyst for Exxon USA.

MARIUS A. JANSON is Associate Professor of Management Sciences and Information Systems, and Fellow in the Center for International Studies at the University of Missouri, St. Louis. During the winter of 1993 he was Visiting Scholar at the University of British Columbia, and at the University of Tilburg, The Netherlands. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Minnesota in 1982. His current research interests center on social and international issues of information systems use. His articles have appeared in Decision Sciences, Information & Management, Journal of Behaviour and Information Technology, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, and Omega.

ABSTRACT: Information systems (IS) researchers have argued the need for using qualitative approaches, such as action research, ethnomethodology, phenomenology, and futures research, to supplement widely used quantitative approaches. Despite the interest in qualitative approaches, almost all IS articles published in leading IS journals during the previous decade continue to report the results of quantitative studies. The disparity between the interest in and adoption of qualitative approaches may be attributed to unfamiliarity with qualitative approaches for analyzing text data and the mistaken belief that all qualitative approaches are antipositivist. This paper makes qualitative methods more accessible to both researchers and practitioners by providing a framework that categorizes various text analysis approaches. The framework classifies methods as positivist, linguistic, and interpretivist, based on assumptions about the nature of text data, the researcher's influence on text interpretation, and the validity checks used to justify text interpretations. Thus, all researchers can consider qualitative text analysis methods regardless of their paradigmatic position.

Acknowledgments: This research was supported by the Center for International Studies, University of Missouri, St. Louis. Part of this work was completed while Marius Janson was Visiting Research Scholar in the Faculty of Commerce and Business Administration, University of British Columbia, Canada.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: information systems research, interpretivist research methods, linguistic research, positivist research.

INFORMATION SYSTEMS (IS) RESEARCHERS HAVE ARGUED THE NEED for using qualitative approaches, such as action research, ethnomethodology, phenomenology, and futures research, to supplement widely used quantitative methods $[13, 21]$ . Despite the interest in qualitative approaches, Orlikowski and Baroudi $[48]$ found that almost all IS articles published in leading IS journals during the previous decade continued to report the results of surveys and experiments. Reluctance to use qualitative approaches is troubling in light of the well-established value of these approaches $[60]$ . This reluctance may be partly due to two reasons.

First, researchers may hesitate to adopt qualitative approaches because they are unfamiliar with methods used to analyze qualitative data. Like all research approaches, qualitative approaches comprise knowledge acquisition and data analysis phases. Even though methods to acquire knowledge through qualitative approaches have been discussed in the IS literature $[21, 48]$ , researchers may question how to analyze text data generated by qualitative approaches. Researchers proficient in statistical methods to understand quantitative data may be less familiar with text analysis methods to understand qualitative data.

Second, researchers may hesitate to apply qualitative approaches because they equate qualitative research with nonpositivist, antipositivist, or interpretivist research. Many qualitative methods, however, are steeped in the positivist tradition. Researchers comfortable with the positivist paradigm may apply one of several qualitative methods to explore IS issues. Likewise, qualitative methods are available for researchers who adopt alternative philosophical positions. Thus, all researchers can consider qualitative text analysis methods regardless of their paradigmatic position.

Given these reasons for the disparity between the interest in and adoption of qualitative approaches, this paper makes qualitative approaches more accessible to both researchers and practitioners by providing a framework that categorizes various text analysis approaches. The framework classifies approaches into positivist, linguistic, and interpretivist approaches based on their underlying assumptions about the nature of text data, the researcher's influence on text interpretation, and the validity checks used to justify text interpretations. The framework is useful for researchers seeking exposure and direction on selecting an appropriate method.

The framework will also be of interest to system developers who need to extract information requirements from users. Because most users communicate their needs to analysts verbally, discourse interpretation is an important analyst skill. IS developers may adopt one or more text analysis methods to understand user needs. For example, system developers may record and transcribe joint application design (JAD) sessions and then analyze the transcripts using the text analysis methods described. Depending on the method adopted, developers may then calculate frequencies to determine the degree of consensus among users, analyze the participants' speech acts, or reflect on transcripts with users until each feels an understanding has been reached.

## Text Analysis Method Framework

THE FRAMEWORK (SEE TABLE 1) CATEGORIES TEXT ANALYSIS APPROACHES into positivist, linguistic, and interpretive approaches based on their underlying assumptions about text data. These assumptions include the nature of text data, the prescribed method for understanding text data, the relationship between the researcher and the text, and the evidence accepted to validate text interpretation.

## Research Method

Even though the goal of each approach is to understand text, each approach prescribes different methods for understanding. Positivist text approaches assume that understanding comes about by identifying nonrandom variations in a text $[8, 9, 44, 45]$ . Linguistic approaches assume understanding comes from studying the type and structure of utterances $[3, 30, 31, 56]$ . Interpretivist methods assume understanding comes from intrusive methods in which researchers try to understand how culture and experiences influence text interpretation $[37, 53]$ .

## Nature of Text

The assumption about the nature of text refers to the relationship between text data and reality. Positivist approaches assume language corresponds to an objective reality; that is, meaning is assumed to be objective—researchers merely need to find it. Linguistic approaches assume that language is not a neutral description of reality but rather an act that shapes reality $[2, 4, 26, 56]$ . Linguistic approaches assume that reality emerges through language because reality does not exist independent of language. Interpretivist approaches assume that the meaning of language is subjective—the speaker, listener, and observer may all ascribe different meanings to language $[12, 20, 46, 53]$ .

## Role of the Researcher

The assumption about the researcher's role involves the degree to which he or she assumes personal biases and experiences influence text interpretation. Positivist approaches assume that the researcher is an outsider who can readily interpret a text from semantics. Thus, the researcher need not interact with the originator of the text because the author's or speaker's ideas are sufficiently expressed by the text. Similarly, linguistic approaches assume the researcher is an outsider and discourse can be understood without interacting with subjects. Furthermore, because interference by the researcher influences the way in which reality emerges, linguistic approaches require detached observation. Only interpretivist approaches assume the researcher must be an insider to understand text data. When the researcher cannot interact directly with the originators of the text, as in the case of ancient manuscripts, the researcher tries to understand how the author, as well as his or her own culture and experiences, influence text interpretation [15, 21].

An example will clarify how the three philosophical positions approach text interpretation. Assume three archetypical researchers—a positivist, a linguist, and an interpretivist—interpret the following conversation between a systems analyst and a payroll clerk:

SYSTEMS ANALYST: I'll change the format of the main menu screen next week.

PAYROLL CLERK: Next week?! Why can't you do it this week?

SYSTEMS ANALYST: Because I thought you guys wanted me to fix the payroll calculation program this week.

PAYROLL CLERK: Okay, next week is fine for the main menu changes.

The positivist assumes each person's words correspond with an objective reality. For example, the system analyst's words correspond with the facts that she will fix the payroll calculation program this week and the main menu format next week. The linguist assumes that reality emerges during the conversation. Thus, through negotiation and feedback, the systems analyst and payroll clerk come to agree about work priorities. Had the payroll clerk perceived the analyst's priorities to be misguided, the reality created through discourse might have emerged in a different direction. The interpretivist assumes the meaning of the exchange is subjective. Hence, the researcher would ideally talk with the systems analyst and the payroll clerk to develop a shared view of the exchange. The interpretivist would also analyze her own biases developed, perhaps, by her experiences as an IS professional or user.

## Validity Checks

The last issue addressed by the framework is the validity checks for each approach, which define the type of evidence used to justify research findings. Researchers should provide validity checks prescribed by their adopted approach to ensure acceptance by other scholars who conduct research in the area. Positivist methods prescribe the most formalized evidence of validity, linguistic approaches prescribe less formalized evidence, and interpretivist approaches prescribe the least formalized evidence.

## Examples

Examples of text analysis methods that illustrate the assumptions behind each approach are provided. Content analysis, verbal protocol analysis, and script analysis exemplify positivist assumptions; speech act analysis and discourse analysis exemplify linguistic assumptions; hermeneutics and intentional analysis exemplify interpretivist assumptions.

The framework is applicable for the analysis of any text data—that is, data comprised primarily of utterances as opposed to highly formalized numeric or alphanumeric data. The types of text material include written or spoken (i.e., transcribed) texts, natural dialogs or structured dialog texts, and primary or secondary texts. Written text, such as manuals, books, journals, and newspapers, implies the author used writing as the mode of communication. Spoken text is discourse transcribed into text. Spoken text can be further categorized into texts created from natural or structured dialogs. Natural dialog texts are generated during candid conversations among participants such as analysts, programmers, managers or users. Natural dialogs may also be unilateral, as is the case with speeches. Structured dialog texts arise from interviews where the researcher controls content and direction of the conversation by prompting participants with questions. Primary texts arise from interviews, experiments, and open-ended surveys, which are then transcribed into written from. Secondary sources, in the form of books, manuals, and other researcher's transcribed interviews, were originally documented by someone other than the researcher. The framework can be applied to any text data that fall within these definitions.

With the completion of the introduction to the framework, each approach is discussed in more detail. The research assumptions of each approach are analyzed, specific methods are discussed, and actual applications of the methods are presented.

## Positivist Approaches

## Assumptions

Positivist approaches hold that understanding arises through the identification of nonrandom variation. This implies that frequency is an indicant of importance—the more a phenomenon occurs, the more likely it is nonrandom and thus important. Positivist approaches, therefore, require researchers to construct a category system, code the data, and calculate frequencies or percentages that are used to test hypotheses about the relationships among variables of interest $[8, 36, 58]$ .

Positivist approaches imply that the meaning of text data is objective in the sense that a text corresponds to an objective reality. Text interpretation is straightforward except when language ambiguities are encountered. Such ambiguities are usually resolved by examining other portions of the text. A simple example illustrates this point. The sentence “I walked to the bank” is presumed to have two meanings that correspond to the two meanings of “bank.” Text analysis requires that the units of text be mapped onto the set of possible meanings $[61]$ . The correct interpretation can then be inferred from local coherence among adjoining sentences. Hence, text is interpreted and understood without extraneous contextual knowledge; the author’s or speaker’s meaning is expressed wholly in the text.

Although positivist approaches assume objectivity and prescribe formal, quantitative approaches, subjectivity is still vital to text interpretation. Consider researchers' creative selection of empirical indicators for coding schemes designed to capture theoretical constructs. For example, a researcher may decide that the number of customer references to an automated teller system indicates the importance of that system to customer satisfaction. The researcher must also judge which phrases constitute reference to the automated teller system, such as "cash machine," "computer teller," "ATM"—even phrases such as "the thing-a-ma-jig" and "that electric contraption" may all refer to the automated teller. Thus, positivist text analysis methods require creativity, intuition, and judgments to create and map text according to coding schemes.

## Validity

A validity check is the evidence offered to justify research findings. Although issues of validity are important in all research approaches, positivism has in particular formalized the concept. Under positivism, researchers have rigorously explored and divided validity into construct, content, statistical significance, predictive, internal, and external validities. Mathematical algorithms have also been developed to measure these characteristics [34].

Positivist text approaches use statistics to demonstrate validity. Because the positivist tradition equates understanding with identifying nonrandom variation, positivist methods use samples to make statistical inferences about population parameters and correlations among population variables. When the text sample is representative and all corollary assumptions of independence, homoscedasticity, and non-collinearity hold, statistics can provide compelling evidence of validity. For example, Campbell and Fiske [16] developed the multitrait, multimethod (MTMM) system to demonstrate construct validity. MTMM validates one research instrument against other research instruments that are designed to capture the same theoretical constructs.

## Examples

Three text analysis methods illustrate positivist approaches. A brief history, method description, units of analysis, and applications of each method are discussed.

Content analysis: Content analysis was popularized during World War II [36]. Content analysis helped the allies infer the impact of the war on Germany from the contents of Germany's local newspapers. Researchers estimated the number of German soldiers killed from printed casualty lists and learned that the country's supplies, production, transportation, and food situation were rapidly deteriorating. Content analysis was so successful that Japanese newspapers were also monitored [8, 44].

Content analysis, like all positivist text approaches, proceeds as follows: develop a coding scheme, code the text, calculate frequencies or percentages, conduct hypotheses tests. The coding scheme uses one of five units of analysis: referential, physical, syntactical, propositional, or thematic units [36]. The automated teller machine example analyzed referential units—multiple words or phrases that refer to the same object, event, or person. Physical units count the number of sentences, paragraphs, pages, or books about a certain topic. For example, the number of biographies about a person indicates the importance of that person. Syntactical units count the number of occurrences of specific words. For example, the number of times a person uses a particular swear word may indicate his or her degree of vulgarity. Propositional units require that the text meet certain linguistic structures, such as every sentence must have a subject and predicate. Thematic units are conceptual units that require significant interpretation to code properly. Thematic units are commonly found in IS research—one example is described below.

Jarvenpaa and Ives [32] used content analysis to analyze over 600 letters to shareholders contained in annual reports. They used the number of information technology (IT) phrases in the letters as indicants of the importance of IT to corporate strategy. By using the chairmen's letters to stockholders, the authors had a common basis for comparing different firms and industries. Among other findings, they discovered that the number of IT-related phrases contained in the letters positively correlated with the firm's yearly net profits as a percentage of sales. Although the authors do not claim causality, the statistical relationship is intriguing.

Verbal protocol analysis: Verbal protocol analysis was developed by cognitive psychologists concerned with understanding human decision-making processes. Early approaches for studying decision-making processes required subjects to explain their thought processes retroactively. These retrospective approaches were criticized for allowing the subjects to theorize about their behavior. An alternative method, verbal protocols, which requires subjects to verbalize their thought processes while they perform tasks [58], assumes that protocols are isomorphic to the subject's actual cognitive processes. Researchers who use verbal protocol analysis generate text data by transcribing subjects' utterances during controlled experiments. Verbal protocol analysis concentrates on the frequency and sequence of utterances and is therefore used to understand processes as they unfold [35, 58].

Todd and Benbasat [59] used verbal protocol analysis to determine how decision support systems affect subjects' selection of decision-making strategies. In a series of three laboratory experiments, subjects were given varying degrees of decision support for a preferential choice task. Subjects were asked to verbalize their thought processes during the experiments. Once the utterances were transcribed, the authors used verbal protocol analysis to determine which decision-making strategies subjects used to choose among alternatives. Their findings suggest that decision makers select decision strategies that minimize their cognitive load, not maximize decision quality.

Script analysis: Script analysis was derived from script theory. Script theory was developed by Abelson, a cognitive psychologist, and Schank, a computer scientist who specialized in artificial intelligence [1, 54]. Abelson and Schank studied how the human mind structures and processes knowledge. They theorized that the human mind structures knowledge in the form of scripts, “structures that describe appropriate sequences of events in a particular context” [54, p. 41]. These scripts guide interpretation of a situation and provide expectations for appropriate behaviors. Their famous restaurant script illustrates the script concept.

While dining at a restaurant, Schank and Abelson assume each participant, such as a patron, waiter, chef, or hostess, activates a mental script of an expected sequence of events. A restaurant script includes the following sequence of events: seating, scanning the menu, ordering, being served, eating, and paying the bill. Furthermore, each participant understands and adopts his or her role within the scripts. A customer, for example, expects to be seated by the hostess. A waitress expects to be tipped by the customer [1, 54].

Barley [6, 7] used script theory to confront the paradox posed by previous organizational studies that show that identical technologies can occasion similar dynamics and yet lead to different structural changes. He studied the implementation of two identical CT scanners at different hospitals. He recorded and analyzed conversations among radiologists and technicians during a one-year period and used script analysis to determine how the scanners affect structural outcomes. He categorized conversations into the following scripts: unsought validation (radiologists retroactively approve technicians' actions), anticipatory questioning (technicians confer with radiologists prior to taking action), preference stating (radiologists state and justify instructions to the technicians), clandestine teaching (technicians teach the radiologists about the machine), role reversals (radiologists ask technicians to make a diagnosis based on a scan), and radiologists blaming the technicians for machine problems. He found that the frequency of recurring scripts correlated with the amount of decentralization caused by a new technology. These research findings support the reasoning that technology changes the roles individuals play within organizations. (See [39, 50] for other applications of script theory.)

The examples from content analysis, verbal protocol analysis, and script analysis illustrate the shared assumptions of positivist approaches. All positivist text analysis methods presume that text material is isomorphic to a set of facts and that the reader can infer the meaning of the text without interaction with the author or speaker. The methods employ similar procedures for generating and validating results: take a text sample, create a coding scheme, map the text onto the scheme, calculate frequencies or percentages, and conduct hypothesis tests. The differences among these methods lie primarily with the units of analysis: content analysis focuses on static units, verbal protocol on process units typically generated by one subject, and script analysis on process units generated from multiple subjects.

## Linguistic Methods

## Assumptions

Linguistics comprises several competing philosophies about the nature of language. Lyytinen [41] discussed five philosophical views towards language. The Fregean core assumes natural language corresponds to facts in the world that can be assessed as true or false. The Chomskyan grammar assumes natural language expresses syntactic and semantic regularities that can identify linguistic competence. That is to say, linguistic competence is an individual's ability to produce syntactically and semantically correct sentences. The Piagetian schema assumes reality corresponds to a speaker's psychological reality. The Skinnerian response assumes language is a mechanism causing observable behavior. The ordinary speaking view assumes language corresponds to the performance of social actions. Lyytinen suggested that the Fregean core and ordinary speaking views are most promising to IS. The Fregean core, however, seems most appropriate for data modeling and mathematical relationships. Therefore, the discussion focuses on text analysis methods that fall within the ordinary speaking view of language.

Before the ordinary speaking view of language was developed, most linguists assumed that human utterances described objective facts—a continuing assumption of positivist approaches. Austin, the originator of speech act theory, believed, however, that language does not merely neutrally describe reality, but instead language itself acts to shape, create and define reality $[2, 57]$ . Through language, people declare couples married, anoint ships, grant academic degrees, and arrest suspects. Without the utterance, the reality is absent. For example, an officer's failure to fully verbalize the declaration of arrest could result in the later dismissal of the suspect's case. Put simply, people do things with words $[2]$ .

Given that language is action, linguistic approaches assume that an understanding of reality arises through the study of linguistic structures. Through language, researchers study the use of power, discrimination, decision processes, norms, and virtually any other social actions of interest. Wittgenstein argued in Philosophical Investigations [62] that belonging to a culture requires learning a culture's "language games."

Language games are the combination of words, attitudes, and actions expressed and created through language. He argued that children do not merely learn words, but rather they learn how to use certain words within certain contexts to do certain things [56]. Thus, understanding social reality requires studying language games.

Linguistic methods assume that researchers are outsiders. Because reality emerges through language, researcher intrusion would alter the creation of reality in an unnatural direction. Hence, detached observation is required.

## Validity

Given these assumptions, linguistic approaches study the expression and interpretation of subjective human experience rather than causal explanations. Linguistic approaches seek to explain how experience is organized, perceived, and constructed. Classification schemes are often used to categorize what speakers are trying to accomplish—are speakers or writers trying to convince, intimidate, or motivate? Multiple coders may categorize utterances differently and statistical analysis can generate numeric measures of dispersion among the interpretations of different individuals. So, to the extent that interrater reliabilites may be calculated, validity has a quantitative component. However, the classified utterances are not submitted to the rigors of hypothesis testing. Instead, users of linguistic approaches tend to focus on the emergent properties of interaction through which humans create their own realities rather than merely react to a presumed immutable world $[15, 55]$ . Validity, therefore, rests on the strength of the analytical arguments used to defend the interpretation rather than on quantitative measures.

## Examples

Two text analysis methods subscribe to the three aforementioned assumptions—speech act analysis and discourse analysis.

Speech act analysis. Speech act theory, as noted before, originated with Austin and was further elaborated by Searle [55]. A central assumption is that to communicate is to perform an act, such as stating facts, making requests, making promises, or issuing orders. To illustrate, by making the statement, "I will complete the job tomorrow," the speaker commits to a future course of action, which in turn affects the hearer. Hence, by uttering the sentence, the speaker says something, does something in saying the sentence, and affects the hearer by saying the sentence. Speech acts are performed to make factual statements, to request someone to do something, to make promises and commitments, to effect change, and to express a personal feeling: they are called assertives, directives, commissives, declaratives, and expressives, respectively [5, 33, 55].

Speech act theory originally focused on the analysis of verbal utterances. However, theorists have extensively expanded it during the last two decades; its current applications include the analysis of verbal discourse, written discourse, and textual material $[5, 49]$ . Examples of the application of speech act theory to information systems are becoming more widespread $[2, 30, 31, 42]$ .

Speech act concepts can be illustrated by analyzing several of the cryptic messages exchanged between a bank customer and an automatic teller machine (ATM). Because the purpose of this communicative interaction is uniquely goal-oriented, this example accentuates the performative aspects of language. The assertive informs the hearer of facts or states of nature. For example, the speech act “Your Transaction Is Complete” describes the condition of the transaction: its specific function is informing. The directive requests the hearer to do something. Thus, the function of the speech act “Please Take Your Card” is requesting. Its purpose is to impel the hearer to bring about the condition referred to by the directive. The commissive commits the speaker to accomplishing specified acts. Thus, the speech act “Our CDs Pay 8.25% Interest” has the specific function of promising and commits the speaker to making good on a promise. The declarative effects change by merely saying so. The speaker brings about a match between the state of the world and the content of the sentence by the act of vocalizing the utterance. The speech act “Your Card Canceled After 3 Invalid PIN Entries” hinges on the speaker’s authority to make such decisions. It also follows that on successfully enunciating the sentence, the card is canceled. Finally, the expressive communicates the psychological state of the speaker or the transmitter of the message. The speech act “We Apologize For The Inconvenience” has the specific function of apologizing by expressing concern. The implicit goal is to make the inconvenience more acceptable to the bank’s customers. The ATM developer can further use speech act theory to evaluate and improve the ATM’s performance and the interaction between the bank client and the ATM.

Winograd and Flores [60] used speech act theory for enhancing the effectiveness and quality of organizational communication. The authors contend that communication directed toward action consists of speech acts of the form request and promise, offer and acceptance, offer and counteroffer, and report and acknowledgment. Based on these concepts, Flores developed the Coordinator, which is a software program that enables computer-supported organizational communication.

Discourse analysis. Like speech act analysis, discourse analysis assumes that language is an action that shapes reality and that language can be used to study behaviors. The primary difference between speech act theory and discourse analysis is the unit of analysis. Whereas speech act analysis uses an utterance or an utterance-pair as the unit of analysis, discourse analysis studies the structure of the entire conversation. In particular, discourse analysis allows researchers to relate the sequence and organization of a dialog to the social relationships that arise from a conversation $[14, 17, 19, 25]$ .

Unlike the previously discussed text analysis methods, discourse analysis fails to prescribe an exact method:

There is no method to discourse analysis in the way we traditionally think of an experimental method or content analysis method. What we have is a broad theoretical framework concerning the nature of discourse and its role in social life, along with a set of suggestions about how discourse can best be studied and how others can be convinced findings are genuine. [51, p. 175]

The following example from Stubbs [57] illustrates the applicability of discourse analysis to the study of candid conversations.

Stubbs [57] used discourse analysis to study the interaction between teachers and students. He noted that teachers in an Edinburgh elementary school engage in eight different types of discourses that occur in the following sequence: (1) attract the students' attention, (2) control whether a student may or may not speak, (3) check students' understanding, (4) define something that has been spoken or read, (5) provide editorial comments on students' spoken or written expression, (6) correct students' spoken or written mistakes, (7) focus the topic, and (8) summarize the discussion. The discourse categories and sequences can be used to compare teaching styles, classroom arrangements, students, and the like. For example, Stubbs notes that foreign students engage in many of the discourse types that are traditionally the teacher's domain. As reflected in their discourse, these students have not assimilated the classroom environment.

From the examples provided from speech act analysis and discourse analysis, the shared assumptions of linguistic methods become apparent. Linguistic methods assume that language creates reality and that the study of behavior begins with language. The purpose of linguistic methods is to understand text material by focusing on the emergent realities created through language.

Interpretivist Approaches

## Assumptions

Interpretivist text analysis methods assume that researchers cannot objectively analyze a text and assign arbitrary units of analysis to tidy categories. Instead, interpretivist approaches are concerned with the contextual circumstances that influence authors as well as the contextual circumstances that influence researchers' interpretations. The notion that interpreters' biases must also be considered before understanding text is a distinguishing characteristic of interpretivist approaches.

Interpretivist text analysis methods assume the meaning of text data is subjective and requires extraneous information about the originator of the text. Since writers' and speakers' expressions reflect their epoch, culture, and unique experiences, many implicit assumptions are not articulated in the text. An author's text, therefore, is merely a small window that conveys his or her intentions. A researcher must learn more about the author, his or her culture, and time period to understand the text.

In addition to understanding how the originator's background influenced the text, interpretivist approaches suggest that researchers must understand their own cultural filters and biases. The interpretations that researchers ascribe to a particular text reflect their own culture, faith, and experiences. The same researcher may even ascribe different meanings to the same text after several rereadings. Thus, multiple interpretations—even by the same researcher—are possible for any text [63].

Given these assumptions, interpretivist methods assume that literal interpretations of a text shed little meaning. One cannot simply rely on semantics to yield meaning because writers and speakers bring their cultural assumptions to a text. In addition, interpreters bring their own emotional baggage to a text. Thus, the goal of interpretivist methods is to understand text data through an analysis of the cultural biases of both the writer or speaker and the interpreter.

## Validity

As is evident from the assumptions, interpretivist approaches are not readily amenable to quantification. Indeed, quantitative validity checks based on frequencies or percentages are viewed as fallacious because interpretivists reject the notion that frequency is an indicant of importance. Speakers or writers may neglect crucial experiences that are politically sensitive; they may repeat platitudes they believe are safe, they may subconsciously ignore crucial events, they may be reluctant to discuss taboo subjects, or they may omit important aspects that they presume the audience already understands. Therefore, interpretivist validity checks are largely based on the acceptance of the scientific community. If fellow scholars find meaning in the research, it is deemed valid and worthwhile. Daft [18] aptly articulated this concept:

Ultimate proof of an idea or theory is its acceptability to common sense. An important test of validity is liking an idea, feeling right about it, being able to use it to throw light on a previously hidden aspect of organization. Objective proof seldom will exist somewhere outside one's self that will demonstrate correctness or validity. No statistical test will do this for us; no amount of replication will make acceptable an idea that does not square with experience. [p. 543]

Other researchers, such as Lincoln and Guba [40], attempted to formalize interpretivist validity checks. They defined the types of evidence researchers can offer to demonstrate validity: rich descriptions, triangulation of sources and methods, peer review and participant review. This evidence, although qualitative, is used to support the researcher's interpretation of the text.

## Examples

Two text analysis methods that can be classified as interpretivist approaches are hermeneutics and intentional analysis. Of the two, hermeneutics has a longer tradition. However, because hermeneutics presumes that the interpreter is from a different epoch or culture than the writer, its procedures may be unnecessary for analysis of contemporary texts and texts where participants share similar cultures.

Hermeneutics began as the science of interpretation of sacred or ancient texts; its practical application is exegesis. Hermeneutics originated in the seventeenth century when Renaissance scholars sparked renewed interest in early versions of the Bible and in Greek and Roman classics. The term hermeneutics is related to the Greek deity, Hermes, the messenger that brought the gods' divine messages to man. Hermes not only announced the gods' messages verbatim, he also acted as interpreter to render meaning to their words.

The goal of hermeneutics is twofold: first, to ascertain the exact translation of a text, and second, to discover the instructions contained in the text. The exegesis method achieves this aim by developing rules that allow researchers to identify the intentions of the author and to place his or her meaning within its historical and cultural context. These rules, however, reflect the background of the interpreter, although they are presumed to capture the author's intentions [10, 11, 22, 23, 24, 27, 28, 29, 43, 64]. For example, a biblical interpreter extracts meaning from the Bible allegorically, literally, or liberally, depending on the interpreter's assumptions about the nature of the Bible. Ramm [52] pointed out that Luther used six hermeneutic principles to interpret the Bible literally. The psychological principle mandates that biblical interpreters are members of the Christian faith. The authority principle views the Bible as the supreme and final authority in theological matters. The literal principle views Bible stories as historical facts, not metaphors. The sufficiency principle suggests that any Christian may interpret the Bible without guidance from the Roman Catholic Church. The christological principle views the purpose of interpretation to find Christ. Finally, the law-gospel principle views that biblical laws (such as circumcision) are separate from biblical gospel. Luther considered the Bible a sacred text, divinely inspired by God. However, a different interpreter may develop an entirely different set of principles for reading and interpreting the Bible.

Although the goal of exegesis to “reproduce a photograph of the thought as it was conceived in the mind of the original writer or speaker,” researchers always prejudice interpretation $[52]$ . Although researchers are never free from bias, they can converge on the meaning of text through immersion. That is, hermeneutics prescribes that an interpreter should live with a text to understand it. This advice comes from Gadamer’s concept of a hermeneutic circle—that the meaning of a text is contextual and depends on the moment of interpretation and the experiences brought to it by the interpreter. Multiple iterations of a text (Gadamer calls these interactions between the reader and the text) are required to dispense with many preconceptions. Through the iterative process of reading and interpreting text material, a better understanding of the author’s intentions arises $[23, 24, 29, 61]$ .

In addition to emersion into the text, words or phrases may be compared with other text materials created within the same culture. For example, interpretation of the Dead Sea Scrolls requires comparison with other manuscripts written at approximately the same time. Authorship, translation, and meaning can often be inferred through such comparisons.

The empirical work of Newman [46, 47] demonstrates the hermeneutic principles of emersion and cross-comparison to understand user resistance to a university admissions system. He interviewed thirteen users and systems personnel about the implementation issues of the new system. All interviews were tape-recorded and transcribed. His primary interest was to understand a fragment of text material generated from the project leader's interview. This fragment contained twenty-eight sentences that Newman further divided into five clusters or themes. These themes were: (1) users do not want change, (2) systems are developed under technical constraints, (3) current systems are flawed, (4) historically, the computer center has been unhelpful, and (5) the overall system must succeed. To interpret each theme, Newman referred to the other transcribed interviews to find alternative insights and interpretations. The result of this analysis is an understanding of implementation issues—especially user resistance—from different stakeholder positions.

Intentional analysis aims to understand a speaker's intentions. This text analysis method is particularly appropriate for the study of transcribed interviews. Because the researcher is presumed to have much in common with the subject, such as living in the same epoch, speaking the same language, and living in the same culture, intentional analysis requires less effort than hermeneutics to understand the historical and cultural backgrounds that influenced the subject's dialog.

Intentional analysis prescribes four steps for helping researchers make sense of text data $[53]$ . For example, assume that the interpretist researcher follows up the earlier discourse between systems analyst and payroll clerk by separately interviewing both parties. In the first step of the intentional analysis, the researcher describes the “facts” of the phenomenon. “Facts” are socially shared realities agreed upon by all participants. For example, the analyst and payroll clerk may both attest to the “fact” that two payroll programs need to be changed.

In step two, the researcher determines the way participants ascribe meaning to their separate realities by how they perceive cause and effect. For example, the analyst may claim, “I decided to fix the payroll calculation first because if it were not fixed this week, people’s paychecks would be wrong next week.” Here the systems analyst perceives her behavior in terms of cause (erroneous payroll calculation program) and effect (erroneous payroll checks).

In step three, the researcher identifies themes—or invariants—that emerge from the text. The researcher then identifies themes that are used to develop common interpretations for an entire class of phenomena. For example, assume the researcher studies the entire set of relationships between information systems personnel and payroll personnel. A potential theme may be that systems personnel consistently prioritize users' requests.

In step four, the researcher abstracts the “essences” from the text. Essences are wholly subjective gestalts of what is learned from studying the phenomenon. Abstracting essences requires creativity, intuition, and reflection. The researcher no longer asks “What do the participants think about the phenomenon?” but rather, “What do I think?” For example, the researcher may conclude that systems analysts should not prioritize user requests because the researcher believes it leads to a power imbalance, increased backlog, or other negative consequences.

Lacity and Hirschheim [38] used intentional analysis to understand practitioners' perceived intentions, decision-making processes, and consequences of information systems outsourcing decisions. Unstructured interviews were conducted with thirty-six senior executives and IS managers in thirteen Fortune 500 companies involved in the outsourcing decision-making process. Interviews were transcribed and analyzed using the four steps of intentional analysis. The intentional analysis yielded many insights for practitioners. Among them are (1) outsourcing was viewed as a symptom of the problem of demonstrating the value of IS to senior management, (2) most large companies can replicate savings without vendor assistance, and (3) the concept of vendors as “strategic partners” has caused many companies to sign weak contracts.

These insights are validated with rich descriptions, direct quotes from participants, and practitioner review of the interpretation. The validity checks are particularly important because the researchers wanted to minimize their biases, which they feared predisposed them against outsourcing. Although the findings are not verified with numbers and hypotheses tests, the study has high face validity, particularly to practitioners who relate to the experiences of individuals holding similar positions in other IS organizations.

The examples from hermeneutics and intentional analysis illustrate the shared assumptions of interpretivist approaches. Interpretivist text analysis methods presume that text material is subjective and that the researcher must intrude into the author's or speaker's world to understand a text. With hermeneutics, which typically deals with ancient manuscripts, the researcher "intrudes" into the author's or speaker's world by studying the author's history and culture. This enables the researcher to place the work in the context of the author's world. Hermeneutic principles, however, can be applied to modern text, as exemplified in Newman [46]. With intentional analysis, the researcher discusses with participants the meaning they ascribe to their experiences. Its principles are less comprehensive than hermeneutics because the researcher is presumed to be a member of the same culture as the speaker.

## Conclusion

ALTHOUGH IS RESEARCHERS HAVE CALLED FOR MORE QUALITATIVE RESEARCH to supplement quantitative approaches, qualitative research continues to be under-applied. This fact is disturbing given that many believe methodological pluralism will serve to broaden the understanding of information systems.

Two reasons for the discrepancy between interest in and adoption of qualitative approaches may be that researchers are unfamiliar with qualitative methods and they may mistake “qualitative” research for “nonpositivist” research. This article advocates a greater adoption of qualitative methods by showing that these methods are available to researchers, regardless of their philosophical positions.

A framework has been developed to assist researchers in understanding the philosophical assumptions behind various text analysis approaches. Text analysis approaches are categorized as positivist, linguistic, or interpretive. The assumptions behind each approach include the nature of text data, the prescribed method for understanding text data, the relationship between the researcher and the text, and the evidence accepted to validate text interpretation.

We do not advocate one particular method but rather illustrate where alternative methods are useful, given a set of assumptions. Specific studies from IS as well as other behavioral sciences are offered as examples of the understanding that can emerge when text analysis methods are appropriately applied. The authors also believe that practitioners will find text analysis approaches useful for systems analysis and design. Given that users primarily communicate their IS needs verbally to analysts, discourse interpretation is an important analyst skill. Practitioners may find that text analysis approaches improve communication between users and IS personnel.
