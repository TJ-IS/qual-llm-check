# Researching the Costs of Information Systems

LAWRENCE A. WEST, JR.

LAWRENCE A. WEST, JR. is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Information and Management Sciences in the College of Business at the Florida State University. He teaches decision support systems, expert systems, database design, and management science courses. His research interests center around the economics of information including how information systems change information constraints and so affect the organization of firms and industries and how the IS resources themselves are acquired and organized. Dr. West received an A.B. in economics from the University of California, Davis, in 1976, an M.B.A. from the University of Arizona in 1981, and a Ph.D. in business analysis from Texas A&M University in 1991. He has previously published in Decision Sciences.

ABSTRACT: This paper explores the issue of scale economies in the construction of information systems. Economic theory is used to identify transaction attributes that contribute to scale economies and the concept of an information system is then decomposed into separable components. Each component is analyzed with respect to the availability of one or more of the sources of scale economy. The analysis finds that each component possesses at least some potential for realizing scale economies and that some components may realize very large per-unit cost savings as scale of system use or capability is expanded. The implications of these findings for the organization of information services, including outsourcing, are highlighted and illustrated with consistent examples from IS-intensive industries. Future research is proposed with respect to the actual nature of IS scale economies; the presence of scope economies with respect to multicomponent systems; the relative importance of production costs, transaction costs, and benefits as determinants of acquisition choice; and the impact of IS acquisition method on the organization of firms and industries.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: economies of scale, information economics, information services, transaction costs.

## 1. Introduction

IT IS A FUNDAMENTAL TRUISM THAT ORGANIZATIONS should acquire a resource when the benefits accruing from the resource exceed its cost. Unfortunately, many factors serve to conceal from decision makers the full range of some resources' benefits and costs. Interaction of a resource with other organizational elements, realizing streams of benefits and costs over time, and the vagaries of the environment all make evaluation of a resource's net worth problematic for long-lived assets—including information systems.

Considerable attention has been paid in the MIS literature to the value of information and information systems with a view toward structuring the information resource investment decision (see, e.g., [1, 2, 4, 9, 12, 22, 24, 28, 37, 55, 65, 74]). Though several factors, including the separation of most information systems from the direct profit-producing activities of the firm and the high fixed costs and flexibility of modern information technology, make evaluation of information system benefits difficult, a research stream has been established and remains ongoing in this area.

The same cannot be said of research on the cost of information systems. There has been a regular series of papers on the applicability of Grosch's Law to hardware costs $[10, 21, 29, 34, 35, 39, 40, 42, 48, 60]$ and the field of software engineering boasts significant empirical research on the costs of software production (see section 4). Gurbaxani $[36]$ explored the trade-offs between hardware and software costs but little work has been accomplished to define appropriate methodologies for costing entire information systems, or to determine how IS costs influence acquisition strategies or organization and industry performance.

This paper has three goals with respect to this research gap. First, it suggests that traditional methods for evaluating the costs of IS are incomplete. Economic theory is used to suggest a new (for IS research) method of evaluating the costs of IS components and the expanded explanatory power of the new methodology is discussed. Second, this methodology is applied to a range of IS components as part of a broad overview. This preliminary application of the new methodology suggests that there are patterns of costs in the acquisition of IS components that have important implications for outsourcing decisions, the structure of information-intensive industries, and the internal structuring of IS activities in organizations. Third, the paper suggests new research streams to build on the preliminary results presented here.

Readers are cautioned that this paper does not purport to present complete research results for the topic under discussion. It is specifically intended to raise more questions than it answers, but with the realization that the worthiness of the questions so raised must be supported with enough evidence to justify continued research. The paper can be regarded as a survey, not of the complete literature in the field, but of research issues connected by a common theme and whose solutions, together, expand our knowledge of information systems in an important direction.

## 2. Costs

THE GOAL OF THIS SECTION IS TO SUMMARIZE THEORIES OF COSTS in a way that highlights the importance of the costs of information systems as determinants of design choice. It first presents a brief review of the importance of costs in economic research with particular attention paid to the importance of costs as determinants of market structure and of decisions such as make or buy, outsourcing, and other methods for accomplishing productive activities. These concepts are then applied to the demand for information systems by organizations to highlight a key factor in the costs of producing these systems and to accomplish the first goal of the paper, suggesting a new way to look at the costs of IS. Finally, traditional sources of cost economies are drawn from economic research for use in the analysis of IS costs that follows.

## 2.1. Developments in the Theory of Costs

In traditional microeconomic theory, costs are coequal in importance with demand as determinants of production, purchasing, or pricing decisions for buyers and sellers. Elementary mathematical analysis shows that buyers and/or sellers will select the price/quantity combinations that satisfy the profit-maximizing condition, price equals marginal cost, subject to the condition that there be no losses. This nonnegative profits constraint further ensures that, in competitive or contestable markets, price will equal the minimum long-run average cost achievable with available technology.

More recent theoretical developments closely associated with Oliver Williamson have expanded the theories of cost to include transaction costs as components of the overall cost of a “transaction,” a purchase, sale, or other economic event $[77, 78]$ . Transaction costs are the nonproduction costs associated with a transaction and include costs of coordinating internal production of resources (including agency costs), searching for external suppliers and prices, writing contracts for external acquisition and other acts to guard against opportunistic behavior by others, and the costs of uncertainty associated with external acquisition of resources. Like production costs, transaction costs have many determinants.

An important aspect of both production and transaction costs is that a decision maker does not usually face just one cost function for required products or services. Instead, he or she may face a number of cost functions, each corresponding to a discrete option regarding the organization of the transaction. These discrete options may include make-or-buy decisions, selection of contractual arrangement with external providers, organization of internal production (e.g., end-user or central development of DP applications), and choices of capital investment. The decision then includes both the selection of transactional form and the determination of the optimum quantities of inputs and outputs, given the selected form. In particular, the decision results in a specific total cost of production and a specific mix of production and transaction costs where the total and the mix are dependent on both aspects of the decision. The focus of this paper is on how the “production costs” of information systems may influence the acquisition decision. It briefly returns to the issue of transaction costs as a research stream once the production cost perspective has been established.

## 2.2. Costs, Market Size, and Industry Structure

The long-run average cost (LRAC) curve for the production of a good or service represents the minimum cost at which each quantity of the good or service can be produced with available technology. These curves are traditionally held to be U-shaped wherein economies are realized over some range of production, possibly followed by a flat region, but eventually followed by a region of diseconomies of scale. Figure 1 shows forms of LRAC curves where the cost-minimizing level of production is realized at differing levels and over varying ranges of production. These LRACs could be thought of as being associated with alternative configurations of plant, equipment, personnel, or procedures (including IT resources) available to a firm.

There are three reasons why understanding the cost structures of available production processes is important. First, the various cost curves in figure 1 may be associated with different technology options available to the decision maker. If demand is high, the decision maker should choose the technologies represented by either curves C or D in figure 1. Moderate demand should indicate the technologies producing curves A or D, while low demand should support the technology producing curve B. Structuring the investment decision is therefore dependent on understanding the nature of the costs associated with each choice.

Second, costs are determinants of market structure. If $c_{m}$ is the minimum average cost achievable by a single firm, $q_{m}$ is the quantity at which $c_{m}$ is realized, and $Q(c_{m})$ is the total market demand for the item at a price $p = c_{m}$ , then Baumol et al. [13] showed that, in a contestable market (a market with no entry or exit costs), there would be $Q(c_{m}) / q_{m}$ firms in the market and price would equal $c_{m}$ , the socially optimal “competitive” price. $^{1}$ Further, the size of each firm (measured in terms of output) is inversely proportional to the cost of production. Because of the pervasiveness of $c_{m}$ as a determinant of market structure, if information (and information system) costs are an important component of overall costs in an industry, their nature will have proportionate strategic consequences for market participants. Section 11 provides examples of industries where changes in industry structure are consistent with the discussion above and with changes in IT costs.

The third reason is a hybrid of the previous two and follows from an understanding of what constitutes the market for information systems or services and also from an understanding of an appropriate level of analysis for the costs of IS. Traditional research on the costs of IS components has focused on the costs of acquiring these components, such as the relationship between cost and processing power in the acquisition of computing power or the cost per some unit of software (where source lines of code [SLOC] or function points have been used as measures of output). In economic theory, though, the level of analysis is typically on the average cost of producing the firm's output(s). Since IS are inputs in productive processes, the appropriate level of analysis for the cost of IS is the cost of the IS input per unit of the firm's output, however that output may be measured. Subsequent sections show that this subtle shift in focus reveals important insights when applied to IS. In particular, many IS components are characterized by (possibly high) fixed costs, some constraint on the number of transactions (measured from the using organization's point of view) they can support, and only negligible variable costs. It follows, then, that in addition to initial costs, capacity constraints and demand form an important part of the overall analysis of IS costs, especially the ability to realize scale economies. For example, for a single firm that is not in the business of selling information services, the market for these services is strictly limited to the internal needs of the firm. A centralized DP department's ability to realize economies of scale in the production of information services is limited to the needs of the parent company. An end-user developer's "market" is even more limited. On the other hand, the ability of a specialized information services firm to realize economies of scale in the production of these services is subject to the demand for these services by all firms. Choices between internal and external production of information services are then influenced by differences between production costs available to the firm and to the external producer and by transaction cost differentials.

## 2.3. Sources of Production Economies

The preceding section showed how production costs in general and declining average costs in particular can influence firm and industry organization and production decisions. Before turning specifically to the costs of information systems, this section reviews the sources of economies in the production of goods and services. The various sources are presented below and then used in the analysis of IS components that follows. This discussion draws heavily on developments in Alchian [3] and Gold [31].

## 2.3.1. The Specialization of Labor

Adam Smith's classic analysis [58] focused on the returns to specialization of labor. When each step in the production process is performed by a separate individual, no time is lost moving between tasks and the training period is shorter, leading to lower overall costs. However, a certain scale of operation is necessary to fully employ at least one individual at each task in the production process.

## 2.3.2. Full Employment of Capital

Two issues relate to the employment of capital. When each worker performs each step in the production process, each worker requires a full set of tools, only a portion of which are in use at any time. The idle tools represent a cost overhead that is eliminated when each worker requires only the tools for a single task. Second, when capital is needed in some quantity that is a noninteger multiple of available sizes, the excess capacity also becomes a cost overhead. The proportion of this overhead in the average cost of each unit produced as well as the possibility of avoiding the cost completely if mixed sizes of capital are available falls as the size of the operation grows.

## 2.3.3. Cost of Capital

In many instances the equipment necessary to produce greater volumes and/or rates of output is cheaper per unit of capacity than multiple increments of equipment with smaller capacities [3, 31]. This characteristic is embodied in the “six-tenths rule” in engineering [31, p. 12] and in Grosch’s Law for computers [34] (see section 3).

## 2.3.4. Public Good Properties of Resources

A second cost-of-capital issue is not traditionally considered a source of scale economies but will be seen to have implications for the production of information systems. Some components of information systems have public good properties in that they can be created once and then replicated or used indefinitely at little cost and with little change in the benefit realized per replication or per use. The average cost of these components then declines continuously with scale of replication and use.

## 2.3.5. Adapting to Random Deviations

In an uncertain environment, the law of variances indicates that the large firm can reduce the proportionate overhead of inventory and/or capacity held against peak demand. In particular, “inventories need not increase in proportion to the rate of output if the variance of random deviations in output rates does not increase more than proportionally to the expected output rate" [3, p. 25].

## 2.3.6. Learning

The activities of labor become more efficient as experience increases knowledge regarding the outcomes of choices or simply increases dexterity. Spence wrote that learning curves

[confer] cost advantages on early entrants and those who achieve large market shares. These market shares are not permanent. But with moderately rapid declines in unit costs, they have significant impacts on market shares and profitability. The effects are similar to (though more pronounced than) those caused by economies of scale. [61, p. 68]

## 2.3.7. Market Power

Increased size of operations generally implies increased demand for factors. If demand is large enough, the firm can expect to exercise monopsony power vis-à-vis its suppliers, leading to lower costs. In addition to the purchase of raw factors of production, this power can extend to advertising, marketing, and shipping activities [57, ch. 6].

Each of the potential sources of economies identified here will be seen to be applicable to the construction of IS. Of interest to designers is how they are applicable, whether they match the designer's circumstances, and, especially, whether choices available to the designer provide differing access to scale economies. Because choices are often made at the component (or functional) level for IS, it is at this level that the following analysis focuses.

Table 1 lists the components or functions from which any IS can be constructed. The list itself was derived from the components and topics frequently cited as necessary or potential parts of IS in intermediate texts (see, for example, [5, 51, 62]). The seventh element—supervision, administration, and facilities—was included since these factors become important in large systems. The next seven sections review the individual elements of Table 1 with respect to the sources of production economies identified above. The analysis leads to inferences regarding the importance of IS costs as determinants of industry structure and behavior and, by extension, to the importance of further research in this area. Attention is restricted throughout to the choices practically available to designers. For example, since computer manufacturing is not a practical alternative for most IS users, purchase of computer hardware is the only option explored for hardware acquisition. For software creation, though, the make-or-buy decision is of more practical consequence and so is examined.

Grosch's Law states that the cost of computer processing power increases proportionately with the square root of the power of the processor, a relationship indicating significant cost-of-capital economies of scale with respect to computer processing. Periodic assessments of the applicability of Grosch's Law to new technology demonstrated its relevance through the 1970s. In 1985, Ein-Dor [29] published a new study on Grosch's Law using 1981 data, including data on mini- and microcomputers. His results validated Grosch's Law with certain findings of interest. Ein-Dor first showed that MIPS were an appropriate measure of computer power and that MIPS were highly correlated with other aspects of computer power such as main memory, I/O channels, and cycle time. Ein-Dor's second finding was that computer processors no longer displayed economies of scale across all computers. Instead, each class of computer (supercomputers, mainframes, small mainframes, minicomputers, and microcomputers) displayed economies of scale closely approximating Grosch's Law within the class but that there were diseconomies of scale between classes as processing power increased.

In 1987, however, Mendelson [48] published a critique of Ein-Dor's paper that suggested that Ein Dor's methodology contained flaws. Specifically, Mendelson showed that the grouping of processors into classes artificially generated Ein-Dor's finding of economies of scale within classes. Analyzing the same (1981) data as well as 1985 data, Mendelson found that computer processing power displayed constant returns to scale. Mendelson stated that a trend toward “commodization of hardware” was reducing the average cost differentials associated with differences in computer power.

Mendelson's analysis, as well as reasoning advanced by Rappaport and Halevi [53], indicates that cost-of-capital economies may no longer be relevant in the processing component of information systems. There are, however, other sources of economies that may apply, one of which is full-employment-of-capital economies. Full employment of capital with respect to processing power can relate to three considerations in information systems. The first is the simple concept of capacity utilization. Although the aforementioned studies examined the average cost of computing power (MIPS in the cases of Ein-Dor and Mendelson), the effective cost to the user is also an inverse function of the proportion of available power actually used. Idle processing time increases the cost of active time. A second issue was exposed by Ein-Dor in a finding that Mendelson did not challenge. Because there is a correlation between processing power and other characteristics of the computer, heavy demand for one of these other features may require processing power in excess of what is actually needed for computation. An I/O- or data-storage-intensive application, for example, may require a much larger computer than would be justified simply by CPU activity needs. The third full-employment-of-capital issue involves the necessity of adding processing power in integer increments. If processing capability is added by replicating processors as demand grows, an average of one-half the capacity of a single machine will be underutilized at any time. However, the proportion of unutilized capacity attributed to this cause is a strictly decreasing function of total processing power used, indicating weak economies of scale with respect to full employment of capital.

Finally, it is likely that some economies with respect to the cost of processing power accrue from the exercise of market power and the ability of large systems to adapt more readily to random deviations in demand. Market power reflected in “most favored customer” clauses and volume discounts represent economies of scale with respect to the cost of the equipment so acquired. Also, larger operations may enjoy a lower average cost of processing power by reducing the overhead of average excess capacity retained to handle peak demands.

In summary, weak to moderate economies of scale for the use of computer processing power accrue from several of the sources listed in section 2.3.

## 4. Software Costs

SOFTWARE IS THE SET OF ESSENTIAL OPERATING INSTRUCTIONS that cause computers to perform desired activities. While software is a factor in the production of the information output of IS, it has a unique economic characteristic not shared by hardware. Like data and system design, software is an abstract input that is not consumed by use nor is use limited (in a technical sense) to the operation of a single system. The following discussion of software costs therefore develops in two streams, the cost of using existing software and the cost of software creation.

## 4.1. The Public-Good Properties of Software

Software can exist in many forms. It can be compiled into machine language instructions ready for execution by the appropriate processor, it can be stored electronically in source code, or it may be listed on paper or even printed in a book. In any of these forms, only artificial legal prohibitions constrain the natural ability to reproduce and reuse software. Software has public-good properties in that it is not consumed by use. Further, one person's ability to execute a copy of a piece of software does not interfere with another person's ability to do the same thing (though it may damage any competitive advantage unilateral possession of the software provides). Two aspects of this characteristic affect the cost of software components of information systems.

First, there is no limit to the number of times a single piece of software can be run. Whether purchased or licensed from an external producer or developed in-house, ownership of a piece of software (or license) involves a fixed payment with rights to run the software an unlimited number of times. While the fixed expense may be (and often is) substantial, the average cost per use of the software is a strictly decreasing function of the number of times the software is used. Software therefore displays continually increasing cost-of-capital scale economies with respect to use, subject to technical constraints imposed by hardware or constraints established by the user's needs. $^{2}$ Second, there are no technical limits to the number of times a piece of software may be copied or on the number of machines that can simultaneously run a copy of the software [17, 50]. Instead, only artificial legal limits and the ability to enforce them influence the number of copies of a piece of software that may be made and used. Such limits are embodied in license agreements and enforced in civil courts through application of copyright laws. In fact, unless the unauthorized acquisition of software involves theft of physical media or some other property crime such as trespass or burglary, no criminal statutes are available to control distribution of software [54]. Note that much of this discussion applies whether the production of the software is by an organization for its internal use or by a software company for sale or license to others, with the impact of licensing issues and software piracy being more pertinent to the latter group. Though beyond the scope of this discussion, the impact of licensing and piracy issues on software production and pricing has been reported in [23].

With respect to the authorized copying and use of software, then, there are continuously declining average costs with respect to the number of copies made and used up to the limit price of the physical distribution media and limits imposed by congestion effects (e.g., the disutility realized by software users if the vendor's product support lines are constantly busy). Further, these public-good economies of scale enable software sellers to respond to market power by offering government or academic discounts, discounts to large-volume resellers, and site licenses. In view of the preceding discussions, then, an existing software product has potentially unlimited economies of scale with respect to use. Lower bounds on the minimum average cost achievable for a software product are then due to two factors—the cost of the initial creation effort and the demand for the product.

## 4.2. The Cost of Software Creation

There are intuitive arguments for expecting economies of scale with respect to software production and empirical evidence from research in software engineering (an overview of which is presented below) supports these expectations. The remainder of this section addresses three factors from the software engineering literature that are related to forms of scale economies in that their presence could lower software production costs as quantities increases. These factors are the effects of learning, code reusability, and the use of productivity tools.

## 4.2.1. Learning

It is reasonable to expect that the second time a programmer writes a module for a menu or an input screen, it will take less time than the first. Similarly, the second time a programmer uses a particular statement or function, it is less likely that he or she will need to check syntax in a reference manual. Likewise, it would be expected that debugging skills would improve with experience, as would the efficient implementation of stylistic skills such as code formatting, variable naming and scoping, and code documentation. Of interest, of course, is how much improvement is experienced for these activities when they are performed the third time, the sixth time, or the fiftieth time.

Empirical research has shown the existence of learning effects on programmer productivity. Vosburgh et al. [71] showed that programmer experience positively affects productivity and that there is less variation in productivity among experienced programmers than among novices or programmers of intermediate skill. In a 1974 study, Wolverton [79] (cited in [15]) showed that software development costs were significantly lower (on the order of half) for the development of large-scale programs when the project was not novel to the development team. The results were universal across six different types of programs. Perhaps more significantly, he showed that for new applications, the cost of development increased at an increasing rate as program complexity grew, while for familiar programs development cost grew at a decreasing rate as program complexity increased. His results indicate that experience in team production may be more important than increased individual productivity. In a different approach, Boehm [14, 15] empirically developed a set of effort multipliers for software development (Table 2).

Boehm's results indicate that, while programmers with little experience in a particular language can hinder development, there are only marginal (5 percent) improvements associated with a high level of experience with a language. On the other hand, he indicates the potential for an 18 percent improvement in productivity (reduction in effort) when very high levels of experience in developing similar applications are present. Boehm goes on to itemize observed sources of the experience-based improvements highlighted in the table. In the explanation of the improvement between nominal and high experience levels, for example, he repeatedly uses the phrasing “Less learning, data gathering, analysis, false starts, errors,” “Less documentation effort,” or “Fewer design versus language mismatches” [14, Tables 26–4, 26–8, 26–10]. Swigger and Wallace [67] reinforce these observations in their report on learning assembly language programming. They report that students tended not to repeat errors and, if they did, they were able to debug problems more efficiently.

This evidence indicates the presence of economies derived from learning as the number of similar software products created increases, though there are diseconomies associated with increasing the size of individual projects. Learning effects seem to occur both in individual productivity and in the coordination of teams. There are indications of sharp initial learning economies with respect to language and application experience, although application experience seems to provide the greatest overall economies as experience increases. The availability of learning economies from all sources seems to diminish fairly quickly as the number of projects increases and seems to be nonexistent as project size increases.

## 4.2.2. Code Reusability

The issue of code reusability is strongly related to the previous discussion of the public-good properties of software. Reused code is code that, once written for one application, is incorporated into another either wholly, in part, or with modifications. The economically pertinent characteristic of reused code is that design, implementation, and testing costs may be reduced or avoided in subsequent uses of the code.

According to Wegner [72], reusing previously tested modules and data structures is the most heavily weighted factor increasing productivity during initial [program] development. Each module and each data description macro that can be used unaltered is a module which does not have to be written and tested again. [59, p. 63]

The reusable nature of computer code underlies significant applied and theoretical work in information systems. The entire purpose of libraries in programming is to provide simple access to organized collections of useful code. Software vendors sell collections of functions, data management, graphics, or other routines. Other efforts aimed at managing an organization's code have been reported. Prieto-Diaz [52] reported on GTE's system of managing for software reusability. In a system that incorporated database and library science techniques as well as individual incentives for code reuse, he reports that in the program's first year 14 percent of code used in new applications was reused, for an estimated savings of \$1.5 million. He predicted a 50 percent reuse rate by the end of the fifth year, with savings of over \$10 million. The ability to maintain libraries of reusable objects is one of the significant advantages of object oriented programming [30].

This ability to reuse code represents public-good economies with respect to software production. Since reusable modules represent a sunk cost, the average cost of the module and of any application in which it is employed is a strictly decreasing function of use.

## 4.2.3. Software Development Productivity Tools

Organizations may take a wide range of actions to affect the environment in which software productivity takes place. Providing computer-assisted software engineering (CASE) tools to support various aspects of the software development process is one of the best-known approaches but other methods include the provision of programmers' workstations [16] incorporating windowing technology for code editing, debugging, documentation, and execution; the new generation of development environments provided by Microsoft, Borland, and others; reusable code library support systems; and Boehm even reports that providing private offices for software personnel has increased productivity by 8–11 percent [16]. Many of the studies on software production mentioned earlier also address the effectiveness of tools [14, 16, 59, 71].

While most studies report that these tools positively affect productivity and quality, Henderson and Cooprider report that “the impact of these tools on the productivity of software developers and, ultimately, on time-to-market is unclear” [38, p. 228]. They report conflicting results from previous research indicating that room remains for additional specific research on the effects of these tools. A possibility to be investigated is that poor results from the application of software productivity tools could be explained by their use in circumstances where the organization is unable to realize the specific economies which they provide.

One aspect of productivity tools corresponds to a theme that has been developing throughout this section. Each of these tools is an effort to achieve cost-of-capital economies by lowering the per-unit (e.g., Source Lines of Code—SLOC) cost of software production. There are then full-employment-of-capital and learning economies that apply to the tools themselves. The majority of these tools represent a single sunk investment (or collection of sunk investments) that has effects spreading over all the projects or modules to which they are applied. The purchase of a CASE tool, the development of a library management system, or the purchase of a developer's workstation all represent fixed costs that may be used for a large number of projects. Again, the cost per use of such an investment is a strictly declining function of the number of projects in which it is engaged.

## 5. Data

LIKE SOFTWARE, THE DATA IN AN INFORMATION SYSTEM has public-good properties in that it can be replicated (used by multiple users) indefinitely. This “replication” may take the form of data being read from the same storage device repeatedly or the data may be copied repeatedly and the copies then distributed. Use of data by one consumer does not impair use by another (again to the extent that there is no competitive advantage accruing from sole possession of the data) unless file or record locking or demand on the host computer create congestion effects for a single copy of data. It follows then that the distribution of existing data would enjoy the same continuously declining LRAC as was seen for the distribution of existing software. Again, as with software, a critical focus of the analysis shifts to the demand for data and to its cost of production. The question with respect to the production of data is how the average cost per unit of data behaves as the quantity produced increases. A related issue is how the costs of quality assurance and corrupted data behave as the quantity of data produced and distributed increases.

Different types of data require different acts of creation which, in turn, may have different cost characteristics. Whereas an author applies creative effort, research, and imagination in the preparation of a technical article, the creation of data regarding sales in a supermarket is generated automatically by the passing of items across a scanner. The efficiency of production of either type can be affected exogenously, but the nature of the improving factors differs. Technical writing may benefit from learning as authors apply previous experience, perhaps even replicated boilerplate text, to the creation and presentation of new information. It is unlikely, though, that a supermarket checkout scanner benefits from learning. Other factors may contribute to the generation of information (data) through creative processes. Universities, news gathering agencies, R&D departments, and governments each have (or are) infrastructures that support the generation of information. Fixed investments in these institutions have declining average costs with respect to the number of uses to which they are put, up to any capacity limitations they may have.

Certain observations can be made with respect to the cost of data production. First, some data can only be produced as a byproduct of another activity. Generating data on supermarket sales necessarily requires a supermarket with customers and inventory, as well as data gathering and management capabilities. To the extent that a supermarket (for example) is viewed as a data-producing activity, there are cost-of-capital and full-employment-of-capital economies with respect to the number of transactions recorded by the checkout/inventory management systems. These economies apply to the costs of the store, merchandise, and employees, but also to specialized investments in bar code scanners, inventory management software, and computing capacity.

Second, there may be economies with respect to the production of certain types of information. Stigler [64] wrote that economies in the production of trade and industry information will lead to a single information source for each industry. On the other hand, market size may enable the existence of multiple information providers, especially if the economies are not unlimited to the extent that a natural monopolist is able to dominate the market. The viability of multiple large news gathering services such as UPI, AP, CNN, or the major networks is testimony both to economies that cannot be achieved by their customers and also to market demand that supports multiple providers of general news information. The maintenance of worldwide bureaus by certain “local” news organizations (such as The New York Times or The Washington Post) also indicates that these economies are not unlimited and may be approximated by other large organizations.

In summary, there are public-good economies with respect to the use of existing data. There are indications of learning and cost-of-capital economies with respect to the production of data but the structures of some information-producing industries also indicate limits to these economies. There also seem to be full-employment-of-capital and cost-of-capital economies with respect to the application of capital to support information creation. The importance of computer-readable information in the “information economy” should motivate additional research in this area.

## 6. System Design

AN IMPORTANT ELEMENT OF AN INFORMATION SYSTEM is the collection of decisions that determined its final form. IS are not a homogeneous collection of identical configurations. Instead, each was tailored to the needs and environment of the system's owner. Further, decisions made during the design stage can have a crucial effect on the performance, profitability, and success of an IS, so the decisions made at this stage can be considered as much a “component” of the system as the processor or software.

For the purposes of this work, design can be thought of as the transition from needs analysis to the issuance of directives to programmers, purchase orders to vendors, and the implementation of organizational changes associated with the new system. The visualization here is the design of the entire system. Specifically, and unlike many texts purporting to discuss system design, the focus here includes selection of hardware, operating systems, procurement sources for applications software, and other environmental features as well as the design of system software components. The special subset of this process for hardware needs was called “configuration” by Sviokla [66].

It is intuitive to expect the system design process to display cost and constraint characteristics similar to those reported for software development. In fact, software development can be viewed as system design without the inclusion of choices for hardware, organization of the MIS functions, or (re)design of the organization, and without the impact on future courses of action. System design should display economies resulting from learning as similar problems are encountered and solved and from public-good properties as design solutions are applied across multiple units in an organization or across multiple organizations. Costs might also be mitigated by the application of capital investment such as DEC's XCON AI application for hardware configuration to the process [66]. The additional considerations in system design increase the magnitude and importance of the problem.

Despite these intuitive expectations, it turns out that little is known about economies and costs associated with systems design. With respect to system design, Turner wrote:

Quite simply, the fact is that we do not understand very much about designing complex, computer-based information systems. I mean that we don't know what system design consists of, we don't know how it is done, and we don't know how to teach it. [68, p. 97]

The fact that systems design, including the reengineering of entire organizations, is a major activity of outsourcers provides preliminary evidence that economies of some sort are achievable in this field. Determining the nature and extent of these economies is an important research need with both theoretical and practical consequences.

## 7. Data Storage

INFORMATION SYSTEMS VARY WIDELY WITH RESPECT TO the amount of online data storage they require. The costs of this storage are important for three reasons:

1. Online data storage is a separable component in most computer systems: that is, the same processor can be equipped at the purchaser's option with a wide range of storage devices with a correspondingly wide range of capabilities and costs.

2. Storage requirements may narrow the choices for other system components, especially processor capabilities (section 10).

3. The costs of data storage can be significant. As the cost of direct access storage devices (DASD) has fallen, there has been an increase in the demand for storage to the extent that storage can become the largest hardware expense for some systems [63].

The usual configuration of data storage is the connection of a drive controller to the CPU and the connection of one or more storage devices to the controller. There are two ways in which the costs of such configurations can vary with capacity. First, the drive controller represents overhead with respect to the storage itself. Since most controllers will operate more than one drive, the average cost of this overhead decreases with the number of drives attached, up to the limiting number of drives that may be controlled by a single device. For example, controllers for the IBM 3390 series of drives will handle up to six drives each, while comparable figures for other vendors range from one to eight [25]. Similar economies apply to other fixed costs of disk operation, including disk management software and the premium price charged by some vendors for the first drive attached to each controller [25]. These are full-employment-of-capital economies.

The second way in which storage costs vary with respect to capacity is through the cost of the drives themselves. With modern drive technology, increased capacity is usually achieved by a combination of factors, including increasing the density of storage media. Since the increased capacity is not accomplished by the physical replication of components in lower-capacity drives, the per-unit manufacturing cost for the better drives is not much higher than for lower-capacity drives. For example, an Amdahl 6380J drive unit has a capacity of 2.52 GBytes and a list price of \$55,755. $^{3}$ The 6380E drive has a capacity of 5.04 GBytes and a list price of \$63,000. In other words, achieving a 100 percent increase in storage capacity requires only a 13 percent increase in purchase price. Similar cost-of-capital economies are found within product lines for a number of vendors [25]. In addition, the increased density of storage media leads to a reduction in disk access time as rotational delay and seek time are reduced. To the extent that disk access time constrains the ability of the organization to accomplish tasks, there may be cost-of-capital economies with respect to data storage.

In combination, the two effects listed above serve to offer economies of scale with respect to online data storage for large computer systems. Small storage requirements can be met with investment in a single controller and a small-capacity drive for a particular cost per quantity of data stored. The average costs of larger storage quantities, though, falls off as more devices are hooked to each controller and as higher-capacity drives are employed at a lower average cost. When available mainframe storage devices are configured into the lowest capacity configurations (one controller and one drive) and highest capacity configurations (one controller and the maximum number of drives), the resultant combined population of storage capacities displays an interesting relationship between average cost and capacity. Approximately half of the variation in average cost is explained by a function of the inverse of the total capacity available:

$$
\text { Average   cost } = \frac {\text { Total   cost   (000) }}{\text { Total   capacity   (GBytes) }} = 1 9. 5 + 5 1. 5 \frac {1}{\text { Capacity }}.
$$

Ein-Dor offers other evidence of interest here. His 1981 data showed that both disk drives and printers “appear to conform to Grosch’s law throughout the range of products offered” [29, p. 150]. Further, for large systems only, there are increasing returns to the price of the entire system when the system is expanded along the single dimension of mass storage.

Overall, then, full-employment-of-capital and cost-of-capital aspects of data storage lead to economies with respect to this aspect of system cost. The range of these economies may be substantial relative to the costs of storage devices but economies are also limited by physical constraints. Future research in this area should seek to determine whether the cost of data storage and the ability to realize economies in this area are or have been determinants of firm or industry structure and their importance as a design determinant.

## 8. Communications

THE COMMUNICATION OF DATA BETWEEN SEPARATE PROCESSING NODES of an information system, between geographically removed parts of an organization, between trading partners, and between users and purveyors of information is a part of many modern information systems. Fundamental to the implementation of this capability is the availability of two components of a data communication system, the interface between the processing/data storage components and the communications channel and the communications channel itself. This section looks at the costs of each of these system components.

## 8.1. Communications Devices

Evaluating the cost of communications interfaces is extremely problematic because of the dizzying variety of available devices and the high variation between them with respect to supported device and channel compatibility, speed of operation, memory, sophistication (e.g., accuracy checking, protocols supported, etc.), and the number of hosts and terminals supported. At the simplest level of analysis, we can see that the cost of modems for PC use varies by supported speeds with the most expensive modems being the fastest. Modems, therefore, display cost-of-capital economies with respect to the volume of data transmitted. Among more sophisticated devices, there is weak evidence $(R^{2}=38)$ of cost-of-capital advantages with respect to the number of communicating devices (host computers and terminals) front-end processors can handle. Cost and feature data from the Datapro Research Group [27] indicate that the average cost per channel supported by front-end processors declines as the number of channels increases. $^{4}$ No statistical significance was found with respect to other capabilities of the processors (speed, memory, data storage). Finally, given the selection of a particular front-end device, full-employment-of-capital advantages accrue with respect to the cost of connected terminals as the number of connected devices increases. Similar full-employment-of-capital advantages accrue with respect to the cost per station on an LAN once cables and servers have been put in place.

## 8.2. Communications Channels

Economies exist with respect to the volume of data sent over communications channels up to the capacity limits of the channels. These economies arise from the fact that most communications channels require a significant fixed cost for installation and have relatively low costs for actual use. The availability of these economies has given rise to certain patterns of use. In the United States, data communicated beyond individual processors or LANs typically travel over channels owned by common carriers, including the Regional Bell Operating Companies and the long distance carriers, the three largest being ATT, MCI, and U.S. Sprint. Other common carriers own satellite channels or regional transmission facilities.

Common carriers will install transmission channels between points in a network with channel capacity, as well as the number of paths between any two points, determined by estimated demand. In particular, capacity will be at some level between average demand and peak demand. If a channel between points has a cost (including normal return) per period of time (fixed cost + expected life + operating costs) of c, a capacity of v, and an average demand factor (proportion of capacity actually used) of $0 \leq x \leq 1$ , the carrier must charge p = c/xv per unit of capacity to recover costs. Note, though, that for some subset of total capacity, $v_{1} \leq v$ , the carrier can recover costs for that subset by charging price $p_{1} = v_{1}c/v$ as long as the demand factor for that subset is equal to 1 ( $x_{1}v_{1} = v_{1}$ —the carrier charges for that subset as if it were fully employed, regardless of the actual traffic). The carrier comes out even by permanently leasing some portion of capacity to an interested user at the actual cost of the capacity. From the user's perspective, the arrangement makes sense if the total cost (and average cost) of the permanent commitment is less than the cost of normal service. Such conditions would occur at some level of demand, $v_{2} \leq v_{1}$ , where $v_{2} / v_{1} > x$ , or where the average proportion of the permanently leased capacity used is greater than the average usage level of the entire channel.

The preceding discussion is reflected in observed patterns in the use of data communications channels. Low volumes of data sent between any two points typically use common carrier channels wherein the circuit is temporarily used (or leased) at the prevailing tariff and then disconnected at the conclusion of the transmission (as in a conventional phone call). When a sufficient volume of data is transmitted between two points ( $v_{2}$ from the discussion above), an intermediary such as Telenet or Tymnet can permanently lease a percentage of available capacity ( $v_{1}$ ) and make a permanent data channel available. Users then subscribe to the intermediary service at some price less than the normal tariff but high enough to provide a return to the intermediary. This arrangement also supports investment in improvements to voice-grade circuits to “condition” them for more reliable data communication. Finally, when a single user has a sufficient volume of data traffic between two points, the user can permanently lease capacity directly from the common carrier, thus directly realizing the economies available to the intermediary. These arrangements are commonly (but erroneously) referred to as “private lines,” though they are, in fact, leased lines. In extreme cases, users may construct actual private telecommunications (including data communications) channels wherein the user actually owns the physical communication channel. These arrangements are typically found in “metropolitan area networks” and in “bypass systems,” private networks that bypass the local telephone company and connect directly to the long distance carrier [70].

To summarize, economies of scale are available with respect to data communications, though these economies vary in nature and extent. Though only weak cost-of-capital economies can be shown with respect to data communications hardware, there can be full-employment-of-capital economies based on the ability to fully use the capabilities of a given hardware configuration. The case for economies in transmission channels is stronger. Users realize cost-of-capital economies when they are able to buy (reserve) larger proportions of a communication channel. Carriers are able to offer the volume discounts since they realize full-employment-of-capital economies from the users' high-volume demand. These same economies support intermediate providers of data communications services.

## 9. IS Overhead

THE FINAL COMPONENT OF IS FUNCTION AND COST in Table 1 is system overhead. Overhead includes those organizational elements whose presence supports or enables the operation of an IS but that do not directly participate in the processing of data. Examples are real property (computer rooms, administrative offices, etc.), supervisory and administrative personnel, and machine operators. Specifically excluded from this discussion are programmers (whose costs are included in the software components of systems) and analysts (whose costs are included in either software or design elements).

Discussing overhead as a component of IS is problematic because of the number of dimensions to overhead and the attenuation of the relationship between measures of system output and system overhead. Further, the cost and contribution of what we classify here as overhead have received virtually no attention in the MIS literature. Despite these problems, however, certain conclusions can be drawn with respect to the costs of overhead as system scale increases.

There seem to be economies of scale for supervision and administration with respect to the operation of existing IS. Since existing systems are machine-intensive as opposed to human-intensive, these operations approximate Mintzberg's machine bureaucracy [49], a characteristic of which is low levels of supervisory overhead (larger spans of control) as the operating level (number of functions performed by the system, in the case of an IS) expands. In terms of the discussion in section 2, these factors are full-employment-of-capital economies. These economies should be found for machine operators and maintenance technicians with respect to the number of computers and other hardware devices to be operated and serviced. The introduction of technology substitutes for human operators can help to reduce the diseconomies associated with supervision while enjoying full-employment-of-capital economies in their own right. Examples of these technologies are automated tape and CD-ROM libraries or IBM's expert system for monitoring system performance [6].

Supervisory overhead associated with the creation of IS—that is, supervision and coordination of analysts and programmers—is associated with the rate of creation (since higher rates are achieved with larger staffs and/or closer supervision), not necessarily with the size and scale of the finished product. In other words, systems can reach great size with respect to many usage factors (such as data, number of transactions performed, etc.) with relatively low levels of supervisory overhead as long as the rate of expansion involves lower levels of human participation (for software creation and system design) and commensurately lower levels of supervision and coordination per period of time.

It can be inferred, then, that supervisory overhead for IS is directly related to environmental turbulence since this turbulence produces requirements for new systems and software, including software maintenance. In stable environments, systems and software can accumulate gradually with lower staffing levels and less supervisory overhead. As the environment becomes more turbulent, however, development activity, development resources (programmers and analysts), and supervisory and administrative overhead must increase [43].

Real property costs (consisting principally of computer rooms) also display some economies of scale with respect to system size. Since many aspects of facilities construction such as design, contracting effort, and regulatory costs do not vary much with respect to project size, there are cost-of-capital economies with respect to the physical size of specialized computer rooms. Standard construction costs per square foot of commercial office space decrease from \$74.95/sq. ft. for a 10,000 sq. ft. facility to \$54.85 for a 78,000 sq. ft. building [45, pp. 162–163]. The specialized air conditioning units for computer facilities display similar scale economies with materials and labor for a 3-ton unit being \$2,500/ton while the cost of a 23-ton installation falls to just under \$1,000/ton [46, pp. 323–324].

In addition, real property can display cost-of-capital economies with respect to the growth of certain aspects of system performance. Secondary data storage expansion, for example, can often be accomplished in proportionately less space than would be required by the physical replication of low-capacity storage devices. This aspect of overhead cost has proved to be significant in data-rich applications such as online information services [18]. Similar economies may be realized with respect to increasing computational capability, including expanding main memory.

In summary, there seem to be specific economies with respect to IS overhead based on physical system size (cost of capital for real property), number of transactions (full employment of capital), and data storage requirements (cost of capital). There seem to be diseconomies with respect to rate of system development. Overhead costs for system operation, supervision, and administration may enjoy full-employment-of-capital economies at lower levels while suffering from diseconomies as the number of supervisory levels increases with scale expansion. There is room for new research to determine, first, whether the costs classed here as overhead form a significant (relative to other factors) cost of system development and/or operation. If so, specific research on the supervision and coordination of MIS professionals could lend insight into the cost of software development and system design. More specific information on the cost of constructing specialized computer facilities may be relevant in certain cases (see section 11).

## 10. Scope Economies in the Production of Systems

THE PRECEDING SECTIONS HAVE EACH DISCUSSED THE NATURE of IS costs as system use is expanded along the single dimension of the subject component. The finding of actual or potential scale economies in each of these component areas is an important result, the significance of which is discussed in section 11 below. Note, however, that decisions made in regard to one functional component of an IS may affect the costs, capabilities, and performance of other components and therefore of the entire system. While it is not the goal of this paper to study these interactions at even the limited detail devoted to the costs of individual functions, the presence of these interactions forms a caveat for the preceding findings as well as new research directions. This section, then, points out a few of these interactions and the variety of forms that they take.

In general, economic interactions between the functional components of an IS can take three forms. Decisions with respect to one component can (1) impose technological limitations on the choices available for other components, (2) limit the scale of growth (and therefore of cost economies) for other system components, and (3) shift the costs of other choices.

There are several examples of ways in which the choice of one aspect of a system imposes technological limits on other components. An extreme example is provided by the relationship between secondary storage devices and central processors. Selection of a mainframe computer, for example, automatically limits choices for secondary storage to a select class of devices, even within the range of storage devices available for all mainframes. Similarly, selection of a processing environment necessarily limits the availability of prewritten software, including operating systems. Entire development languages or techniques may be limited to certain operating system or hardware platforms.

A number of examples also exist wherein a decision regarding one system component limits the scale at which other system components can effectively operate. For example, while equivalent levels of MIPS can be achieved through either multiple small processors or a single large processor, processor selection limits the scale of data storage and I/O that can be effectively accommodated. Though recent technological advances have made multi-gigabyte storage devices for microcomputers commercially feasible, constraints on the I/O and processing power available in these machines limit mass storage to large numbers of relatively small data files. DBMS or transaction systems requiring very large files and I/O-intensive operations are still limited to larger classes of machines. Also, the choice of either processor or data communications technologies influences the scale at which the other can effectively operate.

Finally, selection of a system component may change the costs of other components. A common example of this is the practice of bundling software, especially operating systems, with processors so that the total cost of the bundled package is less than the cost of the individual components purchased separately. Similar bundling arrangements may be available between system design and hardware purchase and between system design and application development. Hardware acquisition strategies can influence overhead costs as when lease arrangements include maintenance support not provided under a purchase option.

A final comment on cost interactions concerns the effect of technology on system capabilities. Technological progress has improved the price/performance ratio for virtually all components of IS, usually through downward shifts of entire cost curves, and also through changes in the shapes of curves. One of the most dramatic effects of technology has been the extension of system capabilities beyond previous boundaries. In particular, extensions in the capabilities of small systems have relaxed constraints on choices for system design. Since Ein-Dor's previously cited research $[29]$ , small systems have acquired data storage in the multi-gigabyte range, processing speeds in the 15+ MIPS range, and multiuser capabilities. Further, the availability of more powerful small-system software, CASE tools for PCs, and the ability to connect PCs to databases resident on large systems increases the usefulness of small computers. In other words, systems that would automatically have been constrained to be built around medium to large hardware platforms due to data sharing, data volume, or processing requirements may now be constructed around significantly less expensive processors.

## 11.1. Planning for Scale

Each component of an IS generally has two cost elements: a fixed cost (of which a large percentage may be sunk, i.e., nonrecoverable) and variable costs for each unit of output attributable to the component. In many cases, increasing the fixed investment in a resource lowers the variable cost, at least for some scale of operations. For example, a programmer with a terminal requires some level of investment (training, terminal cost, etc.) and produces some volume of code (SLOC). Investing more resources in a specialized development environment (workstation, 4GL, private office, additional training) requires a higher cost but, presumably, results in greater productivity. In addition, each component has some limit on productivity such as the limiting number of SLOC produced per period in the programmer example. ATC $_{2}$ is the ATC curve for the same system component implemented via different technological choices. It has a higher fixed cost but lower variable costs and a productive capacity of $q_{d} > q_{c}$ .

If each unit of output has an identical value to the organization regardless of production method, costs become the entire determinant of profitability. Technology 1 is the cost minimizing/profit maximizing choice for any level of output $Q < q_{c}$ and technology 2 is the preferred choice for any level of output $Q > q_{c}$ .

There are three direct implications of this situation. First, understanding the nature of costs associated with acquisition options is fundamental to the decision process. Second, estimating the scale of output (use) is equally critical for the decision maker. Overestimating demand for output may result in the investment in technology 2 leading to higher per-unit costs at the realized level of demand. Even accurate estimates of eventual demand present these problems when demand is initially low but projected to grow. If technology 2 is selected to provide an eventual capacity greater than $q_c$ , the cost of unused capacity during low-demand periods becomes a capital cost of the investment. Underestimation, on the other hand, will result either in the inability to realize the optimum system size due to constraints on technology 1 at $q_c$ ; in replication of units of production, including replication of associated fixed costs, to achieve output levels beyond $q_c$ ; or in the abandonment of technology 1 in favor of technology 2, including the loss of sunk costs associated with technology 1. These results are consistent with Alchian's presentation regarding planned levels of output [3].

Third, many current practices have the effect of mitigating the effects of incorrect decisions of the type discussed above. Hardware lease arrangements, portable source code, and standardized communications protocols lower the costs of switching between technologies. Vendors can facilitate this transition for competitive reasons. Upward compatibility, service, and leasing arrangements are ways in which vendors have sought to lessen the impact of uncertainty on decisions associated with scale of operation.

To summarize, the presence of scale economies in conjunction with relatively high fixed (and sunk) costs increases the importance both of the system analysis process and of understanding the nature of system costs with respect to scale. While it is beyond our scope to provide a complete guide to the practical and theoretical literature of this field, Table 4 provides some references that can serve as starting points for both practitioners and researchers.

## 11.2. Transaction Costs

Of transaction costs, Williamson wrote:

[Their] analysis supplants the usual preoccupation with technology and steady-state production (or distribution) expenses with an examination of the comparative costs of planning, adapting, and monitoring task completion under alternative governance structures. [77, p. 2]

A significant stream of research has focused on transaction costs as determinants of organizational form and the structure of vertical production processes. $^{6}$ Transaction cost theory has been important in recent MIS research because of the prominence of information constraints in transaction costs and the ability of information systems to overcome these constraints. Work by Malone et al. [47], Gurbaxani and Whang [37], and Bakos [7, 8] has suggested how IS can affect market organization via their impact on transaction costs. However, MIS research on transaction costs has focused on how information technology can affect these costs, not on how these costs apply to the acquisition of IS. This section briefly discusses transaction cost theory with respect to the acquisition of IS resources and in light of the findings of this paper.

The contrast between fundamental tenets of transaction cost theory and contrary behavior observed in the acquisition of IS resources highlights the contribution of this paper. In particular, decision makers regularly select IS acquisition strategies (market governance structures)—namely, outsourcing—with high transaction costs. It will be seen that this behavior is only rational if the selected acquisition strategy provides production cost savings (of the sort discussed here) which at least offset the increase in transaction costs.

Transaction cost theory holds that three attributes of a transaction—frequency of occurrence, asset specificity, and environmental turbulence—influence choices between discrete structural alternatives for organizing a transaction [78]. These alternatives include market, hierarchical, and mixed forms where each is “distinguished by different coordinating and control mechanisms and by different abilities to adapt to disturbances" [78, p. 269]. If the environmental turbulence for any particular class of transactions is given, then frequency of occurrence and asset specificity become the determinants of transaction governance. In particular, Williamson writes that asset specificity plays a significant role in the determination of organizational form.

The problem here is that observed behavior differs sharply from the predictions of transaction cost theory. Specifically, there are few organizational resources with more asset-specific properties than custom-designed systems or software, yet the design and creation of these assets is routinely outsourced to extraorganizational agencies (consulting or IS service firms). Since market or hybrid (long-term contract, joint venture, etc.) organization of transactions for highly specific resources incur the highest transaction (governance) costs, organizing these transactions outside the firm only makes sense “if these added governance costs are more than offset by production-cost savings and/or increased revenues. A full analysis will necessarily make allowance for effects of all three kinds” [78, p. 282].

The analysis here indicates that, for the components of information systems, production cost savings embodied in economies of scale may be of such degree that they “more than offset” the increased costs of external organization of the transaction. In particular, the strong economies associated with the “intellectual” components of systems (system design, software, and data) can lead to external acquisition of these components despite the possibility of high transaction costs associated with this arrangement.

## 11.3. The Organization of Firms and Industries

Finally, if the opportunities for achieving scale economies suggested here are correct, there should be evidence of decision makers adapting to take advantage of the savings. This section briefly examines structural and behavioral evidence from a number of sources that is consistent with the cost patterns reported in this paper. This discussion is by no means comprehensive and is based, as stated, on a preliminary survey of IS costs. Nor does it claim to prove the patterns of economies suggested here. Nevertheless, consistency of results lends support for a working hypothesis that economies in IS costs can help determine transaction organization, and motivates a program of more rigorous research into both the production costs of IS and their roles as determinants of industry and organization form.

## 11.3.1. Banking

Mergers and acquisitions have been prominent features of the banking industry in recent years and there are intuitive reasons to lay some, though certainly not all, of the motivation for this trend at the foot of economies in IT. The banking industry is one in which, unusually, participant “inventories,” deposits, and the like are similar to information resources in that they both take the form of records in computer systems. Data communications for check clearing, automatic teller machine operations, and multibranch banking are also important. If there were economies to be realized with respect to IT application, it would be expected that they could be observed in the banking industry, especially in light of recent regulatory changes that have removed effective caps on service areas and therefore on market size.

In fact, observers do report on economies with respect IT in banking as influencers of industry reorganization. Bryan writes in the Harvard Business Review that “computer technology helped create new economies of scale that generated pressures for consolidation. Regional banks, which were able to operate at significantly lower costs than smaller banks, moved to acquire and rationalize smaller competitors” [20, p. 76]. Time reported that “after a merger . . . banks can reap big savings by combining their computer systems and clerical operations. All that can ensure higher profits” [33, p. 48]. Reports on the 1991 BankAmerica/Security Pacific merger include expectations that considerable savings will be realized by combining IS operations [11, 76]. Lederer and Mendelson discussed the impact of regulatory changes that expose local banks to out-of-state competitors with more highly developed IS [43].

## 11.3.2. Online Databases

The online database industry has displayed tremendous growth since the early 1980s, with U.S. sales reaching \$8 billion by 1990 [44]. Database producers are faced with choices of distribution methods, with two important choices being direct distribution by the producer and distribution via a specialized database vendor such as Dialog Information Services. West [73] showed that scale economies in hardware, software, telecommunications, and marketing played a significant role in determining distribution method. In particular, small-scale databases tended to be self-vended on smaller, low-cost systems. Very large database producers could also distribute databases directly when their scale of operations provided cost structures that matched those available on smaller systems. The study also showed, however, that intermediate scales of operation would often be accomplished by large specialized vendors whose scale of operations gave them low costs and enabled them to pay database producers royalties in excess of profits available through self-vending.

## 11.3.3. Computer Service Bureaus

Time-sharing computer service bureaus were a prominent feature of the early information services industry. Changes in cost structures, however, have resulted in changes in the way these time sharing bureaus provide value. According to the U.S. Department of Commerce [69], $^{7}$ computer service bureaus are shifting their mix of services. In particular, there is a recognizable decline in the amount of pure time sharing (access to high-powered processing) which is consistent with Mendleson's description of shifts in the costs of computing power. Instead, these bureaus are offering a mix of management services; software, design, and network services; and commoditylike transaction processing for banking, medical billing, and transportation industries. These changes represent shifts away from services where cost advantages of large-scale operation are eroding and toward services with better cost advantages accruing to scale and/or specialization. Further, there is a noticeable concentration in the industry with the top 20 firms (out of 2,000 in the industry) receiving over half the revenues.

## 11.3.4. Off-Site Data Storage

Cost trends in data storage and data communications, as well as economies in the construction and management of specialized computing facilities, are consistent with the growth and increasing concentration in the niche market for off-site data storage. Organizations offering this service archive computer data (usually on tape) as part of disaster recovery plans for users. The services provide “space, security, environmental controls, backup systems, a method for record management, a delivery service, and more security” [26, p. 201]. At least one facility was built to withstand the physical and electromagnetic effects of a nuclear explosion. Some vendors provide online vaulting, concurrent, or batch backup of transaction records via telecommunications links.

As the information on overhead and data storage economies would indicate, the off-site data storage industry has seen increasing concentration in recent years $[26]$ . Market leaders have transformed themselves from local or regional providers into national companies. Part of this transformation has occurred through growth, but much has been accomplished through acquisition. Increased size has resulted in distinct advantages:

As the big off-site storage vendors get bigger, it becomes increasingly difficult for the smaller vendors to compete. The national organizations have the capital and the facilities to drive smaller vendors out of the market. The multi-branch companies have the national link necessary to transfer data quickly to a hot site located across the country, whereas regional facilities could run into difficulty. [26, p. 103]

## 12. Conclusions

ALL COMPONENTS OF IS HAVE ACTUAL OR POTENTIAL SCALE ECONOMIES to varying degrees. This finding has implications for the organization of industries, firms, and operating units. Note, though, that this conclusion rests on preliminary survey evidence and that there is a great deal of room for further research. Valuable research of three types can build from the findings reported here.

The first type of research involves expanding the depth of information available on IS component costs, including cost interrelationships (scope economies/diseconomies). Such research can seek to identify the prevalence of economies, especially in the cases of components such as software, where multiple forms of economizing have been identified, and data where economies in production and distribution may be offset by diseconomies in quality control.

The second research direction concerns the transaction costs and benefits associated with the acquisition of IS resources. This research assumed no difference in benefits between sources for IS components—that components are, in effect, commodities with respect to performance. If they exist, however, benefit differentials may influence transaction organization in certain cases. Similarly, the paper assumed high transaction costs for the external acquisition of IS components, especially intellectual components. The degree of these costs and any mitigating or exacerbating factors need also to be more fully identified before profitable application of theory to specific cases can be attempted.

Finally, the third research stream addresses the implications of findings from the first two. Industrial organization theory predicts tendencies for the organization of transactions based on costs and benefits, especially when costs and benefits differ by scale or scope of production or by acquisition method. Testable hypotheses can be constructed from this body of theory, and evaluated with respect to their applicability to IS acquisition. Given the strength of the outsourcing market for information services, understanding the motivations for choosing this method of acquisition is certainly a worthwhile research endeavor.
