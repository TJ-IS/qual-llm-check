ABSTRACT: Information technology (IT) projects can fail for any number of reasons, and can result in considerable financial losses for the organizations that undertake them. One pattern of failure that has been observed but seldom studied is the runaway project that takes on a life of its own. Such projects exhibit characteristics that are consistent with the broader phenomenon known as escalating commitment to a failing course of action. Several theories have been offered to explain this phenomenon, including self-justification theory and the so-called sunk cost effect which can be explained by prospect theory. This paper discusses the results of a series of experiments designed to test whether the phenomenon of escalating commitment could be observed in an IT context. Multiple experiments conducted within and across cultures suggest that a high level of sunk cost may influence decision makers to escalate their commitment to an IT project. In addition to discussing this and other findings from an ongoing stream of research, the paper focuses on the challenges faced in carrying out the experiments.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: escalating commitment, escalation, information systems failure, runaway, software project management, sunk cost.

RUNAWAY INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY (IT) PROJECTS—projects that seem to take on a life of their own—represent a type of IT failure that can cost firms millions of dollars. While such projects have been frequently reported in the press [6, 29, 34], they have received little attention from information systems researchers. Although runaway IT projects are eventually terminated or significantly redirected, anecdotal data suggest that many of these projects are allowed to continue for too long before appropriate action is taken. Consider the following examples:

\- California Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV): In 1987, California's DMV began developing a new distributed information system that would combine the state's driver and vehicle registration databases [13, 19]. By 1990, after \$14.8 million had already been spent, there were warning signs that the project was in serious trouble. Yet, according to a recently released state auditor's report on the project, these problems were ignored. Even in the face of new reports in 1991 and 1992 showing more evidence of "serious problems with the project," DMV officials pushed on. Instead of terminating or redirecting the project, they "continued to pour money into the project . . . and, as a result spent an additional \$34.6 million on a project that ultimately failed" [15].

\- AMR Information Systems (AMRIS): In 1988, AMRIS began developing a state-of-the-art reservation system integrating airline, car rental, and hotel information. The \$125 million project known as Confirm, was to be a joint effort with substantial funding supplied by partners Hilton, Marriott, and Budget Rent-A-Car. Shortly after the project was initiated, a host of problems began to emerge involving technical design flaws and project management difficulties. Still the project continued. Finally, in 1992, after several years of work, the Confirm project was terminated, resulting in numerous lawsuits and countersuits among the companies involved in the project [21, 22, 30].

\- U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS): In 1992, the General Accounting Office (GAO) criticized HHS for failing to pull the plug on several state child support enforcement systems. GAO investigators put the spotlight on three state systems that “continued for a period ranging from three to eight years, at a total cost of more than \$32 million in federal funds, before the projects were stopped and redirected” [6].

\- Huber Corporation: In 1988, Huber, a Finnish corporation specializing in heating and ventilation, wanted to take advantage of modern information technology and decided to replace its technical platform and all major transaction-processing applications at the same time. The project, which involved a \$7 million investment in new hardware alone, was launched with great fanfare [42]. The new system, which was to be based largely on application software packages, involved a significant amount of customization in order to fit the company's operations. In 1989, the project was already over budget and the CEO admitted in the annual report that there were problems with this investment [1]. By 1990, after spending an undisclosed sum of money on the new system, the CEO had to report that the corporation was experiencing major trouble because of the project [2]. The survivability of the entire corporation was threatened when Huber began losing many of their most important customers who had become dissatisfied with the company's poor level of service. The negative impact on Huber's performance ultimately led to a significant redirection of the project. The final cost of the project remains a company secret.

These vignettes represent what can be described as escalating commitment to a failure course of action $[9]$ and we believe that many IT failures can be described in such terms. In order to gain a better understanding of runaway IT projects, we have developed a broad-based research program that includes both field-based and laboratory-based studies grounded in escalation theory. The purpose of this research program is to investigate the following questions:

\- Does escalating commitment to a failing course of action occur in an IT project context?

\- What factors promote or inhibit escalating commitment on the part of individual decision makers?

\- What theories best explain the phenomenon of escalating commitment?

\- Does the phenomenon occur less frequently in some cultures than in others?

\- Are IT projects any more susceptible to escalation than other types of projects?

This paper presents the results of a series of laboratory-based experiments conducted from 1992 through 1994. The primary focus of the laboratory experiments was to determine whether escalating commitment could be observed in an IT context (i.e., to address the first of the five questions listed above). Of course, laboratory experiments have their limitations; while they can answer some of the above questions, other questions will require field-based research. Therefore, the experiments described here are part of a larger research program that includes field-based research.

In the spirit of what Van Maanen [43] refers to as a “confessional tale,” the results reported here focus on the research challenges that were encountered as we attempted to answer the first of the five questions listed above. Thus, this paper serves two purposes: (1) to report the results of an ongoing series of laboratory experiments focusing on IT project escalation, and (2) to describe the research “process” instead of simply reporting the results, so that other investigators can appreciate some of the challenges that are often encountered in conducting laboratory-based experiments.

## Theoretical Foundations

THE CONCEPT OF ESCALATING COMMITMENT REFERS TO THE HUMAN TENDENCY to adhere to a course of action even in the face of negative information concerning the viability of that course of action [9, 40]. $^{1}$ Anecdotal evidence suggests that this behavior occurs in both governmental and business organizations [33, 38] and experiments have shown that the behavior can be reproduced and studied in a variety of laboratory settings [10]. Brockner and Rubin [10] provide an excellent overview of this phenomenon and the various ways it can be manifested in everyday situations.

Recent work suggests that many runaway IT projects exhibit characteristics indicative of escalating commitment to a failing course of action and that theories of this phenomenon might usefully be applied in the context of IT failure $[27, 28, 32]$ . The most promising theories that have been put forth to explain escalating commitment are self-justification theory (SJT) and prospect theory.

## Self-Justification Theory

Self-justification theory (SJT), which is grounded in Festinger's theory of cognitive dissonance [16], is based on the notion that “individuals seek to rationalize their previous behavior . . . against a perceived error in judgment” [39] and suggests that in the presence of negative feedback, individuals who have made a prior commitment to a project are more willing to commit additional resources than those without any prior commitment.

Staw's [38] study of SJT in the context of business decision making is probably the earliest and most frequently cited research in this area. Using a role-playing methodology, Staw measured the effect of prior commitment on subjects' willingness to allocate resources between two divisions of a hypothetical corporation. In Staw's experiment, personal responsibility and feedback were manipulated as independent variables and allocation of resources was the dependent variable. He found that subjects' allocation of resources toward a previously chosen alternative was greatest under conditions of high personal responsibility for the previous investment decision and negative feedback. Staw interpreted these findings to be consistent with a self-justification theory of escalating commitment.

## Prospect Theory

Whyte [44] has suggested that prospect theory may offer a better explanation for escalating commitment. Prospect theory provides a framework for understanding the cognitive biases that influence human decision making under conditions of risk and uncertainty [25, 26]. As such, it describes the heuristics that people use to evaluate risky choices. Prospect theory posits that individuals exhibit risk-averse or risk-seeking behavior depending on how a problem is framed. In short, prospect theory suggests that individuals will exhibit risk-seeking behavior in choosing between two negative alternatives (especially when the choice is between a sure loss—the initial loss on the investment—and the possibility of a larger loss combined with a chance to return to the reference point [44]). Garland [17] has invoked prospect theory to explain the so-called sunk cost effect—the tendency to “throw good money after bad.”

## Research Methodology

TO ADDRESS THE RESEARCH QUESTIONS AT HAND, we considered the choice between laboratory experiments and field-based research. Bonoma characterizes this choice as a trade off between integrity and currency [8]. Normally, exploratory (i.e., theory-building) research would argue for a field-based approach whereas explanatory (i.e., theory-testing) research would point toward a laboratory experiment. Since there is no established base theory regarding IT failure, this would seem to argue for an exploratory approach involving field-based research.

Recognizing the legitimacy of both arguments and the nature of the research questions to be addressed, we developed a research program that combined both laboratory-based experiments and field-based research. The laboratory experiments to be described here focus on the decision processes that may underlie IT project escalation, while the field-based work is aimed at understanding IT-specific factors and management practices that can either promote or inhibit escalation.

Most of the subjects who participated in the research were undergraduate and graduate business students enrolled in introductory information technology courses at a large urban university in the United States. In subsequent efforts to replicate one of the experiments, masters' level business students at two other universities (one in the United States and one abroad) served as subjects. While the choice of students may limit external validity $[20, 24]$ , there were several reasons for taking this approach. First, the literature on the use of students as subjects suggests that the nature of the experiment itself is a factor in determining whether or not students are an appropriate choice for subjects. From the standpoint of external validity, there is some support for using students as surrogates for managers, particularly when the tasks being studied involve human information processing and decision making [5], which was certainly the case here. Second, since we were interested in knowing if the escalation observed in other decision contexts could be replicated in an IT context, we wanted the other conditions in this study to match those used in prior research so as to avoid introducing additional sources of variance. Since previous laboratory experiments on escalating commitment have used students as subjects, we chose to do so as well.

To date, we have conducted five different experiments on IT project escalation. In each experiment, undergraduate or masters' level business students were given a case narrative describing an IT project and then asked to decide whether to abandon or continue the project. The first experiment was designed to test the self-justification theory of escalation in an IT context. Following this, four experiments were conducted to investigate the sunk cost effect that is consistent with a prospect theory interpretation of escalation.

## Results and Discussion

## Experiment 1: Self-Justification in the Context of IT Projects

A MODIFIED VERSION OF THE STAW [38] EXPERIMENT was conducted during the summer of 1992. The experiment was performed at the beginning of an introductory graduate-level course in information technology at a large urban university in the United States. Our subjects were M.B.A. students with an average work experience of approximately six years. Subjects were informed that they were being asked to participate in a study on business decision making and that their participation was voluntary. Nearly all students chose to participate.

Subjects were randomly assigned to one of two experimental conditions. The independent variables were prior commitment (high, low) and feedback (negative). The dependent variable was willingness to commit additional resources. The treatment group $n = 22$ , who were asked to play the role of project initiator, received an “A” case that provided positive feedback on the early history of an IT project and were asked whether they would authorize the funds needed to complete the project. In all but three cases, subjects in this condition chose to pursue the project. Subjects were then given a “B” case in which they were informed that the project had run into several obstacles and were asked to make a second funding decision.

The control group $(n = 24)$ received a combined “A and B” case. These subjects were informed that they were taking over the project midstream (after the initial funding decision) because the project initiator had moved to another position within the company. They were asked to make the second funding decision. Consistent with a self-justification theory of escalation, we hypothesized that members of the treatment group would be more willing to commit additional resources to the project due to their prior commitment.

The results, however, revealed no significant difference between the treatment and control group in terms of subjects' willingness to commit additional resources to the project. Hence, the evidence did not seem to support an SJT-based interpretation of escalating commitment. While these results were unexpected, there are several possible explanations for them.

First, role-playing situations involving case studies constitute inherently weak manipulations; it is hard to get subjects to feel emotionally committed and hence difficult to create the conditions that are believed to be important under SJT [10]. A second explanation is that the manipulation was effective but that the control group may have felt just as committed as the treatment group since they too were exposed to a positive project history before receiving negative feedback. Finally, the rich case study used may have introduced too much “noise” into the system. As Swieringa and Weick [41] point out, we had attempted to address external validity by adding “mundane realism” to our experiment. Initially, the idea of using a realistic case scenario seemed appropriate, but the results that were obtained reinforced for us the tradeoff between making the case scenario as realistic as possible versus maintaining a high degree of experimental control.

Since much of the literature seemed to be based on SJT, which pointed in the direction of Staw's work, our first inclination was to try another variation on Staw; but we also considered replicating a study by Garland [17] involving the effect of sunk costs on subjects' willingness to commit to resources to a project. Frustrated by our inability to replicate Staw's results, we conducted a more exhaustive review of the literature and found that at least two other attempts to replicate Staw's experiment had failed [36, 37]. In light of this negative feedback, we decided not to escalate our commitment to Staw's design!

## Experiment 2: An Initial Exploration of Sunk Costs in an IT Project Context

Instead, we tried replicating a variation of Garland's [17] experiment in which sunk cost levels were manipulated parametrically in order to determine the effect on subjects' willingness to continue with a project. In his experiment, Garland observed a linear sunk cost effect: subjects' willingness to continue with a project appeared to increase linearly with the percentage of budgeted funds already spent. The case narrative used in this experiment was similar to the one used by Garland; instead of an R&D project, however, the scenario described an in-house software development project (see appendix, scenario 1 and scenario 2, to compare our scenario with Garland's scenario). After being presented with some negative information concerning the viability of the project, subjects were asked to decide on continuing or abandoning the project given that a particular fraction of the budget had already been spent.

The subject pool for our experiment consisted of 124 M.B.A. students enrolled in an introductory information technology course at a large urban university in the United States. Subjects were randomly assigned to one of four treatment conditions representing different levels of sunk cost (15 percent, 40 percent, 65 percent, and 90 percent). After they read the scenario, subjects were asked: How likely is it that, if faced with this situation, you personally would choose to continue with the CONFIG project? Responses were measured on a scale that ranged from 0 percent to 100 percent with 10 percent intervals (with 0 percent indicating “definitely would not continue” and 100 percent indicating “definitely would continue”). The results of the experiment did not reveal the expected upward sloping sunk cost effect; instead we obtained a horizontal line with a mean response of approximately 40 percent to the “willingness to continue” measure.

## Experiment 3: A Second Attempt to Understand Sunk Costs in an IT Project Context

Based on the results obtained in experiment 2, we decided to align our experimental protocol more closely with the one used by Garland. First, we decided to administer the experiment to undergraduates rather than M.B.A.s. This was done to match Garland's conditions more closely and for sampling convenience. Second, we decided to change the context of the case scenario from a project for internal application (limited potential payoff) to a project for external sale (high potential payoff). Third, to insure that subjects were responding to our manipulation of sunk cost levels, we added a table summarizing the numbers and included a manipulation check. At the same time, we decided to extend Garland's previous work by deliberately investigating whether the inclusion of an alternative course of action would have a moderating influence on the sunk cost effect.

One important factor that may affect how individuals respond to sunk costs is the presence or absence of an alternative course of action. In a review of the escalation literature, Staw and Ross [40] consider “infeasibility of alternatives” as one of several project variables that can heighten escalation by reducing the “perceived utility of withdrawal.” They do not, however, cite any empirical studies to support this supposition. A study conducted by Northcraft and Neale [31], however, suggests that decision makers can be made less susceptible to the sunk cost effect when the opportunity costs of continuing with a particular course of action are made explicit.

In the new experiment, two independent variables were therefore manipulated: the level of sunk cost and the presence or absence of an alternative course of action (see appendix, scenario 3 and scenario 4). The subject pool consisted of 322 undergraduate business students enrolled in an introductory information technology course at a large urban university in the United States. Participation was voluntary and 313 students agreed to participate by signing an informed consent form, resulting in a participation rate of 97.2 percent. Fifty-four percent of the subjects were male; 44 percent were female. Missing values accounted for the remaining 2 percent. Descriptive statistics concerning the subjects' age and work experience are shown in Table 2.

The results of the experiment (figure 1) showed that both variables, the level of sunk cost and the presence or absence of an alternative, had a discernible effect on subjects' willingness to pursue a prior course of action.

## Experiment 4: Replication of Sunk Cost Experiment in a Different Culture

Based on Hofstede's work, it seemed reasonable to assume that subjects of different cultures might behave differently when exposed to the same decision-making scenario [23]. Of the cultural dimensions identified by Hofstede, “uncertainty avoidance” is the one that is most relevant to decision making under conditions of risk or uncertainty. Since escalation situations involve a high degree of uncertainty [40], it is reasonable to assume that cultures that exhibit a high degree of uncertainty avoidance should be less prone toward escalation than cultures that exhibit a low degree of uncertainty avoidance.

As an initial test of these ideas, the sunk cost experiment described above was replicated in a different culture. The country chosen for the experiment was Finland, which scored somewhat higher than the United States (59 versus 46) on Hofstede's uncertainty avoidance index [23]. Hofstede's research would predict that the Finnish subjects would have less tendency to escalate than the U.S. subjects. Thus, the purpose of the study was twofold: (1) to provide a second replication of the sunk cost experiment with subjects from a different culture, and (2) to determine if subjects would be less likely to escalate their commitment in a country with a higher degree of uncertainty avoidance than the United States.

The subject pool for this experiment consisted of 254 master's students enrolled in an introductory information technology course at a Finnish university. Forty-two percent of the subjects were male; 58 percent were female. Nearly 85 percent of the Finnish subjects were either currently employed or had some previous job experience. Descriptive statistics concerning the subjects' age and work experience are shown in Table 3.

The Finnish study was a replication of experiment 3 using a Finnish translation of the same scenario. In order to ensure that the meaning was preserved in the translation process, the scenario was translated into Finnish by one individual and then back-translated into English by another individual. Based on this process, minor corrections were then made to ensure that the Finnish scenario corresponded to the English scenario as closely as possible. As in experiment 3, the “without alternative” manipulation involved four different treatment groups representing various levels of sunk cost (15 percent, 40 percent, 65 percent, and 90 percent). Due to a limitation in the number of subjects, however, the “with alternative” manipulation was limited to two treatment groups (15 percent and 90 percent sunk cost levels).

The results were consistent with our expectations. As shown in figure 3, the Finnish replication revealed a sunk cost effect that was similar to the one observed in the U.S. experiment.

A two-way factorial design ANOVA again indicated significant main effects, but no significant interaction effect. In other words, both the level of sunk cost $p = 0.01$ and the presence or absence of an alternative course of action $p = 0.01$ were shown to have an effect on the subjects' willingness to continue. As in the case of the U.S. experiment, an upward sloping sunk cost effect was observed regardless of whether or not the decision maker was given the choice of an alternative course of action. As before, subjects were less likely to escalate their commitment in the presence of alternative course of action.

The most discernible difference between the Finnish data and the U.S. data is that the Finnish subjects had less of a tendency to escalate their commitment at high levels of sunk cost. For the “without alternative” manipulation, separate one-way ANOVAs were performed to compare the Finnish group with the U.S. group at both the 65 percent and 90 percent sunk cost levels. In both cases, the difference between the means of the two groups was found to be significant (p = 0.02). For the “with alternative” manipulation, an ANOVA comparing the Finnish group with the U.S. group at the 90 percent sunk cost level was only marginally significant (p = 0.09).

While it seems reasonable that the difference in outcome between the U.S. experiment and the Finnish experiment was due to cultural differences, further study is needed to substantiate this. It is tempting to conclude that the observed outcomes are due to differences in uncertainty avoidance, but there may well be other cultural and noncultural factors involved here that cannot be ruled out given the design of the experiment. Conclusions about the effect of culture could be drawn with greater confidence if the study had included measures of uncertainty avoidance for each subject rather than relying solely on Hofstede's country-specific index values. It should be noted, also, that during the time of the experiment Finland was in the midst of a recession and this may have caused people to be even more risk-averse than normal. For these reasons, it is important to conduct additional research to confirm these findings and to explore further the effect of culture on the sunk cost effect.

Having demonstrated that the sunk cost effect can occur within an IT project context and across cultures, we turned our attention toward understanding the effect of sunk costs that exceed the amount budgeted. The motivation behind experiment 5 was the observation that IT projects are notorious for being over budget [4, 7, 12, 14]. Two-and threefold budget overruns are not uncommon. Anecdotal evidence suggests that budget overruns may be indicative of “runaway” projects. Thus, from a project management perspective, this type of information is likely to be interpreted as negative feedback.

The impact of negative feedback on the escalation phenomenon has been studied by others (e.g. [18]), and there is suggestive evidence that when negative feedback is strong enough it can lead to deescalation and project withdrawal. Prior work, however, has only examined the interaction of negative feedback and “within-budget” sunk costs. In this experiment, we examined the effect of sunk costs that exceeded the amount budgeted to determine if this information alone might be interpreted as negative feedback and hence lead to deescalation.

Q: How likely is it that you personally would choose to continue with the CONFIG Project?

The subject pool for experiment 5 consisted of 247 undergraduate business students enrolled in an introductory information technology course at a large urban university in the United States. Eight levels of sunk costs were manipulated ranging from 15 percent to 610 percent (see appendix, scenario 5). The results of the experiment are shown in figure 4. An analysis of variance revealed no significant differences across treatment groups. Nevertheless, the results shown in figure 4 reveal three interesting patterns.

First, as one would predict based on the results of experiments 3 and 4, there does appear to be an upward sloping sunk cost effect (although not statistically significant) as we move from 15 percent to 90 percent sunk costs. It is reasonable to ask why this apparent sunk cost effect in moving from 15 percent to 90 percent was not found to be statistically significant, as it had been in the previous experiment. We can only surmise that this result is a reflection of subtle differences between the two case scenarios. In experiments 3 and 4, where there were significant effects due to sunk cost, the scenario (see appendix, scenario 3) indicated that the time required to complete development was six to eight months, whereas in experiment 5 the scenario (see appendix, scenario 5) indicated a time period of ten to twelve months. This change alone may have attenuated any sunk cost effect by making continuation appear less attractive from a competitive standpoint.

Second, there also appears to be some deescalation of commitment occurring (again, not statistically significant) as we move from 90 percent of budget to 10 percent over budget (i.e., sunk costs of 110 percent in relation to the amount budgeted).

Finally, and perhaps most interesting, there does not appear to be any further deescalation of commitment as we move from 10 percent over budget to a factor of more than six times over budget (i.e., 610 percent). Although there are several possible explanations for this, one possibility is that the potential payoff perceived by subjects outweighed the sunk costs—that is, however much had been spent already seemed insignificant relative to the profit potential associated with successful completion of the project.

In any case, the unwillingness of subjects to deescalate their commitment in the face of extreme budget overruns is counterintuitive and is worthy of further investigation, as it may offer some explanation of why runaway systems projects occur.

## Summary and Conclusions

RETURNING TO OUR RESEARCH QUESTIONS, ANALYSIS of the results indicates that escalating commitment to a failing course of action does occur in an IT project context and that theories of escalating commitment may be useful in explaining runaway IT projects. While we did not conduct a definitive experiment to determine which of the two competing theories represents a better explanation of IT project escalation, our results appear to be consistent with a prospect theory interpretation of escalation. Our failure to replicate Staw's design suggests that further work is needed in order to test the applicability of self-justification theory. It is possible that both SJT and prospect theory offer viable explanations of escalating commitment and more work must be done to understand the IT ramifications of each.

The first two experiments described here aptly illustrate the difficulty of conducting laboratory-based experiments. Conducting high-quality experiments that will provide results that are both meaningful and reproducible requires successive refinement of experimental methods and procedures. Experiment 3, for example, demonstrates that the sunk cost effect can occur within an IT context and that the results can be replicated. In this experiment, both the level of sunk cost and the presence or absence of an alternative course of action were shown to have an effect on the subjects' willingness to continue with an IT project.

An analysis of the results yielded two major findings. First, an upward sloping sunk cost effect was observed regardless of whether or not the subjects were given the choice of an alternative course of action. Second, in the presence of an alternative course of action, subjects were less likely to remain committed to a prior course of action than those subjects who were not given an alternative project. Experiment 4 not only shows that the sunk cost effect can be replicated in a different culture, but also suggests that cultural factors may influence the strength of the effect. Finally, the results of experiment 5 suggest that sunk costs that exceed the amount budgeted may lead to some deescalation of commitment, but far less so than might be expected. Since these findings are somewhat counterintuitive, further experimentation is needed before any conclusions can be drawn.

As with any research, there are limitations to the work described here. Since these results are based on laboratory experiments, one must be cautious when generalizing from them for two reasons. First, results obtained using students as subjects may be somewhat different from results obtained using actual managers with relevant work experience. The study should be replicated using more experienced subjects to determine if there is any difference. Second, the laboratory experiments conducted here take a necessarily narrow focus in order to achieve a high degree of control. There are many organizational and political factors that may also affect an individual's willingness to proceed with a project. These factors have not been investigated here and may not lend themselves to laboratory study.

Despite the limitations discussed above, the findings reported here may have important ramifications for managing IT projects. While additional research is needed, the results suggest that managers should be made aware of the sunk cost effect to avoid making escalating commitments to a failing course of action. The results also suggest that managers can be made less susceptible to the sunk cost effect if they are trained to consider alternative courses of action. Additional research is needed on the factors that lead to sunk cost effects.

More research is also needed on techniques that would help managers avoid the sunk cost trap $[35]$ . Project management techniques that force decision makers to generate alternatives may be one effective means of reducing escalation. Further work is needed to determine which deescalation strategies are most effective.

It would also be interesting to investigate whether IT projects are any more prone to escalation than other types of projects. IT projects are notoriously difficult to control $[3, 11, 14, 45]$ ; the intangible nature of software makes it difficult to determine project status and to obtain accurate estimates regarding time completion. We would expect, therefore, that IT projects may be particularly prone to escalation. While the problem of runaway IT projects is not likely to be solved overnight, we believe this paper establishes a strong foundation for understanding, and preventing, the phenomenon.
