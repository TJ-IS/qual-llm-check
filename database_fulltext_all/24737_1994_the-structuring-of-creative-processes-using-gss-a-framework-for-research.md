# The Structuring of Creative Processes using GSS: A Framework for Research

MURLI NAGASUNDARAM AND ROBERT P. BOSTROM

MURLI NAGASUNDARAM is Assistant Professor of Computer Information Systems in the College of Business at Boise State University. He has a bachelor's degree in engineering from the University of Madras, an M.B.A. from the Indian Institute of Management, Calcutta, and is completing his Ph.D. in MIS at the University of Georgia. He has several years of managerial and entrepreneurial experience in the information technology and services industry. As a cofounder of Collaborative Technologies Corporation, he helped design a group support system called VisionQuest. His current research interests include group support systems, creativity, and the design of information technology-based organizations.

ROBERT P. BOSTROM is Professor of Management Information Systems in the Department of Management at the University of Georgia. He holds a B.A. and an M.B.A. from Michigan State University, an M.S. in computer science from the State University of New York, Albany, and a Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Minnesota. Besides numerous publications in leading academic and practitioner journals, he has extensive consulting and training experience in the areas of MIS management, MIS design, organizational development, high-performing professionals, and computer support for groups/teams. Dr. Bostrom is president of a training and consulting company that focuses on the effective intergration of people and technology, especially within teams. His current research interests focus on high-performing individuals, user-designer relationships, group support systems, and effective design of organizations via integrating human/social and technological dimensions.

ABSTRACT: Radical and discontinuous change is the order of the day in the 1990s. Organizations are seeking to improve their fit with a constantly changing environment through initiatives such as business process redesign (BPR) and total quality management (TQM). Getting people to think creatively is critical to the success of these change efforts. Group Support Systems (GSS) have emerged as a potential means of supporting and augmenting creativity in the modern team-based organization. GSS research, however, has largely ignored the nature (i.e., creativity) of ideas that are generated.

This paper seeks to move GSS research on idea generation beyond its previously limited focus on the quantity of ideas generated when using GSS. Drawing on multiple theoretical perspectives and research streams—creativity and idea generation, Kirton's cognitive style, GSS, and adaptive structuration theory (AST)—we distinguish between two orthogonal dimensions for measuring creativity (of ideas, individuals, and creativity processes)—creativity level and paradigm-relatedness; identify

RADICAL AND DISCONTINUOUS CHANGE IS THE ORDER OF THE DAY in the 1990s. Through initiatives such as business process redesign (BPR) and total quality management (TQM), corporations are exploring new organizational forms that will enable them to be successful. Even very large corporations are completely reformulating their strategic directions. To stay ahead in today's highly competitive market environment, organizations rely heavily on a steady stream of new products and services that will constantly differentiate them from their competition [6].

Organizations have begun to recognize that establishing such new paths calls for new and creative ideas [42, 79]. At Motorola Inc., creativity serves as the foundation for the company's five key initiatives [77]. While employees are an organization's most vital and distinctive resource, institutionalizing creativity requires much more than exhorting employees to be creative in their work. One result is the increasing emphasis on employee empowerment through nonhierarchical, team-based organizations [46, 58]. This helps the organization tap into the collective intelligence and innate creativity of the entire work force, rather than rely on an elite group of planners who may have no direct contact with a problem context.

Supporting these organizational trends is an emerging class of information technologies called group support systems (GSS). GSS are typically based on personal computers and local area networks and support groups engaged in a variety of tasks requiring collaboration and coordination (see $[13, 44]$ , for extensive reviews of the state of GSS research and technologies). Group members may interact either synchronously (i.e., in real time) or asynchronously, and may be either colocated or geographically dispersed (i.e., distributed). GSS are particularly well suited for group creativity tasks $[26]$ .

Although creativity has been a subject of speculation for hundreds of years, formal research in structured idea generation dates back to the publication of Alex F. Osborn's book on "brainstorming" [59]. The first study by Taylor, Berry, and Block [71] to test Osborn's claims for his techniques seemed to contradict him—nominal groups (where individuals work separately without interacting) "outperformed" brainstorming groups. Numerous studies since then have almost consistently supported the Taylor et al. results, with more detailed studies attempting to determine why brainstorming fared “poorly” in experiments (see [25, 50, 55] for reviews).

GSS researchers have paid considerable attention to the phenomenon of idea generation. Even among GSS studies whose focus is different from idea generation, a significant proportion at least report statistics on the number of ideas or alternatives generated [51]. There is fairly strong evidence that electronically interacting groups do outperform nominal groups [21, 30, 75] in the generation of ideas. The performance difference is usually attributed to GSS structural features such as the simultaneous and anonymous input of ideas [30, 75].

In almost all GSS and non-GSS idea-generation research, performance has been measured by the quantity of ideas generated. This practice followed from Bouchard's [14] finding that if a large quantity of ideas was generated, then the idea pool very likely would contain high-quality ideas. Despite the poor laboratory performance of techniques such as brainstorming (based largely on quantitative measures), the business world continues to rely on them [27]. Perhaps those techniques help generate ideas of the kind that organizations find especially desirable, even if they are generated in fewer numbers. Aspects of the ideas generated or process used other than the quantity of ideas generated may govern the continued use in industry of discredited techniques. We need, therefore, to explore one or more alternative dimensions or criteria for evaluating ideas.

This article is concerned with the focused (or “targeted” [33]) use of GSS and creativity techniques to generate ideas that are best suited to the specific needs of an organizational context. Previous approaches to characterizing the quality of ideas have been based typically on some measure—usually quite subjective and context-specific—of their level of creativity. We argue, however, that creativity level is insufficient for characterizing the creativity of an idea. Two ideas may be ranked equally on their creativity according to some criterion (i.e., they are of the same creativity level) but may be creative in significantly different ways. We introduce an additional measure called “paradigm-relatedness” (PR) to capture this distinct dimension of an idea. The PR attribute of an idea is independent of and orthogonal to the creativity level (CL) attribute. Together, they constitute a two-dimensional space on which to map ideas, and thereby provide a richer and more global understanding of the nature of ideas that might be generated by individuals or groups.

While one conceivably could formulate additional dimensions for characterizing ideas, we emphasize that PR is significant enough to merit attention by itself. The PR concept is echoed, for instance, in reengineering efforts where a basic question is whether change should be incremental (largely preserving prevailing organizational paradigms) or whether it should be revolutionary (thereby changing existing paradigms). Organizations, at various times, seek ideas that either preserve the status quo or make a clean break with the past. We refer to ideas that tend to operate within the assumptions of a prevailing paradigm as “paradigm-preserving” (PP) and call ideas that challenge or break away from a prevailing paradigm, “paradigm-modifying” (PM). We argue that the concept of paradigm-relatedness fills a key need in GSS and idea-generation research by providing a basis for analyzing the effects of different techniques and technologies.

An understanding of the PR concept will help organizations choose among different creativity support processes and technologies when faced with a particular problem or decision. For instance, change initiatives may range from incremental steps to radical and discontinuous reorganization. The choice of creativity process and technologies will depend on the type of change desired. Understanding the implications of a change initiative requires that we consider the degree to which it represents a paradigmatic change, and take appropriate steps to manage that change.

We have synthesized theoretical perspectives from multiple research streams into a research model based on Adaptive Structuration Theory (AST) [23, 62]. AST describes how individuals and groups appropriate structures from procedures and technologies and adapt them to their own purposes in the course of accomplishing their tasks. Guided by the research model, we identify a number of structuring mechanisms in creativity techniques and supporting technologies (specifically GSS), and examine their possible effects on the paradigm-relatedness of ideas generated.

In summary, our objectives are:

\- to distinguish between and develop two orthogonal dimensions for measuring creativity (of ideas, individuals, and creativity processes)—creativity level and paradigm-relatedness;

\- to identify and characterize fundamental structuring mechanisms present in creativity techniques and GSS that influence one or the other of the above dimensions;

\- to explore the implications of the above for research, design, and the targeted deployment of GSS and creativity techniques in organizations.

In the next section we review the key elements of research in the field of creativity, emphasizing the aspects of interest to this research. Based on the issues examined, we then develop a research framework and a set of propositions for investigating the effects of various influencing mechanisms on the creativity of ideas produced in an idea-generation process, and analyze some existing idea-generation processes. In the fourth section we discuss some research issues and then present concluding comments.

## Creativity

CREATIVITY IS A COMPLEX AREA OF STUDY that defies precise definition [72]; this is reflected in the numerous ways in which the concept has been defined. Given its roots in psychology, early research in creativity focused on the creative personality. Since then, however, the scope of creativity research has expanded to include:

• the person (or group) that is creative;

• the product of creating;

• the process of creating; and

\- the press, or environment in which creation comes about (i.e., climate, or context) [54].

Fellers and Bostrom [26] point out that each of the four components influences and is influenced by all the others (figure 1). Each of the four components is discussed in detail in the following subsections in terms of paradigm-relatedness.

## The Creative Person: Adaptors and Innovators

Researchers have studied the creative individual from a variety of perspectives. Treffinger [74] observed that “creativeness is a multivariate affair requiring more than one test variable to assess it.” A variety of tests exist to measure creativity from different perspectives such as the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking [73]. With most creativity-measuring instruments, an individual’s “creativity level”—that is, his or her capacity to be creative—is determined on the basis of some criteria (e.g., fluency and flexibility) and all individuals are ranked on a scale. Individuals receiving higher scores are deemed “more creative” than those receiving low scores.

Michael Kirton and his associates [47, 48, 49] have taken a radically different approach to assessing an individual's creativity. Kirton asserts that individuals have different preferred cognitive creativity styles which are manifest in their varied approaches to formulating and solving problems: creativity style is a preferred, stable, manner of bringing about change. Two individuals could be “equally creative,” but in different ways. Creativity level measures capacity for creativity, while creativity style measures the manner in which creativity manifests itself (figure 2). ## Creativity style: How are you creative?

In studies replicated in a dozen different countries including the United States, United Kingdom, Australia, and countries in Asia and Europe, it has consistently been found that preferred creativity style is a construct distinct from and orthogonal to creativity level, which is the more conventional approach to measuring creativity. Kirton identifies two principal preferred creativity styles: adaption and innovation. The adaption/innovation distinction is critical to understanding the way in which organizations function, and could explain their success or failure in various contexts [49].

Adaption is the characteristic behavior of individuals who when confronted with a problem, turn to conventional rules, practices and perceptions of the group to which they belong—and derive their ideas towards the solution of the problem from these established procedures. When there is no ready made answer provided by the repertoire of conventional responses, then the adaptor will seek to adapt or stretch a conventional response until it can be used in the solution of the problem.

![](/api/attachments/PFUWAQRV/fulltext/images/e5c2227cb6d0c6957c6b6d40677b30f2d687cc926ca864c55e7fff25c7638178.jpg)  
Figure 2. Creativity Style versus Creativity Level

Innovation is the characteristic behavior of individuals who, when confronted with a problem, attempt to reorganize or restructure the problem, and to approach it in a new light, free from any of the customary perceptions or presuppositions which would be the conventional starting-point for its solution. Innovators thus produce answers which are less predictable and thereby sometimes less acceptable to the group. [48, pp. 283–284]

Adaptors prefer to do things better, while innovators prefer to do things differently. Kirton [48] also identified a set of individual traits on which adaptors and innovators tend to differ markedly. Individuals having the same creative capacity (based on some creativity level test) could have distinctly different creativity styles. Kirton emphasizes that neither of the two styles is necessarily superior; on the contrary, specific contexts of application demand one style rather than the other, and organizations typically experience both kinds of contexts at various times.

Organizations and groups are typically made up both adaptors and innovators in varying proportions. Adaptors tend to enter professions that encourage stability and order, such as accounting and production, while innovators are likely to be found in departments, such as marketing and R&D, which need to interact with changing environments and deal with uncertainty $[48]$ . Most organizations require a healthy blend of adaptors and innovators, each playing a special role in its growth and prosperity, and each style complementing the other. While innovators are constantly exploring and formulating new opportunities for the organization, adaptors ensure that those ideas are implemented and monitored efficiently $[67]$ . Nevertheless, depending on the needs of a specific task, a working team may be constituted entirely of adaptors, or innovators, or of varying proportions of the two. The distinctive (and antithetical) styles often surface as intragroup conflict in ongoing group interactions.

An instrument called the Kirton Adaption–Innovation Inventory (KAI) measures individual creativity style [47]. Scores on the KAI lie on a continuum theoretically ranging from 32 (“pure” adaptors) to 160 (“pure” innovators), with a mean value of

96. The stability of the KAI style dimension has been observed also over time, incident, and even culture, and may be measured quite accurately even early in an individual's life. KAI scores do not correlate significantly with IQ, achievement tests, creativity (level) tests, and tests for cognitive complexity [49], providing additional evidence that creativity style is a separate dimension.

Using Kirton's adaption-innovation framework, we now proceed to examine the nature of the products of creative processes.

## Creative Products: Paradigm-Preserving and Paradigm-Modifying

Ideas, like individuals, could be viewed as being equally creative in different ways. We introduce a dimension called “paradigm-relatedness” along which to measure the degree of paradigm preservation or modification (figure 3).

This dimension corresponds to the creativity style dimension for persons discussed earlier. Ideas may be placed on a continuum ranging from paradigm-preserving (PP) to paradigm-modifying (PM). Kirton's findings suggest that adaptors are most likely to prefer formulating PP ideas but might be capable of generating PM ideas. Likewise, innovators are most likely to produce P M ideas, but they may, in some circumstances, formulate PP ones.

Paradigm modification in ideas may come about in two ways, each acting by itself or together with the other, resulting in three different classes of PM ideas (figure 4). New ideas may be generated by introducing new elements into a problem context (quadrant EXTEND), by altering the relationships between the elements of a problem (REDESIGN), or both—by introducing new elements as well as by altering the relationships between elements (TRANSFORMATION). Ideas that introduce neither new elements nor new relationships are REFINEMENTS—PP ideas.

## Examples

Using an in-line four-cylinder internal combustion engine as base idea, an in-line six-cylinder engine is a REFINEMENT idea. Other examples of incremental (REFINEMENT) ideas are the use of oval pistons and combustion-chamber redesign. An in-line twin-cam four-cylinder engine is an EXTENSION idea (the relationships between elements are largely the same, but a new element—a second camshaft—has been introduced). Another instance of an EXTENSION idea is the use of multiple valves and turbocharging. The Wankel rotary engine is radically different from any of the above engines—it uses entirely new elements and new relationships among those elements—and is therefore a TRANSFORMATIONal idea.

The Apple Macintosh computer's graphical user interface represented a radical departure from prevailing interfaces when it was introduced—it involved new elements such as icons, mice, and windows, and new relationships among those elements as well as with the user, and was therefore a TRANSFORMATIONal idea. When the hierarchical file system was introduced in the MS-DOS operating system, there was a change in relationships but no change in elements—it was a REDESIGNED idea. Major new features that are added to operating systems are often EXTENSION ideas—new capabilities that do not alter relationships among the element of the system, or the relationship of the system with the user.

It may happen that the introduction of a set of new elements (EXTENsion) into a problem context motivates an alteration of the relationships between those elements (REDESIGN), and likewise, an alteration of the relationships between the elements may suggest the introduction of new elements or the elimination of some existing elements, resulting ultimately in a TRANSFORMational idea. In terms of paradigm-relatedness, TRANSFORMational ideas are clearly the most paradigm-modifying, while EXTENsion and REDESIGN ideas represent intermediate levels of paradigm-modification. Reengineering efforts in organizations often involve radical change (TRANSFORMATION)—new elements (such as technologies and services) and new relationships (new procedures). Total Quality Management (TQM) stresses continuous, incremental change (REFINEMENT), and sometimes new processes are introduced (REDESIGN). Traditional change efforts often introduced new elements such as technologies and departments (EXTENSIONS) but had a minimal impact on interrelationships.

## Operationalizing and Studying Paradigm-Relatedness for a Specific Task

While the elements-relationships framework is a general guide for analyzing paradigm-relatedness, the categories to be used in a specific context are content-dependent.

The above framework could be used to guide the development of task-specific coding schemes. We present a sample codification scheme below.

A task used by Gryskiewicz [32, 33] involved a teabag manufacturing company seeking ways to utilize its excess capacity for manufacturing teabags. Participants in his study were asked to generate ideas for different uses of teabags. Gryskiewicz classified their ideas into four ordinal groups: direct, supplementary, modify, and tangential. Direct ideas were consistent with the prevailing paradigm. They did not redefine the basic problem for the company—they substituted tea with some other material that could be placed in the bag. In our terminology, they were paradigm-preserving (PP) ideas. Tangential ideas, on the other hand, broke away from the assumption that the company could only manufacture containers for some substance, and instead thought of uses for the material used to produce bags—they redefined the paradigm itself. We would call them paradigm-modifying ideas (PM). The other two ordinal categories represented intermediate degrees of paradigm modification.

## Creative Problem-Solving Processes

VanGundy [76] lists as many as thirty-one group creativity techniques that are employed to various degrees in industry. Some of the techniques apply specifically to nominal rather than real groups. Many of the techniques were developed for specific application contexts, types of groups, or desired outcomes. The choice of a technique in a given context should be dictated by its performance with respect to the nature of the desired outcomes—that is, ideas. Thus, if the need is for a radical idea, one would use a technique that tends to generate PM ideas. A variety of idea-generation techniques could promote the generation of PM ideas by requiring participants to artificially force relationships between unrelated concepts [76], one of which is called guided fantasy.

Gryskiewicz [32, 33] employed four different techniques in his field study—guided fantasy, brainstorming, and two forms of brainwriting (see [76] for detailed descriptions of various techniques). The major distinctions between the techniques are:

\- Guided fantasy helps participants step out of their current frame of thought into a fantasy frame where they are asked temporarily to suspend disbelief. They are then asked to generate ideas by relating their fantasies to the problem.

\- Brainstorming uses a set of four rules that help lower inhibitions and criticism in a group and thus promotes idea generation.

\- In brainwriting, participants write down ideas based on a problem statement, but the technique provides no additional structuring mechanism such as that provided by guided fantasy. In the second form of brainwriting employed, participants were provided the instructions given to brainstorming groups.

Consistent with previous research [25, 55], brainwriting generated more ideas than brainstorming and guided fantasy. Nevertheless, although guided fantasy generated the least number of ideas in Gryskiewicz's study, the ideas produced while using this technique were judged (by domain experts) to be the more PM than those generated by brainwriting. A mere count of the number of ideas generated would not have revealed a key distinction between the two processes.

While all creativity techniques are designed to promote the generation of ideas, their products may be characterized as largely tending toward paradigm-modification (PM) or paradigm-preservation (PP). Thus, even if two techniques, X and Y, generate an equivalent number of ideas, the ideas they generate may differ on paradigm-relatedness (figure 5).

We will now examine the effects of using a GSS with creativity techniques.

## The Use of GSS in Support of Creativity Techniques

Organizations such as IBM [52] are making extensive use of GSS for a variety of group activities, many of which involve idea generation. Group support systems were developed, in part, in response to some of the inefficiencies inherent in group creative problem-solving processes. Many GSS tools and mechanisms provide some level of support for creativity techniques. No existing GSS, however, provides direct support for fully implementing a creativity technique such as guided fantasy. Of the many idea-generation techniques discussed by VanGundy [76], most GSS at best provide direct support for some form of brainwriting. A major research and design task in the future is to explore ways of fully integrating one or more creativity techniques into GSS and thereby provide more direct GSS support for the techniques.

Results from several studies provide suggestive as well as strong evidence of the benefits of using GSS. The main observed result with respect to idea generation is that the use of a GSS significantly increases the number of ideas or alternatives generated (see meta-analyses of GSS findings by McLeod [51] and Benbasat and Lim [11]; specific studies are described in [21, 30, 31, 45, 75]. Little is known, however, about the effects of using a GSS on the nature (i.e., paradigm-relatedness) of ideas generated. Further, different idea-generation techniques have not been studied in a GSS context.

## Evaluating Creativity Techniques

Early studies of creativity techniques attempted to make comparisons among techniques based on the “average quality” of ideas that each technique helped generate. The determination of idea quality has always been a vexing issue since the “quality” construct has been hard to operationalize and tedious to measure $[25, 55]$ . Comparisons among studies of creativity techniques have been rendered nearly impossible by the variety of measures used as surrogates for the quality construct, several of which were found to be uncorrelated $[25, 55]$ . The findings for quality of ideas generated have been heavily dependent on the type of measure used.

Based on his investigations, Bouchard [14] suggested that the average quality of ideas generated by a process correlates very highly with the total quantity of ideas that it helps generate. Differently stated, the greater the number of ideas that a process helps generate, the greater the likelihood of generating “high-quality” ideas. Bouchard’s suggestion finessed the idea-quality issue by eliminating the need to measure quality altogether. Since then, almost all idea-generation studies (whether or not they involve GSS) have used “quantity-of-ideas” as the principal means of comparison between treatments, while the few that have attempted to operationalize measures of “quality” have produced mixed results.

![](/api/attachments/PFUWAQRV/fulltext/images/10d4ee5b461bf1b8a8cc16f30a1bc8e59ae5ed127288a4cec4dc290d02ec998d.jpg)  
Figure 5. Creativity Technique and Ideas Generated

Both of the current approaches to evaluating techniques—quality-of-ideas and quantity-of-ideas—are flawed. The major drawback in using quantity-of-ideas alone as a measure is that, given a set of techniques that generate an equivalently large number of ideas, we have no means of making distinctions among them as to the nature of ideas they generate. The choice of a technique, then, may be driven by factors that have little to do with the nature of ideas one seeks. Quality-of-ideas measures have confounded the two distinctively different dimensions of creativity level and paradigm-relatedness. In most instances, the quality construct has been operationalized using measures of creativity level, such as feasibility, rather than paradigm-relatedness.

In order to evaluate and compare idea-generation techniques, it is important to be aware of the criteria used by groups that employ them. Perhaps the techniques that are used in industry satisfy a different set of criteria from those that researchers use in their studies. The focus on quantity of ideas alone may obscure the existence of these other criteria (and may explain the continued use of techniques that research appears to have firmly discredited). We believe that paradigm-relatedness and creativity level taken together represent a more useful way of characterizing ideas and evaluating creativity techniques.

## The Creative Environment

The last component, press, gained salience through the work of scholars such as Amabile [2], Csikszentmihalyi [17], and Tardif and Sternberg [70], who recognized that creativity exists in a larger system of social networks, problem domains, and fields of enterprise. There is a growing understanding of the importance of an appropriate environment conducive to creativity [68]. For instance, certain periods in history (e.g., the European Renaissance) and certain cities (e.g., Paris and Vienna) are associated with the rapid growth and support of the arts and artists. Organizations such as Xerox's Palo Alto Research Center, 3M Corporation, IBM's Thomas J. Watson Research Center, and AT&T's Bell Laboratories are widely known to be leaders in technological advancement, and manage to attract and retain the most talented researchers in their respective fields.

Several studies by Amabile and her associates [1, 2, 4] have shown that creativity can be undermined by a number of environmental factors such as evaluation, surveillance, competition, contracted-for reward, restricted choice, and a focus on extrinsic motivators. Subsequent studies have suggested that the same factors apply also to organizational contexts [5]. In a recent study conducted in an organizational context, Amabile and her associates [3] investigated the environmental factors that differentiated high-creativity from low-creativity projects. Factors that stimulated creativity were freedom (control over one's work), challenge, sufficient resources, organizational encouragement and recognition, and support from work group (including trust and free communication among group members). Factors that impeded creativity were organizational impediments (including politics, harsh criticism of new ideas, and an atmosphere of risk-avoidance) and extreme workload pressure. High-creativity projects were significantly higher on the stimulating factors, and low-creativity projects were higher on the impeding factors. The single most important distinguishing factor was a sense of challenge in the work pursued. In another organizational study, Scott and Bruce [68] found a strong relationship between support for innovation and quality of supervisor-subordinate relationship (including autonomy and trust) on the one hand and innovative behavior on the other.

It is clear that the organizational environment can affect creativity. There is no research, however, that links environmental factors to the paradigm-relatedness dimension. Also, the environment in turn can be shaped by the person, process, and product components. For instance, a predominance of adaptors or innovators in a group tends to create a climate that supports the corresponding creativity style $[48]$ . A new supervisor with a different style may, over time, restaff a department with personnel of a compatible creativity style, effectively altering the departmental climate. Departments that tend to focus on internal issues (e.g., production) attract adaptors, while departments that tend to deal with issues that arise in their environments (e.g., sales, R&D) attract innovators.

The organizational climate is generated and maintained through the processes of communication in the organization, among others. Altering the content and pattern of communications could result in a changed organizational climate. Specifically, the use of GSS (as a process-enabling technology) can shape organizational climate through its structural features. Providing global electronic access to all employees in an organization opens up paths of communication that may never before have existed. These paths, if appropriated by employees, could alter both their perceptions of the organizational environment as well their behavior. Another way to influence the organizational climate could be through the institutionalized use of creativity techniques. For example, over time, as employees appropriate a technique such as guided fantasy, there may be a shift in the climate toward paradigm-modification.

## The Structuring of Idea Generation: A Research Framework

WE HAVE DOCUMENTED THE NEED TO CAREFULLY CONSIDER the nature of outcomes desired, the preferred styles of individuals, the effects of various creativity techniques, as well as the influence of the environment. For instance, it is not clear that the idea-generation and creativity techniques employed in GSS research are equally efficacious when applied to both styles of creativity. Perhaps some creativity techniques produce a better “fit” with the available technology than others. One may need also to consider the “fitness” of a creativity technique to an individual’s creativity style, keeping in mind the nature of ideas desired. Do adaptors and innovators react differently to a particular technique? Do adaptors prefer paradigm-preserving techniques, and innovators techniques that help break paradigms? What happens when we mix different styles and techniques? How do groups of innovators and adaptors behave—is the distribution of products relatively more PP or more PM than those produced by individuals alone?

By recasting the creativity framework of figure 1 in terms of the Input → Process → Output model used widely in group [37, 53] and GSS research [20, 60], we have developed a research framework for investigating the structuring of idea generation (figure 6) to answer some of the above questions.

The person (group member) who generates ideas has a Kirton score located somewhere on the adaptor–innovator creativity style continuum (represented by a two-headed arrow). The creative product—that is, the ideas generated—may be placed on a paradigm-relatedness continuum that ranges from paradigm-preserving (PP) to paradigm-modifying (PM). The creative process is represented via the metaphor of a lens that causes the person with a preferred creativity style to generate ideas of a certain paradigm-relatedness. The lens is a composite of structures provided by a creativity technique. Where a creativity technique is implemented with or augmented by a GSS, the lens is a composite of structures drawn from both the technique as well as the GSS. The interactions among person, process, and product occur within the context of some creative environment (press).

While we have emphasized the importance of all four components of the creativity framework, we consider person, process, and product to constitute the core, and have chosen to focus on the interactions among these components in a given context, while controlling for press. The basic question driving this research is:

How do individual creativity style, creativity technique and the use or nonuse of a GSS influence the nature (i.e., paradigm-relatedness) of the products (i.e., ideas) generated?

(We are interested in the distribution of products along the paradigm-relatedness dimension rather than the paradigm-relatedness of a specific product).

Our model suggests that creative processes shape the cognitive and social processes of a group idea-generation session in generally predictable patterns. We would like to discover these patterns in order to say that a particular process used with a particular group of individuals will yield a predictable type of result. Specifically,

1. How does the use of a GSS interact with the creative problem-solving process used? Are the paradigm-modifying/preserving effects of the creativity techniques increased, diminished, reversed, or otherwise altered?

2. How does the use of a GSS interact with individual creativity style? Does a GSS augment one individual creativity style more than the other? If so, how and why?

3. Do adaptors and innovators have different preferences in terms of creativity techniques and GSS technologies used?

To explore and understand the effects of creativity techniques, it is essential to analyze the mechanisms and structures on which those techniques are founded. Adaptive Structuration Theory (AST) is a useful guide for the pursuit of this research [63]. According to AST, groups appropriate structures embedded in procedures and technologies and adapt them to their own purposes in the course of accomplishing their tasks. The meaning of a structure cannot be defined in absolute terms—the structure must be viewed as valuable by the group and assumes implications depending on how it is appropriated by the group. An analysis of structures and the roles they play in a group process will help in understanding their effects on the behavior of a group that uses them [62].

Creativity techniques with or without GSS-support provide a set of structures that enable, promote, or constrain certain kinds of group interaction and communication. For instance, the use of a GSS allows all group members to generate ideas simultaneously (i.e., “talk” at the same time), a process that is typically not feasible for interacting groups without the use of a GSS. The group, however, may choose not to utilize the inherent parallelism provided by the technology, and may adopt a roundrobin idea-generation procedure. The effects of the structuring mechanisms, therefore, are not deterministic, but emerge from their interpretation and constant reinterpretation by a group with respect to the task at hand.

In order to test and apply AST in the domain of creativity support, it is necessary to analyze the component features or mechanisms (structures) of creativity support techniques and technologies. For instance, Poole [61] studied several group process techniques such as Robert's Rules of Order, Nominal Group Technique, and Synectics. He analyzed these techniques according to their scope, restrictiveness, comprehensiveness, group control, and member involvement. His objective was to help group facilitators select the technique(s) that most suited the needs of a specific meeting context. Other authors have also attempted to identify structural characteristics of GSS [8, 12]. Most of these efforts, however, have focused on GSS or group process methodologies in general rather than on a specific area of application such as idea generation.

We focus here on one general class of group processes or techniques—those designed to support idea generation. The question we ask is: What are the fundamental structuring mechanisms present in idea-generation techniques and GSS that could influence the paradigm-relatedness of ideas generated? Each creativity technique specifies the use of one or more explicit mechanism for structuring an idea-generation process (these will be discussed in detail in subsequent sections). Some mechanisms are unique to a technique, many are to be found in more than one technique, while a few may be common to all techniques. The mix of mechanisms employed in a technique shapes the idea-generation process, and consequently, the outcomes of the process.

When a GSS is employed to implement a creativity technique, one or more of those mechanisms may be provided within the GSS itself. For instance, a GSS provides the optional ability for participants to generate ideas anonymously if the technique requires it. The GSS-provided mechanisms may not directly match the manual mechanism, except in concept. As an example, a technique such as brainwriting requires participants to write their ideas on a sheet of paper using a pencil or pen. The same general technique, when implemented using a GSS, requires participants to type ideas on a computer keyboard. Whether such differences in implementation affect the outcomes of an idea-generation process is also a research issue.

A GSS may also incorporate additional mechanisms not required or specified by a creativity technique (such as identifiability of participants). Sometimes these supplemental GSS structures may be “switched off” so as to preserve the “spirit” of the technique as originally specified. When these supplemental mechanisms are included in an implemented process, the technique as implemented may be considered a modification of the “manual” (i.e., non-GSS) technique. Indeed, a creativity technique implemented with the help of a GSS might change the nature of the technique itself in terms of both process as well as outcomes.

## Structures in Creativity Techniques and Supporting Technologies

GSS [12, 61] and creativity researchers [76] have identified a variety of structuring mechanisms to support group idea generation. We list some key mechanisms, and have categorized them under the labels, cognitive, social, and procedural. Cognitive mechanisms address the limitations of the human information-processing system (IPS [56]). By manipulating these mechanisms, we can alleviate the limitations of the IPS, or stimulate the production of ideas. Social mechanisms address phenomena that arise from group interactions. Given that idea generation is fundamentally a cognitive process, cognitive structures provide the most direct stimulation for the generation of ideas. Procedural mechanisms help to sequence the various activities required in a session and thereby assist cognitive and social mechanisms.

Social and procedural structures work indirectly, often modifying or moderating the effects of cognitive structures, and in effect may be experienced by participants as cognitive structures. By manipulating these mechanisms, we may alter the idea-generation process. The following list includes the major mechanisms that have been identified and/or studied by GSS and creativity researchers and is not meant to be exhaustive. For each mechanism, its possible states are indicated in parentheses.

## Cognitive Structures

1. Stimulus availability (choice or no choice);

2. Stimulus relatedness to problem (related or unrelated);

3. Stimulation method (free association or forced relationships);

4. Nature of stimulus (visual, textual, aural, etc.; variety and number of stimuli);

5. Collective memory (available or not available);

6. Idea filtering rules (yes [e.g., “best,” “most feasible,” “most outlandish”] or no).

## Social Structures

7. Identifiability of participant (anonymous or identified);

8. Relative location of participants (colocated or distributed);

9. Interaction among participants (none or interacting);

10. Evaluation (allowed or disallowed).

## Procedural Structures

11. Expression mode (speaking, writing, typing, drawing, miming, etc.);

12. Simultaneity (simultaneous or turn-taking);

13. Duration (minutes, hours, days, weeks, etc.).

Our focus is on structures provided in creativity-supporting technology and techniques. There are other important sources of group process structuration such as group facilitation and the preexisting social and organizational relationships between group members—these are outside the scope of this paper. Facilitation, for instance, is a dynamic process that helps to structure and implement the mechanisms embedded in a technique in a faithful manner. It deserves separate and complete analysis and investigation, and some researchers are already investigating it in depth (e.g., [9, 12]). The subject of facilitation, therefore, is not discussed here.

Creativity techniques are based on the use of one or more of the above mechanisms. The mechanisms are typically implemented via rules, procedures, and instructions to participants. For instance, classical brainstorming specifies four rules to which a group must adhere in order to obtain the best results $[59]$ : defer judgment (mechanism no. 10, criticism disallowed); quantity breeds quality (no. 6, no filtering); the wilder the idea the better (no. 6); combine and improve upon ideas (no. 5, collective memory; no. 3, freely associate). Also, brainstorming involves interaction (no. 9) among participants who are colocated (no. 8), are identifiable (no. 7), and verbalize their ideas (no. 11) by taking turns (no. 12). We expect that the manner in which the structures are combined will influence the nature of the outcomes.

## An Analysis of the Effects of Various Structures

For want of space, we limit our discussion to the possible effects of a subset of the above structuring mechanisms. (A discussion of the entire list of structures is presented in a working paper available from the authors.) Based on prior GSS and creativity research, we have selected five structures that appear to be key influencers of PM outcomes. Each structure will be analyzed in terms of its tendency to promote paradigm-preserving or paradigm-modifying ideas.

We saw that each of the structures listed above can assume two or more states. A specific state of a structure may be termed paradigm-preserving, paradigm-modifying, or paradigm-neutral, depending on the kinds of ideas it tends to promote.

1. A paradigm-preserving (PP) structural state promotes the generation of paradigm-preserving ideas.

2. A paradigm-modifying (PM) structural state promotes the generation of paradigm-modifying ideas.

3. A paradigm-neutral (PN) structural state does not explicitly promote either style of ideas. Instead, it creates a context in which ideators are free to adopt their preferred styles. Thus, such a structural state will allow an innovator to generate paradigm-modifying ideas, and an adaptor to generate paradigm-preserving ideas.

The effects of each structural state are discussed individually. We know very little about the relative salience of each structural state when it is combined with others in different ways. The structures may combine in complex ways—some may influence the idea-generation process more strongly than others, some may amplify or neutralize the effects of others.

## Stimulus Availability

A key mechanism in most creativity techniques is the use of some sort of stimulus [76], perhaps a story, an object, a joke, a sound, or a visual. A stimulus helps provoke a stream of thought that could lead to one or more ideas. Note that a problem statement itself is a stimulus for thought: an additional stimulus is especially useful when one is stuck for ideas, and no thoughts appear to form on their own. A stimulus could help focus attention on the problem, and even by itself, suggest some possible solutions. In the absence of any stimulus, on the other hand, there may be little impetus for thought and any ideas generated may revolve around the prevailing paradigm or conventional wisdom. Adaptors and innovators are likely to utilize stimuli differently, with innovators striking out on paradigm-modifying paths and adaptors tending to remain within the paradigm.

Stimulus availability is largely a PN structural state—that is, adaptors are likely to generate PP ideas, while, innovators are likely to generate PM ideas. The lack of a stimulus is a PP structural state.

In the presence of innovators in a group, relatively more PM ideas will be produced with stimulus availability than otherwise.

## Stimulus Relatedness

A problem-related stimulus is one that remains close to the frame of the problem itself. Techniques that use unrelated stimuli (such as guided fantasy) are usually employed to generate ideas that are potentially far-removed from the problem [76]. Hence,

The more related the stimulus, the more likely is the generation of PP ideas. The less related the stimulus, the more likely is the generation of PM ideas.

VanGundy notes that “not everyone can use unrelated stimuli productively, and that many useful ideas can be prompted by related stimuli” [76, p. 76]. It may be that innovators are able to utilize unrelated stimuli better than adaptors, suggesting thereby that even stimuli need to be carefully chosen according to the composition of the group.

## Stimulation Method

A technique may either ask participants to force together a stimulus and a problem, or permit them to freely associate. In free association there is no deliberate, externally motivated intervention to change the direction or content of their thoughts. Typically, the most cognitively “available” associations are the ones that would surface first in participants’ minds. With forced relationships, participants may be asked to associate two concepts that they have never before considered together. PM ideas are more likely to be generated [76]. Conversely, free association may generate more PP ideas.

Free association is likely to generally result in PP ideas, while forced relationships are likely to result in PM ideas.

VanGundy [76] notes that free association relies largely on chance and incubation rather than on deliberate attempts to generate ideas. Also, one must bear in mind that individuals differ in their abilities to force relationships between concepts. Innovators may find such a task easier than adaptors.

## Identifiability of Participants

A number of studies have investigated the effects of identifiability on group idea generation with GSS. Jessup, Connolly, and Galegher [45] found that anonymity resulted in more inputs from participants. There was also a small, though statistically insignificant, increase in the number of original ideas generated. Similar results were obtained by Connolly, Jessup, and Valacich [18], and Jessup and Tansik [43]. Connolly et al. [18] also found that the ideas produced were of a higher “quality” when groups worked anonymously. Any factor that enhanced relative anonymity increased the effect observed. Participants found the system less threatening because individuals were not identified. Anonymity promotes deindividuation, a phenomenon that encourages an uninhibited expression of ideas [28]. The mere opportunity to act in an uninhibited manner, however, does not imply that people will indeed act uninhibitedly. It may be that anonymity encourages individuals to generate ideas that are consonant with their preferred cognitive styles. Thus,

Anonymity is a PN structural state—that is, adaptors will tend to generate PP ideas, while innovators will tend to generate PM ideas. On the other hand, if participants are identified, they are likely to demonstrate greater inhibition, and both adaptors and innovators are likely to generate ideas in a generally PP manner.

While anonymity does not actively encourage uninhibited behavior, the presence of innovators in a group will cause relatively more PM ideas to be generated by anonymous groups than by identified groups.

## Simultaneity

Some idea-generation techniques allow participants to generate ideas simultaneously, while others require them to take turns. Participants may take turns according to some predetermined sequence (e.g., round-robin) or on a first-come, first-served basis. Generally, techniques where the ideas are expressed verbally require some method of turn-taking, while methods that involve writing or keyboarding ideas (as in brainwriting or GSS) do not—although a turn-taking procedure could be imposed here.

During simultaneous idea generation, although each participant's ideas may be available for inspection by all other participants, it is possible for an individual to concentrate solely on generating his or her own ideas and to ignore those generated by the others, at least for limited time periods. The phenomenon of "cognitive inertia" occurs when an individual's thought processes remain uninterrupted and tend to follow a train that remains within a paradigm or subject area [15, 41, 50]. The lack of verbalization in a GSS idea-generation process largely prevents each participant from disturbing or interrupting the thought processes of the others. Further, the fact that ideas are being constantly recorded and not being lost might reduce the need to constantly track ideas generated by others (as in verbal idea generation). During this period of undisturbed idea generation, each participant is likely to generate ideas that are closely related, and remain within a paradigm. Such uninterrupted sequences of PP ideas are less likely when using techniques that require turn-taking procedures—each time a participant utters an idea may represent a paradigm shift.

Simultaneity could result in the generation of PP ideas. Conversely, turn-taking could potentially encourage the generation of PM ideas, relative to simultaneous idea generation.

Table 1 summarizes the effects of different states of the subset of structures chosen for detailed analysis. Table 2 presents the structural profiles of some idea-generation techniques, including a GSS-based brainwriting procedure that may be called electronic brainwriting (eBW). eBW is a common feature in most GSS.

In Table 2, guided fantasy stands out among the other techniques in providing the greatest number of PM structural states. This analysis supports the results obtained by Gryskiewicz [33]. In comparison, nominal brainwriting provides the least number of PM structural states. The lack of PM structural states apparently does not hinder (or perhaps, passively promotes) the generation of a large quantity of ideas. Nevertheless, these ideas are likely to be largely PP in nature. Brainstorming provides more PM structural states than brainwriting, which might make it a relatively more attractive technique in industry. The profile of structural states (for the subset of structures considered) is identical to that for nominal brainwriting. One might expect the ideas generated while using eBW to be similar to those produced by brainwriting.

## Research Issues

## Research Program

THE EFFECTS OF THE STRUCTURES DISCUSSED HERE NEED TO BE INVESTIGATED systematically. This calls for a program of research that exploits any relevant research methodology, including laboratory experimentation and field studies. The objective of the research program would be to explore:

• Different idea-generation techniques;

• The effects of using GSS;

• The development of new GSS creativity tools;

\- Individual differences, including differences in creativity styles and levels.

One approach would be to commence with baseline studies addressing the key questions concerning creativity techniques, creativity styles, and GSS that were raised in the previous section. One kind of study within this research program would examine the effects of different creativity techniques in both manual and electronic treatment conditions on the paradigm-relatedness of ideas generated. These studies would provide the background for further studies aimed at isolating the effects of individual structures and their interactions. Cognitive structures have the most direct and immediate effects on idea generation. It might be efficient to initiate research with a detailed examination of their main and interaction effects. The next research phase could focus on how social or procedural mechanisms moderate or modify the effects of the cognitive mechanisms. It is possible that the relative salience of cognitive structures changes significantly with respect to the nature of the social and procedural structures present. This could be determined by testing the effects of various combinations of cognitive, social, and procedural structures.

One area for applying appropriation analysis is the comparison of manual and GSS-based creativity tools. The apparently identical structural profiles for BW and eBW might suggest that they will generate largely similar patterns of ideas. Nevertheless, since the structures are physically realized in different ways, the appropriation process for the techniques might be different. In general, the fundamental structural states in eBW may be used in a much more integrated manner than in manual procedures. Further, these structures may be integrated more intimately with the cognitive processes of participants. Consequently, the two techniques might produce different results. It would be of interest to determine whether and how the physical implementation of a set of structural features in a technique influences how it is appropriated.

In an earlier section we indicated that the introduction of GSS-based creativity techniques could significantly transform the organizational environment. Barriers to free and open communications are a major impediment to enhanced organizational creativity. Structural features such as simultaneity and anonymity could lower or eliminate such organizational communication barriers, effectively altering the pattern of interactions and eventually changing the organizational structure and climate. The transformed organizational climate might be one that promotes innovative thinking among all employees. GSS could be used to mediate communications that drive both continuous improvement and radical change through ideas that may be contributed by any employee in the organization. This is another area that deserves investigation.

## Measurement Issues

Measuring paradigm-relatedness is a key aspect of this research program. The framework discussed above could be used to guide the development of codification schemes for idea-generation tasks used in research. Codification schemes are task-dependent and should be validated with a set of ideas generated for specific tasks by independent coders.

In experimental and other studies, data need to be gathered at the individual level, but group effects must also be factored in. One interesting research issue is the ability to characterize a group in terms of the KAI scores of its members. The effects of different group compositions (on the adaptor/innovator dimension) are not entirely clear, and need to be systematically studied. A lone high innovator in an otherwise average KAI group might have a significant effect on aggregate group outcomes, especially in a GSS environment.

## Designing New Idea-Generation Techniques

Current GSS techniques for idea generation are largely an “automation” of manual idea-generation techniques using information technology (e.g., electronic brainwriting). Automation, however, may introduce a qualitative difference in a technique so that its appropriation is different from the manual version. A detailed understanding of the structures provided by a GSS and idea-generation techniques—as presented here—and of their manner of appropriation is essential to the design of new GSS-based idea-generation techniques targeted at specific organizational contexts. A crucial task is to find ways to embed creativity techniques in GSS in a more integrated manner than before. We are currently working on incorporating the guided fantasy technique in a prototype tool.

In Table 2 the presence of several PN structural states in both BW and eBW suggests that the nature of ideas generated when using those techniques will be shaped by the cognitive style of participants. Adaptors and innovators are likely to appropriate the same techniques in different ways, thereby generating different results. To reduce the dependency of the outcomes of an idea-generation process on group composition, and thereby to increase predictability and consistency in outcomes, it would be necessary to incorporate explicit PM or PP structural features (depending on the need) in eBW. For instance, if it is established that drawing actively promotes the generation of highly PM ideas of a particular kind, then a new idea-generation technique might be developed using those results by exploiting the benefits of GSS-technologies such as group drawing tools. Much exploratory work is needed in order to find ways of incorporating such structural features.

## Concluding Comments

CORPORATIONS TODAY REALIZE THE NEED TO LIVE WITH CHANGE, and hence to plan for it. Organizational change could be effected in incremental steps—preserving the prevailing paradigm—or could shift away from the current paradigm to varying degrees. Ideas for change apply to different aspects of the organization—its structure, strategies, products, processes, among others. Total Quality Management and reengineering represent two contrasting styles of organizational change, the first focusing on incremental, continuous change, and the latter on radical, discontinuous change.

Whatever the nature of change desired, the targeted deployment of idea-generation techniques is needed by organizations to generate and implement the kinds of ideas that will help them survive and remain ever-competitive in a rapidly changing marketplace. At present, there is little guidance available to organizations on the techniques and technologies that are most appropriate to a specific context. We have argued that group idea-generation research needs to go beyond its limited focus on the quantity of ideas generated, to examine idea-generation techniques, technologies, and ideas in greater depth. To alleviate the shortcomings of current approaches, we developed a research framework that includes person, process, and product and a dimension for characterizing ideas called paradigm-relatedness. We then identified a set of structures incorporated in idea-generation techniques, explored the possible effects of each individual structural state on the paradigm-relatedness of ideas, and examined the structural profiles of a sample of idea-generation techniques.

This research framework is independent of any specific technology and technique and is therefore immune to the effects of advances in technology and techniques. It is intended as a starting point for research. Future studies may lead to an augmentation of our framework with new structures and more detailed insights. We offer this paper as a challenge to researchers to become involved in an important but neglected area of organizational research: the enhancement of organizational creativity through GSS and related technologies.
