# Politics and the Function of Power in a Case Study of IT Implementation

HAROLD G. LEVINE AND DON ROSSMOORE

HAROLD G. LEVINE holds a doctorate in cultural anthropology from the University of Pennsylvania and is currently an Associate Professor in the U.C.L.A. Graduate School of Education. He has both substantive and methodological research interests in the study of organizational change and development, with a particular focus on IT implementation. He has been actively involved in the development of methods for organizational diagnostics, in the explication of organizational culture, and in the understanding of individual action stemming from privately held theories-in-use.

DON ROSSMOORE is Managing Partner of Rossmoore Associates, Inc., a Los Angeles-based management consulting firm with specialities in strategy formulation, implementation, reorganization, and reengineering.

ABSTRACT: Viewing organizations as political coalitions has a long history in management studies and has generated a great deal of research on the roles of organizational politics and power in decision making. Information technology (IT) researchers generally have followed the general trend by providing structural-functional accounts of politics, power, and control strategies. Although such studies underscore the importance, nature, and complexity of politics in IT implementation, they are constrained by the lack of both a general theory of human behavior as well as a model of power that bridges the individual actor and the wider interpersonal/organizational setting. In this paper we present a case study of power and politics in a large financial transactions company, but seen through the lenses of the action science perspective of Argyris and his associates and the model of power developed by A.A. Berle. More specifically, we examine the power vacuum in the IT effort that resulted from the senior executive abrogating his power and failing to delegate it to his subordinates. The resulting political havoc, in turn, caused competition and confusion, wasted effort, and a significant lack of productivity with regard to the implementation of a multimillion-dollar IT effort. We conclude with several hypotheses about how principles of action science and interpersonal power must be used by executives to manage the politics of IT design and implementation.

Acknowledgments: This paper is a substantial revision of a paper entitled “Understanding the Political Threats to IT Implementation: A Case Study” presented at the Twenty-Seventh Annual Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences. The authors would like to thank Eric Clemons and two anonymous reviewers of the original paper for their thoughtful comments and suggestions.

ORGANIZATIONAL POLITICS AND POWER HAVE BEEN TOPICS of much interest and theorizing in the organizational development and strategic management literatures since James March's seminal work in the early 1960s. March reexamined the value of economic models of business firms because they were predicated upon a rational "preference ordering," usually in the form of profit maximization. He observed that much business decision making was characterized not by uniformity of goals and decisions, but rather by their inconsistency, both over time and from one part of the organization to another [24]. March, and others [e.g., 10, 35], described business organizations as sociopolitical conflict systems subject to economic constraints. In this view, the business firm is best characterized as a political coalition, and the executives operating within it as political brokers.

This view of the organization as a political coalition continues to be significant as a model of organizational decision making, departing in significant ways from rational choice and bureaucratic models $[31]$ . An underlying assumption of the rational choice model is the existence of common goals and objectives in organizations from which rational decisions about the likely outcomes or consequences of competing alternatives can be evaluated. The bureaucratic model, on the other hand, assumes that choices among competing courses of action are made according to rules and procedures that the organization has found effective in the past. In contrast to both of these models, a key assumption of the political model is its fundamental incorporation of a diversity of goals and objectives. In this model, conflict and negotiation are inherent—and central—to the system. Courses of action are determined not by rationality or habit but by the political power of individuals or subgroups $[31]$ .

Much of the recent thinking about politics and power in organizations has tended to be either descriptive or theoretical in nature, with a relative disinterest in well-grounded, empirical work. Many researchers continue to be concerned with issues of definition—what are organizational politics; what is power and in what forms does it exist within an organization; or how do politics and power differ from one another. Others address questions concerning the sources of power (or with what properties of organizational structure or process power may be associated), how it is used, and its organizational or strategic consequences [e.g. 21, 22, 32, 33, 34].

Of course, there are a large number of theoretical approaches to the study of social power, including power as a characteristic of the individual, as an interpersonal construct, as a commodity, as a causal construct, and as a philosophical construct [9]. Many theorists in the management science literature have adopted the interpersonal point of view. One recurring formulation takes the following form: Conflict over scarce resources, divergent priorities, access to specialized knowledge, and the like, is normal in organizations. Such conflict, when it occurs, leads to political behavior in which individuals or units attempt to control unilaterally (and, implicitly, covertly) the actions of others and to overcome their resistances. An individual's ability to control resources, priorities, or knowledge is a measure of his or her power. Political behavior, then, is the use of power to resolve conflict in one's favor, usually at the expense of others [9, 11, 14, 20, 27].

In the area of information technology (IT), research on the roles of power and politics in IT implementation and organizational change is relatively uncommon; and actual empirical studies—particularly case studies—that focus on the power and politics of IT implementation are scarce [e.g., 19, 25, 26, 29]. To date, few IT researchers have critically reexamined the basic concepts of power and politics, preferring instead to adopt—consciously or not—the interpersonal framework discussed earlier (e.g., “A group’s intraorganizational power is its ability to influence the task behavior of another organizational member” [6, p. 15]) and to search for sources of power in the organization’s control over the availability, accuracy, and access to information [e.g., 6, 8, 12, 15, 17, 18, 28].

As a result, our insights into the role of power and politics in the design and implementation of IT projects are constrained in several important ways. First, we have few descriptive accounts of how individuals in real organizations respond both to everyday situations and to unusual crises with political behavior. Second, we have little information on how individuals make use of power to achieve organizational ends or on what happens when power relations in an organization are unclear or in a state of flux. Third, the relative lack of attention to individual power holders has precluded an analysis of individuals' privately held motivations, strategies, and goals. As a result, we cannot understand the logical base of individual actions; how it is, in other words, that seemingly dysfunctional, immoral (or amoral), self-interested, and/or counterproductive behavior can make perfect "sense" in organizations where everyone apparently also "agrees" on its goals and strategies.

Finally, even though we now know something of the structural conditions and consequences of power and politics in IT implementation, the broad and abstract nature of this knowledge provides little specific guidance on how to apply an analysis of power to manage the unproductive politics and use of power in an IT implementation where not all of the units are well integrated and quietly efficient.

Our own research on IT implementation and decision making has been informed by two distinct, but complementary, theories. One is a unified theory of bureaucratic power, articulated by A.A. Berle. The other is a general theory of human behavior formulated by Chris Argyris and his colleagues known as action science.

Berle's [7] encyclopedic review of historical and contemporary leaders and both public and private bureaucracies led him to theorize that power is the fundamental concept of social science—in the same sense that energy is the fundamental concept of physics. Berle defines power as the capacity to determine the behavior of other individuals in accordance with one's own wishes, and to mobilize resources to attain one's own goals. A critical point of Berle's thinking is that power is invariably exercised by individuals. No collectivity of any kind in and of itself wields power. The ostensible power of a group stems from the fact that the group has become organized, and has conferred decision-making power upon certain individuals formally or informally recognized as power holders. However it is attained, the expression of power can be only the decision and act of an individual.

Berle's model of power does recognize that power may be extended from one individual to another. In this case, the power holder delegates power to his or her subordinates who, in turn, exercise it in the power holder's name over their own subordinates. This dynamic is characteristic of effective organizations.

Berle observed, however, that ineffective organizations are common and that organizations once quite functional can rapidly become dysfunctional and unproductive. Berle tried to explain this by looking for the personal or organizational factors that might hobble an organization. Berle's important contribution to the theory of power was to associate ineffective or dysfunctional organizations with the absence of power. When power is absent, Berle discovered, an organization experiences a form of chaos. Furthermore, this experience of chaos is more severe for organizations whose component units are highly interdependent; such an organization is more vulnerable even to minor disruptions caused by a power vacuum. When this happens, individuals at all levels of the organization experience a “compulsion” [7, p. 45] to fill the power vacuum. The immediate result of a power vacuum is to force power downward, to the next lower echelon of the organization. It is at these levels that individuals now begin to compete for power.

Berle's model of power helps bridge the gap between individual and interpersonal/organizational views of power. However, it, too, suffers from being an abstraction of actual social relations. What is missing from Berle's theory are the specific mechanisms by which individuals hold power, delegate it, or create chaos and organizational dysfunction by failing to use or delegate power. In the case study presented here, we explore the idea that the key element of power in organizations is the authority to hire, fire, promote, reassign, reward, and allocate valued resources. Individuals may hold these prerogatives themselves or delegate them such that the person to whom they have been delegated has unilateral authority over some, or all, of them over the individuals upon whom the subordinate power holder must depend. We will argue from our case data that when subordinates attempt to manage their units without a clear understanding of their powers in these areas, the organization experiences chaos and confusion.

Why individuals act as they do to hold power, delegate it, compete for it, or otherwise act upon their “political” thinking is a further crucial piece missing from Berle’s theory. Berle states merely that it is the secret operation of a person’s mind that ultimately will guide that individual in his or her actions. Our own answer to this question makes use of a general theory of human behavior developed by Chris Argyris and his colleagues known as action science $[1, 2, 3, 4, 5]$ .

From an action science perspective, individual action stems from privately held, often unconscious, theories-in-use that individuals naturally maintain to help explain the world they inhabit and over which they wish to have some control. Unfortunately, however, extensive research evidence shows that the common features of our theories-in-use result in behaviors that lead to conditions in which perceived threats or concerns cannot be discussed, self-fulfilling prophecies can easily occur, instances of escalating error and miscommunication increase, and self-scaling processes become prominent.

Such defensive routines lead to further communication problems: win–lose group dynamics, conformity, polarization between groups, and organizational games of deception. Organizations characterized by these processes are referred to as Model O-I (Model “Organization”-I). They are limited learning systems with win–lose dynamics, individuals and subgroups that learn to protect themselves first and their organizations second, groups that form alliances with other groups to enhance their own positions, and individuals and groups that withhold or distort information for their own interests [5, p. 93]. In Model O-I organizations the role of politics is threefold: (1) to enhance an individual’s status and power; (2) to make an individual’s goals the organization’s goals; and (3) to reduce an individual’s risks and vulnerabilities caused by a particular theory-in-use. Dysfunctional politics, in short, is the result of the uses of power and interpersonal maneuvering made possible by the learning limitations of Model O-I organizations.

We present here a case study of Bremerton, Inc. (BI), a financial services company (all company names are pseudonyms), and its IT project (nicknamed Threshold), adding a more general theory of human political behavior than has been seen in other political accounts of IT implementation. An earlier presentation of some of the Bremerton data $[23]$ showed how the substantive issues of an IT implementation effort were mismanaged in ways that undermined the rationality of decision making. In the presentation of the case data we also uncovered how the behavior of senior executives appeared to be defensive and political, further undermining rationality. This left us puzzled about the underlying reasoning and motivations behind this political behavior, and with a more general concern about the role of politics and power in IT implementation. We also found ourselves wondering what kinds of intervention strategies might help senior managers understand and better manage the politics of IT implementation. We concluded that this level of understanding required an additional level of organizational diagnosis.

This paper has three goals. First, we describe the organizational culture of Bremerton in broad terms, showing how it conforms to a classic Model O-I organization. Second, we examine how the theories-in-use of key BI executives created a power vacuum resulting in political havoc, wasted effort, and significant lack of productivity with regard to the implementation of a multimillion-dollar IT effort. More specifically, we show how the abrogation of power at the top of the organization forced the power in the organization, and the fight for it, to reproduce themselves at the next four lower levels of the organization. Finally, we hypothesize how principles of action science and power can be used for better understanding and managing the politics of IT design and implementation.

## Methods

THE STUDY OF BREMERTON TOOK PLACE over a three-month period. Primary data collection was through a series of interviews (sometimes more than one) with a total of seventy-seven individuals. These included the CEO, COO, the executive vice-presidents (EVP), most senior vice-presidents (SVP), foreign affiliates of BI, key vice-presidents (VP) involved with Threshold, and a large number of development staff (managers and technicians) responsible for IS and its integration with current Brem erton data-processing systems. All functional groups were represented in our interview survey. In addition, all management levels and functional groups involved with Threshold were represented in our interview sample.

We have described elsewhere the action science interview procedures and protocols we used $[23]$ . Briefly, our interviewing strategy was designed to distinguish between the theories or values that individuals publicly acknowledge and defend—their espoused theories—and the unconscious theories that can be inferred from what people actually say and do—their “theories-in-use.” These theories-in-use are motivated by their needs to protect their interests and to minimize risks associated with particular courses of action. They are known to have a greater impact on behavior than espoused theories $[2, 4, 5]$ .

Theories-in-use are harder to uncover because they are largely unconscious. Thus, action science interviewing must use a different set of procedures than those typical of traditional organizational research. Specifically, our interviews started with both general and specific questions about Bremerton's culture and the IT project in particular. We challenged the inferences and conclusions interviewees made. We then focused interviewees on the task of making explicit the inferential steps they used to progress from data to conclusion(s), and we actively challenged them with regard to both the data and the conclusion(s).

Often we asked interviewees to role play for us a conversation in which they had communicated their conclusions to a superior or colleague. Argyris [5] has demonstrated that the same theory-in-use that governs actual conversation governs role-play approximations, since neither is under the conscious control of the individual. The role-play technique helped the interviewees reconstruct their perceptions of the events, their cause-effect chains, attributions, and the like. We then pointed out discrepancies between what they said to us in the privacy of the confidential interview, and what they reported saying—during the role play—in the actual event. In almost all cases, interviewees were surprised by the discrepancies, evidence of their lack of awareness of the differences between their espoused theories and their theories-in-use.

Finally, we asked them to predict what would have happened if they would have publicly stated what they had said during the privacy of our interview. In almost all cases they predicted that they would have provoked an unacceptable response from others. This provided us with further evidence of their theories-in-use.

All of our interviews were conducted under conditions of explicit confidentiality. The interviews lasted between thirty and ninety minutes each, and examined basic questions relating to the employee's involvement with, understanding of, and reservations about Threshold.

Our analysis of the interview data consisted of three separate activities. First, we compared the substance of interviewees' remarks to identify the key issues confronting the Threshold implementation effort, the managers' honest disagreements about how to address these issues, and the conflicts to which the executives occasionally alluded when trying to explain why the disagreements occurred in the first place. Second, we used the role-play data to construct inferences about theories-in-use. This allowed us to see how the managers themselves undermined their espoused values, setting in motion covert political maneuvering. Finally, we used both analyses to make inferences about the genuine consequences of particular political decisions and moves for individual managers. Who stood to gain power, or lose it, and how?

## Bremerton and its Culture

BREMERTON INC. (BI) IS A WORLDWIDE INTEGRATED FINANCIAL SERVICES COMPANY employing over 1,000 people. It operates in a highly competitive market environment where market share depends upon name recognition and convenience and versatility of services and products. Bremerton and its rivals are attentive to consumer trends, each attempting to predict the directions that financial services will take into the next century. Bremerton and its competitors are also extremely sensitive to the cost structure and processing times of their transactions.

BI's main operations center is the hub of a sizable electronic communications and data-processing network. In fact, electronic communications capabilities form the core of its business. Within the last few years, BI's management team convinced the company's board of directors of the need for a \$50 million IT initiative that would replace its increasingly obsolete core applications systems and prepare BI for the new products and market services that would provide a competitive edge into the next century. Management also acknowledged its mission of redefining its business strategies. These strategies would drive the functional specifications for the project.

The resulting IT project (known as Threshold) was promoted as having far-reaching consequences for Bremerton's business. It would enhance the current rate of transaction processing, at significantly less cost, and support new products and services desired by customers. When interviewed, almost everyone agreed that Threshold was a business necessity.

However, well into the third year of planning for Threshold, many in Bremerton—from senior executives down through the organization to programmers—privately voiced major disagreements about what the nature of this contribution would be, and how the new system would be integrated with current company products and services. Developmental milestones were slipped or missed, and many individuals raised serious doubts about the ability of Bremerton to deliver a business plan that would both justify the expense and effort for Threshold and lead to actual design specifications for the system.

Bremerton is a traditional functional organization with Operations (Ops), Human Resources (HR), Marketing, Finance, Product Services, Customer Relations, Development, IT Systems, and Technical Services (see figure 1). Each of the major functional areas, however, has remained a semi-autonomous operation. Part of the reason for this is a frequent change of leadership at the top of the organization. Several CEOs left Bremerton over the preceding ten years. Between administrations, EVPs found themselves with a great deal of operational leeway, with few requirements for accountability. These, in turn, helped create a climate of distrust, suspicion, and secrecy among the functional groups.

In our interviews, for example, executives frequently made invidious comparisons between "them" and "us" and attributed mistakes or delays to others. Said one marketing SVP:

People feel there is something to lose when there is change, such as the disagreement over who owns the business. [The EVP-Ops] thinks he should decide how money gets spent. The regions think they should decide. Operations people do not think of themselves as members of a service organization. There are turf battles and everyone is afraid of losing their fiefdoms.

A manager from the Operations group commented on the secrecy within the groups and his views of the consequences of this for Threshold:

I feel sometimes there's a turf control situation. For Threshold it has to be told to everyone that it is for everyone's good; [let's] not worry about our own little worlds. I'd call it control of turf. Keeping confidentiality within the department when something goes wrong. One thing I hate is CYA memos. There are an awful lot of memos going back and forth.

Finally, one SVP in Marketing commented on the lack of accountability at the senior level:

There is a reluctance at BI to confront people with inadequate performance at the SVP level. A lot could be resolved if [the CEO and COO] sat down and identified a few managers who are not performing and [then] deal with them. There is a need to change the present tendency to maintain turf, reward on span of control, control data, and avoid accountability.

Aside from the autonomy of the functional organizations and the distrust and territoriality this appeared to reinforce, the Bremerton culture encouraged a “hands-off” approach to the management of lower levels. This resulted in a lack of clarity regarding who held power over the development of Threshold. It also prevented the effective surfacing of key issues and disagreements so that little or no public accountability was possible.

The shortcomings of the Bremerton culture had important political consequences for the implementation of Threshold, since basic issues of who would run the Threshold project, and for what purposes, went unresolved. Additional strategic and organizational issues, listed below, also were unresolved:

1. Defining the business plan to support and justify Threshold: As we have discussed at length elsewhere [23], there was controversy and likely honest disagreement about the purposes of Threshold from a business point of view. One executive audaciously asked, at a public meeting, whether Threshold was "a hog-caller or a one-speed bicycle." Others privately wondered whether Threshold would be a competitive advantage because it was primarily an operations innovation or, rather, systems support for new business packages. While many had clearly defined, but mutually contradictory, goals for Threshold, virtually all of those interviewed agreed that the proposed technical requirements for Threshold were not being sufficiently driven by the business requirements.

2. Determining the philosophy and general direction of the new IT system capabilities: Key executives privately debated whether transaction processing should be done centrally, or regionally. The decision, of course, had important economic and performance consequences. Issues of reliability, cost, and customer preferences created an unclear picture of how best to approach system architecture. In the absence of solid data, it was possible for each executive to support either alternative.

3. Ownership of new business products and services: Since the business direction for both Bremerton and Threshold were not yet defined, the array of new products and services was also indeterminable. Each executive had his or her own priority list for products and services that needed developing, though who should control or decide these was subject to much speculation and covert bickering. A primary disagreement emerged between marketing and operations over control and sign-off of new products.

4. Possible elimination of current business products, services, and processes: If

BI's business were to change, certain products, services, and transaction processes might need to change as well. Many might become obsolete. Who should decide which, if any, of these might be changed or discontinued, and who would control the ones that remained was a matter of some concern and speculation.

5. Day-to-day management and design of Threshold: The COO, EVP-Operations, and SVP-Development all had a stake in how, and who, would actually implement Threshold. Decisions had to be made on who would run Threshold meetings, who would attend, how the workload would be distributed, and who would control which aspects of the development effort. We found an intense level of political maneuvering and indecisiveness within the Bremerton executive group on these issues.

## The Politics of Threshold

THIS SECTION EXAMINES THE THEORIES-IN-USE and power/politics relationships of each key executive at Bremerton who was involved with the Threshold project.

## The CEO

The CEO created a committee—known as the steering committee—composed of a cross-section of senior management to plan the design and manage the implementation of Threshold.

Early in the life of the steering committee the CEO withdrew from participation. Before he did so, and at the request of an outside consultant, the CEO presented his vision of the future and the role of Threshold at a steering committee meeting. It was his first such public presentation in the two-year life of Threshold, but no time was left for discussion. When interviewed privately, the executives in attendance agreed with his statements. However, neither the CEO nor anyone else in attendance specified a mechanism to implement the CEO's vision or an accountability procedure to judge its completion.

One senior executive, while agreeing with the CEO's decision to remain aloof, commented on the downside of this for the organization of Threshold:

In working this kind of stuff, the CEO ought to stay away from a great deal of this—let it be worked out in an adversarial mode among the staff. This can never develop fully because the CEO is not an equal in the room. He should charge people to think these things through for him, set a direction and tone for the response he wants back. Then express disappointment if it is not done. There isn't this. People can not do things and it doesn't matter.

The SVP of HR, when asked to reflect on the CEO's actions, noted that the CEO was “pathologically afraid of conflict. The reason he withdrew from the steering committee was to remove himself from the conflict.” When asked what, if anything, he had tried to do about his, the SVP said, “Are you kidding? If I tried to do anything about the CEO’s behavior, I would be putting my relationship with him at risk.”

In order to divest himself of day-to-day responsibility for Threshold, the CEO brought in a new COO, someone known as a “no-nonsense” manager with an eye for detail. Others within Bremerton had hoped to be named COO and were disappointed by this appointment. The CEO privately voiced his intentions: “I hired [the COO] so I would not have to get involved in projects like Threshold.” He added that his COO and his EVP-Marketing were “mature professionals”: “if they cannot resolve disagreements among themselves, then I have the wrong people in those jobs.”

None of the individuals interviewed questioned the wisdom of the CEO's decision. However, the CEO's stance effectively bypassed questions of who was actually in control of Threshold and how genuine disagreements would be resolved. It also sealed off the CEO from any mediation or decision making since asking for his advice was tantamount to showing incompetence.

Problems with the official control of Threshold began almost immediately, Privately, the CEO reported his strategy:

My strategy on Threshold is to let [the EVP-Ops] come up with the operating specs and then make the steering committee even smaller. Once the [EVP-Ops] comes up with the operating specs, [the COO] will take over and run the implementation. [The COO] must understand everything. I want him to eat, sleep, and breathe the business and its finances.

He then confidentially expressed his reservations about the EVP-Ops.

The EVP-Ops cares too much about revenues. He has a basic desire to be a huge processor of transactions. We are not in that business.

In summary, the CEO avoided involvement in daily administration, delegated key tasks to a manager of his choosing, avoided disclosure of his vision, strategies, and designated chain of command, and harbored doubts about key members of his staff that were never publicly discussed or made discussible.

The CEO's stance had several important consequences for Threshold and the politics of its implementation. In effect, the CEO avoided the politics of the Threshold project while, at the same time, increasing both the uncertainty and the stakes for those who wanted the responsibility for it. This occurred because those left in charge of Threshold were all of a similar rank, were unaccountable to each other, and were able to hold onto their private development agendas since none had been ruled out.

## The COO

The COO's vision of his responsibility for Threshold paralleled that of the CEO:

[The EVP-Ops] reports to me. As soon as the spec is defined, I will run Threshold. All decisions will be mine.

The COO did report his misgivings about the direction that Threshold seemed to be taking and his own lack of control of the process:

A decision has been made regarding hardware and operating system. Implicit in the decision is a continuing commitment to telecommunication architecture already in place. I cannot detect whether or not this was discussed or debated. The issue of regional processing—it lends itself, begs for very strong analysis. The thing I am concerned about in the process of meetings [is that] we haven't really dealt with substantive issues. It is all well and good to get SVP opinions but that is no substitute for strong analysis. I am not sure that what we are doing is getting to the point where we have done enough analysis; and it is time to make a decision.

He also commented on the lack of structure and decision-making accountability at the senior level:

The problems of Threshold are symptomatic of broader organizational problems. Problems should not get solved at [the EVP-Development], SVP level. I am leery of cobbling together solutions that come from that level. These should be resolved at the level of [the CEO] and me. And the answers are not terribly clear.

As with the CEO, the COO failed to voice his serious misgivings about the lack of solid data with which to make informed decisions, the way in which decision making was occurring—and by whom—and the decisions already made. The COO also did not effectively communicate to the EVP-Ops his limitations on the latter's area of task responsibility. As a result of the COO's relative inaction, much of the Bremerton culture supporting semi-autonomous decision making and organizational fiefdoms was left intact. Political maneuvering around Threshold was driven to lower levels in the Bremerton organizational hierarchy.

## The EVP-Ops

The decision of who controlled Threshold voiced by the CEO and COO was not, however, shared by the EVP-Ops:

It is clear to me the [steering] committee structure is not effective. We need to stop giving false expectations. We need to ring it in again. I want to participate in Threshold. I will make all decisions.

The EVP-Ops saw a success with Threshold as a major step up on the corporate ladder:

My ambition is to move into [the COO's] job when he replaces [the CEO]. The best way to do that is make sure that Threshold succeeds and insure that Ops continues to play a central role at Bremerton.

The EVP for Operations ran the steering committee meetings, controlling both the agenda and the flow of information. Typically, thirty to fifty people (senior management and most members of the working groups) attended the key meetings. The meetings consisted of the EVP-Ops and his direct reports making one-way presentations with little room for discussion, questions, or disagreement. The EVP and his lieutenants discounted and downplayed disagreements that others tried to surface in the meetings by assuring them the disagreements were actually superficial. All hard copy distributed at meetings was labeled draft. When committee members asked for final copies, they were told these would be forthcoming. Meanwhile, the Operations group proceeded with development of the new applications for operations before agreement was reached within the committee.

As a result, major disagreements between the EVP-Ops and others, most notably the EVP-Marketing, were never publicly discussed, though they were actually quite well known by most of those in attendance. These issues included questions of who should control the development of the new applications systems, whether the electronic processing should be done centrally or regionally, and what business opportunities the new system should support and who should define these.

Our confidential interviews revealed the covert, but widely recognized, existence of tensions over business direction and control between the Operations and Marketing groups. For example, the EVP-Ops' commitment to the central position of operations put him in direct conflict with the vision of BI and Threshold articulated by the CEO. It also put him in conflict with the EVP-Marketing, who subscribed to the idea of regional processing. Centrally run processing would be controlled by the EVP-Ops, with his organization receiving payment for each transaction. The different regions, on the other hand, were controlled by the EVP-Marketing and thus regional processing would strengthen his business owners. One Marketing SVP, vocally loyal to his Marketing boss, summarized the situation:

Ops gets its power from the revenues generated by the central system. If customers bypass the central system, Ops revenues and influence decline in this company. They will fight to the death to avoid that.

The same executive added:

It's crazy that profit centers are run out of Ops. They are techies, without business or marketing savvy. If we are to retain market share, marketing and business should run the profit centers, and Ops should treat them like their customers.

After extensive interviews with all senior management on the steering committee, we discovered that no one knew how these differing points of view could be resolved, or, more importantly, who had power to resolve them. Our private conversations also revealed signs of strain within Threshold project team members because of the perceived lack of direction or ownership of the project by senior management.

One senior consultant pointed the finger at the EVP-Ops:

He tries to listen and wants to make some things happen. I can see he's frustrated since he wants to take things forward. He often just jumps in. He doesn't really get at solution strategies. Rather, he says we'll talk, get together, schedule another meeting.

An SVP and a VP, respectively, referred to the hands-off style of the EVP-Ops and to the general lack of direction from the top of the organization:

There are other managers down there more interested in control, old ways. [The EVP-Ops] has to get better in touch with some of managers who work for him, and what they are doing. He needs better control of products under him.

The entire senior management team, including [the CEO], has not publicly condoned and promoted this project [Threshold] as it should. For example, [the CEO] should be talking about Threshold every time he talks outside Bremerton. He should be forcing his direct reports to do that, also. Some may not be entirely behind the project. [The CEO] hasn't told them they have to be, and also they have not been sold on what Threshold will do, nor are they part of any strategic direction for Threshold, nor has

any direction been articulated to them.

Another senior consultant pointed to the lack of accountability among senior managers for the Threshold project:

I really believe that top management should have a group of itself (three to four people) who are the guys that really watch Threshold. These are the guys really watching Threshold, and accountable to watch it. No, I don't have candidates in mind. They would need to do it, be right, scream, come from the senior management team (i.e., those who report to the CEO).

Other steering committee members commented on the large size of the meetings and the difficulty of doing focused work and on the lack of real work between meetings.

The work of the steering committee was hampered because of the lack of clearly drawn and empowered leadership and because key decisions that needed to be made were unavoidably political in nature. Since senior executives were jockeying behind the scenes to define the business direction or to affect the decision regarding the centralization of processing, the public work of the committee was effectively stymied.

## The EVP-Marketing

The EVP-Marketing had been hand-picked by the current CEO and came to Bremerton with him. He therefore enjoyed access at the top of the company not held by the EVP-Ops. The EVP-Marketing responded to the EVP-Ops' style and strategy by complaining, privately and confidentially, to the CEO. His direct reports, in turn, privately advocated that marketing have sign-off rights on all requirements before any code was written by the Operations group for the new system. They also began to question the need for a new system, privately arguing that it would be difficult to make a good business argument for investing in a new system. They argued that their customers had not been asking for the new services, which a new system promised, and would not likely pay for these when offered.

## The SVP-Development

Both the SVP of Development and his boss, the EVP of Operations, thought he should control maintenance and updates of the current platforms and systems. The SVP-Development fought with each of the two SVPs of product lines over control of upgrades of existing products as well as the development of new products.

Both the SVP and the EVP instructed their direct reports to maintain control of development requirements and schedules. For every project, a group representing each of the involved executives would design their own requirements, creating costly duplication of effort. The product group members would talk directly with customers, keeping the development people out of the loop. Meanwhile, the development people would repeatedly ask to be included in the discussions, or at least to be kept up-to-date on emerging decisions regarding system requirements. But, the product personnel typically kept the development staff isolated from negotiations with customers until they had obtained final agreements on both requirements and delivery dates. These would then be presented to the development group as a fait accompli. Said a development group manager:

We keep asking to be part of the negotiating with the customer and they keep saying, yeah, we will do it that way the next time. But the next time, it is always the same old story. And then we have to work all this overtime to come close to meeting deadlines, but that always pushes us over budget. And development becomes the bad [guy] for going over budget.

One clear objective of the SVP-Development was to be, in his words, “the top strategy person at Bremerton. I want to report directly to [the CEO].” Because the Threshold project was widely, but privately, seen as off-track and hopelessly delayed, the SVP reasoned that his leadership in this area might accrue greater visibility and prestige for him.

A major obstacle for the SVP-Development for reaching his objective was his VP for IT Systems. The VP had been recruited and hired into Bremerton by the EVP-Ops, and placed into the SVP-Development's organization. The VP fought with the other VPs in the Development group for control of the requirements, resources, and processes to be used in developing the new system for Threshold. The SVP-Development refrained from participating in these discussions, leaving disagreements unresolved. The VP for IT relied, in part, on the resources of the other VPs to do the work on the new applications programs, and he kept his discussions with the others at a high level of abstraction, encouraging ambiguity. He reasoned that he was the only one within the group, including the SVP himself, who knew how to design large systems since he was the only one who had done so previously.

After careful interviewing with the SVP-Development, we constructed, with him, his theory-in-use. It had the following seven elements:

1. The VP wants my job.

2. He is bypassing me and privately establishing his own personal relationships with the CEO and key members of the board.

3. He is enhancing his support in the corporation at my expense.

4. He is hated by the other Development VPs, directors, and managers; therefore, he cannot possibly lead the development of the new applications programs successfully.

5. If this development effort fails, I will be fired.

6. If I don't fire him first, he will have me fired.

7. I must be careful; the EVP-Operations will get frightened. "Blowing the VP away is not a small thing."

Based on this line of reasoning, the SVP asked the EVP-Ops for permission to fire the VP. The latter refused and instructed the SVP to learn to work with his VP. First, he reassigned the VP's most important tasks and responsibilities to himself. He cut the VP's travel budget by 80 percent. He replaced the VP as chair of the most important meetings and even barred the VP from them. He removed a layer of management within the Development organizations. He then initiated and maintained informal communication both vertically and horizontally throughout the company, trying to build support for himself against the VP. Part of his reasoning for doing this was also to use this informal network to link him with individuals who might have information regarding any alliances the VP might have with the CEO, EVP-Ops, and the board of directors, and to find individuals with influence who might side with him against the VP in a showdown.

Six months after taking these actions, the SVP of Development concluded that the VP did not have a special alliance with anyone of senior rank. He then doctored a consultant's survey of the Development group, removing all negative references to himself while highlighting negative references to the VP. He gave copies of the doctored survey report to the EVP-Ops and the CEO and again asked for permission to fire the VP.

One month after receiving the survey from the SVP, the CEO ordered the EVP-Ops to fire the VP. Two hours before the EVP-Ops was scheduled to meet with the VP to fire him, the SVP of Development was notified of this action by the SVP of Human Relations. Two months later, the CEO ordered the EVP-Ops to fire the SVP-Development, which he did. Control over the development of Threshold remained in disarray.

Conclusions: Managing Power and Politics in IT

BREMERTON EXECUTIVES MAY HAVE HAD HONEST DISAGREEMENTS about what businesses their company should be in, how best to structure and take advantage of new technology, who was best qualified to lead the implementation effort, and so forth. However, these discussions were sufficiently data-free that no rational discussion or judgments about the relative merits of conflicting points of view were possible.

Further confusion stemmed from the fact that at least five managers at each of four levels below the CEO believed that he should control one or more of the following: strategy, development projects, development processes, resources. Unfortunately, most disagreements, and the consequent political maneuverings, remained undiscussed and undiscussible, though both the disagreements and the maneuverings were widely known throughout the company. However, even with regard to issues that did surface, few mechanisms existed to resolve them in a timely and productive manner. We found that at each level of management—Executive, Marketing, Operations, Development—the highest-ranking individual frequently withdrew from even discussing, much less resolving, key conflicts among his subordinates.

Thus, Bremerton represents a classic case of a Model O-I organization as described by Argyris [5]. The privately held values and goals of Bremerton executives were more salient and powerful to them than the publicly espoused goals of the organization and, to a more significant degree than the public goals, determined their consequent behaviors. The theories-in-use for these executives constituted a kind of “road map” of their own obstacles and risks. Because they were private, they could not be surfaced and managed in productive, and accountable, ways. Rather, the individuals’ unconscious management of these risks became unproductively political.

Beyond this, however, our analysis of Bremerton suggests that a political crisis occurred because the CEO effectively abandoned his power by withdrawing from any leadership role in the Threshold project. Furthermore, he failed to delegate his power. No one was assigned unilateral authority to hire, fire, promote, reassign, reward, or allocate valued resources to their subordinates. Particularly telling, the COO had not been granted unilateral authority over the EVPs. In addition, the CEO held unilateral authority over both the SVP of Development and the VP of the IT project, rather than their respective line supervisors, the EVP of Operations and the SVP of Development. As predicted by Berle's model of power, Bremerton found itself with a lack of clarity about who held power and, therefore, in a state of virtual chaos. The resulting power vacuum brought confusion, but also extreme competition, in the next-lower levels in the organization. The COO, EVPs, and SVPs vied with one another to fill the vacuum. Since no one emerged, or was designated, as a key power holder, the substantive issues that divided the development effort for Threshold continued to plague it.

This paper argues that political considerations must be a part of any organizational IT diagnosis. If the politics and power relationships are not diagnosed, we will have overlooked a key factor in managing the human threat to IT implementation. Furthermore, the substantive issues cannot be managed effectively.

We conclude with several hypotheses that are suggested from our case study of Bremerton, an organization that is admittedly a relatively extreme example of political dysfunction. First, we hypothesize that the appearance of negative political behavior will be coupled with the absence of exercised power within the organization. The absence of power, in turn, we believe will be caused in one, or more, of three ways: (1) withdrawal by the primary power holder from the field of action; (2) insufficient delegation of unilateral authority to subordinates to hire, fire, reward, promote, reassign, and/or allocate necessary resources; and (3) withdrawal of subordinate power holders even though they have been delegated sufficient unilateral authority.

We further hypothesize that the absence of exercised power will result not merely in negative political behavior, but also in the absence of any real learning or decision making that will produce meaningful agreement on all of the substantive strategic issues confronting managers of the IT project. Without this agreement, the actions of individuals will continue to support their private goals and values while simultaneously, and often unconsciously, undermining the publicly espoused goals and values.

Finally, we argue that productive, effective organizations require the presence of a power holder willing to exercise his or her power or to delegate sufficient unilateral authority to subordinates. Primary power holders must be encouraged to believe that power is not “America’s last dirty word” [16, p. 65]. Rather, they must be helped to understand what power they have, and how to use it. However, they must also be helped to use their power (1) with justice and against a background of a well-articulated value system, and (2) in ways that neither model, replicate, nor encourage the consequences of unproductive politics that we saw at Bremerton and that are characteristic of Model O-I organizations: distrust, win-loss dynamics, and the perception of vulnerability with covering, defensive postures.
