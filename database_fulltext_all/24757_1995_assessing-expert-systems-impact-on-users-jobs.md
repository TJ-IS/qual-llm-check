# Assessing Expert Systems Impact on Users' Jobs

YOUNGOHC YOON AND TOR GUIMARAES

YOUNGOHC YOON is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Computer Information Systems at Southwest Missouri State University. She received her M.S. from the University of Pittsburgh and her Ph.D. from the University of Texas at Arlington. She has recently published articles in the Journal of Neural Network Computing, Data Bases, Information and Management, Journal of Operation Research Society, and Expert Systems. Dr. Yoon is a member of the Decision Sciences Institute, International Neural Network Society, American Association for Artificial Intelligence, ACM, SIGBDP, and IEEE Computer Society.

TOR GUIMARAES holds the J.E. Owen Chair of Excellence in Information Systems at Tennessee Technological University. He has a Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Minnesota. He was a Professor and Chairman of the MIS Department at St. Cloud State University and earlier was an Assistant Professor at Case-Western Reserve University. He has spoken at numerous professional meetings and consulted with many leading organizations including TRW, American Greetings, AT&T, IBM and the D.O.D. He has published many articles in Information Systems Research, Communications of the ACM, MIS Quarterly, Decision Sciences, OMEGA, Computers and Operations Research, Information and Management, and Database.

ABSTRACT: A comprehensive list of ten major expert systems (ES) related factors likely to affect users' jobs has been defined, including problem importance, problem difficulty, developer skill, domain expert quality, user characteristics, user satisfaction, shell quality, user involvement, management support, and system usage. Impact on the job has been defined in terms of eleven items dealing with changes in job importance, amount of work, accuracy requirements, skills needed, job appeal, feedback about performance, freedom in how to do the job, opportunity for advancement, job security, relation with peers, and job satisfaction. Data were collected on sixty-nine expert systems developed through IBM's Corporate Manufacturing Expert Systems Project Center in San Jose, California. The results show that the major variables having the most impact on users' jobs are problem importance, problem difficulty, domain expert quality, user satisfaction with the ES, shell quality, and user involvement in ES development. Based on the results, recommendations are made for corporate and ES development managers to increase the likelihood that ES will have a desirable impact on users' jobs.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: expert systems, job impact of technology, success factors.

many organizations have harnessed the power of computer technologies to improve their strategic positions as well as the effectiveness and efficiency of their daily operations. At the individual level, computer technology has also become an essential instrument for users to accomplish their daily tasks and to make decisions. With the many success stories, there has been a tremendous growth of various computer-based systems to meet the demand of industries for computer techniques to handle business problems effectively. Among a variety of computer-based information systems that emerged during the last decade, expert systems (ES) have become increasingly important to individual users as well as to entire organizations.

An expert system is a computer program employing expert knowledge to improve decision-making performance in solving the problems of a specific domain area. By providing novices with expertise in the field, ES have changed the ways in which users perform their tasks and, in turn, have had a significant impact on users' jobs and organizations. Many organizations that have successfully implemented ES applications have reported major benefits from ES in gaining competitive advantages $[11]$ . However, because of the potentially significant impact imposed by ES on users' jobs, the fear of job loss has been frequently observed when ES techniques are introduced into the workplace $[38]$ . Such user fears can cause resistance to ES implementation and make it nearly impossible to obtain the advantages of ES. In order to derive the expected ES benefits, it is crucial for management to gain a better understanding of the impact of ES on users' jobs. Despite the importance of the topic, very little empirical research has been reported. Of the few studies, there is one by Byrd $[10]$ that reported on two issues surrounding ES job impact: fear of job loss and fear of control. Sviokla $[55]$ described the significant impact of XCON on the business task it was designed to support, and on the roles and responsibilities of its users. These changes were credited with estimated savings of 15 million for Digital Equipment Corporation. However, the study did not include an analysis of the factors related to the impact of XCON on users' jobs.

The vast majority of the studies assessing computer-based system success factors deal with transaction processing systems (TPS), decision support systems (DSS), executive information systems (EIS), or other computer-based information systems besides ES. The term ES in this case refers to systems that comprise at least a knowledge base, an inference engine, an explanation module, and a user interface that are used to mimic expert decision making $[30]$ . While, obviously, there are many similarities, ES are quite different from other systems in many ways. For example, the basis for ES is the capture and use of knowledge from high-level experts to assist less proficient ES end users. Other systems deal with the flow of data and information through the organization unit, and their development requires considerable end-user input to define the nature, functions, and features of the system. Therefore, for TPS, EIS, and DSS the end users are the “domain experts.”

Expert systems also differ dramatically from other types of systems in other ways. TPS perform routine data processing and focus on business procedures. DSS support decisions and focus on computational and statistical models. On the other hand, while ES also make or support decisions, they focus on knowledge/expertise rather than on transaction processing or computational techniques [20]. ES are heuristics-based and are particularly viable for problems that cannot be solved algorithmically [66]. Even a summary discussion of the many differences between ES and other systems would require several pages; thus, only the main references are mentioned. Jih [30] found differences between ES and TPS in seven areas: basic mission, knowledge structure definition, requirements for input certainty and completeness, system shareability, system human interfaces, data abstraction, and validation. Turban and Watkins [58] discussed the differences between DSS and ES along eleven attributes: objectives, who makes the decisions, major orientations, major query direction, nature of support, data manipulation method, characteristics of problem areas, repetitiveness of problems addressed, content of databases, reasoning capability, and explanation capability. Subsequently, Turban [57] addressed the differences among TPS, MIS, DSS, EIS, and ES in terms of application types, support focus, nature of the databases, decision-making/support capabilities, numeric versus symbolic manipulation, types of information produced, highest organizational level served, and primary impetus for the system.

There are four major reasons why we believe this study is important: First, despite its significance in practice, there is very little information on the impact of ES on users' jobs (the dependent variable). Second, given the dramatically different nature of ES, results obtained from previous studies are likely to provide at best a partial picture of the major issues surrounding ES development and implementation in practice. Managers responsible for the development and implementation of ES cannot assume that the set of determinants for success with other system types are equally important for ES. Even if one believes that to be so, this assumption must be tested empirically. Third, many important factors are unique to ES, such as their "expert mimicking" nature, the domain-oriented problems addressed, the quality of ES shells, the required activities and quality of domain experts and knowledge engineers, and their unique relationship with end users. And fourth, despite the widespread use and increasing importance of ES technology, little effort has been made to systematically identify and empirically test the determinants of ES success. Thus, we are left mostly with personal opinion and limited case studies. Most reports about ES [6, 27, 33, 44, 47, 53] are based on the opinion and personal experience of individuals and have not been empirically tested. Empirical studies conducted by Byrd [10] and Tyran and George [59] report on general issues and factors surrounding ES implementation, but much more research is needed to synthesize previous findings, to formulate and empirically test hypotheses regarding the likely determinants of ES success, and to build a theoretical foundation in this important area. The purpose of this study is to empirically test the various factors proposed in the literature as important to ES implementation success, which in this case is defined as the desirable impact of ES on users' jobs.

## Theoretical Framework and Measurement

BECAUSE OF THE DEARTH OF KNOWLEDGE ON THE DETERMINANTS of ES impact on users' jobs, a broad literature search was undertaken to develop a comprehensive list of potentially relevant factors. Prior studies have identified many factors related to ES successful implementation. Barsanti [6] reported five ES critical success factors: top-down corporate support, domain expert selection, support group, tool selection, and project selection. Four major variables encompassing factors regarding the organization, the implementation process, technical aspects, and ES ease of use were included in an experimental study of ES implementation success conducted by Tyran and George [59]. In another study by Byrd [10], the factor analysis revealed three factors related to ES success: knowledge engineering, job impact, and knowledge acquisition. These studies provided numerous clues as to factors that may potentially affect the impact of ES on users' jobs.

Ten factors thought to influence ES job impact have been identified from the literature: problem importance, problem difficulty, developer skill, domain expert quality, user characteristics, user satisfaction, shell quality, user involvement, management support, and system usage. But before we discuss each of these constructs (independent variables) in more detail, let us look at the measure of ES impact on users' jobs (dependent variable).

## ES Impact on Users' Jobs

Expert systems are thought to have significant impact on users' jobs by providing problem-solving expertise on specific problem areas (domains), thus improving user productivity. An expert system automates routine decision-making tasks and sets users free to do more creative work [10]. The ES impact on users' jobs has been recognized as an important indicator of its success. Several measures have been used to assess system impact on jobs. Byrd [10] assessed impact based on fear of loss of control and fear of loss of jobs. Sviokla [55] measured the impact of XCON on users' jobs by examining the changes on input and output, the increase in the task accuracy and the amount of work completed, the shifts in the user role and responsibilities, and job satisfaction. Most studies have used one or two items to measure system impact on users' jobs. In this study, we employed eleven variables to measure the impact of an ES on users' jobs; these include changes in the importance of the user's job, amount of work required on job, accuracy demanded on the job, skills needed to do the job, job appeal, feedback on job performance, freedom in how to do the job, opportunity for advancement, job security, relationship with fellow employees, and job satisfaction.

Respondents were asked to indicate their agreement or disagreement with statements like “on the average, the ES has increased the job’s appeal,” using a seven-point rating scale from (7) completely agree to (1) completely disagree. The average score for the eleven items is used to measure ES impact on users’ jobs. The internal consistency reliability coefficient (Cronbach’s alpha) for this scale was 0.89. The same seven-point scale (standard scale) has also been used for rating all the independent variables discussed next.

## Problem Characteristics: Importance and Difficulty

The relationships between problem characteristics and system success have been studied by several authors $[14, 15, 25, 49]$ . Selecting the appropriate problem for ES is absolutely critical to its success [12]. Because ES requirements are primarily defined by domain experts rather than users (as for other system types), the project selection process is quite different [38]. For ES development, several authors have stated that selection of an improper domain is likely to result in ES implementation failure [6, 7, 33, 37, 51, 64]. Prerau [47] stressed that the selection of an appropriate ES domain is more closely related to the ultimate success of the project than any other factor. Slagle and Wick [51] stress that an ES should perform a needed and useful task (nontrivial and important to the organization) so that the ES solution has a high payoff. The successful ES that were widely reported all address important problems for the organization involved [6]. For example, Authorizer's Assistant at American Express performs a function critical to the firm by assisting the credit approvers in approving or denying credit in a timely manner. XCON at DEC configures computer components according to customer request. Other authors have emphasized that special attention must be paid to the selection of ES applications and proposed special application, evaluation, and selection methods [38, 51]. Therefore, we propose:

H1: Problem importance is directly related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

A single item is used to measure problem importance. Respondents were asked to rate the importance of the task being supported/performed by the ES using the standard scale.

Because of ES's special nature, problem difficulty is also of special importance. Compared to other types of systems that usually require users to define the functions and features of the system for system developers, in the case of ES, the more advanced knowledge of domain experts is used to assist the system users. Thus, for ES, increased problem difficulty is likely to provide a better opportunity for the system to be of greater service to its users. For these reasons, we propose:

H2: Problem difficulty is directly related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

Problem difficulty has been defined by Van de Ven and Ferry [60] as the degree to which an individual problem solver can follow a formal and well-defined search procedure to solve a problem. Many variables have been employed to assess problem difficulty in previous studies. In order to measure problem difficulty, the following eight variables were operationalized for this study on the basis of Beckman [7], Guimaraes et al. [25], and Waterman [64]: problem size, complexity, interdependence among variables, the level of expertise required, uncertainty, instability, labor intensity, and problem unstructuredness.

Problem size refers to the number of variables and the depth/width of the problem addressed by the ES. Problem complexity refers to the number and variety of ways in which the output variables in the problem relate to input variables, and how well these relationships are understood by ES developers and users. Interdependence among variables is defined as the degree to which input variables are related with each other. The level of expertise required refers to the level of education and experience domain experts are required to have to effectively solve problems in a specific domain. Data uncertainty is defined as the degree to which input values are unknown. Problem instability refers to the frequency with which domain knowledge changes over time. The degree of labor intensity deals with the amount of people's time and effort necessary to solve the problem. Degree of problem structuredness refers to the extent to which the problem is programmable.

For each of these items, respondents were asked to indicate their degree of agreement or disagreement with such statements as the ES deals with extremely unstable knowledge, and the ES task is extremely unstructured. The internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha) of the eight-item scale was 0.69, only slightly below the 0.70 considered acceptable by most researchers. The average response for the eight items is the measure of problem difficulty.

## Developer Skill

The importance of skillful ES developers has been emphasized by several authors $[46, 50]$ . Compared with developers for other system types, ES developers must deal with special tasks, tools, and methods for ES development that may affect the composition and relative importance of the required skill set. For example, the construction of an ES requires developers to elicit the decision rules employed by domain experts. In order to elicit the decision rules, developers must ask relevant questions and quickly comprehend the decision procedures reasoned by domain experts in problem solving. The knowledge elicitation procedure, which requires a lengthy process of interviews with high-level domain experts, is widely known to be a bottleneck in ES development. It is desirable for a developer to possess special communications skills in order to alleviate the difficult knowledge elicitation process. A developer without the ability to communicate with high-level experts will not be able to properly perform the critical knowledge acquisition task, thus causing project implementation failure $[47]$ . Without skillful ES developers, the final product is less likely to positively affect users' jobs, as defined in this study. Therefore, we propose:

## H3: Developer skill is directly related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

System developers should possess a range of skills and abilities including technical and behavioral skills $[2, 8]$ . Cheney and Lyons $[13]$ presented twenty-five different behavioral and technical skills for information systems professionals. A comprehensive study of all the skills necessary for systems analysts was reported by Guimaraes $[24]$ . Another study reported on the skills necessary for developers $[63]$ . The skills and abilities required by ES developers may be similar to those of system analysts since they perform similar duties, but there are some unique skills required of ES developers. For ES development, Debenham $[16]$ listed four essential skills of knowledge engineers—the ability to extract accurate and complete knowledge from human experts, to represent and implement knowledge, to design an ES for maintenance, and to design an ES that exploits existing investments in information processing. A comprehensive list of knowledge and skills necessary for ES developers developed by Payne and Awad [46] includes knowledge of computer technology, knowledge of general fact-finding techniques, knowledge of prototype methods, knowledge of human factors, knowledge of functional areas, communications skills, project planning skills, human relations skills, organizational skills, and personal attributes. Although the range of developers' skills and abilities varies slightly between studies, they can be classified into six broad categories, according to Nunamaker et al. [43]: people, modeling, systems, computers, organizations, and society. People skills refer to communications and interpersonal skills. Modeling skills are defined as the ability to formulate and solve models of the operation research type. Systems skills refers to the ability to view and define a situation as a system—specifying components, scopes, and functions. Computer skills refer to knowledge of hardware/software, programming languages, and ES techniques. Organizational skills are defined as knowledge of the functional areas of an organization and organizational conditions. Last, society skills generally refer to one's ability to articulate and defend a personal position on important issues regarding the impact of information technology on society, or more specifically, to one's ability to perceive and describe the impact of an ES on a particular part of society.

Respondents were asked to rate statements regarding developers' skills in each of these six categories via statements such as “developers of this ES have shown excellent people skills” and “developers of this ES have shown excellent modeling skills.” The average response to the six items represents the measure of developers' skills. The internal consistency reliabilities (Cronbach's alpha) of the six-item scale was 0.68.

## Domain Expert Quality

A unique feature of the ES development process is the involvement of domain experts whose problem-solving expertise in a specific domain is the essence of an expert system $[64]$ . While the benefits of ES development by users have been proposed by some authors $[65]$ to tap user knowledge and experience, and some ES development techniques are less dependent on domain experts $[67]$ , most ES today are heavily dependent on domain experts for their development. That is certainly the case with the knowledge-based ES in this study. The importance of domain experts to ES success cannot be overemphasized since it is often the only source of domain knowledge. Thus, one should expect that the quality of the domain experts' implementation is critical for successful ES implementation $[27]$ . Barsanti $[6]$ stressed that when developing an ES, the issues regarding domain experts must be carefully considered. Domain experts must be articulate and willing to participate in the project $[47]$ . Without skillful domain experts, the final product is less likely to affect users' jobs positively, as defined in this study. Therefore, we propose:

H4: Domain expert quality is directly related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

Prior studies have addressed various domain experts characteristics thought to influence the likelihood of ES implementation success [27, 47, 51]. The important skills and abilities are a high-level expertise, capability of articulating the reasoning process and communicating with knowledge engineers, and willingness to cooperate in ES development. Based on Prerau [47], domain expert quality was measured by education level, work experience, communications skills, cooperation, availability, and computers/AI background. Respondents were asked to indicate their agreement or disagreement with statements such as “the domain experts were extremely cooperative” and “the domain experts have shown excellent communications skills.” The internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s alpha) for the six-item scale was extremely low (alpha = 0.23). Deletion of education level and work experience from this scale raised the alpha coefficient substantially to 0.63. There were no significant relationships between these two variables and ES impact on users’ jobs at the 0.05 significance level. The average response to the four items represents the measure of domain expert quality used in this study.

## User Characteristics

Several user characteristics affecting MIS impact on users' jobs have been identified: user attitude, user expectation, user education, user training, and user resistance [22, 68]. In the ES literature, similar user characteristics are thought to be relevant to ES success, including user attitude, user expectation, and user knowledge of computer and ES technology [53]. However, the users' role in ES development tends to be quite different, thus raising the likelihood that different user characteristics are more or less important. For example, users are not expected to know system process requirements as well as domain experts. There are other major differences. Users often have more intense fears about the ES affecting their job security; thus, they may develop more strong negative attitudes and may challenge the system implementation [38]. The problem of negative user attitude and resistance is more apparent in an ES field since an ES may significantly change the nature and requirements of a job and replace human tasks with artificial systems, such as the effect of XCON [55]. Another user characteristic mentioned in previous studies is the user knowledge of computer and AI techniques. Users also are expected to know how to interpret ES results and to incorporate them into their job performance [47]. Without skillful users, the final product is less likely to affect users' jobs positively, as defined in this study. Therefore, we propose:

H5: User characteristics are directly related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

Using the same seven-point scale as above, respondents were asked to indicate their agreement or disagreement with statements such as: users have positive attitudes toward the ES, users have high expectations about the ES, and users have computer and AI knowledge. The last item reflects user training and experience with ES technology. Cronbach's alpha for this scale was 0.76. The average response to these three items is the measure for user characteristics.

## User Satisfaction

User satisfaction has been widely recognized as an important indicator of system success $[23, 29, 32, 41, 42, 48]$ . DeLone and McLean $[18]$ also presented user satisfaction as an important factor affecting DSS impact on users' jobs. However, there is very little empirical evidence available, which strongly supports the decision to include this variable in the study. The rationale is that if users are dissatisfied with their ES, they are less likely to use it. That, in turn, will make the ES less likely to affect users' jobs positively. Therefore, we propose:

H6: User satisfaction is directly related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

Raymond [48] adapted the instrument developed by Bailey and Pearson [3] and constructed a twenty-item questionnaire. Based on the results of factor analysis, the items in the instrument were grouped into four factors: output quality, user-system relationship, support factor, and EDP staff. Because some of these variables are already represented in this study, instead of measuring user satisfaction with all four areas, we concentrated on user satisfaction with ES friendliness and the quality of the ES output. This approach is similar to the one used by Lucas [40] to assess the quality of a model in terms of its output, problem solution, and systems interfaces with users. The measure of user satisfaction in this case is a nine-item instrument adapted from the questionnaire used by Raymond [48]. The nine items include output value, timeliness, reliability, response/turnaround time, accuracy, completeness, ease to use, ease to learn, and ES documentation usefulness. Respondents were asked to indicate agreement or disagreement with a statement regarding their satisfaction with each item. For each subject, the average response to the nine items is the measure of user satisfaction. The Cronbach's alpha of the nine-item scale was 0.87.

## Shell Quality

There are two major alternatives in developing an ES: one is to develop a system from scratch using a programming language; the other is to construct a system with an ES shell. Using a programming language, an ES developer will have to build a knowledge base as well as an explanation facility. On the other hand, a full-blown ES shell provides a knowledge-base editor, a debugging aid, an explanation facility, and other useful supporting facilities. Thus, employing an ES shell helps a developer save a substantial amount of time and effort in building an ES $[38]$ . The commercial availability of ES shells with highly sophisticated features has significantly raised shell usage. The shell used to develop an ES can be an important determinant of its quality $[6]$ . Therefore, employing an ES shell appropriate to the ES tasks is vitally important to how well the ES will perform. The desirable features of an ES shell will vary significantly depending on the tasks to be performed by the ES. The ES reasoning process is required to match that of human experts. If an ES cannot be constructed to reason in the same fashion as human experts due to shell inflexibility in controlling methods, the system will not behave “naturally” to users, and this will discourage ES use. For many applications, shells must enable the ES to be easily integrated with other computer-based systems. However, many ES are capable of only limited interface with existing systems due to the ES shell used. This impediment forces the ES to acquire data through an interactive process with users even though the raw data may already be stored in existing databases. Needless to say, entering data in an interactive manner is tedious and inconvenient, which discourages users from utilizing the ES, and thereby decreases its usefulness. On the other hand, a shell that provides a friendly user interface and other desirable characteristics is likely to encourage ES use and create a more positive impact on the users' jobs. Therefore, we propose:

## H7: Shell quality is directly related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

The long list of a shell's desirable features found in the literature includes flexibility of knowledge representation, flexibility of the inference engine, ability to monitor session activities, knowledge-base editing facility, knowledge acquisition facility, debugging aids, uncertainty management, user interface, explanation facility, integration with external databases and other systems, integration with other programming languages, ease to use, ease to learn, portability, response time, real-time support, documentation, and vendor support [9, 26, 34, 61, 62]. Obviously, some features are applicable only to specific expert systems; for example, the capability of real-time support is applied exclusively to a real-time ES application.

Based on Harmon et al. [26], Kim and Yoon [34], and Waterman [64], the general features deemed to be applicable to a wide range of ES applications were selected and categorized into ten variables in this study: flexibility for knowledge representation and inference engine, developer interface, user interface, system interface, portability among different platforms, ease to use, ease to learn, training and vendor support, response time, and shell's appropriateness to the problem. The flexibility of knowledge representations and inference engine refers to the variety of knowledge representation methods available for building a knowledge base (such as rules, frames, and hybrid methods) and of reasoning methods used for inferring new knowledge (such as forward and backward chaining). The developer interface refers to the supporting tools for knowledge engineers, including knowledge-base editors, debugging aids, rule induction, and so forth. The user interface includes input/output facilities and explanation mechanisms. The system interfaces provide connections with other systems such as text editors, databases, or programming languages. The portability from/to different platforms refers to the ease of transferring an expert system from one hardware to another. The ease to use and ease to learn items refer to the user friendliness of a shell. Vendor training and support describes the availability of self-paced tutorial programs and training sections, and the extent of vendors' assistance. Response time refers to the reasoning speed of the inference engine. Finally, a shell's appropriateness to the problem refers to the overall fit of a shell to the ES task at hand. Respondents were asked to indicate their agreement or disagreement with separate statements regarding the extent to which the shell used to develop their ES provided each of these ten features. The average score for these items was computed to measure ES shell quality. The internal consistency coefficient for the scale was 0.94.

## User Involvement

User involvement has long been considered an important variable for system implementation success $[4, 17, 21, 22, 28, 39, 56]$ . Based on a literature review, Ives and Olson $[28]$ concluded that user involvement is appropriate for unstructured problems or when user acceptance is important. In the case of ES development, user involvement is less understood and its importance more controversial since domain experts are often the only source of knowledge and inference about the problem. Nevertheless, in general, high levels of user involvement are considered important to implementation success $[53]$ . Keyes $[33]$ argued that if users were not involved, they would not use the ES. Turban $[57]$ claimed that user involvement becomes more important in the ES verification and validation phases. In general, for ES development, user requirements are unknown at the early stages. Instead, they evolve during an iterative development process in which a working model is used as a vehicle to help communications between users and developers/domain experts. Therefore, a high level of user involvement may indeed be necessary to enhance ES impact on users' jobs. For these reasons, we propose:

## H8: User involvement is directly related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

In this study, user involvement was measured along nine different activities: initiating the project, establishing the objective of the project, determining user requirements, accessing ways to meet user requirements, identifying the sources of data/information, outlining the information flow, developing the input forms/screens, developing the output forms/screens, and determining the system availability/access. Respondents were asked to express their agreement or disagreement with statements saying that users were active players in each of these nine activities. The average score for the nine items is the measure of user involvement. Cronbach's alpha for this scale was 0.94.

## Management Support

This variable has been included in this study primarily for confirmatory purposes. The importance of management support to ES success has been emphasized by several authors $[19, 33, 53]$ . Keyes $[33]$ reported that lack of management support was a critical barrier to ES success. Barsanti $[6]$ said that a key predictor of ES success in an organization is the existence of top-down corporate support. The importance of management support for the successfully implemented ES (XCON) at Digital Equipment Corporation was illustrated by Leonard-Barton $[35]$ and Leonard-Barton and Deschamps $[36]$ . The survey conducted by Byrd $[10]$ also revealed management support as an important factor to ES success.

There are some major reasons why management support is especially important in ES development. First, management support is essential to obtain the personnel and monetary resources necessary for large ES projects $[38]$ . Second, ES is a relatively new technology, and the adoption of a new technology by organizations affects the manner in which decisions are made, business tasks are performed, and power is allocated. Changes in the work environment frequently increase user fears about their jobs and, in turn, may generate resistance against a new ES [52]. The psychological impact and the organizational changes brought on by ES technology can be more marked than those created by any other type of system [38]. In such cases, management support is crucial to mitigate users' negative attitudes toward ES, and to overcome user resistance. Third, a desirable application area for an ES project is where expertise is scarce or expensive, or where the experts are overworked [38]. In such cases, the direct supervisors of domain experts may find it difficult to allow time for ES development. However, higher-level management may take a longer view and be more amenable to investing substantial amounts of time over the period necessary for effective ES development [47]. Management can also play a vital role in dealing with uncooperative domain experts by reassuring them about their jobs and convincing them of the potential ES benefits to the organization. Without management support, the final product is less likely to positively affect users' jobs, as defined in this study. Therefore, we propose:

H9: Management support is directly related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

Management support was measured by four items: management understanding of ES potential benefits, encouragement by management to use ES in the job, providing the necessary help and resources for effective use of ES, and management interest in having employees satisfied with ES technology. Respondents were asked to express their agreement or disagreement with statements saying that their management exhibits the behaviors described by each of these four items. The items were averaged to compute a measure of management support. The internal consistency reliability coefficient for the scale was 0.84.

## System Usage

DeLone and McLean [18] presented this variable as a direct antecedent of the impact of DSS on users' jobs. Barrier and Iyer [5] reported the use of ES as improving the productivity of decision makers. This variable is included in this study for confirmatory purposes. While there is no empirical evidence supporting it, intuitively, one can easily accept the notion that, if it is not used, an ES will have no impact on users' jobs. Therefore, we propose:

H10: ES usage is directly related to a desirable ES impact on users' jobs.

When measuring system impact on users' jobs, prior studies have used one or two items: the estimated hours of use each week [54], whether the system was used or not used [1], and usage frequency and regularity [48]. System usage in this study was measured by a single item. Using the standard seven-point scale, participants were asked to indicate the frequency of ES usage through the degree of agreement or disagreement with the statement “the ES is used all the time.” The next section discusses the study setting, the sampling process procedures and the data analysis followed in this study.

## Study Methodology

## The Study Setting

DUE TO THE LIMITED LITERATURE AVAILABLE ON THE TOPIC and a serious lack of empirical evidence about the determinants of ES impact on users' jobs in practice, instead of a multicompany survey, an in-depth one-company field-test approach to data collection has been used. This approach is expected to reduce the possibility of confounding results due to interorganizational differences based on ES development sophistication, budget, methodologies used, development resources available, policies, and conflicts between user departments and the company's ES development group.

The company in this study is International Business Machines (IBM) which has been selected because of its sophisticated ES development environment, with many experienced ES developers and an impressive collection of ES applications known to have provided substantial benefits to the user community $[45]$ . IBM created its Corporate Manufacturing Expert Systems Project Center (CMESPC) in San Jose, California, in 1988 to develop, implement, and measure the results from Knowledge Based Systems on internal manufacturing operations. Since the beginning, over 460 ES manufacturing applications have been implemented or are being developed internationally throughout the organization in areas such as process control, integrated testing and analysis, manufacturing repository, multimedia systems, and the “factory of the future” in Fujisawa, Japan.

## Sampling Process

The 170 project managers who are in charge of the development, as well as the maintenance, of expert systems throughout IBM installations worldwide were invited to participate in this study via telephone and e-mail. Subsequently, questionnaires were distributed to project managers via e-mail. They were explicitly asked to provide information on an ES whose development they had managed that was in operation for at least one year. Project managers should be considered the most appropriate sources of information for this study mainly for three reasons: (1) They are closely involved with the business processes. (2) They are the only people familiar with the overall ES implementation process: the domain experts, the developers, the development tools, and the end users who participated in the development process of specific ES. Furthermore, through the company's performance evaluation and reward system, project managers are induced to assume an end-user perspective and a long-term view of project success. In other words, the project managers' performance is judged on the ability of the ES to accomplish specific end users' objectives. An unduly favorable project manager view of an ES is meaningless in this case. Overall end-user satisfaction with the system in the long run, regardless of this study, is the only performance measure that matters. (3) To have ES developers or domain experts rate their own performance would unduly raise the risk of response bias. Nevertheless, readers are cautioned that most of the information collected for this study represents the opinions of project managers.

For the measures of user satisfaction and ES impact on users' jobs, it was deemed important that end users provide their own opinion of the ES. The primary end user for the particular ES (the one who uses it most often) was chosen by one of the authors, a manager in the host company, to provide his or her response directly to him via e-mail or hard copy. Of the 170 questionnaires sent out, 69 were returned, providing a response rate of 40 percent. Table 1 shows selected demographic information on project managers. Based on the wide representation of project managers in terms of country of origin, education, and job experience, no sampling bias is apparent. Further, one of the authors (a host company manager) found no reason to suspect sample bias in any way. Some of the respondents did not provide some demographic information—hence the unknown category in the table. For the other variables, missing items were negligible.

## Data Analysis Procedures

The collected data were analyzed in three steps. First, the relationships between ES impact on users' jobs and the ten major study variables were tested by using Pearson's correlation coefficients and multivariate regression. Second, nonparametric Mann-Whitney (M-W) tests were performed to determine whether the types of ES applications affect the relationships among study variables. Given the relatively small sample size and the possibility that the relevant variables are not normally distributed, nonparametric tests were considered more appropriate to analyze the statistical differences between groups of different ES applications along major study variables. Similar to the question about potential ES application types bias, the ES shell used for development may play a very important role in the results of this study. If only one shell were used, one might question whether the results were applicable to ES developed with other shells. If several shells are used, it is possible that the ES shell might affect the relationship among study variables. Again M-W tests were performed to assess the difference between ES developed with different shells.

## Other Interesting Results

Besides providing evidence to test the hypotheses formally proposed in this study, Table 5 shows other interesting relationships that in some cases shed light on some common wisdom about system implementation. For example, system usage has been proposed [18] as a measure of system success, a dependent variable. On the other hand, without using a system, obviously, one can derive no benefits or satisfaction from it, making it a good candidate for independent variable. The results from this study encourage the use of usage as a dependent or intermediary variable by showing that usage alone is only a moderate factor for derived benefits (desirable impact on users' jobs). Intuitively, one can interpret the results as indicating that management support, ES shell quality, user satisfaction with the ES, user characteristics, domain expert quality, developer skill, problem importance, and difficulty are motivators for using the ES. As said above, ES usage in this study is positively related to every major variable except user involvement. As discussed earlier, given the different nature of ES which is based primarily on domain expert knowledge, one may conclude that user involvement is not as important for system success as for other system types. However, user involvement in ES development is related to user satisfaction, another widely used measure for system success. The latter, in turn, is directly related to ES usage. A likely interpretation of these results is that employees are using ES regardless of whether or not they are involved in the system's development. However, ensuring user satisfaction with their ES is important to improve its impact on users' jobs.

## Results from an Integrated Model

While the intercorrelation analysis provides evidence for the relationships of each independent variable with the dependent variable, it does not address possible interrelations among the independent variables as related to the dependent variable. To examine that, a preliminary integrated model for ES job impact determinants is tested via multivariate regression analysis, with results shown in Table 6.

Using a stepwise method for first entering the independent variables that contribute most to explaining the variance in the dependent variable, the results indicate that, in total, close to 39 percent of the variance can be explained with this integrated model. To test the possibility of a multicollinearity problem among the independent variables, the well-established “condition number diagnostic” approach was used $[31]$ . If the condition number is less than 100, multicollinearity is not to be considered a problem. Table 6 indicates the highest condition number is 37.75, well below the safe limit.

Based on this multivariate regression model, there are five statistically significant (at the 0.05 level or better) determinants for ES desirable impact on users' jobs: problem difficulty, which explains 14.10 percent; domain expert quality, which explains 7.99 percent of the variance in ES job impact; followed by management support (4.94 percent), and developer skill (4.79 percent). The contribution from the other determinants in this model, given the order in which they have entered the regression equation, are not statistically significant.

It is interesting to note that considered alone (Table 5), developer skill shows no direct relationship to ES desirable impact on users' jobs. However, in conjunction with the other three variables that entered the regression equation before, it can explain 4.79 percent of the variance. Developer skill is also directly related to user satisfaction and system usage, which are widely used measures for system success [18]. For these reasons, to increase the likelihood of desirable ES impact on users' jobs, developer skill development deserves managerial attention and should be ensured in ES development cases dealing with difficult problems being addressed by high-quality domain experts with management support.

## Managerial Recommendations

SEVERAL INSIGHTS CAN BE GLEANED FROM THE STUDY'S RESULTS which have important implications for ES technology management in organizations. We can conclude that ES can have substantial positive impact on users' jobs, providing organizations with a major opportunity to enjoy many of the promised benefits. Every major variable addressed in this study, directly and/or indirectly, shows significant influence in determining a desirable ES impact on users' jobs. Separately, developer skill seems to have no influence on ES impact on users' jobs. Its influence seems to be primarily indirect through relationships with system usage, user satisfaction, shell quality, problem importance, and problem difficulty. They, in turn, are directly related to positive changes in jobs due to ES implementation.

Considered individually, the most important major variables affecting the impact that ES have on users' jobs are the nature of the problem being addressed by the ES (its importance and difficulty), the quality of the domain experts involved, the degree of user satisfaction with the ES, and the quality of the shell being used to develop the ES. User characteristics, user involvement in the development of the ES, management understanding and support for ES technology utilization, and system usage also deserve managerial attention. Considered together, according to the integrated model developed in this study, the proper selection of difficult business problems and the assignment/recruitment of high-quality domain experts to address such problems are the two strongest factors determining favorable ES impact on users' jobs. Based on the results, the following recommendations can be advanced:

Project managers should carefully select domain experts to favorably affect the ES impact on users' jobs. The important attributes of domain experts, such as their communications skills, their willingness to cooperate, and the extent of their background in computers and AI technology, should be developed and cultivated. Organizations heavily involved with the development of ES should establish training courses to improve expert/developer communications, to enhance domain experts' computer and AI backgrounds, and to take measures to reduce job security fears $[38]$ . It is also important that companies reward effective cooperation of domain experts in ES development.

The selection of suitable applications is indeed worthy of careful management attention. Many useful general recommendations have already been made (e.g., [38]) for the systematic selection of applications with the objective of deriving long-term leverage of ES development resources. The results from this study provide additional and more specific criteria for ES application selection. Under the reasonable assumption that favorable ES impact on users' jobs represents a valuable contribution to the organization, ES project managers should deliberately seek applications that deal with important, large, labor-intensive, and/or complex problems whose variables are highly interrelated, and whose data are characterized by high levels of uncertainty.

User satisfaction with an ES is one of the most important factors determining the impact of the ES on users' jobs. The importance of this factor calls for renewed management motivation to improve ES quality assurance processes by motivating active user participation, and to introduce or refurbish methods and tools that focus on enhancing the user–ES interface. User characteristics (attitude and expectations about the ES) are directly related to user satisfaction with their ES, as well as with a favorable ES impact on users' jobs. Short seminars on the potential and limitations of ES technology, and other measures to keep user expectations in line with the organization's capabilities in this are likely to increase user satisfaction and favorable ES impact on users's jobs.

The selection of an appropriate shell that matches the business problem, as well as user and developer requirements, is an important event. Yoon and Guimaraes [67] have provided guidelines for matching specific problem characteristics with shells supporting four main ES development techniques (knowledge-based, neural network, model-based, or case-based). Unfortunately most organizations today are woefully unprepared in this area, with few organizations providing the main alternatives. The results from this study regarding shell quality may be applicable only to the more widely used knowledge-based ES that comprise our sample. Nevertheless, developers and project managers should carefully select shells along the important features studied here—namely, flexibility in knowledge representation and construction of the inference engine, interface with other systems, ease of learning and of using, training and vendor support, response time, and appropriateness to the problem. As the cost of ES shells continue to decrease, managers are quickly running out of excuses for not making available to developers and encouraging the use (that is, providing developer training) of appropriate shells for the development of new ES applications, particularly those dealing with problems of major importance to the organization.

Despite the fact that the knowledge for an ES will come from a domain expert who expectedly has more knowledge about the problem than most users, user involvement in the ES development process seems significantly to affect user satisfaction and the degree to which the ES will positively affect the users' jobs. It is interesting that management support is not related to user involvement. Even though management support is having a significant impact on user characteristics (user attitude and expectations about ES), apparently, project managers are not using available top management influence to recruit users for ES development. High-quality shells and high-quality domain experts seem to be doing more to attract user involvement to the development process. While user involvement shows no significant relationship to ES usage, it is significantly related to user satisfaction with their ES. Project managers should ensure appropriate level of user participation by discussing the project needs with department and top managers, and by following many of the recommendations to ensure management support previously proposed in the context of more traditional systems such as DSS and transaction processing systems.

## Study Limitations and Research Opportunities

THERE ARE SEVERAL ASPECTS OF THIS STUDY that may be viewed as weaknesses. By the same token, these possible weaknesses also represent opportunities for further research in an area of technology that has captured people's imagination, has already produced great benefits to business organizations in practice, and continues to expand its promise. The most significant limitation is the relatively small sample size which, nevertheless, produced many significant results.

As mentioned earlier, another limitation is that due to the lack of general information and empirical evidence about the ES-related factors affecting users' jobs in practice, ES from one company were used. This approach reduces the possibility of confounding results due to a wide variety of reasons such as interorganization differences as to ES development sophistication, budget, methodologies used, development resources available, policies, and conflicts between user departments and the company's ES development group. On the other hand, this approach raises the question of generalizability. The results from this study provide researchers with more focus for defining hypotheses that can be better tested with a multicompany survey.

The general application of this study's results can also be questioned in terms of industry and the sophistication of the ES development environment. We have no reason to suspect that the results and conclusions are applicable only to manufacturing organizations; however, that needs to be formally tested. On the other hand, one should assume that the organization studied indeed has an extremely sophisticated ES development environment, with a wide variety of development tools and methods, and hundreds of ES developers and users with many years of experience using ES technology. It may be vital to take that into consideration while attempting to apply some of the results to other organizations. Future studies need to control for industry type and the sophistication of the ES development environment. Similarly, we attempted to control for ES shell and application types and the test results showed no cause for alarm. However, the tests need to be repeated with larger sample sizes and wider representation of ES shells and application types.

Despite its limitations, this study represents the first systematic attempt to identify a comprehensive list of ES-related factors that are likely to influence the degree to which ES can have a desirable impact on users' jobs. Given the growing investment, the impressive results many organizations are deriving from effectively applying ES technology, its versatility and future potential, it is imperative that research on the use and on the management of this technology be expanded quickly.
