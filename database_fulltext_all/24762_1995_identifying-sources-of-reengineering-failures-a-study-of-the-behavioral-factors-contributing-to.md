# Identifying Sources of Reengineering Failures: A Study of the Behavioral Factors Contributing to Reengineering Risks

ERIC K. CLEMONS, MATT E. THATCHER, AND MICHAEL C. ROW

ERIC K. CLEMONS. See Guest Editor's Introduction.

MATT E. THATCHER is a Ph.D. candidate in the Operations and Information Management Department of The Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania. Mr. Thatcher also works with the Reginald H. Jones Center's Project in Information Systems, Telecommunications, and Business Strategy. His education includes a B.S. in economics and an M.A. in information technology from The Wharton School. His research interests include strategic information systems, the impact of information systems on retail distribution channels, and the economic modeling of information technology productivity.

MICHAEL C. ROW. See Guest Editor's Introduction.

ABSTRACT: Recent experience suggests that many reengineering efforts fail, and that they fail for reasons unrelated to the technical ability of organizations to implement information systems. Our research suggests that the two principal reasons for failure are functionality risk and political risk: respectively, the organization's inability to understand its uncertain future strategic needs, and its inability to make painful and difficult changes in response to these future strategic needs. Recent research in the organizational change literature suggests that these risks are the result of conflict among the organization's current strategy, its espoused degree of change, the actually accepted and generally smaller degree of change, and the generally larger degree of change that would be in some sense optimal. Moreover, the conflicts among these may be unperceived or undiscussable within the organization, exacerbating the risks. We summarize in a few testable hypotheses our experience with managing the risks of reengineering, and use a small set of representative case studies to examine these hypotheses informally.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: business reengineering, implementation of information systems, risk of information systems.

Acknowledgment: We acknowledge the careful and insightful reading and significant contributions of Harold Levine, without whose contribution our own “strategy espoused” for this paper might never have been made clear.

ACCORDING TO RECENT SURVEYS. 88 PERCENT OF LARGE CORPORATIONS are involved in business process reengineering projects, and many others plan to begin projects soon [5]. However, failure rates for large-scale information technology (IT) projects and reengineering efforts are extremely high, with many firms either abandoning their efforts after making significant investments or failing to achieve anticipated results from their redesigns [5, 14]. The direct costs (e.g., project delays and cost overruns) associated with these reengineering failures are enormous, sometimes costing organizations millions, or even billions, of dollars. The indirect costs (e.g., failure to achieve competitive objectives) may be even higher. The high failure rates associated with reengineering efforts, combined with the implications of such failure, suggest that organizations need to improve their ability to identify and to manage the associated risks. This paper proposes a descriptive framework, based on literature from the areas of information technology, organizational behavior, and strategic management, that highlights sources of risk of reengineering efforts, which in turn will help managers identify and better manage these risks.

An organization involved in developing and implementing a large-scale IT project typically faces risks that threaten the successful completion of the project. The inability of managers to identify and to manage these risks significantly contributes to reengineering failures. We categorize these risks as follows [6]:

\- Financial risk—the risk that the IT project will not generate the expected returns on investment for the organization.

\- Technical risk—the risk that the technologies or the methodologies necessary to develop and implement the IT project simply do not exist.

\- Project risk—the risk created when firms attempt to develop systems in areas outside their traditional strengths and expertise or beyond the competence of the development team.

\- Functionality risk—the risk that the completed system will not meet the present or future needs of its users, either because the systems designers misunderstood the organization's needs, or because these needs have changed.

\- Political risk—the risk that the systems will not be completed, because of organizational resistance, or because of the gradual loss of commitment to the project.

Earlier research notes that, if properly identified early in project development, these risks can often actively be managed and controlled. Moreover, steps can be taken that transfer exposure from one risk component, perhaps difficult to manage, to another, which may be easier to reduce $[6]$ .

The degree to which each risk identified above threatens organizational systems efforts has changed significantly over the past decades. The risk profile facing innovating firms from the late 1960s through the early 1980s was dominated by technical risk and financial risk. First, the cost/performance ratio for hardware was hundreds of times higher than today. Second, estimation procedures for project size and for project cost were far less accurate during this period. Third, the technological capabilities (e.g., memory, speed, and storage capacity) were quite limited by today's standards and could easily be exceeded by overly aggressive systems design. Moreover, most large-scale IT projects from the late 1960s involved the automation of existing processes; political risk posed little threat, as lower-level employees commonly affected by such innovations were typically unable to resist the IT project successfully. Finally, functionality risk was limited and defining requirements for an accounts payable system, or other typical projects of the period, was a relatively easy task.

Conversely, the risk profile facing innovating firms today is dominated by functionality and political risks. This shift in the risk profile away from technical and financial risk has been facilitated by changes in the technological environment and research advances in the area of IT development and implementation. Dramatic increases in technological capabilities have been accompanied by dramatic decreases in hardware costs. There have been significant improvements in estimation procedures for project size and for project cost and methodological advances in software development (e.g., object-oriented programming languages, prototyping, code reusability, and CASE tools). While not reducing total software costs, these advances have reduced uncertainty in development and hence the associated cost overruns. Another reason for the growing prominence of functionality and political risks facing innovating firms is that today, large-scale IT projects tend to be more strategic in nature and involve more than simple process automation. In fact, we believe that for reasons described below, this change in risk profile is most extreme for the class of strategic systems projects that have been termed business reengineering.

Business reengineering efforts are frequently described as “greenfields” or “clean slate” attempts to determine the most appropriate future design for an organization. Such reengineering efforts generally attempt to start without preconceptions about the company’s future strategy or operating environment that are restricted by the company’s current strategy, business environment, or technical capabilities; this creates considerable uncertainty about the precise details of the future for which the systems are being built. Developing a system for an uncertain future increases functionality risk, since future needs and requirements for the system and its users may be poorly understood by the organization. In addition, reengineering efforts requiring “clean slate” changes will involve the redistribution of organizational power and resources as certain tasks and operating units become obsolete. Personnel threatened by the changes will try to protect the value of their existing skills and expertise by resisting the change (political risk); senior personnel may be able to do so quite effectively.

This paper examines the effects of factors outside of technology on the organization's ability to initiate, develop, and implement large scale IT projects that meet the present and future needs of the firm and its environment. Changes in the risk profile threatening the organization's ability to do this, and the increasing importance of nontechnical issues, are most extreme for reengineering projects. The next section introduces the basic ideas, terms, and structure of a framework that identifies the risks associated with large-scale reengineering efforts. We then elaborate on this framework, review relevant literature, and present hypotheses that formalize the basic ideas of our framework. In the fourth, fifth, and sixth sections we present case examples investigating the issues described in the previous sections and explicitly relate them back to the hypotheses; while these cases neither prove nor conclusively support the hypotheses, they do provide empirical support for their plausibility. The final section presents our conclusions and explores the managerial implications of our findings.

## Framework Development

WE DEVELOP A FRAMEWORK THAT EXAMINES THE RISKS ASSOCIATED with reengineering efforts. This framework is based on normative theories describing how firms should develop and should implement organizational strategy, and also on descriptive theories describing how firms actually do develop and do implement strategy. These theories are drawn from literature in organizational behavior and strategic management (e.g., [1, 2, 20, 21]). First, we present a brief review of relevant literature.

Our framework is inspired by concepts and terminology introduced by Chris Argyris. Argyris $[2, 3, 4]$ has developed a descriptive framework that examines how individuals within organizations deal with each other; how they communicate, manage issues, and resolve conflict. This framework, termed “action science,” claims that individuals unknowingly possess two different theories of action that largely determine the structure of their interpersonal interactions; espoused theory and theory-in-use. Espoused theory represents the values and beliefs individuals claim to hold and publicly support, while theory-in-use represents the values and beliefs individuals actually follow when they communicate with each other and when they implement actions to resolve conflict. Argyris further states that while espoused theory varies widely among individuals, theory-in-use varies very little. In particular, Argyris claims that the goals that govern and motivate the actions of most individuals (i.e., theory-in-use) include: (1) maximizing winning and minimizing losing, (2) being in unilateral control over others, (3) saving face unilaterally, and (4) being rational. To achieve these goals, individuals typically implement defensive routines, psychological defenses against change, intended to reduce risk, stress, pain, and embarrassment associated with interacting with others. These defensive routines are behavior programs that are typically entrenched in individuals early in life (and may even be the result of millions of years of evolution) and include the avoidance of discussing and addressing critical issues. In general, Argyris has accumulated a considerable body of evidence (e.g., $[2, 3, 4]$ ) demonstrating that significant differences exist between individuals’ theory-in-use and espoused theory. He has convincingly argued that these differences are undetected because of, and are facilitated by, individuals’ tendencies to engage in unproductive, entrenched behavior programs.

We attempt to apply these terms and concepts, which Argyris developed to describe individual-level interaction and communication, to organizational strategy. In particular, we believe that most firms have at all times two classes of strategy held by personnel within the firm; in the style of Argyris, we term these strategy espoused and strategies-in-use:

\- Strategy espoused is the official description of the firm and its future; this can frequently be determined from public sources, such as the chairman's letter in the annual report;

\- Strategies-in-use describe what personnel actually believe about the firm and its strategy, about their business unit, and about prospects for the future; these are privately held, generally differ significantly among personnel, and are almost universally at odds to some degree with the official strategy espoused.

Differences between strategy espoused and strategies-in-use arise from two principal sources:

\- Defensive routines that cause the individuals to avoid potentially painful or embarrassing discussion of critical issues [2, 3, 4], and

\- Organizational tendencies to reward behaviors that have preceded past successes, and may have contributed to or even have directly caused those successes; thus, long after these behaviors have ceased to be productive, individuals may repeat them, and cling to beliefs about their strategic effectiveness [13, 18, 19, 20, 21].

Our direct personal experience demonstrates that these differences can occur even among personnel in frequent contact, and among the most senior personnel within an organization. Unfortunately, as Argyris demonstrates, defensive routines generate behavior that frequently causes these differences to remain undetected and unacknowledged or may in other ways make them difficult to resolve.

Having provided this brief background for the concepts and terminology used in our framework, we now use figure 1 to show the foundation of the framework, with each concept identified representing a separate vector. $^{1}$ As we will show in the next section, these four vectors interact to define the risks facing innovating firms. The vectors are:

1. Current: This vector represents what an organization is presently doing prior to the development of proposed changes within the organization. For our purposes, this vector describes the organization's position with respect to resources, skills, and competencies prior to the reengineering effort.

2. Strategy-in-use: This vector represents what the personnel within the organization actually believe the strategy of the firm should be. In a stable business environment, strategies-in-use and current strategy are likely to exhibit considerable agreement within successful firms. In turbulent and changing times, there is likely to be considerable disagreement between strategies-in-use and strategy espoused, and even among strategies-in-use of different individuals. However, the tendencies of individuals within the firm to avoid conflict and to preserve the value of existing skills and resources creates an organizational atmosphere where critical differences among strategies-in-use and strategy espoused are undetected and undiscussable. Our experience suggests that strategies-in-use largely dictate the strategy implemented by the firm, and the systems actually developed to support them. For simplicity, our figure displays a “representative” example of strategies-in-use.

Figure 1. Framework Definition

3. Strategy espoused: This vector represents what an organization claims to believe and claims it will do in response to changing environmental conditions and changing customer needs. A strategy espoused is publicly held and publicly acknowledged and often coincides with organizational objectives, ideals, or mission statements. Because of the cognitive limitations and the bounded rationality of individuals within the firm [11], a firm's strategy espoused typically falls short of what they normatively should do. Employees often "satisfice" by setting good enough, instead of optimal, standards of performance that fit their limited view of reality. While individuals within the firm typically view strategy espoused to be an unambiguous statement of organizational objectives, as described earlier, their interpretations of the firm's strategy espoused (i.e., their strategies-in-use) often differ widely.

4. Optimality: This vector represents an idealized strategy, reflecting what an organization should do as its optimal response to its changing business environment; this is dictated by its current strategy, its resource endowment, and its technological capabilities. It in turn dictates the ideal, but unrealizable, objectives of business reengineering efforts, including strategic change and systems development efforts.

In figure 1, the angles separating each vector qualitatively describe the extent to which these factors identified above differ. For example, the greater the angle formed by the vector “optimality” and the vector “current,” the more the organization needs to change to respond effectively to changing environmental conditions. If the angle between these two vectors is very small, this suggests that the organization’s environment is relatively stable, requiring only incremental changes on the firm’s part. When examining large-scale strategic IT projects and reengineering efforts that have been motivated by significant environmental change, we expect this difference to be relatively large. In addition, the larger the angle between strategy espoused and strategy-in-use, the bigger the difference between what an organization’s personnel claim to believe about what they need to be doing and what they actually believe. An organization's strategy espoused typically falls short of what the firm should do, as shown in the optimality vector. It is the gaps between these vectors and their relative positions that interest us, not their precise directions or the nature of the underlying space in which they are drawn.

As noted earlier, we expect that strategies espoused and strategies-in-use will typically differ within an organization; that strategies-in-use will, in turn, exhibit significant disagreements as well; and that these disagreements will usually remain undetected. Strategies-in-use will frequently provide the basis for all individual behavior within the firm, and will thus frequently be the basis for requirements specifications and systems development efforts. Thus, differences between strategy espoused and strategies-in-use will frequently cause actual reengineering efforts to fall short not only of optimal possibilities but even of those strategies publicly embraced by the firm. This is summarized in figure 1 and is formalized in the following hypotheses:

H1a: An organization's strategies-in-use will typically be more consistent with the firm's current operations than will the organization's strategy espoused. That is, organizations have a tendency to propose to make, and to claim to support, changes that are more radical than the changes they actually support and implement. $^{2}$

H1b: The reengineering program eventually developed and implemented by an organization will typically be more consistent with the firm's existing competencies and power structure, as reflected in its strategies-in-use, than with the firm's strategy espoused.

## Literature Review and Hypothesis Development

THE PRECEDING SECTION IDENTIFIED FOUR MAIN CONCEPTS (VECTORS) that define our framework. By relating the framework to the findings from literature in the areas of IT, strategic management, and organizational behavior, we identify the sources of functionality and political risk, identify the trade-offs between these risks, and develop a series of hypotheses that will formalize our framework. Our goal is to synthesize findings from previous literature into a coherent framework that will help managers predict, control, and reduce the risks associated with reengineering efforts.

## Political Risk

Several researchers have studied how political risk threatens the successful implementation of proposed innovations within the organization $[1, 12, 20, 21]$ . These authors suggest that the capacity of a proposed innovation to influence the value and applicability of existing organizational competencies defines the risks of an innovation and the threats to its development and implementation. In particular,

\- Competence-enhancing changes preserve the value of existing skills, knowledge, resources and relationships (competencies) within the organization and further entrench them within the organization.

\- Competence-destroying changes, in contrast, devalue or render obsolete existing competencies within the organization [21].

Tushman and Romanelli [21] suggest that competence-destroying changes (relatively short periods of discontinuous change where strategy, power, structure, and controls are fundamentally realigned), will be associated with an increase in organizational turbulence and conflict as the firm's political subsystems react to each other and attempt to protect the value and applicability of existing competencies. The more a proposed strategic reorientation, or strategy espoused, threatens to disrupt or devalue existing competencies (e.g., entrenched, rewarded patterns of activities and processes), the greater the potential redistribution of power within the firm, and thus, the greater the internal conflict among different interest groups as groups try to protect the value of their existing competencies. Conversely, the more a proposed strategic reorientation enhances the value and applicability of the organization's existing competencies, and thus, the less it affects the potential redistribution of power and resources within the firm, the smaller the internal conflict among different interest groups. If resistance to change is sufficient, the organization will fail to develop or implement projects, including reengineering projects, that are consistent with the proposed strategic reorientation rather than with the established order. Therefore, organizations attempting to develop and implement IT projects inconsistent with their existing culture and competencies typically fail to complete the projects due to organizational resistance.

H2a: The more a strategic reorientation threatens to disrupt or devalue existing skills within the firm, or requires elimination of previously successful and previously rewarded behavior, the more likely it is to be resisted; this resistance will be manifest as a significant gap between the firm's public strategy espoused and the collection of individuals' private strategies-in-use. Therefore, the greater the gap between strategy espoused and strategies-in-use, the greater the organizational resistance and the more likely the organization's strategic change initiative is to fail. This suggests that high political risk, a frequent cause of failure of business reengineering efforts, is associated not with the degree of radical change espoused (difference between current practice and strategy espoused) but by the degree of unaccepted change proposed (difference between strategy espoused and strategies-in-use [see figure 2]).

## Functionality Risk

Several researchers have addressed the ways that functionality risk threatens the successful implementation of proposed innovations within the organization $[13, 18, 19]$ . Individuals and organizations create and use behavior programs, learned behaviors that can be performed without thinking, to reproduce their past successes $[11, 13, 18, 19]$ . While the use of behavior programs helps conserve individuals' limited cognitive and analytical resources, it also encourages individuals to respond to their environments in inappropriate ways $[18]$ . For example, when confronted with a new problem, organizations and individuals often attempt to fit that problem in terms of experiences with previous problems for which previously successful solutions exist. One particularly dangerous result of attempting to preserve these behavior programs when they have outlasted their usefulness is that organizations often propose incremental changes that are too small and thus inappropriate to meet changing environmental demands, but that further entrench existing skills and competencies $[20, 21]$ .

H2b: The less proposed strategic business reengineering requires competence-destroying change within the organization, the less it will be resisted and the smaller will be the gap between the firm's public strategy espoused and the collection of individuals' private strategies-in-use; this will reduce political risk and increase the likelihood that proposed reengineering efforts are successfully completed. However, the more the proposed reengineering effort avoids competence-destroying change that may indeed be required by radically changing environmental conditions, the more likely it is that the project will fall far short of supporting an optimal new strategy, creating significant functionality risk. To the degree that the reengineering effort does not adequately position the firm for the future, it will fail (see figure 3). The area in figure 3 between the strategy espoused and optimality represents the minimum level of functionality risk; that is, it represents the functionality risk of systems that will not even be considered because the need is not perceived or acknowledged. Additional functionality risk may be caused by organizations' inability to realize their strategy espoused.

In summary, hypotheses 2a and 2b address the political risk and functionality risk caused by the relative gaps among optimal strategy, strategy espoused and strategiesin-use. The gap between optimality and strategy espoused defines the minimum level of functionality risk; it is the level of change that the organization has not acknowledged it needs to make. The gap between strategy espoused and strategy-in-use contributes to political risk to the extent that the organization is not attempting to make changes that its personnel do not wish to make; it contributes to additional levels of functionality risk to the extent that the organization fails to attempt these changes its personnel do not wish to make. These hypotheses suggest that a firm's choice of strategic objectives for its business reengineering efforts require effectively balancing or trading off political risk and functionality risk [6].

## Other Factors Affecting the Risk Profile

Employees at more successful firms generally receive more positive reinforcement, both from the environment and from satisfied managers; thus, these employees at successful firms are more likely to codify their behavior into learned patterns, and to rely upon these learned behavior programs to allow them to repeat their past successes $[18]$ . Actions that personnel believe have led to previous success tend to be rewarded and thus are reinforced; therefore, they are often replicated even when they are no longer appropriate. When employees perform certain actions and they seem to get along well, the employees tend, often mistakenly, to attribute the “good performance” to those actions $[15]$ . Employees often wait until long after their behavior programs have been explicitly disconfirmed before considering alternative actions. Thus, rewarded success often sows the seeds of future failure as successful organizations typically suffer from overconfidence and intellectual arrogance, and therefore fail to perceive the need for change or to discuss critical factors in their strategy.

The inability to perceive the need for change or to discuss critical factors can also be perpetuated by individuals' use of certain unproductive behavior programs, termed defensive routines. Argyris also calls these defensive routines skilled incompetence. They are “skilled” because they are examples of learned behavior that, when perfected, enables the individual to avoid embarrassing himself or his organization; they are termed “incompetence” because they frequently lead to a dysfunctional inability for an organization to confront critical issues effectively. Although Argyris believes that this behavior must be learned, he believes that most individuals learn it early, and that there may be cultural or even biological bases for the frequency with which these same patterns emerge. Three examples of defensive routines used by individuals to avoid organizational conflict and discomfort follow:

\- Argyris has termed the implicit avoidance of certain highly charged subjects “undiscussability,” which he lists as another form of skilled incompetence. Argyris notes that the undiscussability of critical issues may itself be undiscussable; for example, the chairperson may determine that what may and may not be addressed at a board meeting (undiscussability), and the chairperson’s right to determine what may be discussable may itself not be subject to discussion or challenge (undiscussability of undiscussability).

\- Individuals often communicate without precision and at high levels of abstraction while assuming that what they say is clear and unambiguous. This use of vague and ambiguous language may be a purposeful attempt by individuals to avoid creating organizational discomfort and typically leads to misunderstandings as individuals interpret the same message in dramatically different ways.

\- Individuals tend to blame others within the organization for uncontrollable external factors or for problems within the organization while discounting their own role in creating or perpetuating those same problems. In addition, individuals often do not believe, or do not take seriously, what others have told them.

A particularly dangerous result of these behavior patterns is that critical differences and disagreement within the organization are not surfaced or resolved. Frequently, individuals become so dedicated to protecting their own interests, defending their previously announced positions or avoiding stress and confrontation, that they use defensive routines to allow critical issues to remain unsurfaced if they believe their discussion will create further stress. Indeed, since critical differences are frequently undiscussable, it is not widely perceived within the organization that these differences even exist, effectively precluding any successful effort at resolution.

The findings discussed in this section suggest that successful organizations, in particular, are threatened by:

\- inertial forces, which impede the firm's ability to reassess environmental opportunities and initiate new strategic orientations due to reliance upon outmoded, previously successful behavior;

\- defensive routines, which impede the firm's ability to disrupt existing relationships and competencies sufficiently to support the new orientation, due to avoidance of painful discussion of critical issues.

H3: The more successful an organization has been, the greater are the inertial forces for proposing incremental, competence-enhancing changes, and the greater are the defensive routines implemented by individuals to avoid confrontation over competence-destroying changes. These forces encourage people to misperceive the environment and increase the likelihood that the strategy espoused will not meet the changing needs of the external environment. Therefore, when faced with a changing, uncertain environment, the more successful an organization has been in the past, the more likely the proposed project will fail due to high functionality risk. Moreover, given that a firm does propose a strategy espoused in close agreement with a new optimal strategy, entrenched defensive routines, exacerbated by previous organizational success, will typically result in the actual development and implementation of strategies-in-use far less radical than those originally proposed. While reducing political risk, such actions also result in high functionality risk.

The cumulative implications of the literature reviewed in this section are that organizations facing a volatile, uncertain environment succeed when they achieve:

\- external or environmental consistency between strategy espoused and the demands of the environment, as reflected in the idealized optimal strategy for the firm; and

\- internal or organizational consistency among public strategy espoused and privately held strategies-in-use and among these various strategies-in-use, enabling them to overcome resistance and political risk and enabling them to achieve their espoused goals.

In short, for a strategic business reengineering program to succeed fully, it must embrace the right strategic goals and attempt to move the organization far enough in the correct direction, and it must be embraced by the organization and allowed to proceed far enough in the stated direction. The business implications of this are summarized in hypothesis 4.

H4: Strategic business reengineering efforts will succeed, and will produce strategic benefits that allow their implementors to become high-performing organizations relative to their competitors, to the extent that they first develop external consistency between what is attempted and what is ideal for the organization, given its future environment and its current resource endowment (that is, strategy espoused must be correctly determined), and internal consistency between what is attempted and what key personnel actually believe should be attempted (that is, public strategy espoused and private strategies-in-use must be compatible).

The following sections briefly summarize supporting data, which has been developed more fully previously $[7, 10]$ . While these cases alone neither validate the framework presented earlier nor prove the hypotheses developed in the preceding section, they do provide preliminary support for their plausibility. In particular, these cases provide valuable insights into the effect of nontechnical factors on the development and implementation of strategic IT projects, a subject we believe to be significantly underrepresented in the existing IT literature.

## Rosenbluth Travel

THE TRAVEL INDUSTRY CHANGED DRAMATICALLY DUE TO REGULATORY CHANGE in the airline industry in 1978. Rosenbluth Travel (RT), which enjoyed a charismatic leader with a coherent vision of the future, was able to see and embrace an appropriate strategy, and benefited enormously. In early 1980, Rosenbluth Travel was a small, regional, family-run firm in the travel industry, with gross annual sales of \$40 million. In 1990, RT emerged as one of the five largest U.S. travel management companies, with gross annual sales of \$1.3 billion.

Before deregulation, the primary role of travel agents such as RT was to distribute tickets and to provide customers with advice on vacation destinations. Since routes and fares were relatively stable, the travel agency industry was unable to provide differentiated service for business travel. As a result, the primary focus of travel agents was on meeting the needs of the leisure traveler.

In 1978, deregulation created fundamental changes in the travel industry. It dramatically increased the complexity of route and fare structures, changed the products and services demanded by customers, and changed the cost structure of the industry. After deregulation, airlines were able to add or delete routes and to change fares at will. With increased competition between airlines, the number of changes in routes and fares increased dramatically, creating a level of complexity that was staggering to the customers and that needed to be managed. Travel agents (TAs) needed to be able to help customers achieve the best cost and convenience in these complex deregulated markets.

Deregulation also led to the emergence of the corporate travel market, as the costs associated with business travel sky-rocketed. Airlines sought to increase revenues by charging the price-insensitive business travelers much higher prices. This explosion of business travel costs created incentives for corporations to reduce travel costs, which frequently represented the third largest corporate expense after payroll and data processing.

## Optimality

Deregulation led to dramatic changes in the travel industry and had significant implications for firms such as RT. In particular, TAs had a new opportunity to change their primary role in the travel industry in order to meet effectively the needs of the new environment and its customers. The TAs could now play a major role in managing the complexity introduced by deregulation, and could address the growing needs of the emerging business travel market. This dramatic change in roles required radical changes in strategic orientation on the part of RT if it hoped to succeed and to grow in the future environment.

## Strategy Espoused

Hal Rosenbluth took over executive leadership of RT at the time of deregulation. Hal was immediately seen within the firm as having strategic vision and was believed to have an uncanny ability to see the “right stuff.” As documented in the original case study $[7]$ , as the senior officer in a family-owned business, he was able to create a publicly held strategy espoused. Hindsight demonstrates that Hal’s strategic vision and the resulting strategy espoused were correct; indeed, they have been copied by American Express, Thomas Cook, and virtually all major competitors in the United States and abroad $[9]$ . Hal recognized that RT was no longer only in the business of writing tickets, but was now in the “information management business.” Hal proposed to focus RT on providing superior service to business customers, because he believed that corporate travel represented a major opportunity for growth. Hal believed that IT would play a critical role in servicing this market and in helping to manage the complexity introduced by deregulation.

## Strategy-in-Use

In addition to this vision, or his ability to bring strategy espoused in synch with optimality, Hal was also a charismatic leader who provided, and still provides, strong executive leadership. As was also documented in the original case study [7], this combination of energy, personality, and leadership enabled Hal to enjoy an almost cult-like following among employees, and thus enabled his strategy for the firm to be widely embraced within the firm. This led to strategies-in-use within the firm that closely matched Hal's vision, and to enthusiastic and vigorous support for change and for activities and systems that effectively supported his proposed strategic vision. Some of the innovations successfully implemented are summarized below.

In 1983, RT introduced READOUT, a system that significantly changed the flight selection process. READOUT allowed RT to list city-pair flights by order of price (fare) as opposed to order of departure times as listed by the CRS. This innovation provided value-added service to customers by helping them to avoid the biases of the CRS and by helping them to fly at the lowest applicable fare. This led to an immediate increase in corporate accounts for RT. RT also implemented VISION, a back-office system designed to be responsive to the needs of corporate clients. The system increased the gathering, accuracy, and completeness of information, reduced RT's reliance on systems provided by the airlines' biased CRS, and also increased the power and flexibility of reporting. These factors allowed RT to identify opportunities for price negotiations with airlines and enabled RT to pioneer the art of preferred negotiated route-by-route fares with airlines. The development and success of these systems enabled the development of other innovations that made corporate policy and traveler profiles available to all Rosenbluth offices. This helped to consolidate customer information, to simplify the agents' job, and to provide a platform for the development of subsequent innovations.

## Implication for Risks

Figure 4 summarizes the risks facing RT after deregulation (supporting hypotheses 1a and 1b). As shown, the strategy espoused at RT was very close to optimality. Figure 4 also shows that the strategies-in-use and the strategy espoused by the firm were in close agreement. This consistency between strategy espoused and strategies-in-use, and among individuals' strategies-in-use, may be attributed to Hal Rosenbluth's ability to get full and active support from the individuals within the firm. Since optimality, strategy espoused, and strategies-in-use were all in close agreement, functionality and political risks were minimal (supporting hypotheses 2a and 2b). Implementation of radical changes did not result in internal conflict and met with little resistance. Hal's vision reduced the gap between optimality and strategy espoused, resulting in a low level of unattempted change and thus a minimal level of functionality risk. This combination of factors enabled RT to exploit several new opportunities, leading to enormous growth (supporting hypothesis 4). Annual gross revenues increased from \$40 million in 1980, to \$400 million in 1987, to \$1.3 billion in 1990. In addition, by 1990, RT had 400 worldwide locations, compared with only 87 in 1987; by 1993, there were over 1,000. In hindsight, we note that when initiating these dramatic changes to organizational strategy, RT was not hindered by a “successful past.” In other words, RT was a small regional player for the previous ninety years and therefore did not develop a significant set of entrenched behavior programs designed to repeat past global successes or an IT infrastructure and organizational design geared to previous market forces. With fewer inappropriate, entrenched behavior programs to destroy, Hal was able to effectively manage the change process (supporting hypothesis 3).

While RT was able effectively to develop and successfully to implement a radical strategic vision in the 1980s, American Express (AmEx), one of RT's current competitors, was unable to move aggressively into the corporate market for almost ten years. Before deregulation, AmEx was an extremely successful and profitable organization in the travel industry with high levels of consistency among strategy. business planning, and IT planning. However, during the time prior to deregulation, AmEx had invested significantly in systems and expertise that were no longer appropriate in the environment after deregulation. Therefore, radical changes in AmEx's strategic vision would imply changes for AmEx that significantly threatened the value and applicability of the firm's existing skills, systems, and expertise. More important, AmEx's past success may have contributed to its failure to perceive accurately the rapid changes in its environment. These observations suggest that AmEx would face enormous levels of functionality risk due to the firm's inability to perceive accurately, and to propose and implement changes, strategies, and systems that would meet the needs of the changing environment. These observations provide further support for hypotheses 3 and 4.

## A U.K. Banking Industry Example

DRAMATIC CHANGES IN THE U.K. BANKING INDUSTRY DURING THE LATE 1980s, including changes in U.K. banking regulation, entrance of foreign competition, and increased domestic competition in what had been an oligopolistic industry, threatened the profitability of all major English clearing banks. One bank attempted to respond to these changes using its vast financial resources, but found that it was unable either to embrace a new strategic vision or to implement it fully successfully.

The changing competition in the banking environment in Britain adversely affected the bank's profitability. Of particular interest was the evolution of a new group of financial services competitors, the building societies. Traditionally, building societies focused on mortgage lending, lacked the technological sophistication of the clearing banks, and were dependent on clearing banks to perform many necessary functions. However, during the late 1980s, the role of building societies in the U.K. banking environment changed dramatically. First, in addition to their traditional services, building societies began to offer their customers savings, checking, and credit card accounts. Second, they no longer lagged the clearing houses in technological sophistication and they had very low overhead costs relative to the banks. In fact, the cost–income ratio, a measure of bank efficiency, of one building society was about one-third better than that of the major banking clearers in 1989. Third, regulatory reforms in the late 1980s allowed building societies to operate as their own clearer, leaving them no longer dependent on the major clearing banks. In addition to this increased competition locally, the clearing banks in the United Kingdom also faced the possible threat of increased international competition in the future. These dramatic changes in the U.K. banking industry posed major threats to the banks and their profitability.

## Optimality

The dramatic changes in the U.K. banking environment described above put pressure on the major clearing banks to implement dramatic changes if they expected to be competitive and successful in the future environment. Several factors that banks needed to address include:

\- Reducing processing costs: Increasing the internal efficiency of bank operations would help to reduce the cost advantage enjoyed by some building societies over the major clearing banks in the United Kingdom.

\- Increasing customer focus: Increasing competition provided customers with better, and often more cost-efficient, options. Therefore, banks needed to improve convenience and service levels of existing products to meet customer needs, and to understand customer market segments well enough to target new offerings, cross-sell additional services, and determine prices for existing services effectively.

\- Faster introduction of products/services: Increasing competition also placed increased pressure on the banks to introduce new products and services faster in order to retain existing customers and to gain others.

\- Addressing other potential changes in the future environment: The banks would also need to examine the future of the banking industry and to attempt to develop strategies to address effectively potential needs and emergencies of the future environment. In particular, the bank we studied needed a fundamental reexamination of the role branch banking would play in the future. Prior to the dramatic changes in the banking environment, it had considered its branch banking a competitive advantage over the other clearing banks, and a barrier to new entrants.

The changing competition in the U.K. banking environment meant that the bank would have to address these critical issues.

## Strategy Espoused

Bank management decided that it was not serving branch customers, or introducing new products, in a satisfactory manner. Its board felt that their existing systems did not allow them the ability to respond effectively to the changes in the competitive environment. Individuals within the firm agreed that their system “should enable the Bank to be more responsive to customer needs and the demands of the environment.” The bank’s focus in addressing its IT needs was “to build a system driven by the requirements to manage and grow the Bank’s U.K. business in an environment of increasing competition and with a shorter certainty horizon” and “to design the system with an inherent capability to be responsive to accommodating future change easier.” In particular, the bank identified four critical factors necessary to achieve these publicly acknowledged goals. The bank needed: (1) to facilitate relationship banking, (2) to introduce products to the market faster, (3) to provide management with better information, and (4) to reduce processing costs. Management felt that while the first three factors would help the bank better anticipate, and respond to, customer needs, the final factor would help negate the cost advantage enjoyed by many building societies.

Acknowledging the threats of increased competition, the bank proposed a strategy to improve its competitive position in the banking environment; it required the bank to undergo a series of radical changes in the way it did business. However, the strategic reorientation proposed failed to address several critical factors, most notably the future role of branch banking in the United Kingdom. Therefore, although the bank's proposed strategic reorientation implied significant (even radical) changes for the bank's systems infrastructure, it still fell short of optimality as described above. There was limited change in strategy-in-use of senior bank personnel, or executives' view of the image the bank should convey to its customers.

## Strategy-in-Use

The bank's actual response to the changes in the competitive environment included a major reexamination of its strategic technology; £500 million was budgeted to redesign the IT infrastructure at the bank to meet the needs of the changing environment and to accomplish the goals of the proposed strategic reorientation identified above. The redesign included replacing a file-based suite of accounting applications with database management and upgrading branch automation and telecommunications. Although there was superficial agreement throughout the bank regarding the strategy espoused, disagreements were privately expressed within the firm about the specifics of this strategic reorientation. There were several different strategies-in-use held by different individuals within the firm; differences among private and public strategies were never discussed or acknowledged. The existence of these strategies-in-use led to the implementation of changes much less radical than the changes originally proposed. The use of defensive routines and miscommunications by individuals within the bank created an environment where critical factors, causing potential tension and conflict within the organization, were not discussed.

The existence of many different strategies-in-use at the bank was evidenced by the great disagreement among individuals as to the functionality and requirements for its strategic IT infrastructure. At the level of strategy espoused, most individuals agreed that the bank must better serve its customers and do a better job of introducing products to market. However, the individuals' strategies-in-use—what they actually thought those vaguely stated goals meant—differed dramatically, as demonstrated by the inconsistency of the systems requirements across individuals. More critically, there had been no discussion and little attempt to make these goals more concrete or to link them to project design or IT systems.

Predictably, individuals within the bank engaged in defensive routines and other unproductive actions in order to avoid stressful or painful situations (as discussed above). As a result, the bank developed and implemented an IT infrastructure that fell far short of the changes originally proposed. The system actually developed and implemented did not include a customer focus as originally proposed, but instead still had an account focus. In addition, the changes to the IT infrastructure did not address specific issues related to the development and introduction of future products. The project's principal contribution was a simple branch automation system that helped to simplify operations in an effort to improve the bank's cost–income ratio. While the bank spent close to \$1 billion replacing their branch automation hardware and software, the system actually served only one of the key objectives originally proposed: reducing processing costs. Even this implemented change represented the least radical of those originally proposed and did not fully support the desired flexibility to respond to future changes in the banking environment. In fact, the systems implemented helped the bank principally to prepare for the past, not for the future.

## Implications for Risks

The results of the bank's reengineering efforts have been less than satisfactory. The bank's failure to examine their commitment to branch banking, or the future role of branches, has proven costly. In the eighteen months since system rollout, they have reduced the number of branches from 3,300 to 2,500; future reductions in the number of branches and comparable changes in the role of branch banking are likely to emerge. Clearly, it was not necessary to invest in hardware for the branches that have been closed. More significantly, these changes in the use of branches suggest that some fundamental changes will also occur in the way customers use branches; the bank will discover that any changes they wish to make are outside the capabilities of their new branch automation system.

The bank's overconfidence in the effectiveness of entrenched strategies and of entrenched competencies and its culture of subordination and complacency led the bank to produce surprisingly conservative estimates of the future and to produce dangerously conservative strategies (supporting hypothesis 3). In the end, the bank proposed a strategic reorientation that fell far short of optimality but went far past the strategies-in-use and thus far past the strategies and systems eventually implemented (supporting hypotheses 1a and 1b; see figure 5). At first, the gaps between optimality, strategy espoused, and strategies-in-use went unseen as the firm viewed both the reorientation and implementation as successes. Their inability to acknowledge and address these inconsistencies appropriately led to the investment in branches that were soon closed and to the implementation of a system that did little more than serve as a branch banking automation system (supporting hypothesis 4). When the bank eventually realized the inconsistencies between its strategy espoused and its strategies-in-use, it responded by readjusting its strategy espoused back toward in-use. That is, personnel generally did not change what they actually did, actually believed, or actually valued in their daily operations, but instead caused the bank to adopt changes less radical than those originally proposed.

When the problems caused by resistance to change could no longer be ignored, the manager of the reengineering projects was empowered to use incentives and annual bonuses, representing close to a quarter of annual compensation, to force individuals to act in accordance with the public strategy espoused. These actions, while apparently safer than accepting the political risk of attempting to shift privately held strategies-in-use too far, or than accepting the functionality risk of fully ignoring the gap between strategies-in-use and strategy espoused, still proved quite costly to the organization and to senior IT executives. Indeed, a new senior management team is now quite concerned that the recently completed systems, costing hundreds of millions of pounds, provide very limited new functionality, add very little incremental value to either the bank or its customers, and do very little to support new strategic ventures (supporting hypothesis 2b). Just as serious, the actions taken to force implementation beyond the very conservative strategies-in-use have been damaging to the careers of senior IT personnel. Predictably, there has been considerable turmoil and staff turnover within the IT function (supporting hypothesis 2a).

## Bremerton, Inc.

BREMERTON, INC. (BI) $^{3}$ IS A PSEUDONYM OF A LARGE INTEGRATED financial services and credit card firm that experienced significant success prior to the 1980s. However, in the late 1980s, BI determined that its existing systems and IT infrastructure limited its ability to develop and to introduce new products and services and therefore limited its ability to meet effectively the changing needs of its customers. For example, prior to initiation of reengineering efforts at BI, one of the major roles of their systems was to facilitate interbank settlement and clearing, allowing issuers (banks that issued credit cards) and acquirers (banks that bought the paper charge receipts from merchants) to settle up in a timely and cost-effective manner. However, major consolidation in both issuing and acquiring has led to significant changes in BI's environment, which render this and other systems functions less critical to BI's future ability to compete.

## Optimality

In order to respond effectively to its changing environment, BI needed to consider several strategic options. One option available to BI was to focus on cost cutting through downsizing; that is, they could become an internetwork facilitator. Given that most credit card purchases made in New York are done with New York credit cards (e.g., with credit cards issued by, and merchant charge card receivables acquired by, New York banks), and given that authorization is handled by the merchant's acquirer or by a third-party provider, why should BI continue to route these transactions nationally through its processing center? After downsizing, Bremerton's processing center would become involved in the transactions only as an interregional facilitator and only if issuer and acquirer banks were in different regions. Given such circumstances, BI might be better off allowing the major East Coast banks to enter into bilateral settlement agreements within their regions, as is already the case in the United Kingdom. A second option available to BI for increasing its ability to compete would be to focus on creating a new source of revenue. For example, BI could become a value-added information provider. That is, BI might provide stores with information (e.g., statistics on promotions or information on customer preferences) that would enable the stores to perform effective cross-selling of merchandise. As a simple example, if a store recognizes that a particular customer has recently purchased a suit from their store, they might then provide the customer with a discount on his or her next three purchases of shirts; this could be handled automatically, without coupons, and with neither the retailer nor the customer being required to take any action to process the discounts. A third option available to BI was to maintain its current strategy and prepare for a big push in the debit card product. Although not a complete set of options, this brief list provides examples of the issues BI needed to consider and openly discuss when developing its IT project, "Threshold."

## Strategy Espoused

On the surface, there was significant and widespread agreement among personnel regarding the firm's strategy espoused, as publicly voiced by BI's CEO. However, despite appearing specific in his communication, the CEO publicly espoused a vague and ambiguous strategic vision for the future of BI and for the role Threshold would play in that future. The strategy espoused included goals such as becoming global, providing fast, flexible and dependable service, and developing and providing a wide variety of distinct products and services. However, the strategy espoused did not address specifics such as those identified in the preceding section. In addition, the strategy espoused seemed so clear to individuals that they mistakenly assumed that the responsibility for implementing it had already been delegated to groups within the organization. In the end, the CEO's vague and imprecise communication of strategy espoused led to superficial agreement among individuals as they avoided discussing critical details that might surface differences in priorities or objectives and thus might create conflict and discomfort within the firm.

## Strategy-in-Use

Although there was superficial agreement throughout the organization regarding BI's strategic vision, dramatic differences existed among individuals' interpretations of this vision. In other words, there was a wide variance among individuals' privately held strategies-in-use. More important, these differences were undiscussed and undiscusable within the firm. The following provides a brief example of the wide range of strategies-in-use held by individuals.

BI's chairman and CEO, as part of his strategy espoused, stated:

We will be the world's best consumer payment system!

This was heard by all critical decision makers within the organization, and accepted as consistent with their own views for the future of the franchise. However, personnel at BI interpreted the CEO's comment in several different ways:

We will become truly international.

We will offer the best debit-card product, as this is the area of fastest growth.

We will become the low cost transaction processor, and begin processing for more and more organizations.

We will be the best marketer. Processing, settlement, and clearing will become commodities, with no competitive implications.

We will see all the details of our customers' transactions, and will become a provider of value-added information-based services to merchants, hotel operators, and service providers where our customers use our cards. This will increase our transaction processing costs, but will turn transaction processing from an expense into a profitable revenue source!

These interpretations of the chairman's views are not only different, they can be fundamentally incompatible. Reducing transaction processing cost is not compatible with increasing costs to create a profitable revenue source; focus on debit is not compatible with becoming a source of management and marketing information to hotels, airlines, and other participants in the travel industry.

Moreover, as is typical, these dramatic disagreements went unnoticed, unacknowledged, and undiscussed at BI. Most personnel assumed either that the critical issues had already been resolved or that critical changes would not affect their group or their competencies. That is, individuals attempting to protect the value of their entrenched and previously rewarded behaviors failed to perceive their own need to change, while they blamed others for problems with the Threshold project. As a result, they failed to discuss critical issues and even failed to perceive the need to do so. In addition,

One might say, further, that there was tacit collusion among the management group to refrain from surfacing the disagreements and lack of clarity in order to appear to be a “team player.” [10, p. 68]

## Implications for Risks

The chairman's inability to identify clearly a strategy espoused that addressed issues critical to BI's ability to profitably compete in the future created enormous levels of functionality risk (supporting hypothesis 2b). However, the chairman, and his direct reports, thought they had buy-in and agreement on his strategic vision, and they proceeded with reengineering and systems implementation. Unfortunately, beneath this superficial agreement were many disagreements among individuals regarding the future direction of the firm. Not surprisingly, those actually charged with developing a system to implement the chairman's vision found that they had an impossible task (supporting hypothesis 1a). In the absence of clear direction from above, each group took actions to advance its own interests, and actions to counter the actions of other groups that it felt were damaging to its own interests. This atmosphere of defensive routines led to the avoidance of discussion of critical issues and organizational resistance to changes generating inter-group conflict (supporting hypothesis 2a). In an effort to reduce the gaps among strategies-in-use, strategy espoused, and optimality, BI brought in an outside facilitator who attempted to bypass the defensive routines, to force individuals to discuss the undiscussables, and to create awareness of the gaps described previously. Unfortunately, this intervention underestimated the resistance outside the enlightened individual responsible for requesting the intervention and the associated degree of political risk; forcing discussion of undiscussables in this environment merely increased already high levels of political risk. Within two years, both the VP responsible for implementation of this project and the SVP for all systems implementation have been terminated, the EVP for systems and operations has been reassigned, a new chief operating officer position has been created and its occupant changed twice, and the chief executive resigned precipitously.

## Conclusions and Managerial Implications

WE HAVE DEVELOPED A DESCRIPTIVE FRAMEWORK AND A SERIES OF HYPOTHESES to help managers identify the most critical risks associated with IT development and implementation: functionality risk and political risk. These risks are especially severe in reengineering efforts, and we have therefore stressed the implications of these risks for the high rate of failure of strategic business reengineering programs. This framework has also enabled us to make some predictions, in the form of testable hypotheses, concerning when reengineering efforts are likely to succeed and when they are likely to fail. Three case study examples were presented that provided initial support for the framework hypotheses (see Table 1).

We noted that firms were more likely to succeed with their strategic reengineering efforts, and more likely to achieve strategic advantage and become high performing organizations, if they were able to achieve the following strategic alignments:

\- External or environmental consistency between strategy espoused and the demands of the environment;

\- Internal or organizational consistency among public strategy espoused and privately held strategies-in-use and among these various strategies-in-use.

Actions taken to reduce political risk and to achieve internal consistency often result in strategies-in-use that are far too conservative and thus exacerbate functionality risk. While this might appear to protect the project manager, and reduce immediate and short-term political risk, it usually results in reengineering efforts that fall far short of protecting the organization's long-term strategic needs. Moreover, these actions cause firms to adopt incremental and limited change, even in the face of radical change in business environment and the need for radical change in strategy and in supporting information technology applications.
