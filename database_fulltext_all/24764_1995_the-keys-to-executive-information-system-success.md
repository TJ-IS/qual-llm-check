# The Keys to Executive Information System Success

R. KELLY RAINER, JR., AND HUGH J. WATSON

R. KELLY RAINER, JR., is Associate Professor of Management (MIS) at Auburn University, Auburn, Alabama. He received his Ph.D. in management information systems from the University of Georgia. He has published articles in Decision Sciences, the Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, as well as in other journals. His research interests include executive information systems, behavioral aspects of information technology usage, and structural equation modeling. He coauthored Executive Information Systems (Wiley, 1992) with Hugh Watson and George Houdeshel.

HUGH J. WATSON holds the Herman and Mary Virginia Terry Chair of Business Administration in the Terry College of Business at the University of Georgia. He is an author or coauthor of nineteen books, including Executive Information Systems (Wiley, 1992), and over 100 articles. Dr. Watson directs Georgia's Executive Information Systems program of research and is a frequent speaker and consultant on EIS.

ABSTRACT: Executive information systems (EIS) are high-risk/high-return systems, largely because the clientele these systems serve are not only influential in the firm, but have information needs that are very difficult to provide through computer-based information systems. As a result, it is important to understand the keys to successful EIS development and ongoing operation. This two-phase study first interviewed executives, EIS professionals, and vendors and consultants to elicit keys to success. The findings of the first phase rank-ordered the keys to successful EIS development and the keys to successful ongoing EIS operation. The first phase also indicated differences among the constituencies regarding the relative importance of the keys. The second phase of the research surveyed a large sample of EIS professionals to further examine the keys obtained from the interviews. These findings showed underlying dimensions of the two sets of keys, which in turn were used to produce a model of successful EIS development and operation. These factors also provided evidence to support and integrate the existing literature on information systems success.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: executive information systems, executive support systems, information systems success.

TODAY'S EXECUTIVES OPERATE IN TURBULENT ENVIRONMENTS THAT CHALLENGE organizational survival with constant technological, competitive, regulatory, and economic changes. Executives must be able to respond proactively to threats and opportunities and to react quickly to new conditions. In many organizations, this executive challenge is being supported by information provided by executive information systems (EIS). These systems provide “executives with easy access to internal and external information that is relevant to their critical success factors” [25].

Although many organizations receive significant benefits from their EISs $[4, 18, 21]$ , these still are high-risk/high-return systems, as shown by the large number of EIS failures $[17, 24]$ . Not only are EIS prone to fail, they can fail at any point in the system's lifetime. This is why an EIS is best viewed as “an ongoing journey rather than as a destination” $[23]$ .

Important insights for developing an EIS are included in the descriptions of successful EIS [14, 18]. They range from having a strong executive sponsor, to providing timely data, to effectively handling political resistance [10, 18]. While this literature is helpful, especially to practitioners responsible for developing an EIS, it does not provide a comprehensive understanding of the topic. Nearly all of the writings are anecdotal, based upon experiences in a single firm or casual investigations in several firms [14, 18]. Consequently, they lack the rigor associated with academic research and fail to explore potentially important considerations. For example, the keys to success are presented without any sense of their relative importance. The literature also fails to explicitly describe whose perspective is being used. That is, based on their roles in either using or developing an EIS, executives, EIS professionals (i.e., the EIS support staff), and vendors/consultants may view the keys to EIS success differently, yet in meaningful and insightful ways. In addition, the literature does not discuss whether the keys to success vary with the phase of EIS development. Keys may change entirely or shift in importance depending upon whether they are pertinent to the development of the initial version of a system or maintaining an existing one. Because of these factors, it seems appropriate to further explore the keys to EIS success.

## The Conventional Wisdom: What Is Currently Understood about the Keys to EIS Success

THE EIS LITERATURE CONTAINS MANY COMMENTS ABOUT THE KEYS to EIS success. The insights are packaged in a variety of forms: explicit statements of the keys, suggestions as to how an EIS should be developed, and indirectly through discussions of why EIS fail. The various keys can be organized loosely by describing the evolution of an EIS.

An EIS should be developed in response to a specific business need, such as a need to be more responsive to changing customer desires, to improve product quality, or to improve organizational communications $[11, 20]$ . Systems that do not support business objectives are unlikely to succeed.

An executive sponsor who champions the project is crucial [3, 18]. This person should be responsible for developing support for the EIS, allocating the necessary resources, participating in the system's development, and handling political resistance. The executive sponsor assigns the day-to-day development of the system to an operational sponsor [18], someone from a functional area or information systems who understands the business and has a good working relationships with the firm's executives [25].

The EIS support staff responsible for developing and maintaining the system must possess the necessary technical skills as well as the all-important business and interpersonal skills [14, 25]. One of their first tasks is to select the software to use [14]. While a good software choice does not guarantee success, a poor selection can lead to failure [24]. Another challenging and important task for the support staff is to determine the system's information requirements [23]. Executive users must be willing to spend time with the support staff defining the information to place in the EIS.

Because an EIS is data-intensive, effective data management is important. Technical and organizational issues need to be resolved $[1, 2, 3]$ because more time and effort go into data management than into any other activity.

The EIS that is rolled out to users must satisfy several criteria. It should be developed quickly, with a prototyping methodology to capture executive interest while it is still high $[3, 19]$ . The information in the system must be relevant, accurate, and attractively presented $[19]$ . The system must be easy to use, requiring virtually no training, and system navigation should be easy $[3, 5]$ . The response time must be extremely fast $[16, 19]$ .

After the initial version of the EIS is introduced, the system enters the ongoing, or sustaining, phase [13]. Existing applications are maintained, and the system typically adds more applications, users, and capabilities [25]. Users typically demand ever more timely updates of the information contained in the system [10]. There should be a plan for the system's evolution [3, 22].

## The Study

A MULTIPHASE STUDY WAS CONDUCTED TO FULLY INVESTIGATE THE KEYS to EIS success. Its objective was to provide answers to the following research questions:

\- What do executives, EIS professionals, and vendors/consultants perceive to be the keys to success for the initial and ongoing version of an EIS?

\- Do executives, EIS professionals, and vendors/consultants perceive these keys differently?

• What is the relative importance of the keys to success?

\- Is there a model that captures the keys to EIS success?

## Phase One

The study began with a review of the EIS literature in order to develop an awareness of the potential keys to success. Next, structured interviews were conducted with forty-eight persons selected from three constituencies: executive users (18), EIS professionals (18), and vendors or consultants (12). The executive users and EIS professionals (persons responsible for the development and ongoing support for the

EIS) came from seven organizations. $^{1}$ Many of the vendors and consultants had worked with the seven organizations, as well as with many other firms.

The three constituencies were selected because they have a common interest in the success of an EIS but are stakeholders with potentially different perspectives. The authors took this approach because the use of multiple perspectives frequently is suggested in information systems research $[12]$ . For example, the executives are likely to focus on what is necessary in order for the EIS to help them perform their jobs; the EIS professionals will be concerned about the full range of issues; and the vendors/consultants will target those aspects of an EIS that involve their products and services. The executives and EIS professionals typically develop their beliefs on the basis of their experiences with a single system, while the vendors/consultants work with many systems. The keys to success were explored separately for both the development and the ongoing operation of the EIS because it was reasoned that the keys to success may vary during the system's lifetime.

## Phase Two

The second phase utilized a survey to further investigate the keys to success that were generated from the earlier interviews. The survey instrument was initially pretested with five individuals who had been actively involved with one or more EIS development projects and with the ongoing operation of one or more EIS. They were asked to suggest additions, modifications, and/or deletions to the survey. They also were asked to comment on the survey's length and on the clarity of each item. Their suggestions were incorporated into the final form of the questionnaire.

In addition to requesting demographic data on the organization, its EIS, and the respondent, the questionnaire asked respondents to assess the contribution of each key to the successful development or operation of their EIS. Respondents answered each item on five-point Likert scales. The scale points ranged from “1,” meaning “in relation to all other items, will make the least contribution to successful EIS development (or operation),” to “5,” meaning “in relation to all other items, will make the greatest contribution to successful EIS development (or EIS operation).”

Questionnaires were sent to 347 organizations that were known or believed to have an EIS. $^{2}$ The initial and a follow-up mailing generated 149 usable responses (a 42.9 percent response rate). This response rate from an unsolicited mailed questionnaire suggested that respondents found the topic interesting and relevant. Statistical tests were performed to compare the data obtained from the two mailings. No significant differences were found on any variable in the questionnaire, and the authors concluded that nonresponse bias was not evident and the two mailings could be combined for further analysis.

## Findings

PHASE ONE PROVIDED ANSWERS TO THE FIRST THREE RESEARCH QUESTIONS while phase two provided additional insights into the third question and answers to the fourth question.

Phase One

The forty-eight executive users, EIS professionals, and vendors/consultants were asked two questions:

1. What factors do you believe are most important in the successful development of an EIS?

2. What factors do you believe are most important in the successful ongoing operation of an EIS?

The interviewees produced a large number of comments about successful EIS development and operation. The researchers analyzed the interviews to elicit these comments, which are essentially short phrases that often represent entire discussions. In several instances, multiple comments were collapsed into a single key to success because the comments were closely related to one another. However, the researchers were careful to avoid overcollapsing seemingly related comments in order to retain the richness and contextual variety of the interviews. As a result, phase one development and operational keys that appear related may remain in the listings. The inclusion of such keys benefits the study in two ways. First, the flavor of the phase one interviews is retained and the completeness and scope of the questionnaire items (keys) increases. Second, items that appear to be related will tend to group into underlying factors in phase two, thereby increasing the internal reliability of these factors.

The interviewees generated twenty-four keys to the successful development of the initial version and thirty-eight keys to its ongoing success. The relative importance of the various keys was assessed by counting the number of participants who mentioned each key. Tables 1 and 2 show the rankings of the keys from phase one.

The data for the keys for successful EIS development are shown in Table 1. A first impression is that there is general agreement among the constituencies, which is borne out statistically. Kendall's coefficient of concordance allows the researcher to test for the degree of association among several sets of rankings of "n" objects [6, p. 326]. The Kendall coefficient of concordance for the rankings of the keys across the constituencies is 0.9865 ( $p = 0.0042$ ). The null hypothesis for Kendall's coefficient of concordance is that there is no agreement in the rankings across the categories. Therefore, a $p < 0.05$ indicates a significant agreement in the rankings.

There are differences in the rankings, however. The executive users mentioned an average of 3.67 keys, the EIS professionals 4.83, and the vendors/consultants 5.08. It appears that executives have a more limited perspective of what is required for successful EIS development. They either do not recognize or take for granted some of the keys that EIS professionals and vendors/consultants see.

The EIS professionals and vendors/consultants also emphasized several keys that relate directly to their work. The EIS professionals expressed the importance of managing organizational resistance, having a well-qualified EIS support staff, and having good information systems (IS) support. The vendors/consultants stressed the importance of delivering the initial version quickly, linking the contents of the EIS to business objectives, starting with high-payoff applications, using consultants, and creating a benefits statement.

The rankings of the keys found some keys and rankings that were anticipated and some that were surprising. Heading the list of expected keys were executive sponsorship, define information requirements, and manage data. These keys often are cited in the literature. Also high on the list is support from top management. This key too is found in the literature but normally as a reason why an EIS fails rather than as one that leads to success. It suggests that more than just the executive sponsor's support is required.

Cost considerations are perhaps the most interesting highly rated key. The literature suggests that EIS are typically developed by executive mandate without significant cost/benefit analysis. Our interviews found that while benefits are not normally quantified before the development of an EIS, the costs are often estimated. In some cost-conscious firms, estimated costs determine whether and how the EIS is developed.

Two other keys are interesting because they are often cited in the literature but seldom were mentioned in our study: appropriate IS staff and operating sponsor. It may be that it is assumed that the technical skills provided by IS are available in the organization. The failure to recognize the importance of the operating sponsor is more troublesome. It is our experience that the highly successful EIS are managed on a daily basis by someone who has a strong vision for the system, understands the use of information by executives, and knows the business and the firm's executives.

The interviews generated interesting and important observations from the respondents concerning successful EIS development and operation. Table 3 provides a number of them for system development and operation that help put the keys to success in context.

Table 2 lists the keys to success that were mentioned for successfully maintaining the ongoing operation of the EIS. These particular keys focus on the nature of the information provided, how it is accessed, and how it affects executive performance. These keys seemingly presuppose that the factors that are important for the initial version continue to be handled satisfactorily.

The Kendall coefficient of concordance for the rankings of the keys across the constituencies is 0.8509 (p = 0.0052) [6, p. 326]. This coefficient suggests that the constituencies demonstrate significant overall agreement in factor rankings.

Once again, however, there are differences in the factors. On average, the executives mentioned 10.3 factors, the EIS professionals 8.3, and the vendors/consultants 7.6. Although the executives identified the fewest factors for EIS development, they mentioned the most for the ongoing operation of the EIS. Clearly, they are most interested in how the system directly affects them.

The executives placed relatively greater importance on the EIS improving their efficiency and receiving information that is accurate, relevant, current, and convenient to access. The EIS professionals gave relatively high marks to ease of use, access to current status information, and adapting to changing information requirements. The vendors/consultants mentioned relevant and concise information and multiple modes of information presentation.

The top-ranked keys did not provide any surprises. For example, all of the constituencies rated timely information as most important. This finding is consistent with other studies and illustrates the need for executives to keep abreast of current conditions.

It may seem surprising that decision support and analysis was not ranked more highly. The best explanation for this is that relatively few executives do their own number crunching—that is what their staff is for. The information resulting from decision support and analysis may be placed in the EIS, but it is only part of the information made available to the firm's executives.

Few people mentioned improving mental models as a key. For most practicing managers, this is an abstract concept that has not become part of their thinking, which may account for its low ranking. The data collected during the next phase of the study support this contention.

## Phase Two

The findings from the survey are based on responses from 149 firms with operational EIS. This section includes demographic information on respondents, their firms, and the firms' EIS, as well as the results from data analysis of initial and ongoing EIS items.

## Demographics

Respondents represented a variety of industry types, with the highest percentages being finance (27 percent), manufacturing (22 percent), and government agencies (10 percent). The firms were large, averaging \$4.7 billion in assets and \$4.1 billion in revenues. The EIS utilized both vendor software, custom-built software, and a combination of the two. Pilot's Commander EIS and Comshare's Command Center were the most widely used software packages. The average age of the EIS was thirty-six months.

The respondents included 66 EIS managers (44 percent), 40 EIS support staff (27 percent), 22 IS managers (15 percent), 11 IS staff (7 percent), three consultants (2 percent), two executive users (2 percent), and 5 who did not list their position. Even though respondents hold a variety of positions, they have to be actively involved in the actual development and operation of their firms' EIS to be included in the University of Georgia's EIS database.

The respondents reported an average of 19 years work experience, 13 years of experience in the IS field, and 3.25 years of experience with EIS. Ninety-two percent of the respondents stated that they were actively involved in the initial phase of their firm's EIS development project and remained actively involved in the system's ongoing operation.

## EIS Development Items

Arithmetic averages were calculated for each of the EIS development items to determine its mean importance to successful EIS development. Table 1 shows the mean importance, standard deviation, and ranking of each key. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient between the rankings of phase one and phase two is 0.47 (p < 0.01), indicating that the two sets of rankings are directly related. That is, the two rankings are not significantly different [6, p. 300].

However, several keys did differ in importance between the two phases. Two factors, cost considerations and manage system spread and evolution, were ranked highly in phase one but phase two respondents did not indicate that these factors have a major effect on successful EIS development. Two other factors, develop system with users' needs in mind and work closely with executives, were not highly ranked in phase one, but phase two respondents thought they were intrinsic to successful EIS development.

The differences in findings between phases one and two are not surprising for several reasons. First, the findings from phase one reflected answers from three different constituencies to open-ended questions. The findings from phase two resulted from EIS professionals' forced responses to specific, preselected items. Two issues surface here: the sample and respondent makeup differed between the two phases, as did the research methodology. So, naturally, these two issues are likely to produce different results. This two-part study shows the value of using exploratory work to identify and define variables of interest and then using a survey methodology on a wider sample to increase general application and refine the results of the initial study.

Factor analysis is a useful multivariate statistical technique for discovering underlying patterns or relationships among many variables. Factor analysis can condense or summarize the information contained in a number of original variables into a smaller set of new composite dimensions or factors with minimal information loss. These more parsimonious factors often present a clearer picture of the structure and meaning underlying a data set. Accordingly, an exploratory principal factor analysis was employed on data obtained from 149 complete responses to uncover any dimensions underlying the EIS development items.

After each factor analysis, items with loadings greater than 0.35 on multiple factors or with loadings less than 0.35 on all factors were dropped from the subsequent factor analysis. The optimal orthogonally rotated principal component result was an eighteen-item, three-factor solution that accounted for 46.1 percent of the systematic covariance among the eighteen remaining EIS development items. All remaining items demonstrated factor loadings greater than 0.35 on only one factor. In addition, all three factors demonstrated eigenvalues greater than one, acceptable internal reliability coefficients (Cronbach alphas), and were logically interpretable (see Table 4).

The three factors are executive/professional relationship, professional issues, and executive involvement issues. The items describing the executive/professional relationship factor pertain to the working relationship between executives and EIS professionals during the EIS development. The items defining the professional issues factor represent responsibilities and duties of EIS professionals. The items constituting the executive involvement issues factor relate to executive involvement with the EIS development process.

Table 4 indicates the items that comprise each of the three underlying factors of the EIS development items. Using the mean contribution of each EIS development item, the executive involvement issues factor is 4.510, that of the professional issues factor is 3.805, and that of the executive/professional relationship is 3.803. These findings reinforce the conventional wisdom that executive involvement in the EIS development process is of paramount importance. Figure 1 depicts the three factors in relation to the executives, the EIS professionals, and the executive information system.

Arithmetic averages were calculated for each of the individual ongoing EIS items to determine the mean contribution of each item to successful operational EIS. Table 2 shows the mean importance, standard deviation, and ranking of each key. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient between the rankings of phase one and phase two is 0.41 (p < 0.01), indicating that the two sets of rankings are directly related. That is, the two rankings are not significantly different [6, p. 300].

However, as with the EIS development items, several keys did exhibit different levels of importance between the two phases of the study. Two factors, improve efficiency and current status access, were ranked highly in phase one, but phase two respondents did not consider them to make as large a contribution to successful operational EIS. Four factors—access external data, concise information, system reliability, and higher-quality decisions—were not positioned highly in phase one, but respondents deemed that they would contribute significantly to successful operational EIS. The possible reasons for these differences were discussed in the previous section.

## Conclusions

THIS STUDY SET OUT TO EXAMINE THE VARIABLES that contribute to the successful development and ongoing operation of executive information systems. The findings may be extended directly to the literature on successful information systems.

Previous studies of the impacts of user involvement in the information systems development process $[9, 15]$ found that the greater the amount of user involvement in the development process, the greater the chances for a successful system. In particular, Edstrom $[8]$ notes that more user involvement is required for an information system that addresses higher-level, less-structured problems. Clearly, EIS help executives address such problems. The current study supports and extends previous work by showing that EIS need in-depth executive involvement to increase the chances for system success.

A vast body of literature exists on information systems success. In their comprehensive review of this literature, DeLone and McLean [7] propose six dimensions of information systems success: system quality, information quality, use, user satisfaction, individual impact, and organizational impact. Our findings regarding successful ongoing EIS operation confirm their conclusions. The ease of use factor corresponds to DeLone and McLean's system quality dimension, the information quality factor corresponds to their information quality dimension, and the impact on executive work factor corresponds to their impact on the individual dimension. Neither phase of this study generated items that addressed DeLone and McLean's use, user satisfaction, and impact on the organization dimensions. Several reasons potentially explain these omissions. First, interviewees (phase one) and respondents (phase two) may have taken EIS usage for granted. Second, user satisfaction in the literature is typically composed, at least in part, of perceptions of information quality and ease of use. Third, potential impacts of EIS on organizations were not elicited and investigated.

It is interesting that the information systems literature on success seems to have remained divided between information systems development and ongoing operation. The research on successful development typically ends with the beginning of system operation. In addition, DeLone and McLean's [7] work suggests that underlying dimensions of information systems success do not include development issues. The current study combines development and ongoing operation, noting the contribution of each, while supporting the previous literature on each phase.

In summary, the variables that contribute to the success of executive information systems can be categorized as those that contribute to successful EIS development and those that contribute to successful ongoing EIS operation. The most important variables affecting the development process are those that the executives provide through their leadership and continued involvement in the development process. The most important variables affecting successful operation of the EIS are those that affect the executives and their work. Practicing managers should realize that continuous, in-depth interaction with executive users in both EIS development and operation is necessary for these systems to be successful. Clearly, the importance of relating to, and working with, executives outweighs technological considerations.

Directions for further research on the topic of EIS success include the three dimensions proposed by DeLone and McLean [7]; that is, future research should examine actual executive usage of—and their satisfaction with—their EIS. In addition, future research should investigate the impact of EIS on the organization. Research areas could include, for example, the impact on organizational structure, the impact on strategy formulation, and the impact on environmental scanning.

The study has sample size limitations in phase one and phase two that suggest additional research. First, phase one should be replicated with larger sample sizes from all three constituencies to confirm the findings of this study, in particular the answers to the first three research questions. Second, phase two should also be replicated with a larger sample size to crossvalidate the findings of the current study, specifically using confirmatory factor analysis with structural equation modeling software. This replication would ideally include executive users and EIS professionals to allow a comparison between the two groups.
