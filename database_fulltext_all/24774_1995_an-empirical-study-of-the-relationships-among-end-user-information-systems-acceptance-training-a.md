# An Empirical Study of the Relationships among End-User Information Systems Acceptance, Training, and Effectiveness

SANG M. LEE, YEONG R. KIM, AND JAEJUNG LEE

SANG M. LEE is University Eminent Scholar, Regents Distinguished Professor, and Chair of the Management Department at the University of Nebraska in Lincoln. He has published thirty-eight books and over 150 journal articles in the areas of management science, MIS, operations management, and global business.

YEONG R. KIM completed his Ph.D. in MIS at the University of Nebraska. Currently he is a Professor and Chair of the MIS Department at Chung-Buk University, South Korea. His current research interests include end-user computing, client/server computing, and object-oriented database systems.

JAEJUNG LEE is Professor and Chair of the MIS Department at Dong-Seo University, South Korea. His research and teaching interests are in the areas of technological support of world-class organizations, business process reengineering, and information systems architecture design.

ABSTRACT: With the advent of powerful microcomputers and software, end-user computing has become an important competitive weapon for organizations. However, little is known about what factors should be considered in designing end-user training programs. This empirical study investigates the factors that are essential for successful end-user training. The study results indicate both the importance of end-user information system (IS) acceptance and the strong relationships among end-user IS acceptance, end-user IS satisfaction, and end-user job satisfaction.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: end-user computing, end-user training, user satisfaction.

RECENT DEVELOPMENTS IN INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY (IT) have brought significant changes in computer usage. Perhaps the clearest evidence of these shifts in use can be found in the growth of end-user computing $[3, 14, 19]$ . Furthermore, primarily because of the proliferation of microcomputers and the availability of user-friendly software, end-user computing is expected to grow at an increasing rate in many organizations $[12, 14, 18]$ . As end users become more computer literate and end-user computing (EUC) is utilized as a competitive weapon, increased research into the personal and organizational impact of EUC in an organizational context is also required.

A recent survey of senior information system (IS) managers found that organizational learning is the fifth most important IS management issue for the 1990s [39]. Some researchers and practitioners point out that users are an integral part of all IT implementation [25, 49]. Today, more effective human resource development programs, focusing on organizational performance, are required in the end-user education/training area [38, 44].

On the whole, prior research on end-user training (EUT) took a rather narrow perspective, focusing on such aspects as individual differences and specific characteristics of systems. For example, Davis and Bostrom [12] investigated the roles of computer interface and training methods, Bostrom et al. [7] studied the importance of learning style, and Robey and Taggart [42] argued that human cognitive style, task, and format should be considered in designing IS for efficient use. Anderson [2] emphasized understanding the human cognitive learning process and Keller [27] stated the importance of motivation and ability in learning.

However, as Dixon and John [13] argued, the current deep penetration of information technology into the planning and operation of the enterprise, at all organizational levels, requires new considerations for managing the use of technology throughout the organization—such as the evolving integration and interaction of many technologies and their impact on the management structure of the organization. Previously, the assimilation of information systems frequently was not successful. To some extent, this failure can be attributed to the lack of relevant and satisfactory education/training programs provided for end users. Some previous studies on information technology deployment in organizations arrived at the same conclusion: computer-based information systems (CBIS) can have a significant positive impact on an organization, especially on job task performance, if the firm provides adequate education or training services for all levels of end users [38]. White and Christy [48] also point out that basic and advanced training should be integral elements of EUC strategy to enhance efficiency and effectiveness.

Little is known, however, about what factors should be considered in designing end-user training programs. Research focusing on the factors and process of successful end-user training and its effects on an organization has the potential to provide new insights into effective planning and management of end-user training programs, which in turn should lead to enhanced organizational performance.

This study addresses this research need by: (1) developing an extended end-user training model (figure 1) and a detailed end-user training causal model (figure 2), which are useful in examining end-user training-related factors in an organizational CBIS environment; (2) providing field evidence to show how a number of organizations currently provide end-user education/training services for their end-users; and (3) empirically investigating the relationships presented in the proposed causal model.

## Study Model

THIS STUDY ADDRESSES END-USER IS ACCEPTANCE FACTORS within a training/education context by developing an end-user training-related conceptual model that shows explicitly and somewhat comprehensively the causal relationships between end-user training variables in a CBIS environment. It also empirically investigates the significance of the relationships between variables presented in the causal model of end-user training as shown in figure 2. The study model to test is constructed on the basis of D.L. Nelson's individual adjustment model [36] and the interactional psychology perspective [16, 17, 46]. The study model also combines various end-user training-related variables, which came from the prior MIS implementation or organizational innovation literature.

In 1990, a new comprehensive research model of individual adjustment to information-driven innovations was introduced by D.L. Nelson [36]. Her interactional model focuses on the identification of personal and situational factors that affect individual adjustment to new workplace technologies. She states that identification of these factors is important to managers, who should create a work environment conducive to individual implementation of innovation.

The study model in this paper (figure 1) is also based on the interactional psychology perspective. Personality and behavioral scientists have studied the relative importance of person effects versus situation effects as determinants of human behavior. The outcome of this research is interactional psychology [46]. Interactional psychology [16, 17, 33] is an approach to the study of behavior that emphasizes a continuous and multidirectional interaction between person characteristics and situation characteristics. It draws attention to the complex transaction whereby individuals select, interpret, and change situations [46].

In particular, the causal model based on interactional psychology attempts to explain factors as well as process for successful end-user training. According to Terborg [46], the interactional psychology perspective suggests that organizational behavior researchers should focus more attention on personal factors that might be expected to influence the effects of situational factors, and vice versa. In short, interactional psychology emphasizes the importance of considering both the person and the situation as joint determinants of behavior.

Based on the above discussion, a rather comprehensive end-user training (EUT) related causal model (figure 2) is proposed.

Most previous research on end-user training $[18, 35]$ looked at the direct relationships, usually using multiple regression analysis, between the independent variables and dependent success measures such as usage, satisfaction, and other performance variables. One shortcoming of such studies is that the intermediate variables between the independent and outcome variables are not examined and explained.

The suggested end-user training model shows the end-user training implementation process rather comprehensively. In companies where the effects of end-user individual differences and end-user training environment variables are present, the implemented end-user training programs will affect such mediating variables as end-user computing abilities and end-user IS acceptance. The affected mediating variables will then lead to changes in performance variables such as system utilization, end-user IS satisfaction, and further end-user job satisfaction. Especially by including end-user job satisfaction factor which directly related to IS personnel's absenteeism and turnover rate $[31]$ in the model, we attempt to build a more efficient end-user training program.

Although many of the relationships between the variables in the model have been examined by earlier researchers $[10, 11, 32, 37]$ , this extended causal model of EUT is unique to this study. The proposed end-user training models are intended to explain the process by which we can identify how EUT-related variables causally affect other variables, especially end users' job satisfaction, which is regarded as the most important surrogate variable for end-user quality of work life.

The generation of a causal model such as the one presented here is important and useful for understanding the dynamics of the EUT implementation process by which end users' level of IS satisfaction and job satisfaction can be estimated. Once this causal process is understood and the relative strength of the relationship has been identified, strategies to increase end-user IS acceptance, IS satisfaction, and other performance variables can be developed.

An appropriate method for analyzing this kind of causal model encompassing mediating variables is path analysis, which is based on the structured relationships among the variables in the model. The main reason for adopting the path analysis method is to study the indirect effects of the mediating variables as well as the direct effects of the independent variables [15, 40].

## Study Variables and Hypotheses

End-user ability refers to the quality of having sufficient computer-based information system-related skills to accomplish assigned tasks [38]. An organization's desire to improve job performance through increased information system utilization is the primary motivation for the measurement and analysis of end-user abilities. The current increase in end-user computing may support the argument that there will be a positive correlation between system utilization and end users' CBIS related abilities/skills. For the measurement of end-user ability and the specific items of the ability construct, the instrument developed and validated by Nelson and Cheney [38] is employed in this study.

H1: The higher the end-user's abilities, the higher the level of the end-user's acceptance of, or willingness to use, the computer-based information systems.

H2: There is a positive relationship between end-user ability and IS utilization.

H3: There is a positive relationship between end-user ability and end-user IS satisfaction.

H4: There is a positive relationship between end-user ability and end-user job satisfaction.

End-user IS acceptance is the willingness of an individual or a group to utilize information systems. Acceptance is a subjective attitude, and therefore may not be easily measured. In this study, for the measurement of end-user IS acceptance, Davis's instrument [11] is employed because its scales exhibited high convergent, discriminant, and factorial validity, as well as significant correlation with self-reported measures of usage behavior.

H5: There is a positive relationship between end-user IS acceptance level and system utilization.

H6: There is a positive relationship between end-user IS acceptance level and end-user IS satisfaction.

H7: There is a positive relationship between end-user IS acceptance level and end-user job satisfaction.

End-user IS satisfaction is the extent to which users believe the system meets their information requirements $[25]$ . IS satisfaction is assumed to be a good substitute for objective determinants of information system success. Several instruments have been developed to measure user IS satisfaction $[4, 23]$ . In this study, the instrument developed by Doll and Torkzadeh $[14]$ is employed for the measurement of end-user IS satisfaction because their measure of IS satisfaction was developed and validated especially for the end-user community.

H8: There is a positive relationship between end-user IS satisfaction and system utilization.

H9: There is a positive relationship between end-user IS satisfaction and end-user job satisfaction.

In MIS research, system utilization is implicitly defined as either the amount of effort expended by users interacting with information systems, or, less frequently, as the number of reports generated by the information system per unit of time $[47]$ . System utilization or information usage is sometimes viewed as an indicator of system performance. The implication is that if the information system helps improve decision quality, then the end user will use the system. Otherwise, the system will not be used. For the measurement of system utilization in this study, the instrument developed by Bostrom $[6]$ is employed.

Locke [31] defined job satisfaction as a pleasurable or positive emotional state resulting from the appraisal of one's job or job experiences. This study model hypothesizes that information system utilization will lead to higher job satisfaction for end users on the condition that system utilization is voluntary. Hackman and Oldham [21] developed a five-faceted job satisfaction measure while developing their Job Diagnostic Survey. This study employs Hackman and Oldham's job satisfaction measure to determine the level of end-user job satisfaction.

H10: There is a positive relationship between system utilization and end-user job satisfaction.

## Research Methodology

THE FIELD STUDY APPROACH WAS UTILIZED BECAUSE IT PROVIDES both descriptive data on organizations and predictive data for possible relationships among variables within the research model. A sample of eleven large companies (300 or more employees) was selected from the Lincoln and Omaha areas of Nebraska. Managers in these firms who were responsible for end-user education/training programs were interviewed. The structured interview method was employed in order to provide descriptive evidence of the organizations' end-user education/training activities and to identify the actual effects of independent and intermediate variables on the outcome variables.

In addition, in order to make the study results applicable to more situations, data were obtained from small-company (300 or fewer employees) end users as well as those in large firms. Three hundred end-user survey questionnaires were sent by mail to the end users of small business organizations selected from the Directory of Nebraska Manufacturers. Of the 300 questionnaires sent, 61 responses were collected (about a 20 percent response rate).

A total of 236 valid end-user survey questionnaires (about a 37 percent response rate) were collected by asking the company managers to distribute the questionnaires randomly to their end users and by sending surveys directly to end users in small business companies in Nebraska. The relatively high response rate is presumed to be largely due to the initial personal contacts with managers, who agreed to distribute the questionnaires directly to the appropriate end users.

## Results and Discussion

TO TEST THE CAUSAL RELATIONSHIPS AMONG THE END-USER training factors of the model presented in figures 2 and 3, data from 236 valid questionnaires were examined via path analysis. The path coefficients ( $P_{ij}$ ) were calculated and tested for statistical significance.

Of the total ten hypothesized paths in figure 3, four were not significant at the 0.05 level. According to theory of trimming [22, 40], these paths were deleted from the a priori model and a new trimmed model was developed, as presented in figure 4. All of the beta (path) coefficients of the trimmed model were tested and found to be significant at the 0.05 level.

The actual figures of each component effect for both models are summarized and compared in Tables 1 and 2. As seen in Table 1, all the $R^{2}$ discrepancies between the two models' dependent variables are less than 0.05. Therefore, the reduced or trimmed model is assumed tenable and is considered to contain all the important causal information among the variables of the a priori model.

As anticipated, end-user ability alone could not explain most of the variation in end-user IS satisfaction and end-user job satisfaction. Even though ability does not have a strong direct effect on end-user IS satisfaction and job satisfaction, it does have a strong indirect effect via end-user IS acceptance and end-user IS satisfaction.

Figure 3. A Priori Model

Similarly, end-user IS acceptance did not have a significant direct effect on end-user job satisfaction. Instead, it affected end-user job satisfaction indirectly via end-user IS satisfaction.

Statistical testing of the ten causal relationships described in the study model yielded the following statistically significant correlations:

1. End-user IS acceptance was positively related to system utilization (H5).

2. End-user IS acceptance was positively related to end-user IS satisfaction (H6).

3. End-user IS satisfaction was positively related to end-user job satisfaction (H9).

In addition to the above significant relationships, three other significant correlations were found, although they were not quite as strong:

4. End-user ability was positively related to end-user IS acceptance (H1).

5. End-user ability was positively related to system utilization (H2).

6. System utilization was positively related to end-user IS satisfaction (H8).

The statistical findings of this study indicate both the importance of end-user IS acceptance in an end-user training environment and the strong relationships among end-user IS acceptance, end-user IS satisfaction, and end-user job satisfaction.

Although end-user ability had relatively significant correlations with end-user IS acceptance and system utilization, it did not have significant direct effects on end-user IS satisfaction and end-user job satisfaction.

## Conclusions

ACCORDING TO THE STATISTICAL RESULTS, WE FOUND SEVERAL relationships among factors in the study model. The following are some of the important findings that can be implemented in the design of end-user training program.

First, end-user ability did not have a strong direct effect on end-user IS satisfaction. Moreover, the correlation between system utilization and end-user job satisfaction was found to be insignificant.

Second, the statistical findings of this study indicated both the importance of end-user IS acceptance and the strong relationships among end-user IS acceptance, end-user IS satisfaction, and end-user job satisfaction.

These findings suggest an important implication for EUT managers or staff. Even though end users with enhanced skills/abilities use IS more in their job tasks, this higher ability level does not necessarily enhance their IS satisfaction and job satisfaction. According to the interviews we have conducted, most end-user training programs focus on improving end users' technical abilities. The end-user training programs usually do not include components to improve user acceptance. As supported by the statistical testing of the causal model, end-user IS acceptance and end-user IS satisfaction were identified as two major direct causal antecedents of end-user job satisfaction. Thus, end users need to regard the information systems they are currently using and the information provided by the IS as relevant and useful for their job performance as well as efficient and easy to employ, if they are to accept such systems. Consequently, EUT managers and staff should identify end users' specific job performance needs and their job satisfaction factors first, and then provide the most relevant and efficient information systems and the system-related training programs that are most appropriate to the situation.

## Directions for Future Research

ANALYZING THE CURRENT END-USER EDUCATION/TRAINING ACTIVITIES of the participating organizations revealed that (1) overall the end-user training function was not given full consideration, and (2) the resources committed to the function were low across the organizations.

More than half of the organizations surveyed employed fewer than five full-time end-user training staff. On the whole, the surveyed companies' current end-user training function was not fully set up, as it was still in its initial or introduction stage.

End-user training managers and staff were asked to provide their opinions on the factors they thought critical for the successful implementation of end-user education/training programs. They were also asked about problems or difficulties they encountered during the implementation of their end-user training programs. They enumerated the three most critical success factors as: (1) top management support, (2) end-user motivation to use CBIS, and (3) communication skills of training staff. The three most frequently encountered problems or difficulties were: (1) lack of motivation for end-users to use new information systems, (2) difficulty in communications with other department managers, and (3) lack of top management support.

As the above opinion analysis reveals, the current top three issues of end-user training—top management support, end-user motivation to use CBIS, and communications with other department or end users—were believed to be the most critical areas by the end-user training personnel. Research on these three issues has the potential to provide greater insight into effective management of end-user training programs. In particular, investigation of critical success factors (CSFs) that influence end-user training performance is suggested as an important future research area. Factors that most strongly affect progress toward the end-user manager's goals can be identified through analyzing the managers' opinions. The identified CSFs can be used as a means of ensuring successful management of end-user computing.
