# Learning, Working, and Innovation: A Case Study in the Insurance Industry

JOHN C. HENDERSON AND CHRISTINE M.A. LENTZ

JOHN C. HENDERSON is Professor of Management Information Systems and Director of the Systems Research Center at Boston University's School of Management. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Texas at Austin. He is a researcher, consultant, and executive educator, with papers published in journals such as Management Science, MIS Quarterly, IBM Systems Journal, European Management Journal, and many others. Presently his research is focused in three main areas: managing strategic alliances and partnerships, aligning business and information technology (IT) strategies, and valuing IT investments. Dr. Henderson is currently Departmental Editor for Management Science. Prior to joining the faculty at Boston University, he served on the faculty of MIT's Sloan School of Management.

CHRISTINE M.A. LENTZ is currently a doctoral student in the Management Information Systems department at Boston University's School of Management. She has an M.B.A. and an M.S.M.I.S. from Boston University and a B.B.A. from the University of Wisconsin at Madison. Prior to enrolling as a doctoral student, Ms. Lentz was the Manager of Special Projects for Boston University's academic Information Technology department and Director of Information Resources for the School of Management.

ABSTRACT: The worldwide environment in which strategic information technology (IT) initiatives are deployed is changing. Increasingly, these IT investments are intended to produce not only internal operating efficiencies and cost savings but also external market outcomes. The linkage between IT investments and business value is critical in the service sector for industries, such as financial services, transportation, and retailing, where IT is an integral part of the products and services delivered to customers. Using case study research data from six large North American companies and recent theory as our guide, this paper identifies ways in which information systems (IS) organizations can become more innovative by using learning as the bridge between working and innovation. To illustrate the findings, we present data from a case study at Sun Life Assurance Company of Canada, a large, multinational North American insurance company.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: organizational innovation, organizational learning, value management in organizations.

Acknowledgments: We gratefully acknowledge the cooperation of Sun Life Assurance Company of Canada in the conduct of this study. This research was made possible by a grant from the IBM Worldwide Consulting Group.

BY THE EARLY 1990S, THE IS AREAS OF LARGE NORTH AMERICAN insurance companies, in conjunction with their business partners, had automated and restructured their mainline business processes, realizing value in terms of cost savings and efficiency gains [22, 34]. Internal functional departments and lines of business were the primary recipients of these benefits. While IT investments played a significant role in these gains, it remains a challenge to link the IT contribution to business value, particularly to external market outcomes such as market share, profitability, and customer satisfaction [2, 5].

CEOs expect senior IS managers to demonstrate the contribution that IT investments bring to organizational or business unit performance [6]. For example, many insurance companies have invested in laptop computers for their agents. The underlying causal assumption is that the IT investment (laptops and the accompanying changes in IT and organizational infrastructure) decreases the amount of time taken to prepare accurate and complete policy documents, which in turn leads to increased customer satisfaction, client retention, and market share [44]. The success of such initiatives is increasingly defined not in terms of the capability of the technology or even the level of usage but instead by the measurable bottom-line market impact. Understanding this IT-performance link is of paramount importance.

Yet the IT-performance link is rarely clear, direct, or immediate, which makes understanding it a significant challenge $[5, 22, 41, 45]$ . There are a number of key reasons for this: First, strategic IT initiatives, ones that significantly alter the underlying business applications infrastructure, are exceedingly complex $[7]$ . For example, at Sun Life Assurance Company of Canada (Sun Life), strategic IT initiatives generally have worldwide impact, budgets in excess of one million dollars (Canadian), and a two- to three-year time horizon for design and deployment. Invariably they are cross-functional in nature and often impact multiple product areas. Many people participate as members of project teams, steering committees, or project review groups and even more people are eventually affected by the implementation.

Second, strategic IT initiatives are, by their nature, highly customized and unique to the context in which they are employed $[20]$ . Since the late 1980s, Sun Life has implemented at least eight strategic initiatives that range from a client-server-based system for the individual insurance line of business to point-of-sale systems for agents. Such initiatives are unique in terms of the applications affected, the technologies employed, and the people involved.

Third, converting strategic IT initiatives into business value introduces several layers of process complexity $[12]$ . The systems development process itself is complex. Moreover, the processes that establish value (planning, justification), monitor progress, and enable midcourse correction are also complex, involving significant uncertainty and a wide range of stakeholders $[13]$ . Each of these processes directly impacts value attainment. However, we believe, the learning process is particularly critical, and yet it is the least understood value management process $[23]$ .

This process, the manner in which organizations learn, has begun to receive significant attention from both researchers and practitioners [18, 36, 38]: “A learning organization is an organization skilled at creating, acquiring, and transferring knowledge, and at modifying its behavior to reflect new knowledge and insights” [18, p. 80]. Some believe that a major component of any effort to improve organizational performance involves learning [32] and, like most organizational processes, learning is achieved by design, not by chance [18, 39].

Organizational learning provides a bridge between working and innovating $[4]$ . Working includes both the task and the processes for carrying out the task over time. This “unified” view of work implies that managing must include processes for both learning from and enhancing practice (innovation). In the past, innovation has constituted a specialized literature $[35]$ , often separated from emerging areas of study such as organizational learning. In this paper we explore the link between these areas as a way to better understand the link between IT investments and business value.

Using the case study research method, we explore ways in which the bridge between working and innovation can be built. First, we describe the genesis of this research to provide an overall context and to develop our view of learning and innovation based on case study data from six companies. We then explore, in detail, the Sun Life case, providing descriptive data on the company's products, markets, and competitive environment. The next three sections tell the story of working, learning, and innovation at Sun Life, highlighting both successes and improvement opportunities for the IS organization. While we draw our findings across cases, we illustrate the results in the specific setting of Sun Life. The summary offers concluding remarks and some insights for others doing work in this area.

## Background

THE PRELIMINARY FINDINGS ON LEARNING IN THIS PAPER STEM from an ongoing program of research in the areas of strategy formulation [24], strategy implementation [42], and value management [10, 28] sponsored by Boston University's Systems Research Center. Our central thesis is that effective strategy implementation requires an actively managed and adaptive management system that responds to organizational conditions (especially strategies, systemic risk, structure, processes, and people) [42]. A key component in the implementation of business strategy is a value management mechanism. Value management is a system for actively engaging senior executives in the formulation, design, control, and adaptation of business processes by which the firm understands and manages the value of IT investments.

Our initial theoretical foundation for studying value management lies in general systems theory and, specifically, cybernetic theory. Cybernetic theory defines process, which consists of standards of performance, measures of value, a system for monitoring and tracking performance, processes for feeding back the information, and processes for modifying the actions $[14, 19, 25]$ .

Our use of the term “value management mechanism” [23] refers to a structure or system of working, interconnected subprocesses that function together to create value. Each of these subprocesses is in itself a significant management process. Together, they provide the feedback system that enables executives to make sense of their environment and to take subsequent actions. In this context, value management becomes the explicit, adaptive means by which senior managers systematically influence IT investments.

Through an integrated set of four subprocesses, the value management mechanism ensures that IT resources invested throughout the company deliver anticipated and, perhaps, even greater returns $[23]$ . The subprocesses of the value management mechanism, shown in Figure 1, are: formulate value principles; design criteria and measures; deploy tracking and control system; and learn IT–performance link.

In this paper, we examine the learning subprocess in detail. In the context of designing and deploying strategic IT initiatives, organizational learning means that an organization gains understanding of how and why (or why not) an initiative achieved its proposed value. However, learning the IT-performance linkage does not end with understanding; the lessons learned must also be incorporated into practice to realize measurable, market impacts.

Over the last two years case study research and research-focused group discussions on value management that we conducted at companies in North America have suggested an alternative model structure for the value management mechanism (Figure 2). This structural model of value management represents the central role that learning plays as an integrating process linking value management to performance. This model is consistent with emerging theories on organizational learning that reflect antecedents of learning as well as a performance consequence [18, 27]. In particular, we build upon this input, process, output structure to formulate our initial view of the linkage between organizational learning and innovation. Clearly, this paper does not attempt to test this model structure empirically. Rather, we use the model as a lens through which we can interpret the results of our case studies.

Innovation is defined as the implementation of inventive ideas $[9]$ , enhancing practice $[4]$ , and “somehow getting a new thing done” $[37]$ . These definitions imply that innovation, like creativity and learning, results from deliberate activities and processes $[39, 40]$ . The challenge for the IS organization is to capture and communicate the innovations across the company. “The ability of a firm to recognize the value of new information, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends is critical to its innovative capabilities” $[8, p. 129]$ . This theory leads us to describe two innovations at Sun Life—one in the Group Insurance business and one in Pensions.

We view work as the ways in which tasks are accomplished over time $[4]$ . This includes both the task itself and “the ways in which the process of doing the task is structured” $[p. 42]$ . Brown and Duguid propose that work is accomplished through both canonical (formal) and noncanonical (actual) work practices and that the convergence of these practices (or lack thereof) directly affects the resulting learning and innovation. In the context of implementing strategic IT initiatives, we view formal work as the defined governance structure and the standing management committees, and the actual work as the initiative-specific committees that form in response to a particular business need.

Research on organizational learning [1, 29, 31] provided the lens through which we examined organizational learning. Specifically, we explored four attributes as being critical to an effective organizational learning process: (1) target-oriented, (2) context-specific, (3) shared, and (4) routine-based. We present evidence of the existence of these attributes at Sun Life and share our perceptions of how they may enable innovation.

## Method

WE EMPLOYED THE CASE STUDY METHOD TO STUDY the problem of value management for two reasons: the research and theory are at their early, formative stages, and the experiences of the actors and the context in which those actions occur are critical [3, 46]. We collected data from the research sites (four insurance companies and two consumer goods manufacturers) over a two-year period from June 1993 to July 1995. We interviewed senior executives on site at company locations in the U.S. and Canada. Subsequently, we had the voice recordings professionally transcribed and the research team (i.e., the authors) analyzed the content of the transcripts.

Data collection was conducted in three phases. During the first phase, the researchers sent questionnaires to the contact person, the CIO, at the company, requesting general information on the company (e.g., revenues, expenses, IT expenditures), the governance structure (distribution of IT activities between corporate IS, business unit IS, line management, and external suppliers), and the IT function (e.g., budget, number of people, equipment configuration). Questionnaires were completed by the CIO, the vice president for IS Planning (IS Planner), and the heads of IS from two company-selected business units. The second phase consisted of on-site, semistructured interviews with these four informants. During this phase of data collection the researchers also collected internal company documentation, including memos, reports, IS planning documents, and annual reports. The research team assimilated the data and then wrote a draft case report. Phase three of the research design consisted of at least one member of the research team returning to the company to conduct a set of structured interviews with the same key informants.

Case studies are rich because of the contextual nature of the observation and the in-depth knowledge obtained by the researcher [15]. To convey our deep understanding adequately, we will describe one case study focusing on two business units of the Canadian National Office (CNO) of Sun Life—Group Insurance and Pensions. The data were collected on site at Sun Life's corporate headquarters in Toronto beginning in the summer of 1993.

## Environment: Sun Life Assurance Company of Canada

SUN LIFE ASSURANCE COMPANY OF CANADA, which began operations in 1871, is one of the largest Canadian life insurance companies. The parent company along with its subsidiaries serves over four million customers in Canada, the United States, Britain, Ireland, the Philippines, Hong Kong, and Bermuda. Worldwide there are 8,000 employees (excluding agents), of whom 1,140 work in an information systems capacity. Total assets under management at December 31, 1993, exceeded \$88 billion (Canadian) and operating income was \$610 million. The quality of the asset structure is acknowledged by Standard and Poor's and the A. M. Best Company, which have accorded Sun Life of Canada their highest claims-paying ratings.

Sun Life of Canada provides a broad range of financial products and services designed to meet the protection, assets accumulation, and advice needs of their clients. Products and services include life, health and disability insurance; annuities and pensions; mutual funds and savings plans; and trust, banking, and investment management services (1993 Annual Report).

In Canada, Sun Life ranks number one in pensions and number one in sales in force for individual life and group life. This multinational company has stand-alone headquarters in Canada, the United Kingdom, and the United States. These national offices (NOs) are similar to domestic companies in the sense that they have much of the apparatus to operate independently within their territories. Each national office has profit and loss responsibility and is linked to the other NOs through the international headquarters office, located in Toronto, and is responsible for corporate and international interests.

Sun Life and its subsidiaries constitute a leading multinational life insurance and financial services organization. The corporate vision is to meet the needs of its customers through a broad range of financial products backed by quality service. Sun Life's diverse product and service offerings, large customer base, renowned service quality, and strong asset base make it well positioned in the market. These strengths alone offer little comfort in an increasingly turbulent, complex, and diverse competitive, political, and economic environment. For example, in Canada, the changing regulatory environment across the provinces has made it possible for banks, with their substantial asset base, to compete more openly with insurance companies.

The general managers (GMs) of the NOs in Canada, the United States, and the United Kingdom report to the president and are responsible for the business units (product lines and administrative functions) in their territories. In the Canadian NO (CNO), business units include individual life and health insurance, group insurance, and pensions and savings products. Other major areas include IS, finance and administration, human resources, and public relations and communications. The business unit vice presidents report to their GMs and ultimately to the president for achieving value from the IT investments deployed. An assistant vice president for IS manages applications development in each business unit and reports directly to the business unit head.

In addition to the business units, IS activities reside at both the corporate and the national office levels (see figure 3). The vice president for IS (CIO) has responsibility for company-wide IS and reports directly to the president. In the CNO, an assistant vice president for IS manages the information systems and the operations improvement areas. Together, corporate and national office IS constitute the “corporate” IS activities.

In the CNO, customers may buy products such as life insurance, health insurance, and mutual funds from Sun Life. Individuals and families purchase these products from independent agents located throughout Canada. Organizations are serviced directly by employees of Sun Life with products such as group life and health assurance and mutual funds.

In the individual line of business, agents provide personal financial counseling to satisfy customers' preferences for a broad range of insurance and savings products. In the group insurance line of business, both customized or standardized products and services are offered to companies. For example, an employee benefits counselor of a client organization may work directly with Sun Life representatives to tailor product and service offerings specifically to the needs of their own employees. Similarly, the pensions line of business provides a broad range of products and services specifically tailored to customer's needs.

IT support for these key lines of business is extensive and diverse. Applications include point-of-sale terminals for agents, plan administration software for employee benefits offices, electronic data interchange (EDI) for claims submissions, and real-time access to mutual fund information for clients. These systems have been deployed to reduce costs, improve service quality, and improve the competitive position of Sun Life. Similar IT-based initiatives at the corporate level, such as an executive information system (EIS) for corporate officers and new accounting and human resources systems, have been deployed to improve productivity and cost-effectiveness.

Support for such technology-based strategic IT initiatives comes from the highest levels of Sun Life. In the late 1980s, Sun Life made a commitment to replace a significant portion of its legacy systems. This replacement and upgrade of IT (and the related organizational infrastructure and processes) required a substantial amount of technological, operating, and financial resources. The company has systematically and methodically deployed these investments over the last several years and in the process has demonstrated their fiscal ability and technical prowess in this area.

## Business Units in the Study

## Group Insurance

THE GROUP INSURANCE DIVISION SELLS GROUP INSURANCE PRODUCTS and services across the country. Distributed across the country are six claims offices and ten regional sales offices. In support of this business, approximately 900 employees (excluding recently acquired business from another company) work in offices located in Montreal and Toronto, 74 of whom function in an IS capacity.

Group Insurance operates in a highly competitive and fast-paced business. According to the May issue of “Benefits Canada,” they are number one in Canada in group life insurance and number two in group health insurance in terms of 1994 insured premiums.

The group division serves approximately 5,300 companies divided into three business units, segmented by distribution channel—corporate accounts (consultants), group sales (brokers), and small business benefits (predominantly agents); one additional business unit, association, is characterized by type of business. This association unit provides benefits to individual members of various associations and is sold through a number of distribution channels.

Corporate accounts are serviced by about fifteen account executives. Approximately forty group representatives handle the broker business. Small-group (3–19 lives) is predominantly sold through Sun Life agents. Sun Life is prominent in the large case market. The largest portion of their business is in corporate accounts, generally covering cases of 1,000 to 10,000 lives.

There have been recent changes in the senior management of the division. The organizational structure is now separated between sales and marketing, and operations.

## Pensions

The Pensions division products consist of two main components—record keeping/customer service for defined contribution business and investment services. Over the last several years, the division has focused on servicing large clients by continued investments in technology and communications, and by supplying both internal and external investments to the marketplace. To support this strategy, the division has 209 employees, over 50 of whom function in an IS role.

The Sun Life Pensions division is number one in Canada in terms of premiums and deposits (\$813 million, Canadian) and it ranks number one in assets under management (\$6.7 billion).

The Pensions division competes in the transaction-processing portion of the accumulation business. They offer record-keeping services for individuals and these business processes can be very people-intensive. For example, Pensions may receive a \$50 million investment contract for one company, which incurs very little cost. For another company they may receive another \$50 million but may be required to administer individual employee records for the client company's 5,000 employees.

Products and services are sold predominantly to large case buyers (e.g., corporations) via third-party consultants or on a direct basis. Sun Life has sales offices in Montreal, Toronto, Calgary, and Vancouver. The seven salespeople located in each of these offices manage the consultant relationships to ensure a continuing flow of sales opportunities.

Customization, flexibility, and quick response are critical capabilities for the Pensions division. They do not manufacture a product and then go out and sell it. Rather, they have a core set of activities that they tailor to the specific needs of their customers. Commonly, each large corporation is unique and requires a different bundle of products and services.

## Innovation

INNOVATION IS DEFINED AS THE IMPLEMENTATION OF INVENTIVE IDEAS [9], enhancing practice [4], and “somehow getting a new thing done” [37]. The systems development process is inherently innovative [4] and may also include creativity [9, 16]. These definitions imply that innovation, like creativity, results from deliberate activities and processes [40]. The challenge for the IS organization is to capture and communicate the innovations across the company. “The ability of a firm to recognize the value of new information, assimilate it, and apply it to commercial ends is critical to its innovative capabilities” [8, p. 129]. The design and deployment of strategic initiatives appear to be fertile ground for innovation.

## New Products and Services for Pensions' Customers

Top management of the pensions division determined that flexibility and quick response are critical organizational capabilities in the dynamic climate of the financial services industry and, particularly critical, in the market for retirement and savings products. As a result, the IS organization, in conjunction with its business partners, designed and deployed a strategic IT initiative to build a new, wholly integrated applications, hardware, software, and communications platform. After 2,200 person-weeks of work, they had successfully reengineered Pensions' main administration system.

The project steering committee, together with the postimplementation review (PIR) group, discovered that unanticipated benefits had resulted from this initiative. Using both the knowledge acquired during this reengineering process and the capabilities of the new infrastructure, the Pensions division is now positioned to offer their clients new products and services. For example, Pensions split the administration and investment services functions and now offers their customers (large institutions) products and services tailored to the customers' specific needs. If a customer wants Sun Life to administer their employees' benefits and the customer wants to contract with a third party to manage some or all of their investments (such as stocks and bonds), this can be easily and efficiently accomplished. No other competitor offers this type of service. Senior vice presidents at both the division and the corporate level can cite specific times in which organizations became Sun Life customers after seeing a demonstration of the system's capabilities.

## Improved Operating Effectiveness for the Group Insurance Division

The group insurance division of Sun Life found that software purchased for one IT initiative had additional, unexpected capabilities for the business unit and these capabilities may eventually be used company-wide. This discovery was made by the project team in conjunction with an extended PIR. That is, initially the PIR did not recognize the benefit. However, as the division learned more about the product, users provided feedback to the PIR, project steering committee group, and senior management. Thus, the broader, more general capabilities of the software became clear. A component of the software is a dynamic letter-writing capability that produces system-generated correspondence easily and simply through a single package. Based on the reviews and the trial, senior management replaced all letter-writing capabilities throughout the division with this one product. As a result, the division has realized savings in both operations and maintenance expenses (for other software packages that are no longer needed) and personnel time (the new capability simplifies operations).

The next section describes the working environment at Sun Life, highlighting how the formal and actual work practices converge to create a learning environment.

## Working

WE VIEW WORK AS THE WAYS IN WHICH TASKS ARE ACCOMPLISHED over time and these include both formal and actual work practices [4]. In the case of implementing strategic IT initiatives at Sun Life, the governance structure (i.e., the allocation of roles and responsibilities for the IT functions) along with standing senior management committees represent formal work practices. Initiative-specific committees are the actual work practices. In accordance with prescribed theory, we found evidence to indicate that both work practices converge to positively affect the learning and innovation that results. Convergence of the formal and the actual work practices is evident at Sun Life where standing committees, project steering committees, PIR groups, and project review groups are an embedded part of the work environment. Collaboration on strategic IT initiatives occurs in project steering committee meetings and by the more personal and direct contact of individuals across national offices. The evidence for these insights is described in the following sections.

## Formal Work Practices: Roles and Responsibilities

One way to enhance work practice is through a well-articulated governance structure. Structural configurations, appropriately defined, help to reduce complexity and increase rationality $[26]$ . In the IT context, governance specifies the allocation of decision rights for IT activities, such as planning, systems development, and infrastructure, to the various decision makers within the organization as well as to outside vendors and partners $[43]$ . Governance is not concerned with the day-to-day operational decisions but with a distribution of decision rights that is consistent with the logic and perspective of the strategy.

Along with Sun Life's commitment to invest substantially in IT came a restructuring of the IS function. The company purposively distributed applications development responsibility to the business units. Corporate IS operates the large transaction processing systems, backbone communication networks, and shared systems; coordinates IT planning, infrastructure, and security; and manages data administration, training, and consulting. In general, business-unit-specific applications development is the responsibility of the business unit. All other IS activities are the responsibility of corporate IS.

Coordination of strategic IT initiatives is managed through a multileveled structure of standing senior management committees.

\- Senior Advisory Council (SAC): This senior, non-systems-oriented group addresses general management and strategic issues for Sun Life. Sun Life's chairman presides at SAC meetings. Members include the company president, the GMs of the NOs, and the senior vice presidents of Actuarial, Finance, Financial Services, Investments, and Marketing.

\- Systems Steering Committee (SSC): The SSC is the most senior systems group and is chaired by the president. This committee establishes the strategic directions for IS, monitors the value of IT investments, allocates resources for IT initiatives with company-wide implications, sets high-level information systems policies, and provides a forum for discussion and conflict resolution. The SSC includes SAC members, the CIO, the IS planner, and the vice president of Human Resources.

\- Systems International Advisory Council (Systems IAC): The Systems IAC is an international policy body chaired by the CIO and composed of the senior IS managers from the NOs and corporate IS. The council's mandate is to “make the whole more than the sum of the parts” by leveraging IT, sharing knowledge, and fostering innovation.

This governance structure where applications development is distributed to the business units is common in the four insurance companies we studied $[23]$ . However, the multiple layers of active, standing committees are not as prevalent and may well represent an innovation in their own right. This governance structure explicitly recognizes the overlap, economies of scope, and potential synergy among lines of business and national offices worldwide. It provides a means for voicing and resolving conflicts and conflicting priorities and a forum for learning. Finally, it represents a significant resource commitment on the part of senior management; enabling the work practices to support a learning environment.

## Actual Work Practices: Roles and Responsibilities

Recommendations to buy or build major business application software (e.g., software that might be used to administer a significant line of business) are made by the heads of the business units in the NOs that would be using the software. Corporate IS tries to be aware of the business unit IT investment decisions through SSC or Systems IAC meetings and its informal network of business partners. Recommendations to proceed are developed by the business unit heads after consultation with IS resources both within their own business units and with central IS resources within their NO.

Depending on the size of the investment involved or the nature of the technology issues, there may be either consultation or sign-off required from the international corporate headquarters.

Coordination for design and deployment of strategic IT initiatives with company-wide implications is managed by project steering committees and coordinated by the IS Planner with a worldwide mandate. Steering committees are chaired by the senior manager responsible for the IT initiative and composed of the senior business and IS managers involved at the corporate, national office, business unit, and functional levels. For example, the International Accounting Systems Liaison Committee, which is overseeing the design and development of the new general ledger system (GALAXI), is chaired by the company comptroller. Committee members include the comptroller and the heads of IS, finance, and administration of each national office, and the company's CIO.

Since projects (and their steering committees) form in response to a particular business need, they can emanate from any level of the company or area of the business. Some projects may involve changing the IT infrastructure and are not specific to a particular business unit. Below we provide examples.

\- The office of the IS Planner had, through its continuous process of researching emerging technologies, identified client-server as a technology to closely study but had no specific plans for a prototype project. An application using client-server technology was presented in the CNO by the head of the individual life insurance business unit. In response to this request, the corporate IS services group established an international client-server committee whose charter was to build an architecture that will give Sun Life a sense of how the various client-server products will fit together using the individual life application as a prototype. The findings of this committee had broad impact across the company for network management, application development, and database management.

\- When the company wanted to reengineer the core systems used by the individual insurance business units, corporate IS assembled the individual units from the other NOs in an attempt to see if they had similar needs. This international collaboration, coordinated by corporate IS, involved common vendor trips, visits to other companies that were using the products, and joint discussions and evaluations. Corporate IS facilitated a collaborative decision-making process to ensure that, as a whole, the individual life insurance units considered the opportunity for using the same system. Eventually, customized systems, based on the same product, were installed in three out of the four NOs. In the fourth NO a closely related product was installed.

\- Widely used infrastructure investments such as E-mail will be spawned by a centrally based “technology push.” Sometimes the central interest in pursuing this investment comes from intense interest in some other part of the company. The central IS area(s) must make the case for adoption among their counterparts in the central IS areas of the NOs. Ultimately, the business units must also be sold.

## Convergence: Formal and Actual Work Practices

These actual work practices—project steering committees form dynamically based on the work requirements, projects are coordinated by the business manager most directly responsible, and steering committees include the managers most affected—are not new and have been reported in the project management literature for some time. However, several aspects of Sun Life's implementation of these concepts provide a new twist, suggesting that mechanisms to bridge learning and innovation reside in the details of the implementation.

First, the project steering committees for strategic IT initiatives routinely operate horizontally across business units, NOs, and hierarchical reporting relationships. This facilitates Sun Life's strategy of explicitly leveraging its worldwide IT investment. A constant theme is the facilitation role of corporate IS, the use of cross-function, cross-organization teams, and the ability to link these initiatives to a vision or strategy.

Second, these actual work practices dictate that the senior business manager responsible for achieving value from the strategic IT initiative is responsible for the project and chairs the project steering committee. It is that manager's mandate to design and deploy the project to realize the anticipated impacts. Senior business and IS managers directly affected are included as part of the steering committee; steering committees may be composed of more business managers than IS managers.

Third, corporate IS plays an active role in supporting the senior business manager. It provides standard measures for assessing the relative value of the investment (cost–benefit guidelines), a company-wide standard applications systems development methodology (ASDM) for design and deployment of initiatives and corporate guidelines aimed at ensuring that benefits are achieved.

In the next section we describe an attribute-based organizational learning process at Sun Life, highlighting the link to both work practices and innovation.

## Organizational Learning: Key Attributes

LEARNING THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN IT INVESTMENTS and organizational performance is critical to improving operational effectiveness and increasing organizational performance [23]. Yet the link between IT initiatives and business value is rarely clear, direct, or immediate. Organizational learning in the traditional sense, that is, learning from past experience, is difficult because no two events are exactly alike [33]. Similarly, the IT initiatives deployed in an organization are always unique to some degree. Yet, having deployed strategic IT initiatives, the organization may acquire some knowledge and insights that are useful for deploying other IT initiatives. A critical challenge is to gather the knowledge and make it available to those people who have the power to act on it.

Our field studies reveal that companies see merit in the organizational learning concept but employed few specific processes to achieve it [23]. Garvin [18] believes that this quandary is partly caused by learning theorists who portray organizational learning as mystical and utopian and who provide abstract recommendations. Managers need a framework for action from which they can derive distinctive policies and practices for changing the way that work gets done.

Research on organizational learning [1, 29, 31] provided the lens through which we examined the learning subprocess of the value management mechanism. Specifically, we explored four attributes as being critical to an effective organizational learning process. These attributes are not entirely mutually exclusive; in practice they overlap to some degree. Rather these attributes help to guide the implementation of distinctive policies and practices that form the building blocks for organizational learning [18]. "Without accompanying changes in the way that works gets done, only the potential for improving [learning] exists" [18, p. 80]. Below we briefly define each attribute using the available literature and then describe specific examples of each attribute at Sun Life. The attributes and their definitions are provided in Table 1.

## Target-Oriented

Organizations are oriented toward targets $[39]$ : “Their behavior depends on the relation between the outcomes they observe and the aspirations they have for those outcomes” $[31, p. 320]$ . In a similar vein organizational learning is viewed as target-oriented $[20]$ . Targets provide focus for the learning processes and a common frame of reference for discussions. In the learning subprocess, organizations learn how and why (or why not) strategic initiatives achieved their goals $[8]$ .

At Sun Life the focal point of the learning process is the project steering committee. Project steering committees meet on a regular (weekly or monthly) basis to manage, track, and control the strategic initiatives. Steering committees review and discuss current project status reports, cost–benefit analyses, status in relation to expectations, and any critical, project-specific issues. Although these types of processes are common [21], committee meetings are not merely informational in nature. Rather, members play an active role in learning the causes and the reasons for the present status and taking corrective action on the initiatives, as needed. Through this process project steering committee members gain a detailed understanding of the project's costs, a deeper understanding of the application of the ASDM, and a general understanding of the technologies used in the project. Thus, the cross-functional, cross-disciplinary nature of the project steering committees that employ the standards and policies (e.g., ASDM, CBA guide) developed by the corporate IS and approved by the standing committees, focuses discussions on the detailed aspects of the IT–performance link, that is learning.

Another aspect of the targeted nature of Sun Life's learning process is the PIR, which is stipulated in the ASDM. These reviews are designed to provide information that will be used to improve future projects and the development process itself. Nonetheless, even for the very large initiatives PIRs are not always conducted. Out of the six major projects deployed in the last year in the Pensions division, three received a PIR. Yet there is renewed interest on the part of senior management in encouraging project sponsors in the business units to learn from past implementations and use the PIRs as a means of determining whether or not (and how) the company is improving its ability to develop and implement systems. A PIR of the three national offices who implemented securities projects during 1993 was held in May 1994, as was a PIR for the Human Resource system projects. The result of these reviews was a document stating lessons learned. This is not “typical” of post audits. Once again it appears that the convergence of work practices has provided a fertile ground for learning. Without the PIRs, Group Insurance would not have incurred the innovative results they report.

Recently, because of the magnitude of the resources being invested in strategic IT initiatives, the president of Sun Life has mandated an additional learning process to find specific ways in which to leverage the experiences gained, to capture lessons learned from the strategic initiatives, and to acquire an understanding of the results being realized from the investments. The lessons learned and subsequent discussions may include all aspects of the initiative's design and deployment. The review group consists of the president, IS planner, CIO, and the senior vice president of marketing; meetings take place during the implementation phase with initiative sponsors, typically including the business unit head and the GM of the national office. Together these senior managers review the benefits achieved, including project steering committee minutes and PIR reports, in the hopes that this information may be applicable to other ongoing strategic IT initiatives as well as those that may be deployed in the future. The president's direct and personal interest in the review meetings serves both to garner the attention of project sponsors and to focus initiative resources on creating a better understanding of the IT-performance link.

## Context-Specific

Learning is context-specific [29], embedded in the work practices and the conduct of individuals. Organizational learning includes the process of improving actions through better knowledge and understanding [17]. To improve actions and modify behaviors a detailed understanding of the circumstances in which the initiative is deployed is required.

Sun Life's learning phase may be described as context-specific because steering committees, and to a lesser extent the project review and PIR groups, are involved in and informed about the details of the project and the particular context in which it is embedded. Some steering committees meet weekly to review progress and address special concerns. The feedback is context-specific, designed to educate the committees and both senior and project managers on the targets.

It is evident, however, that the project team itself is the most deeply embedded in the specific day-to-day details of the project. Currently there are no mechanisms (policies or procedures) in place that leverage the learning that occurs at the project team level.

Sun Life has attempted to leverage the project steering committee's learning by recording the proceedings in minutes and then broadly distributing the minutes across the company to all stakeholders. For example, the international client-server committee disseminates information to all levels of Sun Life using the company's standard E-mail system. Minutes are designed to record the detailed discussions and reflect specific learning that has occurred during the meeting. It is management's intent that this information be used to modify behaviors or actions of initiative sponsors, steering committees, and project team members.

## Shared

Organizational learning is the result of shared experiences which, although not always wholly accurate, bind the individual experiences into a collective understanding $[33]$ . As this shared understanding grows, it produces learning $[30]$ and, through groups or “communities of practice,” a collective understanding develops that becomes part of the ongoing work practices and serves as a bridge to innovation $[17]$ .

There are several examples of shared learning at Sun Life that we believe contributed to the innovations described earlier in the paper. Collaboration on network planning between Canada and Asia Pacific is one example. Also, a client-server-based application developer's workbench was designed and tested in Canada and then used by both the Canadian and U.S. national offices. Commonly, the development of entire application systems is implemented through shared processes. The SOLAR system, the U.S. individual claims processing system, will be the basis for the Asia Pacific individual system. Similarly, the same base system for securities management is being installed for evaluation in two of the national offices. Each of these initiatives involved research materials being shared, visits to other insurance companies, and employees participating in project team, project steering, and project review meetings.

Corporate IS engages in activities such as researching and pilot testing of new and emerging technologies, hosting issued-based (e.g., security) or functional (e.g., officer-level IS people worldwide) conferences, and producing and distributing papers, policies, standards, and minutes. The office of the IS planner researches and publishes position papers on new or emerging technologies that are likely to impact the business. Conferences are held to communicate policies, share information, and foster interactions. Worldwide IT infrastructure standards for communications (E-mail, wide-area networks [WAN], and routers), PC networks (Novell), and software (Lotus and WordPerfect) help to provide a standard operating environment that is conducive to the sharing of systems and data.

Information is shared across the company through steering committee meetings and minutes, E-mail, internal conferences, policies, and standards. Lessons learned from strategic initiatives are gleaned from project review meetings and subsequently the general findings and opportunities for synergy among the NOs are circulated worldwide. Project review meetings are now being held in the NOs by the GMs in charge with the business unit heads in attendance.

## Routine-Based

Organizational learning is based on routines $[11, 31]$ or “regular and predictable behavioral patterns” $[35, p. 14]$ . An important aspect of a routine, in the context of value management, is that it enables decision makers to concentrate on the content rather than the process of organizational learning; the process becomes second nature, embedded in the work practices of designing and deploying strategic IT initiatives.

At Sun Life, strategic IT initiatives have project steering committees composed of stakeholders from both the IS and the business functions. Members meet weekly or monthly from the initial design phase through the implementation phase. Also, PIRs and project review meetings are becoming increasingly common for strategic IT initiatives. Each of these three types of meetings involves standard processes, procedures, and routines. For example, one project review meeting, typically lasting an hour or two, is convened for each strategic IT initiative during the initiative's implementation phase. Preparation for the meeting consists of the IS planner reviewing the project steering and PIR committee minutes and having discussions with the key people who are involved in the project. From these data the IS Planner prepares advance notes for the review group and takes detailed minutes during the meeting. Later, from the notes, minutes are compiled and general lessons learned are drawn.

Sun Life calls the lessons learned “principles.” The principles seem intuitively obvious at first glance; the company, however, believes that the principles are critical to the success of the initiative. One example of what appears to be “conventional wisdom” is the principle: “If you have a project of any size, you should have an appropriate level of full-time resources and a full-time project manager” (HR learning). While this principle may appear obvious, the actual work practices did not reflect it. In the words of the IS Planner, “some less experienced business managers may try to get by with 30 percent or even 50 percent of their own time or 30 percent of three people’s time devoted to the project, each trying to do some bits and pieces of the project. Also, a less experienced business manager may put someone they can spare on the project as opposed to someone that the project needs or someone who can succeed on the project.” Using this principle, Sun Life has learned that the lack of convergence between formal and actual work practices must be addressed or projects will continue to experience delays in implementation.

Another example of a principle concerns the development and updating of cost–benefit analyses. A critical finding of the project review groups is that business cases need to be updated and reanalyzed at key project milestones, to reassess the project's viability. This principle reflects Sun Life's understanding of the dynamic nature of strategic initiatives and the need for a dynamic mechanism to coordinate these efforts (e.g., value management). These principles also provide the focus and targets for subsequent learning processes.

Using the lessons learned, the office of the IS Planner compiled, published, and distributed project management guidelines. In booklet form, the lessons serve as an implementation guide for project managers and steering committees. The corporate audit group has also incorporated some of the lessons into their procedures and documentation. These documents will now be routinely updated and shared across the company. For example, after compiling the lessons learned from the project review meetings in the early winter of 1993, the IS Planner presented them to the SSC and the Systems IAC. Members discussed the implications of the lessons and suggested ways in which they might be communicated company-wide and incorporated into practice. Following these meetings, the lessons were distributed on the E-mail system to senior business and IS managers worldwide. The lessons, about thirty E-mail screens, were sent out in May 1994.

Organizational learning is an important topic and appears to occur incrementally over time [23]. Using these four attributes, companies can actively manage the learning process to ensure that it occurs by design rather than by chance. The above discussion has sought to highlight some of the activities that can be undertaken to improve learning and shed light on the relationship between investments in IT and realized market outcomes.

## Summary

THE PROCESS OF INNOVATION AT SUN LIFE IS ONE OF continuous improvement and involves several critical components. First, innovation is a natural byproduct and extension of work practices and learning at Sun Life. The company may not have set out to make the IS organizations more innovative. Instead innovation was a second-order effect, emanating from the work practices and the learning processes. Second, innovation required deliberate, thoughtful management processes to bring it to the surface. It did not happen accidentally. Third, dedicated resources, in the form of the IS planner and his staff, facilitated innovation at the corporate level. The IS Planner had a worldwide mandate from the president to produce results. Finally, broad-based communication and detailed documentation were critical and necessary features of the effective innovation processes at Sun Life. Sun Life's emphasis on learning appears to be a main contributor in their ability to motivate and sustain IT innovation.

This case study provides an operationalization of the unified theory of working, learning, and innovation. In doing so it supports the importance of viewing these three separate constructs as complementary and integrated. This exploratory work is designed to develop an understanding of how these constructs may apply to practice in IS organizations and how learning operates in the value management mechanism.

Working encompasses both the formal and the actual routines for designing and deploying strategic initiatives. Sun Life's organizational structure is hierarchical, vertically integrated, and functionally oriented around product lines. However, at an operational level, work is accomplished through a web of cross-functional, multidisciplinary committees. The committees provide a rich environment for learning, and the company has implemented several processes (e.g., convening project review groups, communicating, and publishing lessons learned) to enhance practice.

Brown and Duguid [2] argue that work routines, organizational learning, and innovation are endemic to organizations, embedded in the context of organizational life. These three activities affect each other and are both interrelated and compatible. Learning and innovation are embedded in the daily work practices of business units, functional groups, and project steering committees at Sun Life. Concerted effort by these groups is required to understand and incorporate into practice the lessons learned and to foster the development of innovations.

An explicit management process is required to foster and communicate innovations. In a sense, innovative ideas and practices exist but they need to be harvested if results are to be realized by the organization. This harvesting requires management commitment and specific resources devoted to this purpose. At Sun Life, learning and innovation receive the personal attention of the president and the chairman. In addition, the IS Planner and his staff are dedicated to facilitating, augmenting, documenting, and communicating lessons learned and the innovations that result.

The organizational learning processes and the innovations presented in this paper are part of the company's value management system. We propose that the benefits anticipated from IT investments (e.g., innovation) are marginal unless integrated, dynamic processes exist to actively manage and adapt these investments. Our ongoing research in this area will examine how the four processes of the value management mechanism (i.e., formulate principles, design measures, deploy tracking and control system, and learn about the IT-performance link) operate in organizations to ensure that value is achieved in ongoing strategic initiatives and that the knowledge gained is leveraged in future initiatives.
