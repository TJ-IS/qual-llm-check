# Affective Reward and the Adoption of Group Support Systems: Productivity Is Not Always Enough

BRUCE A. REINIG, ROBERT O. BRIGGS, MORGAN M. SHEPHERD, JEROME YEN, AND JAY F. NUNAMAKER, JR.

For authors' biographical statements, please see the preceding paper.

ABSTRACT: Research shows that group support systems (GSS) have dramatically increased group productivity. However, researchers in the field discovered that users sometimes find themselves feeling emotionally unfulfilled despite exceptionally good results; users report a loss of the affective reward often associated with a challenging meeting where they struggle and succeed. This lack of engagement has been shown to be a cause of user resistance to adopting GSS technology. Team effectiveness may be reduced over time as users seek less-effective but more-gratifying alternatives. This paper frames affective reward as a function of excitation transfer. It describes the development and validation of an instrument to measure affective reward, and presents the results of the first laboratory investigation of the construct.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: affective reward, electronic brainstorming, group support systems, instrument validation.

GROUP SUPPORT SYSTEMS (GSS) ARE AN EMERGING TECHNOLOGY WITH THE POTENTIAL to dramatically increase group productivity. GSS field studies have shown average labor savings of 50 percent to 70 percent, and reductions in project cycle time (days elapsed from start date to finish date) averaging 90 percent [27, 30, 36]. Features such as parallel and anonymous contributions can overcome process losses like production blocking and evaluation apprehension, permitting much larger groups to collaborate than would otherwise be possible [6, 16, 35]. Many studies report higher-quality outcomes and higher satisfaction for groups using GSS (e.g., [3, 10, 11, 15]). However, it is important to distinguish between satisfaction with the meeting product and the affective reward experienced by those who created the product. Affective reward is defined as the positive emotional response sometimes associated with goal attainment. Field observations have shown that affective reward can influence the decision to adopt new technology. The assimilation of GSS into the business routine is now a topic of considerable interest [1].

A great deal has been written about innovation, adoption, and diffusion of information technologies. Much of the existing literature examines these phenomena using an organizational [8, 9, 24], sector [4], or societal unit of analysis [14]. King et al. [19] found that institutions at all of these levels affected technological innovation. Cooper and Zmud [8] argued that characteristics of the user community—job tenure, education, and resistance to change—are important considerations for diffusion of information technology, in addition to characteristics of the organization, task, and organizational environment. Much less has been written about innovation using the individual as a unit of analysis.

Field experience with GSS has suggested a new construct—affective reward—at the individual unit of analysis, that may be critical to understanding the adoption and diffusion of this emerging technology. The issue first surfaced when several executives from a Fortune 500 company used an electronic meeting system for an annual tactical planning meeting. In the five previous years, the meeting had taken three days. With a GSS, the group accomplished its task in under four hours, and all participants agreed that the results were more detailed and of substantially higher quality than in past years. Satisfaction with the outcome was high. Nonetheless, when questioned about how they liked the electronic meeting process, the participants were unenthusiastic. The comments ran along these lines: “It went pretty slowly.” “It left me feeling flat.” “It was a little boring.” “It felt kind of mundane and routine.”

These responses surprised researchers, who were accustomed to enthusiastic reports of satisfaction with GSS outcomes. Further questioning elicited responses like: "I've made my career by being able to pound on the table, talk people down, and ram an agenda through a recalcitrant meeting. Afterwards I feel charged up. It feels like I scored a touchdown. It just doesn't feel like I've won with this electronic meeting system." Despite the productive session, the executives were reluctant to use the technology again. Other GSS users in the field have since voiced similar sentiments; they missed the affective reward, or sense of emotional gratification often expressed by participants after a successful meeting. Thus, potential champions of GSS technology may be dissuaded by low affective reward, leading to slow or no diffusion of the technology.

This paper presents a theoretical model relating affective reward to the diffusion of GSS technology and describes a process of developing and testing a measurement instrument. It then suggests a theoretical foundation for the occurrence of affective reward, and reports the results of early experiments manipulating GSS design and facilitation techniques to impact the construct.

## Theory

PEOPLE EXPERIENCING LOW AFFECTIVE REWARD WITH GSS may choose other, lower-productivity methods in order to strengthen group well-being—methods that engender more desirable patterns of group development [22, 23, 28, 29]. The successful adoption of GSS technology in an organization requires a strong internal champion [18]. Yet, anecdotal evidence suggests that some people who would be likely champions for GSS find it unrewarding. Thus, because low affective reward may be a barrier to adoption of GSS by some groups, it becomes important to reach an understanding of the construct and to determine what role, if any, it may play in group development and diffusion of technology.

It is possible that the affective reward displayed by some participants after rigorous but successful meetings may be an instance of excitation transfer. Excitation Transfer Theory posits that people will attribute whatever physiological arousal they feel to a salient stimulus in their environment, even if that stimulus was not the initial cause of the arousal (Figure 1) [38]. Shachter and Singer [32] found that participants receiving secret doses of adrenaline would attribute their resulting arousal to either rage or hilarity, matching their mood to the behavior of an accomplice in the experimental setting. Subjects in cognitive dissonance experiments were shown to misattribute arousal from counterattitudinal advocacy to a bogus lie detector [34] or to a placebo pill that they believed would make them tense [38]. Participants who became physiologically aroused by running in place misattributed their arousal to anger when placed in situations that predisposed them to aggression, unless they were reminded that their arousal came from running [39].

Table pounding, arguments, and face-to-face confrontations in unsupported meetings may lead to physiological arousal. At the end of a meeting, the participants may interpret that arousal as satisfaction with the process and outcomes, and thus experience high affective reward. GSS eliminates many traditional meeting behaviors that induce arousal—shouting, pounding, and heated debates—which could account for reduced affective reward in people whose normal meeting style is active and confrontational. Thus, the reduction of physiological arousal in a GSS meeting may reduce excitation transfer, leading to lower affective reward, and a lower probability of adoption and diffusion.

## Developing the Affective Reward Instrument

IF AFFECTIVE REWARD DERIVES FROM INCREASED PHYSIOLOGICAL AROUSAL, then one would expect behavioral cues of arousal from those experiencing affective reward. We observed that GSS users who reported high affective reward also tended to manifest such cues. They tended to move into closer clusters (proxemic cues), use louder, faster speech (vocal cues), and exaggerate body movements (kinesic cues) as well as handshakes and backpats (haptic cues) [5]. These cues tended to accompany positive statements about the process and outcomes of their meetings, as in, "We really accomplished something today!" Users reporting low affective reward did not manifest these cues, or the accompanying positive statements. According to Edwards [13], the first step in attitude scale construction is to obtain statements that represent in a particular test the universe of interest. He defines statements as "anything that is said about a psychological object" [13, p. 10]. We interviewed a number of GSS facilitators and observed participants to collect statements that accompany displays of high arousal. We then paraphrased the statements into twenty-eight questionnaire items. Some of the items were rephrased to the reverse of users' quotes. For example, several users said things like, "We won," or "That felt like a win." We paraphrased them into "It felt like we won" (agree/disagree) and "It felt like we lost" (agree/disagree). This was done to include both statements that express favorable feelings about affective reward that would be responded to positively by users experiencing affective reward, and statements expressing unfavorable feelings about affective reward that would be responded to positively by users with low or negative affective reward [13].

We framed twenty of the items as seven-point Likert scales and the other eight items as seven-point semantic anchor differentials. We oriented half the answers to the Likert scale questions so that high affective reward corresponded with a high score and half so that high affective reward corresponded with a low score. We framed some of the questions in a positive tone (e.g., “It felt like we won”) and others in a negative tone (e.g., “It felt like we lost”). The directionality and tone of the questions were approximately balanced across the instrument.

## Validating the Affective Reward Instrument

We collected two datasets to validate the preliminary affective reward instrument; the first to test which of the items appeared to be measuring the affective reward construct; the second to test whether the first results were consistent with a different sample. We collected the first set of data from 175 participants. A preliminary examination of paired correlations showed that one item did not correlate with any other. It was determined to be an ambiguous question and dropped from subsequent analysis. The Cronbach's alpha for the remaining twenty-seven items was 0.93. We conducted an iterated principal factor analysis, without rotation, on the twenty-seven items using squared multiple correlation coefficients for initial communality estimates. A scree-plot of the eigenvalues from the first dataset suggests the data are unidimensional and affective reward can be represented by a single factor (Figure 2). Of the twenty-seven items, twenty loaded heavily (0.5 or better) on the first factor (Table 1) and no item loaded heavily on any other factor.

We conducted the same factor analysis on a second set of data collected from 114 participants. The Cronbach's alpha for the twenty-seven items was 0.93. The scree-plot was nearly identical to that of the first data set. Twenty of the twenty-seven items loaded heavily on the first factor with no item loading heavily on any other factor (Table 1). Of these, sixteen were items that had loaded heavily in the analysis of the first dataset.

We then ran a third factor analysis on the combined data from both sets. This analysis confirmed that these sixteen items loaded heavily on the same factor, and on no other factor (Table 1). The Cronbach's alpha for the group of sixteen items was 0.92. All sixteen items significantly correlated with each of the remaining fifteen items ( $p < 0.0001$ ). Therefore, we concluded that all sixteen items measure components of the same construct and that it is appropriate to combine them into a single affective reward scale. For our instrument to be an effective measure of affective reward, its measures should converge with other measures of affective reward. For example, participants who express a high degree of affective reward in an open-ended question should also score high on the instrument, and those who express a low degree of affective reward should score low on the instrument. The extent to which they correlate provides evidence of convergent validity $[2]$ . We used an open-ended question attached to the original questionnaire to provide an alternative measure of affective reward. The question asked, “Is there anything else you would like to tell us about your experience?” We then distinguished between participants who specifically expressed high affective reward and those who expressed low affective reward. Participants who expressed neither or both were excluded from the comparison. Examples of written participant responses of affective reward included, “This was a very stimulating and interesting way of solving problems. I think this is how it should be in the future. Great job!” and “The process was excellent. We were shipping out ideas that seem to be useful for problem solving techniques. I really think we did a great job as a team.” The following is an example of a participant’s written response expressing a loss of affective reward: “It makes the participants who would be of a high standing feel like very low level production workers, which would, in turn, possibly stifle creativity.” A t-test revealed the affective reward scores for positive (n = 90, mean = 92.58) and negative (n = 17, mean = 68.35) groups were different (t(105) = 7.896, p < 0.0001). Thus, the affective reward instrument possesses convergent validity in that it is in agreement with the measure provided by open-ended questioning.

## Research Hypotheses

ONCE WE HAD ESTABLISHED THE VALIDITY OF OUR INSTRUMENT, we then used it in our first study investigating the causes of affective reward. Although little was known about the phenomenon, we suspected that it might be an excitation transfer effect. As such, we expected to see an increase in affective reward if we induced arousal. We reasoned that it might be possible to induce arousal by putting a group in competition with a mythical “average” group. We reasoned that, as the salience of the comparison to an average group increased, the sense of competition would increase, leading to more arousal, and consequently more affective reward. H1: As the sense of competition increases, the degree of affective reward will increase.

Goal-setting literature shows that groups with a difficult goal are more productive than groups with an easy goal $[20, 21]$ . We reasoned that working toward (and achieving) a cognitively and physically challenging goal might induce physiological arousal which may, in turn, result in affective reward. This line of reasoning led us to our second hypothesis:

H2: As the difficulty of achieving the goal increases, the degree of affective reward will increase.

## Experimental Design

WE TESTED OUR HYPOTHESES USING THE ELECTRONIC BRAINSTORMING tool in GroupSystems®. Electronic brainstorming allows participants to type their ideas into a network of personal computers simultaneously and anonymously. The system distributes contributions to other participants on their screens. Groups use electronic brainstorming to define problems, identify alternative courses of action, identify criteria for alternative evaluation, develop strategies, and many other business tasks [12, 25, 26]. Participants in our experiment used the tool for forty minutes to generate solutions to problems posed in the School of Business task [31, 33, 37], a hidden profile task with high complexity and moderate ambiguity.

In order to establish a basis for competition and goal setting, we developed an electronic graph display which, when projected on a front screen, showed how many lines of text had been contributed over time $[31, 33]$ . The graph permitted experimenters to set goals of varying difficulty, a horizontal line at the middle of the graph was presented to participants as “the baseline”—the performance of the mythical “average” group. The experimenter could assign any value to the baseline. The background of the graph could also be used to instill competition. If users performed at a rate that would not reach the baseline, the background turned black. If they performed at a rate that was on par with the baseline, the background turned gray, and if they performed at a rate that would exceed the baseline, the background turned blue.

## Participants

Two-hundred and seventy-five male and female students in four sections of an “Introduction to Computer Technology” course volunteered to participate and were given extra credit for their involvement. Participants were randomly divided into groups of five and then each group was randomly assigned to a treatment.

## Treatments

We manipulated two independent variables for this experiment: competition and goal difficulty. There were three levels of competition: none, low, and high. The control group consisted of two no-competition subgroups. The first subgroup used electronic brainstorming without a graph. The second subgroup used a graph with neither a baseline nor background color changes. The low-competition groups viewed a graph with a baseline and color changes and were informed of their progress seven times throughout the forty-minute brainstorming session in a very neutral, monotonic fashion, such as, "It's been 20 minutes and you have about 160" or "It's been 20 minutes and the background is gray." The high-competition groups viewed a graph with a baseline and color changes and, received reinforcement of the competition, rather than a dull notice of progress, with the mythical "average" group. Competition was reinforced with statements such as, "It's been 20 minutes and you're less than halfway there. You need to pick it up to be better than average," or "It's been 20 minutes and the background is blue. You are performing like champions. You are an exceptional group."

We established four levels of goal setting: none, low, average, and high. There were two no-goal control treatments: brainstorming without a graph, and brainstorming with a graph but no baseline and no color changes. We found in pilot studies that groups not using a graph averaged 206 lines of text in a forty-minute brainstorming session, so we set the baseline to 210 for the average-goal treatment. We set the baseline at 140 for the low-goal treatment, which is slightly lower than the 145 lines submitted by the lowest group in pilot studies. We set the baseline at 310 for the high-goal treatment, which was slightly higher than the 305 lines produced by the most productive group in the pilot studies.

## Results

## Nuisance Variables

ANOVA TESTS REVEALED NO EFFECT FOR THREE NUISANCE VARIABLES: typing skill, sex, and facilitator (Table 2). There was a small but significant difference in affective reward by task-role $p < 0.039$ but had a relatively small $\varepsilon^{2}(0.039)$ and, consequently, did not pose a threat to our findings. Facilitator, typing skill, and gender all had extremely low $\varepsilon^{2}$ and were not significant.

An ANOVA test revealed no statistically significant effect on affective reward by level of competition $F(2,272)=0.60, p=0.549, R^{2}=0.004$ . The mean scores were similar across competition treatment levels (Table 3).

An ANOVA revealed that there was a statistically significant effect on affective reward by goal difficulty $(F(3,271)=5.21,p<0.002,R^{2}=0.055)$ . However, the effect was in the opposite direction of our hypothesis (Figure 3, Table 4). A Scheffé test revealed differences between both the low and high baseline levels and the control and high baseline levels. There was no interaction effect between goal difficulty and sense of competition $(F(1,268)=0.50,p<0.482)$ .

## Discussion

ALTHOUGH THE INSTRUMENT APPEARS VALID AND RELIABLE, it is clear from our results that we do not yet fully understand the affective reward phenomena. We did not find affective reward increasing with either the sense of competition or the goal difficulty. It may be that a sense of competition does not cause physiological arousal, although experience would suggest otherwise. It may be that creating a stronger sense of competition, perhaps by pitting participants against one another rather than against a mythical group, would generate higher affective reward. It may also be that affective reward is not an excitation transfer phenomenon. This possibility could be tested by having participants walk up several flights of stairs before responding to the affective reward instrument.

Participants experienced less affective reward in the high-difficulty treatment than in the low-difficulty and control conditions. It is possible that we may have set the baseline so high that the participants did not believe they could reach it and, consequently, became demoralized rather than stimulated by it. It is also possible the goal provided the participants with motivation not to fail rather than succeed. This would explain why the control group, not subject to failure demoralization, experienced higher affective reward than the high-baseline groups which failed twelve of fourteen times.

An ANOVA revealed an effect on affective reward by goal attainment $(F(2,272)=10.10, p<0.0001, R^{2}=0.069)$ . A Scheffé test revealed that participants who did not meet their goal had lower affective reward than both the control group and those who did meet their goal. Giving a group a goal they could not meet appears to diminish affective reward, while giving the group a goal they could meet did not seem to improve affective reward.

## Implications for Facilitators

These findings have important implications for facilitators. Field experience tells us that affective reward plays an important role in adoption. Therefore, facilitators must realize what effect their actions have on affective reward. Engaging in high-competition has been shown to result in dramatic improvements in productivity during electronic brainstorming $[33]$ . Clearly, a one-time surge in productivity is not of value if it causes users to abandon the technology. Our findings suggest that facilitators can engage users in high-competition without fear of jeopardizing affective reward, so long as the goal is attainable.

## The Instrument

Only 50 percent (ten of twenty) of the original Likert scale questions were included in the final instrument, while 75 percent (six of eight) of the original semantic anchor differential questions were included. This suggests that anchored semantic differential questions may be more useful than Likert scales for measuring affective reward. It may be that the participants can respond more reliably when asked to express feeling (“I feel great/terrible”) than when asked to express agreement (“I feel great” [agree/disagree]).

The final scale includes questions addressing both the process and the outcome of meetings. The fact that these questions loaded together on the same factor is at least consistent with the possibility that affective reward is an excitation transfer process. Under the excitation transfer hypothesis, both the process and outcomes would be convenient and salient stimuli to which participants could attribute arousal. If the affective reward were engendered by processes and outcomes separately, rather than transferred to them, one might expect process and outcome to contribute differentially, and therefore to load on separate factors. One can easily imagine a participant being satisfied with a process, but not the outcome, or vice versa. This also suggests that affective reward is a different construct than satisfaction.

The authors observed in the field that the groups evidencing low affective reward after electronically supported meetings tended to consist of high-powered, entrepreneurial, action-oriented peers, while groups displaying high affective reward after electronic meetings tended to be either hierarchically mixed or composed of low-key peers. It may well be that affective reward occurs differentially in different management levels or in groups with different goals and values. Further study to investigate these possibilities is warranted.

## Limitations

The results of this investigation may have limited generalizability because the participants were undergraduates $[17]$ . Nonetheless, the reliability of the instrument encourages one to go into the field to see what can be learned among GSS users in industry and government organizations.

We only measured affective reward with electronic brainstorming. GSS is much more than brainstorming, and electronically supported meetings involve much more than silent interactions through the computer. It may well be that results will differ for other tools, and for whole meetings supported by GSS tools.

Finally, this paper focused solely on the face-to-face electronic meeting. Other GSS technology is emerging for geographically distributed meetings, and reports from the field suggest that affective reward may also be a factor for adoption and use of distributed GSS.

## Conclusions

THE AFFECTIVE REWARD SCALE DEVELOPED IN THIS PAPER (see appendix) is demonstrably ready for use in the laboratory and in the field. Much theoretical and empirical work remains to be done, and this instrument provides a starting point. If we can identify the causal relationships associated with affective reward, we may be able to minimize a potential barrier to adoption. We may come to understand why some groups regard the technology as disengaging or unrewarding, and others find the technology exciting or rewarding.

Although a large number of items increase the reliability of an instrument $[7]$ , a sixteen-item instrument may be cumbersome following an intense meeting. Thus, we have selected five items to be used as a short-form instrument. The selection of the short-form items were based on four criteria. The first was that the questions had to be non-task-specific. Some of the questions in the sixteen-item instrument deal specifically with problem solving or idea generation. We realize that not all meetings deal with these processes and that some questions may be inappropriate for certain tasks. We chose items that tended to have lower-than-average deleted Cronbach's alphas, higher variances, and higher loadings. We offer a short form consisting of the following items from the appendix: X9, X14, X15, X19, X21. The five items have a Cronbach's alpha of 0.81. The short-form score has a strong correlation with the sixteen-item score (r = 0.93, p < 0.0001).
