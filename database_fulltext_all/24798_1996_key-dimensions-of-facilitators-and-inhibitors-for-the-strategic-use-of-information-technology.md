# Key Dimensions of Facilitators and Inhibitors for the Strategic Use of Information Technology

WILLIAM R. KING AND THOMPSON S.H. TEO

WILLIAM R. KING is University Professor at the University of Pittsburgh. He has served as President of the Institute of Management Sciences (now, INFORMS) and as Senior Editor of the MIS Quarterly. Currently he is President of the Association for Information Systems. He has published more than 250 papers in the leading journals in information systems, strategic planning, and management science.

THOMPSON S.H. TEO is a lecturer in the Faculty of Business Administration at the National University of Singapore. He holds a Ph.D. in management information systems from the University of Pittsburgh. His research interests include strategic impacts of information technology, information systems planning, and adoption and diffusion of information technology.

ABSTRACT: A field survey was conducted to determine key organizational factors that facilitate and/or inhibit the development of strategic applications of information technology (IT) in business firms. A comprehensive list of potential facilitators and inhibitors for the strategic use of information technology was derived from past research and used as the basis for collecting data from 121 firms. These data were factor-analyzed to determine the key underlying dimensions of facilitators and inhibitors. On the basis of the resulting seven dimensions, stepwise discriminant analysis was used to compare companies that have used IT applications for strategic purposes and companies that have not done so. The results suggest that innovative needs, competitive position, environment, economies of scale, and top management guidance are the most important facilitators, while the lack of IT drivers, the lack of economies of scale, and the lack of innovative needs are the most important inhibitors.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: competitive advantage, factors of information system success, strategic information systems.

RESEARCH ON THE USE OF INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY (IT) for strategic purposes has received significant attention in recent years (e.g., [5, 17, 49]). In the world of practice, the importance of the use of IT for strategic purposes is demonstrated by the results of a survey by Niederman, Brancheau, and Wetherbe [45], who found that strategic planning and competitive advantage remained among the top ten issues facing information systems (IS) managers in the 1990s.

Although there has been significant research on the strategic use of IT applications, existing empirical research focusing on organizational facilitators and inhibitors is mainly anecdotal and exploratory in nature (e.g., [31, 43]). Most studies deal with only a few factors that might facilitate or inhibit strategic IT developments and most use frameworks that are intuitive rather than empirically derived (e.g., [32, 53]).

In this study, we attempt to identify a parsimonious set of factors that may add to our understanding of those things that a company can do to enhance its ability to strategically employ IT. First, we carried out an extensive review of existing organizational, MIS, innovation, and strategy research literature in order to compile a comprehensive list of potential factors that may represent facilitators and inhibitors for the strategic use of IT. From this list, the key dimensions of facilitators and inhibitors are then identified through factor analysis of the data collected from 121 companies. A stepwise discriminant analysis of the data from companies that used IT applications for strategic purposes and from companies that have not done so is then carried out.

## Background

IVES AND LEARMONTH [22] DEFINE AN IT APPLICATION AS STRATEGIC if it changes a firm's product or the way a firm competes in its industry. Sabherwal and King [53, p. 192] define a strategic application more broadly as

one that has a profound effect on a company's success and destiny, by (a) influencing or ‘shaping’ the company's strategy or (b) playing a direct role in the implementation or support of the company's strategy.

Underlying these definitions are the notions that strategic IT applications are being used to gain competitive advantage over rivals, and/or prevent rivals from gaining an advantage.

Since strategic IT applications can have a significant impact on the firm's strategy, and since there is evidence that some firms are much more successful than others in developing them [31], studies of the factors that facilitate or inhibit the development of such applications are of great importance.

Organizational facilitators can be defined as:

a. factors that positively influence the ability of an organization to exploit information resources; information resources include both information technology and information [31], or

b. factors that positively influence an organization's decision to use IT applications for strategic purposes.

Similarly, organizational inhibitors can be defined as factors that negatively influence this ability or those decisions [31].

There have been few empirical studies of facilitators and inhibitors. Most studies concentrate on a fairly small set of potential facilitators. Many focus on only one variety of system. Inhibitors are studied even less frequently. Moreover, few studies have attempted to determine empirically and rigorously the underlying structure of the important facilitators and inhibitors that can aid in our understanding of the situational and process elements related to the development of strategic IT applications.

Various researchers have used intuitive or prescriptive frameworks to study facilitators and inhibitors. For example, Neo's [43] content analysis of firms that use strategic IT applications found that there are ten key dimensions that facilitate the use of strategic IT applications: alignment with business planning, communication between IS and management, consideration of IS role, competitive pressure, internal needs, customer needs, strength in IT, extensive computer facilities, management vision and support, and consultants' recommendation.

Reich and Benbasat's [50] study of factors influencing the success of customer-oriented strategic systems (COSS) proposed different dimensions for factors influencing COSS development, adoption, and competitive advantage. Development was proposed to be influenced by the sponsoring company (including corporate level and IS function characteristics), the industry, and the COSS project itself. COSS adoption, they showed, was influenced by the COSS itself (including its development, functions, and support), the industry, and its customers. Competitive advantage from COSS was influenced by the sponsoring company, the COSS itself (including functions and support), the underlying product supported by COSS, the industry, and its customers.

In a study of factors affecting strategic information systems applications, King and Sabherwal [32] considered three dimensions: the environmental context, the organizational context, and the information systems functional context. Similarly, in a study of facilitators and inhibitors for the strategic use of IT, King and Teo [33] utilized three different dimensions: internal factors, perceived needs, and external factors.

## Objectives

THE OBJECTIVES OF THIS STUDY ARE:

1. to identify a comprehensive list of potential facilitators and inhibitors from prior research and practitioner literature;

2. to identify an underlying structure for facilitators and inhibitors that can serve as a basis for future research;

3. to identify those facilitators and inhibitors that best discriminate between companies that have developed strategic systems and those that have not done so;

4. to provide guidance to researchers and practitioners concerning the situational factors and organizational processes that may facilitate or inhibit strategic IT development.

## Methods

A LIST OF FACILITATORS FOR THE STRATEGIC USE OF IT was compiled from an extensive review of past literature. In compiling the list, we included all facilitators from previous work. A corresponding list of inhibitors can be identified as the absence of factors that make up the facilitators (e.g., if strong market position is a facilitator, then the lack of strong market position may be considered an inhibitor).

Clearly, the development of a list of inhibitors as the absence of facilitators may limit the range of applicability of the results. We decided to use this approach because past research and existing literature do not treat inhibitors nearly as extensively as they do facilitators. This makes the list of inhibitors relatively short. In addition, we felt that in deriving the list of inhibitors from the list of facilitators, we can directly examine whether the absence of a facilitator would necessarily function as an inhibitor. This would provide useful insights about the relative importance of each facilitator and inhibitor.

The initial list of facilitators and inhibitors was jointly reviewed by one of the authors and a doctoral student in order to eliminate or combine repetitive items. From this list, a questionnaire was prepared using a five-point Likert-type scale ranging from “not facilitative” to “greatly facilitative” for facilitators and “not inhibitive” to “greatly inhibitive” for inhibitors. The scale also had a column marked “not applicable” to allow for items that are not relevant to a particular company.

The questionnaire was pretested with seven information systems doctoral students and modified appropriately. It was pretested a second time with seven experienced IS practitioners who were enrolled in a “Management of Information Systems” graduate program. Modifications were again made in the questionnaire, and the final questionnaire was generated. The final list of items used in the questionnaire together with references to source studies are shown in Table 1.

The questionnaires were mailed to a sample of 419 executive M.B.A. graduates from a large northeastern university. The sample was chosen on the basis of job titles. We chose mainly senior-level executives because: (1) such individuals generally hold management appointment in their firms; (2) during the course of his or her duties, such individuals are likely to encounter strategic applications of IT or to be involved in the consideration of such applications; and/or (3) such individuals are likely to be aware of factors that facilitate or inhibit the strategic use of IT in the firm. The sample was not restricted to IS personnel, since we wanted to get an indication of facilitators and inhibitors from various users' perspectives.

The survey instrument provided this definition of a strategic information system (SIS): “IT applications are considered strategic if their use enabled the firm to gain an edge over competitors or prevented competitors from gaining an edge over the firm,” as well as descriptions of various ways in which firms have used IT for strategic purposes. Respondents were asked to classify their firm into either an “SIS” or a “No SIS” category, depending on whether or not they had developed such applications. For purposes of validation, those who classified their firm as having employed strategic IT applications were asked to describe one such prominent application and to answer questions concerning the duration of its use and its degree of success.

The SIS group was then asked to respond to factors that, in their experience, had actually facilitated and those that had actually inhibited the use of strategic IT applications. This assumes that organizations in the SIS group faced some obstacles prior to the development and use of strategic IT applications. The No SIS group was asked to respond to factors that actually had or might facilitate and also factors that actually inhibited the strategic use of IT applications.

## Analyses and Results

A TOTAL OF 143 FIRMS RESPONDED TO OUR SURVEY. Of these firms, fifteen declined to participate and seven questionnaires were rejected because many items were left blank or marked as “not applicable.” Usable responses totaled 121; 55 from the SIS group and 66 from the No SIS group. The final usable response rate was 30 percent (121/[419–15]), which is adequate for exploratory research studies.

## Descriptive Results

Respondents generally held managerial positions in a wide range of industries (see Table 2). Typically, they had more than ten years of job experience. The functional groups of respondents are typically planning, marketing, or IS. For both groups, the size of company was commonly less than 1,000 employees or greater than 10,000 employees. However, the SIS group had more large companies than the No SIS group. This is not surprising since larger companies tend to have more resources to invest in IT. Also, the use of IT for coordination and control as well as to manage interdependence among units becomes a necessity for larger firms.

The IT application described by those who had implemented a strategic application was most often one that is directed at internal operations and/or customers, with less emphasis on suppliers. This is consistent with the results by King et al. [31] and Sabherwal and King [53].

The length of time the reported IT application has been in use ranged from one to ten years. Seventy-eight percent of respondents perceived that their IT application is a success. Sixteen percent of respondents were neutral and the remaining 6 percent of respondents did not claim success. A few of the respondents who were neutral indicated that their strategic IT application is still at an early stage, and that it is too early to evaluate whether their strategic IT application is successful.

## Factor Analysis and Reliability Assessment

In order to determine the key dimensions of facilitators and inhibitors for the strategic use of IT, an exploratory factor analysis was carried out. Since the respondents are from two different groups of firms (i.e., the SIS and No SIS groups), principal component analysis with varimax rotation was carried out based on the combined data for facilitators for both groups. This has an advantage over individual factor analysis for each group in that the ratio of sample size to the number of items is increased, thereby leading to a lesser tendency to produce spurious results. Furthermore, this procedure makes it easier to compare the key dimensions of facilitators and inhibitors between the SIS and the No SIS groups since the same dimensions are used. A similar procedure has been used by Moore and Benbasat [41] who studied both adopters and nonadopters of information technology.

Various authors have given different cutoff values for the retention of items based on the value of factor loadings. Some authors (e.g., [36]) used the cutoff value of 0.35, while others (e.g., [8]) used the cutoff value of 0.50. In order to decide which cutoff value is more appropriate, the first factor analysis was evaluated based on both cutoff values of 0.35 and 0.50. It was found that using the cutoff value of 0.35 eliminated about twice the number of items compared to the cutoff value of 0.50 due to the second criterion which required that we drop items with loadings greater than the cutoff value on two or more dimensions. Since we felt that items should be eliminated gradually, we decided to use the cutoff value of 0.50.

Hence, the results of each factor analysis were evaluated based on the following two criteria:

1. Only items with loadings of at least 0.50 were retained.

2. Each dimension should have a set of items with loadings close to 1 while the remaining items should have loadings that are close to 0. This implies that items with loadings of less than 0.50 on all dimensions or with loadings greater than 0.50 on two or more dimensions were dropped.

We considered using a third criterion of face validity prior to eliminating items but found it difficult to implement partly because of cross-loadings and partly because the factor structures are not very distinct during the early stages of factor analyses. Hence, we decided to follow the above two criteria which have been commonly adopted in previous research (e.g., [36]).

Based on the above two criteria, 11 items were eliminated, as shown in the right column of Table 1 with a disposition of “first factor analysis.” Elimination of items from subsequent factor analyses have a disposition of “second factor analysis,” “third factor analysis,” and so on, in Table 1.

The solution from the seventh factor analysis was stable with 27 items loading onto 8 dimensions accounting for a total explained variance of 73.3 percent. Note that the total explained variance is very close to the value of 80 percent obtained from the first factor analysis, thereby implying a parsimonious result.

In order to ensure that the items comprising each factor were internally consistent, reliability assessment was carried out using Cronbach's alpha. A low value of Cronbach's alpha (say, close to 0) would imply that the items are not internally related in the manner expected. Each item should add to the value of Cronbach's alpha. The values of Cronbach's alpha for six dimensions showed that there were no significant increases in its value from the elimination of any items. However, for the remaining two dimensions, the elimination of an item from each dimension resulted in an increase in the value of Cronbach's alpha. Rather than eliminate these two items mechanically, we evaluated their face validity. We decided to drop one item because of the lack of face validity and to retain the other item, partly because of face validity and partly because its elimination did not increase the value of Cronbach's alpha very much. Hence, one item was deleted as the result of reliability assessment. This item is indicated by a "reliability" disposition in Table 1. With the exception of one dimension, the values of Cronbach's alpha for the remaining seven dimensions are above the recommended value of 0.60 for exploratory research [47].

Since four of the eight dimensions have only two items, we tried to obtain further parsimony by forcing the solution into six and seven factors. The solution with six factors was difficult to interpret because of cross-loadings of some items. However, the solution with seven factors showed that the two items in the factor with a low Cronbach's alpha was absorbed into one of the other factors. Reliability assessments using the seven-factor solution showed that the values of Cronbach's alpha for all the seven dimensions were above 0.60.

We repeated the series of factor analyses using the list of inhibitors to determine whether we would get the same dimensions as facilitators. Prior to factor analyses, we attempted to screen out items with a mean less than 3.0. We found that this eliminated about half of the items. At this point, we felt that factor analyses of the remaining items would not be useful. Even when we did not screen items for a mean less than 3.0 and used all the items during factor analyses, we did not get the same dimensions since different items were dropped (compared with facilitators) at each stage of factor analysis. We repeated the factor analysis for inhibitors by using the final set of items from the final factor analysis of facilitators. We obtained a six-factor solution that is difficult to interpret.

Rather than manipulate the factor analysis for inhibitors to get interpretable dimensions that are different from those of facilitators, we decided to use the same dimensions as the facilitators. This approach would enable direct comparisons between the facilitators and inhibitors, and would provide useful insights about the relative importance of each dimension as a facilitator and as an inhibitor. We computed the Cronbach's alpha for the inhibitors based on the same items in each of the dimensions for the seven- and eight-factor solutions. In the eight-factor solution, the same one dimension with low Cronbach's alpha for facilitators also had low

Cronbach's alpha for inhibitors. However, for the seven-factor solution, all the Cronbach's alphas for inhibitors were close to or above 0.60, indicating that the decision to use the seven-factor solution with similar dimensions as facilitators may be justified.

Table 3 shows the factor structure with the names of the factors being subjectively inferred from the nature of the grouped items, as is commonly done with such results.

The first underlying dimension (F1) of the facilitators in Table 3, called “IT drivers,” comprises items that are IT strengths as manifested by strong IT leadership, strong IT planning, experience with IT, and strong technical support staff. However, strengths in IT are complemented by the results and visibility of IT in terms of the perceived importance of the strategic use of IT and the tangible benefits of IT applications. In addition, adequate knowledge about information assets/opportunities and willingness to explore new ideas are also important IT drivers. The willingness to explore new ideas can result in greater employee participation and suggestions which can in turn lead to innovative strategic IT applications. Adequate knowledge about information assets/opportunities is important in order to effectively respond to and evaluate the feasibilities of new ideas. This dimension has great intuitive appeal and is consistent with past results [25, 31]. The appeal of the use of the complementary factor as an inhibitor is substantiated by Vitale [57] who cautioned that the use of IT without careful consideration of the firm’s IT capabilities, as well as without adequate knowledge to innovate continually, can often have a detrimental impact on the firm’s survival in the long run since it might show the way for stronger competitors with more resources to gain a competitive advantage.

The second dimension (F2) in Table 3 consists of items that relate to “business needs.” Conventionally, IT has often been used to lower costs by improving or monitoring operations, facilitating paperwork, storing and processing information, and providing timely and accurate information for making decisions. Hence, these business needs might make it important for the firm to use IT strategically in order to streamline or reengineer business processes and to lower costs. Conversely, the lack of such business needs might result in a focus on IT for routine automation rather than the strategic reengineering of business processes.

The third dimension (F3) consists of items that relate to the firm's “innovative needs.” Dos Santos, Peffers, and Mauer [15] found that innovative IT investments increase a firm’s value while noninnovative IT investments do not. This implies that firms can often gain a favorable image or reputation by being at the forefront of technology and using new technology to differentiate products or services as the result of the need to be unique and innovative. Firms that do not seek to be unique or innovative may be more likely to adopt a “wait and see” attitude toward the strategic use of IT applications.

The fourth dimension (F4) is made up of items that relate to the firm's “competitive position.” In order for a firm to survive in its competitive environment, it is often necessary to improve or maintain its market position, and its image or reputation. The strategic use of IT can provide the firm with the ability to fulfill these needs. When such needs are lacking, firms may have a greater tendency to maintain their status quo and to make more routine use of IT.

The fifth dimension (F5) consists of items that relate to the firm's “environment.” Favorable market growth, favorable economic growth, and favorable environmental change in industry make it easier to increase investment in IT since resources are more readily available. Conversely, the lack of a favorable environment may make the firm less willing to invest in IT applications.

The sixth dimension (F6) includes items relating to “economies of scale.” Since investments in IT are usually costly, economies of scale can make the strategic use of IT more feasible. The presence of an extensive distribution network can result in greater economies of scale for the strategic use of IT as well as making the use of IT more important for coordination and control.

The seventh dimension (F7) consists of items relating to “top management guidance.” Top management vision and support and well-defined management objectives can be prerequisites for the successful deployment of strategic IT applications. Conversely, the lack of clear objectives, vision, and support from top management often results in poor alignment of IS objectives with business objectives, thereby inhibiting the successful development and implementation of strategic IT applications.

## Discriminant Analysis

Stepwise discriminant analyses of facilitators and inhibitors were carried out to discriminate between companies that have developed strategic IS applications and those that have not done so (referred to as the SIS and the No SIS groups, respectively). The design of the study is symmetrical in the sense that inhibitors are directly derived from the list of facilitators (e.g., if strong IT leadership is a facilitator, then the lack of strong IT leadership can be considered an inhibitor). Hence, the same seven dimensions that were derived as the result of the factor analyses and reliability assessments of facilitators were used to group the items constituting the inhibitors.

Discriminant analysis has an advantage over the t-test in that it compares the two groups in terms of group centroids, thereby taking into account the interactions between the individual variables. Stepwise discriminant analyses were carried out to select the most useful discriminating variables. The stepwise discriminant analysis is a logical and efficient way to seek the best combination of discriminating variables, especially in an exploratory study $[34]$ .

The stepwise procedure continuously evaluates all variables at each step and selects the variable with the greatest discrimination to enter the equation. The stepwise procedure then pairs this first variable with each of the remaining variables, one at a time, to locate the combination that produces the greatest discrimination. This procedure of selecting variables on the basis of the one that adds the most discrimination to those already selected continues until all possible variables have been selected or the remaining variables do not contribute a sufficient increment.

The results of the two stepwise discriminant analyses for facilitators and inhibitors respectively are summarized in Table 4. Because some of the discriminant variables might be correlated with each other, the structure correlations (also known as discriminant loadings) are usually considered more valid than the standardized coefficients in determining the relative power of each discriminant variable [34]. Hence, Table 4 summarizes the relative discriminating power of each significant variable based on the structure correlations rather than the standardized coefficients.

The results of the stepwise discriminant analyses were significant for both the facilitators and inhibitors. The separate stepwise discriminant analyses for facilitators and inhibitors showed that a parsimonious set of dimensions can be developed to discriminate among those firms that have developed strategic systems and those that have not.

Table 4 shows that five dimensions are sufficient for discriminating between the SIS and No SIS groups in terms of facilitators. The five dimensions are innovative needs (F3), competitive position (F4), environment (F5), economies of scale (F6), and top management guidance (F7).

Note that although the structure correlation for business needs is fairly high, it was not selected in the stepwise discriminant analysis procedure. Nevertheless, it is apparent that business needs are more important facilitators for the SIS group than for the No SIS group. This provides a possible explanation why some companies use strategic IT applications while others do not.

In terms of the five significant dimensions, it is interesting to note that the structure correlations for four dimensions (F3, F4, F5, F6) are positive while the remaining one (F7) is negative. This means that the SIS group viewed innovative needs (F3), competitive position (F4), environment (F5), and economies of scale (F6) as more important facilitators than did the No SIS group. Conversely, the No SIS group seemed to emphasize top management guidance items as important facilitators. Previous research (e.g., [20]) has found that successful users of IT have a top management whose vision and strategy include the use of IT. We might infer that one of the major reasons why the No SIS group is not developing strategic IT applications is the lack of clear management objectives as well as the lack of top management vision and support. Another possible interpretation is that firms that have successfully implemented strategic IT applications do not consider top management guidance as important as do firms that have not successfully implemented strategic IT applications. One possible reason is that an IT champion, who might not necessarily be part of senior management, can often lead to successful IT applications despite the lack of top management guidance. This notion is well supported by research on IT champions (e.g., [21]).

For inhibitors, Table 4 shows that three dimensions are sufficient for discriminating between the SIS and No SIS groups. In terms of decreasing discriminating power, these three dimensions are (the lack of) IT drivers (F1), economies of scale (F6), and innovative needs (F3). Note that the IT drivers dimension has an opposite sign to the other two dimensions. This implies that the SIS group identified the lack of economies of scale (F6) and the lack of innovative needs (F3) as more important inhibitors than did the No SIS group. Conversely, the No SIS group identified the lack of adequate IT-related support (drivers) as more important inhibitors than the SIS group. This result is consistent with Harris and Katz [20], who found that unsuccessful users of IT usually do not have the technical skills and infrastructure to use IT strategically. The No SIS group may not be developing strategic IT applications because of a lack of adequate IT-related resources, experience, and commitment.

Note that the structure correlations for business needs (F2), competitive position (F4), and environment (F5) are generally low in the inhibitors analysis, implying that both groups shared similar views regarding their importance as inhibitors.

In addition, also note that the SIS group identified the presence of innovative needs (F3) and economies of scale (F6) as important discriminating facilitators and the absence of the same two dimensions as important discriminating inhibitors. In addition, the SIS group also identified competitive position (F4) and the environment (F5) as important discriminating facilitators. This is perhaps not surprising since industry leaders in terms of market position, image, or reputation are more likely to be larger than other firms. Consequently, they are likely to have resources available to explore strategic uses of IT applications. From the demographic profile given in Table 2, it is apparent that larger companies are more likely to belong to the SIS group as seen by the percentage of firms with more than 10,000 employees. Also, firms that are not industry leaders but that are striving to improve their market position, image, or reputation are more likely to explore the strategic use of IT applications as a potential source of competitive advantage than are nonleaders that are satisfied with their current status.

Previous research (e.g., [12, 25]) has shown that the environment can often encourage or require firms to utilize strategic IT applications in order to survive. It is perhaps not surprising, thus, that the SIS group found the environment a stronger facilitator than did the No SIS group.

The No SIS group also identified management guidance factors (F7) as important discriminating facilitators and the lack of IT-related support (drivers) (F1) as important discriminating inhibitors. This is consistent with Cragg and King's [13] study on motivators and inhibitors for small-firm computing. They found that the strongest motivating factor was related to management in terms of the owners' enthusiasm toward computing. In addition, the strongest inhibiting factor was related to IT in the form of the lack of IS knowledge, support, and so on.

It seems that in order for companies in the No SIS group to develop strategic IT applications, they need not only to build up their strengths in IT, but also to obtain clear objectives, vision, and support from top management. In practice, having strengths in IT might give top management more confidence in successfully leveraging IT for strategic purposes. On the other hand, with clear objectives, vision, and support from top management, the firm may be more likely to increase their strengths in IT through better IS planning, adequate resources, and the training or employment of skilled IS personnel.

## Limitations

THIS RESEARCH IS LIMITED IN SEVERAL RESPECTS. First, ideally the sample sizes should be larger for greater factor stability during factor analyses. Future research using a larger sample can test whether we get the same dimensions. Second, the measurement of inhibitors as the absence of facilitators, as well as the decision to use same dimensions as facilitators, may limit the range of applicability of results. It is possible that other factors might serve as inhibitors as well. This might explain why inhibitors were generally weaker discriminators than facilitators. Third, although we built a discriminant model with the sample, we did not have a holdout sample to confirm the model. The absence of a holdout sample might make the discriminant results more tentative.

## Conclusions and Implications

THIS STUDY EXTENDS PAST RESEARCH EFFORTS ON ORGANIZATIONAL FACILITATORS and inhibitors by identifying a comprehensive list of factors from the literature from which key dimensions for facilitators were empirically assessed using a questionnaire survey. Previous studies commonly only used a small set of factors as facilitators or inhibitors. Furthermore, only users of strategic systems have typically been examined. In this study, facilitators and inhibitors for both users and nonusers of strategic systems were treated by defining inhibitors as the absence of a corresponding facilitator. This facilitated the direct comparisons of the same key dimensions in discriminating between firms that had developed strategic systems and those that had not.

The results of the exploratory factor analysis showed seven key dimensions underlying the facilitators. The stepwise discriminant analysis selected five key dimensions (innovative needs, competitive position, environment, economies of scale, and top management guidance) as the parsimonious set with the most discriminating power to distinguish between the facilitators for the SIS and the No SIS group. Similarly, three key dimensions—the lack of IT drivers, the lack of economies of scale, and the lack of innovative needs—made up the parsimonious set of inhibitors that best discriminated between the SIS and No SIS groups.

It is apparent that differences in perceptions of the relative importance of key dimensions for facilitators or inhibitors exist between the SIS and the No SIS groups. An understanding of these differences may help to explain why some companies are able to exploit IT strategically while others are not.

These results should be of interest to both researchers and practitioners in identifying potentially important dimensions that may facilitate or inhibit the development of strategic systems. Further confirmatory analysis using different samples is necessary toward this end.

The results suggest that firms that wish to enhance their ability to develop strategic IS applications should focus on communicating the need for innovation and change, developing economies of scale and a strong competitive position, and ensuring that top management integrates IS as a key element of the overall business strategy. The focus of these elements, in terms of things that are controllable in the short run, is communications—both up and down—and commitment to IT as a key component of business strategy.

Firms that have not developed strategic systems, but wish to do so, might consider taking appropriate steps to mitigate their inhibitors and to encourage the presence of appropriate facilitators. Specifically, these firms should be willing to explore new ideas and build up their strengths in IT in terms of strong IT leadership, effective IS planning, conveying the potential of IT as the vehicle for tangible strategic gains, and enhancing the experience/knowledge level and level of technical support in IT. Similarly, top management in these firms should provide clear guidance, vision, and support for the strategic use of IT applications.

This research provides the basis for further research such as comparisons of any differences in perceptions of facilitators and inhibitors between various executives within a firm—for example, between business planners and IS executives.

Future research can adapt the key dimensions for study of facilitators and inhibitors for specific types of strategic IT applications, such as interorganizational systems, global IS, EDI, and the like. Whether different facilitators and inhibitors affect various types of IT applications to different degrees can thus be examined. Furthermore, the relative importance of the key dimensions of facilitators and inhibitors during the various stages of development and implementation of strategic IT applications may warrant investigation. For example, Rogers' [51] innovation–diffusion model can provide a framework to examine what key dimensions of facilitators or inhibitors are important in the early stages of adoption, what key dimensions are important during the later stages, and what key dimensions are important throughout the innovation–diffusion process. These findings might further explain why the SIS and the No SIS groups view key dimensions of facilitators and inhibitors differently, since it is possible that the observed differences in this research are due to the two groups being at different stages of the innovation–diffusion process.

Another avenue of future research is the development of a scale of SIS institutionalization—that is, rather than rely on a dichotomous classification of SIS versus No SIS groups, future research could expand it toward a more continuous scale that might be linked to the different stages of the innovation–diffusion process.

In conclusion, this study shows that firms that have successfully developed strategic information systems rely on a strong competitive position, innovative needs, favorable environment, and economies of scale. While these factors cannot be easily changed in the short run, the study also identifies key elements that can be controlled by management to enhance the potential for strategic IS development. Moreover, elements that a firm that has not developed strategic information systems can manage to enhance their potential to do so are also identified.
