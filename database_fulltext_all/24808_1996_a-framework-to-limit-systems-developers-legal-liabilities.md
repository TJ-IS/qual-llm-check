# A Framework to Limit Systems Developers' Legal Liabilities

BIJOY BORDOLOI, KATHLEEN MYKYTYN, AND PETER P. MYKYTYN, JR.

BIJOY BORDOLOI is an Associate Professor of Information Systems and Management Sciences at the University of Texas at Arlington. He received his Ph.D. in MIS from Indiana University, Bloomington. His current research interests include the examination of legal liability aspects of information systems, logical and physical database design issues, software metrics, and economics of information technology. His work has appeared in several journals, including Journal of Database Management, Journal of Information Technology Management, Journal of Information Science and Technology, Journal of End-User Computing, Journal of Information Systems Management, and International Journal of Production and Operations Management.

She has specialized in the impact of computing technology in both the public and private sectors. She is especially concerned with investigating the potential legal liabilities that can impact organizations, systems developers, and the public. He received his Ph.D. in computer information systems from Arizona State University. His current research interests include the investigation of the impact of computing technology on persons and organizations, legal liability issues surrounding computer-based systems, and the use of information technology for strategic advantage. His work has been published in MIS Quarterly, Information and Management, Knowledge and Policy, AI & Society, and Information Resources Management Journal.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: computer systems legal liability, legal aspects of computing, software development, tort liabilities.

COMPUTER-BASED SYSTEMS (CBS), $^{1}$ WHETHER EMBEDDED AS CONTROL DEVICES, off-the-shelf software products, or customized information systems, play an important role in the public and private sectors today. These systems affect virtually every person not only in the United States, but in most countries around the world.

As may happen with new technologies, human injury can occur as a result of defects in CBS. Unfortunately, depending on their severity and/or their rate of occurrence, such injuries could lead to serious legal difficulties for CBS developers. Two of the more tragic examples in the medical area are the Therac–25, a device designed to administer radiation treatment to cancer patients $[26, 30]$ and a patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) pump $[49]$ ; in each instance, alleged programming errors resulted in patient deaths. It is unfortunate that the Therac case was not an isolated incident; rather, injury and death occurred on several occasions in different parts of the United States and Canada. Out-of-court settlements have prevented CBS developers from ascertaining the extent of liability. Bender $[5]$ notes, however, that other legal actions involving the Therac could occur with questions regarding design and testing procedures being predominant among them. Such questions would be asked to determine if flaws in the system development process caused the injury. Further, the threat of injury has caused federal and state agencies and legislatures to consider courses of action to inhibit the more injurious effects of such systems $[51]$ .

It is certain that computer-based technology will continue to form the basis of many new applications in manufacturing, engineering, logistics, operations control, health care, and financial services. How favorably CBS are ultimately viewed by the public and the legal impact on CBS developers, then, will largely depend on risks related to the nature and frequency of their injurious effects and any court actions that may ensue. Therefore, research that would aid in identifying and reducing the causes of injuries attributed to CBS would result in a more favorable public view of these systems and more commercially successful CBS for developers. Ideally, guidelines for developers should make it easier for them to identify such risks during the development process. In addition, positive actions to take, such as those dealing with testing and warnings, would also be recognized. The results of these actions should aid developers regarding proposed systems as well as in choosing appropriate and necessary actions to take pertaining to existing systems—in each instance to avoid both injury and costly lawsuits.

When a harmful impact is realized, restitution for an injury has, for the most part, been established in the courts through common law actions and/or by statutes and regulations. Basically, this means that if a defective system is determined to be the cause of injury, then the injured party can bring legal actions against parties involved in its development and use. Until substantive actions are undertaken by CBS developers that will help curb shoddy development standards and/or system misuse, the courts, through their rulings, will $[48]$ . Samuelson $[43]$ emphasizes this point, noting that in the absence of self-regulation that promotes high-quality software development in the CBS industry, lawsuits will establish liability standards and, thus, regulate the industry.

Mykytyn, Mykytyn, and Slinkman [36] took a step toward addressing CBS development and associated legal difficulties with the publication of their framework for product growth and legal impact. It presented a set of precautionary measures that can be taken during product development to limit potential liabilities arising from system use and to avoid legal difficulties. This paper builds on their work by providing CBS developers with more of a micro-based view of the systems development process from the legal perspective. Specifically, we propose a framework to assess the likelihood of injury, and we provide measures to take to reduce developers' legal liability. Legal cases are cited to provide appropriate foundation. In addition, we feel that certain cases cited in the paper are extremely important to the domain of CBS development, either directly or indirectly, as possible precedent setting. Accordingly, we have included a more in-depth review of selected cases in appendix B. This review provides the reader with the complete legal citation, the court(s) involved in the case, and relevant dates. It must also be recognized that, although one court may decide a case in a particular way, this does not mean that all courts would decide similarly in other cases. Thus, one court's ruling does not imply unanimity among all courts. At the same time, CBS developers should not disregard any court's findings, even one.

The legal issues addressed in this paper are limited to the torts of negligence and strict products liability, hereinafter referred to as strict liability, because they are common grounds for civil suits and because legal actions based on them could seriously affect CBS developers. As these issues are addressed and applied to our framework, legal terminology may be presented that may be unfamiliar to those not trained in the law. Accordingly, we have included a glossary of terms, based in part on Gifis's Law Dictionary [19], to assist the reader. We have also italicized the first occurrence of those terms so the reader may know that further information and explanations about the terminology can be found in the glossary.

The rest of the paper is organized in three sections. First, we discuss strict liability and negligence and relate these torts to the CBS environment. Next, the framework for injury assessment and reduction of legal liability is presented to provide CBS developers with alternatives to preclude tort actions against them. Included in the discussion are measures to be taken to comply with court-mandated obligations along with issues to be investigated by CBS researchers. The paper concludes with a brief discussion and suggestions for further research.

## Torts

WE STATED IN THE INTRODUCTORY PARAGRAPHS THAT WE WOULD RESTRICT this paper to legal remedies based on tort theories of liability. Thus, our discussion does not address problems and issues relating to breaches of contract, warranties, disclaimers, and the like, provided for under provisions of the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC). Although contracts and contractual relationships can present CBS developers with considerable difficulties, numerous cases spanning more than two decades have been adjudicated in the courts. These provide precedents, directions, and actions that developers can take to minimize liability based on contract law. Developers should understand, however, that tort actions and the UCC have a bearing on the distribution of risk among all parties. For example, a manufacturer of air traffic control systems might insist on an indemnity clause in the contractual agreements with designers, thereby minimizing its risk.

## Strict Liability

This theory of liability holds manufacturers as well as everyone else in the chain of distribution of the product liable, without regard to fault, for physical harm or injury to a person or their property [31]. $^{2}$ It is just as important to note that this liability can arise notwithstanding the fact that all possible care has been taken by the manufacturer in the preparation and sale of the product [46]. Section 402A of the Restatement (Second) of Torts [39] is the basis for strict liability.

Relevant to this theory, a system must be a product, since services are not applicable to this type of action, and it must be defective. To be defective, the system must be shown to be unreasonably dangerous in its current state, in that it fails to perform in a manner that is reasonably expected by the ordinary user. In addition, the defect must be the source of the injury, and the product must reach the injured person without substantial alteration. It is assumed for this discussion that CBS include software and hardware components that would be treated as goods, making them subject to the UCC as well. As such, it increases the likelihood that CBS would also be characterized as a good as far as strict liability is concerned $[31]$ .

Birnbaum [6] asserts that some courts have held that computer-based programs are products if they are mass distributed, mass produced, and mass marketed. If a CBS developer, through marketing and distribution activities, places the CBS in the “stream of commerce” where it is made available to the general public, it could be considered a product [11]. One-of-a-kind systems or CBS that clearly provide a service, such as investment advice programs [21], would usually not be considered products. CBS that fall between these two extremes present uncertainties for the courts. Referring to some types of expert systems, for example, Turley [48] indicates that courts may base product or service decisions on policies that pay particular attention to analogous case law. Applicable policies include the overall importance of new development of the CBS in question, the societal benefit, the harm to the plaintiff, and the ability of the CBS industry to regulate itself. Of course, such uncertainty in the courts only adds to the uncertainty that CBS developers might experience.

Birnbaum [6] notes that both design and manufacturing defects can lead to strict liability actions. A design defect can occur when the system is developed according to plan but a flaw in the plan renders the system unreasonably dangerous. For example, medical CBS designed to recommend medication would contain a design defect if the system failed to consider any adverse consequences associated with taking two or more medications concurrently. With design defects the courts will examine the possibility and value of using alternative designs that might be available at the time of development [8]. A manufacturing defect, on the other hand, would result from the incorrect implementation of a safe design. A programmer who failed to carry out a designer's instructions with such failure leading to an injury is an example of a manufacturing defect. This perspective is also supported by Haggendorf [21].

With respect to CBS, the types of defects that the court will most likely examine would be those classified as design defects and warning or labeling defects [6]. Madden [33] and Birnbaum [6] indicate that products may also be considered unreasonably dangerous, and thus defective, due to a lack of adequate instructions and/or warnings. CBS with these types of defects are prime candidates for strict liability actions [20].

Desilets [14] addresses another perspective that is extremely relevant today—computer viruses. Such defects could obviously be extremely dangerous—for example, interrupting an air traffic control system with even an unobtrusive greeting at the wrong moment could be life-threatening. Other aspects of strict liability and CBS have been discussed by Nycum [37], Gemignani [18], Tyde [49], and Samuelson [43]. In addition, Birnbaum [6] provides CBS developers and others with a substantial number of measures to take to minimize the chances for injury to occur. They would also minimize adverse legal actions being initiated against developers.

Three court cases are discussed below to illustrate strict liability actions. In Aetna Casualty and Surety v. Jeppesen & Co. [1], an alleged defect in an instrument-approach chart designed to aid pilots in making instrument approaches to airports contributed to an airplane crash. Although the chart contained accurate information, the court found that it was not safe for its intended use because the graphic presentation of the information was misleading relative to scaling of the data. The defect in the product was thus a design defect, leading to a strict liability cause of action. Those developing graphically oriented applications should take note. Applicable domains would certainly include medicine, such as patient monitoring systems and graphically oriented diagnostic aids, and engineering, such as the use of CAD.

In Brocklesby v. United States and Jeppesen and Company [9], the appeals court held a publisher of an instrument approach procedure for aircraft strictly liable for injuries due to the faulty information contained in the procedure. This case is important for CBS developers because it shows that they have a responsibility to ensure that products they present as factual are verified by them to be such. Finally, in Halstead v. United States [23], an aviation navigation chart, which was determined to be defective, resulted in a plane crash. The court ruled that, since the information in the chart was suitable for mass marketing, it was a product, thus allowing the application of strict liability. From this decision, Birnbaum [6] has inferred that “since a [computer] program is a tangible expression of an idea that can be put to use, it is similar to the idea of a navigational chart” (p. 149). Therefore, the courts may view mass-marketed computer programs as a product, allowing the application of strict liability to software.

One of the most alarming perspectives regarding CBS and strict liability is the ominous warning provided by Gemignani [17, p. 204] who notes:

If imposition of strict liability in tort would make the manufacturers of company hardware and software more careful and thoughtful in their race to develop an ultimate product, that alone would justify its [strict liability] application.

## Negligence

To establish a cause of action based on negligence, a plaintiff must establish a duty owed by the defendant. This means that it is necessary to take reasonable care to perform in a manner that will not expose others to foreseeable risks of injury. In addition, there must be a breach of that duty by the defendant, actual and proximate cause of the injury by the breach of that duty, and actual damages suffered by the plaintiff as a result of the breach of that duty [31]. Negligence is considered more difficult to prove than strict liability because it requires a showing of fault on the part of the defendant whereas strict liability does not [43]. Levy and Bell [31] indicate that many actions taken/not taken by a developer could lead to a breach of that duty and then to a lawsuit based on the tort of negligence. These include a failure to: write or test the program properly, correct significant program bugs, warn of program limitations, instruct users on how to use the program, and provide adequate security for the system.

Cole [11] addresses several perspectives related to duty. For one, CBS developers would have a duty to handle unreasonable inputs reasonably. For example, improper or faulty inputs can produce terribly hazardous results involving expert systems and systems based on the broader area of artificial intelligence, including robotics. This would mean that developers' actions would be examined carefully by the courts regarding the reasonableness of the chosen design process, the usefulness and risk of the design itself, and the feasibility of safer alternatives [11]. Three court cases are discussed below to illustrate the negligence cause of action.

In a case involving North American Van Lines [47], the company was held liable because it failed to include in its information system a program to help detect violations of the Interstate Commerce Commission's "70-hour rule" that prohibits truck drivers from working over 70 hours within an eight-day period. One of the company's drivers, who had exceeded the “70-hour rule,” was involved in an accident in which a motorist was killed. The court held that North American was grossly negligent in that it failed in its duty to incorporate a program to prevent violations of the rule.

CBS developers will also be expected to act with reasonable care in ensuring that data are not in error. This is supported by the ruling in Independent School District No. 454 v. Statistical Tabulating Corp. [25], a service bureau. The court allowed a cause of negligence to be brought against the service bureau for allegedly providing inaccurate calculations of the value of a school that had burned down, causing the school district to incur an underinsured loss. This action by the court is consistent with the opinion expressed by Levy and Bell [31] who indicated that proper testing of a computer program is essential to forestall a cause of negligence.

In Shell Pipeline Corporation v. Coastal States Trading, Inc. [44], the court ruled that Shell was negligent when a defect in its computer system related to the data entry process was instrumental in the misdelivery of 93,000 barrels of crude oil. At the heart of the matter was Shell's computer system which was specifically designed to prevent the type of problem that arose in this instance. The trial court found that Shell and its customers, including Coastal, relied on the system, that the system failed, and that the failure to identify erroneous data entry was the proximate cause of Coastal's damages. On appeal, Coastal was awarded damages from Shell Pipeline in excess of \$1.7 million.

Nycum [37], Tyde [49], and Samuelson [43] provide additional insight into the discussion relating to CBS and the tort of negligence.

## Summary

There are distinct legal differences between negligence and strict liability proceedings. For example, in strict liability actions, actual proof of negligence is unnecessary even though evidence establishing the presence of a defect often reveals negligence on the developer's part. Brannigan and Dayhoff [8] assert that strict liability would focus on the system itself while negligence would focus on the actions of the developer. This means that under negligence principles, CBS developers would be liable for the breach of a duty to design and warn, while under strict liability principles, liability would take the form of a design or warning defect. However, Madden [33] emphasizes that, as far as failure to warn actions is concerned, the characteristics of strict liability and negligence theories are nearly indistinguishable. This type of similarity may serve to heighten the uncertainty that CBS developers may face regarding failure to warn actions.

The torts of strict liability and negligence represent formidable threats to CBS developers. Development efforts could be severely hampered because of these legal actions. Also, since no case law exists that would shed light on the liabilities CBS developers could incur, a “wait and see” attitude may prevail instead of trying to deal with forthcoming legal problems proactively. Other perspectives are the issues dealing with the integrative aspects of these actions. For example, if a developer attempts to overcome the threat of strict liability entanglements by taking measures that could satisfy the development requirements and obligations associated with this action, would such measures also satisfy the development requirements and obligations in negligence actions?

Specific case law has not yet come to grips with the legal issues involving CBS and both negligence and strict liability. However, Gemignani [18] notes that even if litigation seems improbable, one must be prepared for it. He states succinctly that “if litigation represents the plague, then appropriate precautionary measures can help keep you healthy” (p. 16).

One way to deal with the lack of case law and produce meaningful results for CBS developers and researchers is to examine existing cases to determine any similarities or analogies between actions taken by the courts in the past and their relationship to CBS. This is what the courts do, in that they traditionally rely on past judgments and opinions to provide guidance for current litigation. This tradition is based on the doctrine of stare decisis which forms the basis of the common law, and it essentially allows courts to draw analogies of fact between cases. Further, since the law lags behind emerging technologies and their applications, such actions are appropriate with CBS.

In their framework, Mykytyn et al. [36] propose this type of research in order to identify questions that have been addressed by the courts. Based upon a review of case law, that is, by applying the doctrine of stare decisis, some of the more critical development requirements common to both strict liability and negligence will be presented within the context of a framework to aid CBS developers and researchers relevant to new CBS. Although they do not represent the only development requirements associated with these tort actions, they are so significant that, should injury occur, their omission in CBS development processes would be considered inexcusable. Also, while it is not completely possible to avoid all legal actions, it is advisable to prepare for them in a manner that would lead to a judgment in favor of the CBS developers. Gemignani [18] underscores this strategy with his rule stating that, “if there is no way to avoid litigation, then be sure that you win” (p. 16).

## A Framework for Injury Assessment and Reduction of Legal Liability

THE FRAMEWORK CONSISTS OF TWO MAIN COMPONENTS: Injury Assessment Analysis and Liability Reducing Measures, with each component containing several elements; it is displayed in figure 1. The application of our framework should take place during the earliest stages of systems development, beginning with the project definition phase where feasibility, costs, and requirements are investigated, and continuing throughout the development process. A brief overview is presented below with subsequent discussions devoted to a more in-depth analysis of the framework's specific elements.

The Framework for Injury Assessment and Reduction of Legal Liability is robust in that it would be applicable to any development methodology, such as structured approach or prototyping, appropriate for the project or system in question. Given this applicability, one of the initial requirements associated with our framework is the determination of any third-party involvement or impact. A third party is a person, or class of persons, who is not directly involved in the activity; if this person or class of persons were to incur harm, the term “aggrieved party” would also apply $[19]$ . A hospital that purchases a computer-based patient monitoring system and the vendor that developed and supplied the system are the parties directly involved in this transaction; a patient whose condition would be monitored by the system is the third party and, if injured, the aggrieved party.

Figure 1. A Framework for Injury Assessment and Reduction of Legal Liability

What is important for CBS developers is that they understand that potential legal liability exists that could adversely affect them as well as users or third-party individuals who might be affected by a flawed system. Our framework is designed to help developers eliminate, or at least minimize, the possibility of injury occurring and any resulting liability assessment against the developer.

Injury Assessment Analysis allows the CBS developer to reach an early determination regarding the potential impact on third parties. In addition, appropriate cost/benefit and risk analyses are conducted during this phase. Some of the difficulties and related aspects of risk analysis are addressed below. The results would indicate that if insignificant risk of injury or no third-party involvement are contemplated, the systems development process can continue with minimum legal risk based on the principles of strict liability and negligence. On the other hand, third-party involvement coupled with high risk would suggest that developers continue with efforts to minimize or eliminate potential legal consequences. These efforts are integral to the second framework component.

The Liability Reducing Measures component consists of several submodules dealing with environmental analysis and testing, design selection and testing, and actions to take during system development. Given the potentially serious consequences of high risk and third-party injury related to the system, Liability Reducing Measures taken during this phase are iterative and would continue, as necessary, throughout the system's life to minimize harm and adverse legal consequences. These iterative perspectives are indicated with dashed lines in figure 1.

The legal concepts noted in our framework include those items that are common to strict liability and negligence actions described above—system design and warnings and instructions. Should injury occur, omitting any consideration of these details would provide a plaintiff's attorney with ample means for justifying a plaintiff's right to compensation for injury. While the framework does not include all factors that are applicable to every legal question associated with negligence and strict liability, it can be expanded to include other factors that could aid further in limiting damaging liabilities. The framework components are discussed in detail below.

## Injury Assessment Analysis Module

## Third-Party Involvement

To reach this initial part of our framework, it is presumed that the systems development process has been initiated for a project. This would no doubt include a preliminary analysis, feasibility study, and appropriate decisions by a systems analyst as well as a senior project manager and non-IS management to proceed with the project. This is illustrated at the beginning of figure 1 as “System Development Process.”

It is imperative to determine the person, or class of persons, who will be affected by the system and the extent of an injury that could be attributed to flawed development efforts. For example, could a flaw in the design of a system to be used by a physician cause injury or death to a patient, the third party? Third-party involvement would also be an issue regarding the relationship between investors and financial planners who use investment advice systems. If the software produces faulty advice due to unsuccessful investment analysis or strategy, the CBS developer could be held liable for injuries to a third party under negligence actions. Another example central to the business community would be credit reporting agencies that provide incorrect information to clients about third parties' credit ratings. Section 552 of the Restatement (Second) of Torts [40] states that those in the course of business, profession, or employment must exercise reasonable care or competence in obtaining or communicating information relied on and used by others in their business, and that failure to do so may subject them to liability. Gemignani [18] supports this third-party perspective by noting that CBS developers will be expected to do whatever is reasonable to protect against malfunctions that can cause injury. The protection the court provides to third parties dates back to 1916 in MacPherson v. Buick Motor Co. [32], and the North American Van Lines and Shell Pipeline cases cited earlier illustrate dramatically the potential for liability regarding CBS that can be incurred as a result of harm to third parties. CBS developers should note that, given the nature of third-party involvement and possible injury, the problem of flawed development efforts is a system-oriented problem that involves many facets. The software is but a component of the system that includes software engineering principles, component and system testing, and so on, that must also be addressed.

According to our framework, if it is determined that the proposed CBS does not involve third parties, systems development can proceed without risk of possible liability due to strict liability or negligence actions. If third-party involvement is contemplated, our framework addresses the next issue, that of determining the degree of risk and cost and benefits.

## Risk and Cost/Benefit Analysis

Determining the degree of risk associated with a software development project is considered to be imperative if injuries that can lead to litigation are to be avoided. Birnbaum [6] notes that regarding strict liability, the software developer is better able to assess risks associated with product use than the technically illiterate person; the developer can then either prevent them from occurring or can warn of their existence. Risk analysis pertaining to CBS development, however, is at best an extremely difficult task, yet it is one that must be undertaken both to limit potential liability being imposed against developers and to minimize the risk of injury to third parties.

Risk analysis as applied to CBS development has been the subject of substantial scholarly research. Barki, Rivard, and Talbot [4] investigated risks involving cost overruns, project delays, and unfulfilled user needs. They posited that the assessment of software development risk encompasses basically two dimensions: the probability associated with an undesirable event, and the consequences of the occurrence of this event. Examining prior research in this area, such as that by Kangari and Boyer [27], Zmud [52], Boehm [7], Alter [2], and McFarlan [35], they developed an instrument to assess risk pertaining to relevant factors: novelty of the project, project size or scope, lack of expertise, application complexity, and organizational environment. They recommend that the measure be used to assess the risk of individual software projects. They caution, however, that there are no established norms upon which to base one's assessment, other than those associated with their study. They also concede that the measure does not relate the risk components to the outcome of the project itself; these might include user interface shortfalls and poor quality software.

Relevant to the work by Barki et al. [4], specifically to that portion of their instrument dealing with application complexity, the assessment of the likelihood of harm or injury due to a system defect and the resultant consequences and their severity are not addressed. The risk analysis relating to the legal perspective would be more closely associated with what they identify as risk components related to the outcome of a project.

The courts have also wrestled with the problems surrounding risk assessment in determining negligence. Judge Learned Hand, in the Carroll Towing case $[50]$ , suggested that conduct that created an unreasonable risk of harm to others is the function of three variables: (1) the probability of a particular injury occurring, (2) the seriousness of the injury if it occurs, and (3) the offsetting costs of prevention. Judge Hand even went so far as to present these variables in algebraic terms: if P is the probability, L is the injury, and B is the burden of offsetting costs, then liability attaches where B is less than the product of L and P (B < PL). Unfortunately, this analysis cannot be applied where the probability and seriousness of injury is not reasonably foreseeable, and, consequently, unmeasurable $[16]$ . The court may then consider whether circumstances were such that the possibility of injury was reasonably foreseeable and weigh them against the cost of prevention.

With respect to risk analysis and the uncertainties involved with what would be considered reasonably foreseeable risks, the nature of the system should be determined. How likely is it that injury will occur due to a system defect, and what is the assessment of the consequences and their severity? This assessment requires fault forecasting, which means that the developer must test for system malfunction and the circumstances surrounding the malfunction, including that of the system currently under development (during component and system testing phases) as well as that of previously developed systems (testing and operation and maintenance phases). Information about similar types of systems from competitors and other organizations should be investigated as well. This type of analysis is consistent with the suggestions of Mykytyn et al. [36] who recommended a ubiquitous analytical approach to identifying potential problems for CBS, including reviews of pertinent legal cases.

It is not possible in this paper to analyze or review in depth the various approaches to define and quantify the varying constructs of risk; indeed, entire articles and books are devoted to risk analysis. Interested readers are referred to research by Haimes [22], Sherer [45], Charette [10], and Kaplan and Garrick [28].

As noted above, cost is a major factor in risk analysis. Given the seriousness of an injury and the probability that it could occur, how much will the use of any given development technique, safety device, hardware interlock, real-time warning, and the like, increase the cost of development? If it is determined that the probability of serious injury is high, then CBS developers would need to keep records of the additional effort required to apply various development techniques, or to add safety devices, hardware interlocks, or real-time warnings. From the perspective of strict liability, courts will look to CBS developers to supply evidence of this type of analysis and would expect them to incur the liability of a defective product simply because the developer would be in a better position than the purchaser of the system to spread the cost of analysis and testing over all the buyers of the product. The basic presumption is that errors and injuries are inevitable, especially in CBS development. In effect, the cost of protecting against an injury would be built into the cost of the product $[11]$ . It is also understood that by putting products on the market, CBS developers invite their use by the public, implying that the product poses no foreseeable danger $[6]$ . In determining the cost of an alternative design, for example, the courts will look to the cost of incorporating testing and injury analysis into the development process. They will consider minor increases in the cost of a system mandatory if such increases reduce the risk of serious injury to the user $[21]$ . The developer of the system must balance these costs against the foreseeability and the seriousness of such an injury.

As depicted in figure 1, CBS developers must identify the potential for significant risks to third parties. If these types of risks exist, further considerations to minimize liability would be necessary. If, on the other hand, CBS developers determine that there is no risk to third parties or that cost/benefit and risk analysis determines that low risk is contemplated, systems development efforts can continue in the customary fashion. After this analysis, CBS developers can reasonably expect that negligence and strict liability actions will not be brought successfully against them. Fortunately, case law diminishes some of the uncertainty in that it is known that if CBS are not tested, and warnings and instructions are not included as integral parts of the system, strict liability and negligence actions could follow.

## Liability Reducing Measures Module

As shown in figure 1, entering this portion of the model indicates that CBS development impacts third parties, has the potential for high risk that could cause harm or injury to them, and necessitates additional injury-prevention costs. At this point, CBS developers should take the necessary steps to ensure that the potential for liability to them and harm to third parties is minimized to the greatest extent possible. These measures are addressed next.

## Environmental Analysis/Testing

In Cronin v. J.B.E. Olson Corp. [12], the California Supreme Court stated that “the design and manufacture of products should not be carried out in an industrial vacuum, but with the recognition of the realities of their everyday use” (p. 437). This opinion, along with the doctrine of stare decisis cited earlier, makes it clear that the court would consider it necessary for CBS developers to utilize not only their knowledge of fundamental design techniques and principles but also any other pertinent criteria available to them that will inhibit injury before the system is fully implemented. Lautsch [29] notes that safety must be a priority even if efficiency has to be sacrificed to achieve it.

The object of environmental analysis/testing is to identify potential hazards in the environment where the system is to be used and incorporate injury reducing procedures a priori. In Spruill v. Boyle-Midway Inc. [46], the court stated that product manufacturers were expected to anticipate the environment and the reasonably foreseeable risks of the use of their product in that environment. An analogy could be drawn to system developers where they, as manufacturers, would be expected to identify the environment where a system would be implemented and the foreseeable risks involved with system use. Gemignani [18] asserts that the more serious an injury would be as a result of a system malfunction, the more testing should take place, and the more safeguards should be implemented in the system to prevent disastrous failures. Regarding the previously mentioned PCA pump, where defective software controlling the pump caused a fatal overdose, the plaintiff's attorney stated that the pump had no independent fail-safe mechanism that would guard against such overdosing [49]. This also reflects an overall system-oriented problem in that the software is but a component of the system. In this case, it is reasonably foreseeable that any software malfunction could lead to incorrect dosages that could cause death. By anticipating reasonably foreseeable risks associated with the use of a system in a given environment, testing procedures would aid in the prevention of injuries leading to legal actions and, coincidentally, would provide users with a system that has utilized both quality and safety controls [6].

The fact that CBS have become so pervasive only makes the problem related to their possible impact that much more difficult. Consider, for example, the consequences of defective CBS found in the medical arena. Health care providers would want to ensure that any computer-based device purchased was not defective in some other way, such as being based on outdated technology. In addition, both a hospital and a physician would demand that any computer-based device designed, for example, to detect brain tumors does not lend itself to misinterpretation, with obvious dire consequences. In these instances, the CBS developer should actively deal with health care providers to ensure that devices are current in their design and that screen formats, uses of colors, graphics, and the like are not conflicting or inconsistent, which could lead to misunderstanding and misinterpretation. Pilot projects designed and controlled to assess system use in the actual setting would help to identify such system anomalies.

Other environments that would demand proactive examination today are manufacturing, engineering, and aviation. Robotics, process control, and vision systems illustrate the types of CBS that are common today in the manufacturing sector. Their safe operation would demand that developers carry out analysis in this environment prior to developing final versions of their products. In addition, engineers who use expert systems or similar software to assist in the design of buildings and bridges should be consulted regarding the sequence of questions asked, nature of responses, and so on. This perspective illustrates the necessity of carefully scrutinizing knowledge engineers and domain experts whose knowledge might be included in any expert system. Finally, systems designed to aid pilots in avoiding mid-air collisions generally do not pause in their analysis of the situation to request human intervention or require verification $[48]$ ; there simply may not be time. Instead, pilots rely on the systems to warn them. Obviously, the nature of the warnings—tones, visual displays—should be vigorously and thoroughly tested in airplanes themselves in concert with pilots and related crew members.

Finally, the business community should not be overlooked by developers regarding its environment and necessary analysis. Systems involving customer/consumer credit ratings, investment advisory systems, and tax planning and reporting are just three where careful, a priori analysis is critical. Consider, for example, a system that is designed to interact with external databases for the purpose of creating credit reports. Appropriate agencies as well as developers should take all necessary steps to ensure that the incoming data are accurate and timely to preclude incorrect reports from being prepared. In a Vermont case involving Dun & Bradstreet, Inc. (D&B) [15], a case that eventually went to the U.S. Supreme Court, D&B incurred \$350,000 in compensatory and punitive damages because it included incorrect information in credit reports pertaining to a Vermont contractor. Its reports incorrectly indicated that the contractor had filed for bankruptcy whereas a former employee of the contractor had been the one to so file.

Although it is true that testing may not lead to a complete cure for software-related problems, a combination of testing and safety analysis in the intended environment can give the highest probability of detecting and correcting problems. Courts conclude that the manufacturer, that is, the CBS developer, is in a better position to anticipate and control the risks associated with the product. And while even the best of testing procedures will not identify all anomalies, real-world testing in the setting where CBS are to be used will help developers identify those instances where problems can occur. Other actions that developers should consider include: employing third parties as documentation writers, monitoring the correctness of designs and specifications described by advertising and sales personnel, staying in tune with customer complaints revealed during testing, aggressively correcting detected errors, ensuring the compliance of software development/engineering standards by subcontractors or others engaged in the development process, and using simulation techniques and other automated testing procedures.

## Design Selection and Testing

The previous section dealt primarily with appropriate analysis and testing within the environment where the particular system will be used. More specific aspects of designs, however, are also important; these would include particular features or components. Issues of safety may be especially important if human injury or death could occur as a result of system failure or even use.

Legal scholars indicate that there are several actions CBS developers should take during this phase to minimize negative legal consequences $[6, 49]$ . Typical analysis and design of CBS would include factors that have been deemed critical by the courts. These might include appropriate documentation, instructions, and warnings, especially if the risk of injury is high. Designs that incorporate state-of-the-art technology may be necessary, particularly if the system involves a risk of injury to third parties such as hospital patients. Birnbaum $[6]$ has indicated that real-time warnings may be both appropriate and necessary in some types of development, such as machinery controlled by computer software. In addition, Cole $[11]$ has stated that a CBS developer's failure to warn that there may be imperfect results—such as may be found with expert systems—could lead to a cause of action based on negligence. Knowledge of these issues and related factors can be identified through vigorous testing procedures.

CBS developers must ensure that they have rigorously tested prospective systems since courts will surely examine records of such testing to determine whether developers' testing was sufficient. There is no legal definition of "sufficient," unfortunately, so developers are left with a question of how much testing is considered sufficient. Cole [11] has indicated that, from the perspective of design negligence and related testing, CBS developers would be held to a standard for a given system based on what is feasible at the time the system was developed. However, if no efforts are made to identify and moderate reasonably foreseeable system misuse, then developers may find that such inaction may allow the courts to find the system defective.

In strict liability actions, Brannigan and Dayhoff [8] have suggested that one of the most useful tests to determine whether a product's design is defective is the Roach test, cited in Roach v. Kononen [41]. In applying the Roach test, a court would assume that the product's design might cause an injury that it did cause. The court would then judge the manufacturer's—that is, the CBS developer's—choice by asking if, given the knowledge about the possibility of the injury, the manufacturer should have done something about the danger before the product was sold. A greater burden is thus placed on the manufacturer to justify the product's design because the plaintiff does not have to prove that the manufacturer had knowledge of the product's dangerous propensity [8].

The Roach test could be applied to CBS such as expert systems. Errors in a knowledge base, if they can be identified and corrected, would subject the CBS developer to liability if left uncorrected. While the developer is free to make any reasonable choices for the system's design, ordinary errors would be considered manufacturing defects, giving rise to strict liability. These would include placing erroneous information into the system [8]. Avoiding designs that would subject CBS developers to strict liability are imperative. Although precautionary measures to avoid this action may be costly, it is much less expensive to avoid lawsuits than to become involved in them [6]. Two actions related to reducing potential liability associated with CBS designs are addressed next.

1. Usage of State-of-the-Art Technology: During this stage, further analysis and testing can aid in determining whether a particular system should even be developed. If the risks of injury are so great and the current state of technology does not offer methods to overcome such risks, then the decision to discontinue development efforts until the obstacles can be overcome would be prudent. The courts make it clear that a developer would be required to show that the benefits from a selected design outweigh the risk of danger and are reasonable in light of the state of the art at the time of design [3]. Further, they have indicated that if no amount of expertise or caution can eliminate the serious risk of injury arising from the use of the system, then it should not be developed at all [42].

The courts have traditionally looked at the standards of the industry, that is, what is being done by others developing similar systems, and they will look to see if technology existed at the time of development that could have inhibited injury from occurring. The courts would not make unreasonable demands on CBS developers; they only expect them to act upon what is reasonably foreseeable at the time of development [6]. What the courts would demand is that CBS developers utilize known state-of-the-art technology to forestall injury.

At the same time, CBS developers should not promise that a system is perfect, which may not be obtainable under any circumstances. The developer of an expert system could employ the latest in hardware and software technology, thus believing that it would satisfy a court's requirements. In the eyes of the court, however, negligence might still occur simply because the risk of failure was built into the design of the system—that is, an algorithmic solution was omitted from the design because it was not economically feasible or was considered undesirable. Likewise, a developer could likely be found negligent by the court if an expert system's inferencing mechanism were flawed or potentially incorrect. Such an example might be a depth cutoff in the search process or a timeout in the reasoning mechanism process to maximize efficiency over effectiveness [11].

2. Determine Appropriate Warnings: Madden [33] states that even if a product is flawlessly designed, tested, and implemented, injury caused by its intended or unintended yet reasonably foreseeable use may expose the developer to liability. He notes further that under strict liability, the developer's warnings could keep the system from being deemed unreasonably dangerous, and under negligence principles the developer would have a duty to provide adequate warnings of any risks involved in the use of a product. In Ragsdale Bros., Inc. v. Magro [38], the court emphasized this point by extending the liability of the vendor to nondefective products released to the public without adequate warnings of dangerous propensities or adequate instructions for safe use.

The extent of the duty to warn depends on the obviousness of the dangers and the nature of the intended use of the system. The warning must be adequate, specific, and comprehensive $[24]$ . Instructions cannot be substituted for warnings, but they must be sufficient for safe system operation and must educate the user about its safe and efficient use. Instructions must also alert the user to any plausible dangers that could emerge from its foreseeable use. The use of warnings and instructions must not be considered as a legal shield to ill-defined, misdesigned, and/or flawed systems developed for quick financial gain. The courts have held manufacturers liable for products that pose a danger, even with adequate warnings, if the danger can be eliminated from their design at a reasonable cost. The courts have noted that when design changes would prevent injury, liability cannot be avoided by merely warning of the possibility of injury. Furthermore, when the public is not protected by the strongest of warnings, the defect itself must be removed if liability is to be avoided.

## Discussion

To forestall harm or injury from occurring and, at the same time, to minimize the possibility of adverse legal action, the process of environmental testing and analysis, determining appropriate warnings, standards, use of state of the art technology, and so on, should not be considered a one-time event. Rather, CBS developers should continue to investigate possible system defects, interpret customer feedback, and carefully examine related actions that may be occurring within the industry. Such actions are integral to our framework and would serve to persuade the court that positive, timely, proactive, and reasonable measures were taken to ensure that system defects leading to harm or injury were minimized.

In cases involving negligence and strict liability, court opinions rely on what is reasonably foreseeable at the time of an injury and, under the circumstances of a particular case, what actions a reasonable person would take to avoid the incidence of injury. If one can be held liable as a matter of law for injury caused by system use or misuse, providing safeguards against every conceivable possibility of such use or misuse may appear to be an unending and futile enterprise. However, the court has been quite specific in its determination of what is reasonably foreseeable. In Deromedi v. Litton Industrial Products, Inc. [13], the defense cited prior cases where the court had absolved the duty of manufacturers to design machines that would protect against their unforeseeable unsafe uses. The court pointed out, however, that there were significant factual distinctions between the cases cited by the defense and the Deromedi case, in that in the Deromedi case the machine was “a single purpose system and its specific use was known to the defendant at the time of purchase.... Prior knowledge of the potential for unsafe use distinguishes this case and therefore the defendant cannot escape liability as a matter of law” (pp. 394, 395). In this case the court supports the premise that CBS developers could be obligated to include safety measures in their design that would account for reasonably foreseeable events in system use that could lead to injury. At the same time, the court recognized that in the cases cited by the defense, circumstances leading to injury do arise that cannot be reasonably foreseen or taken into account.

The legal issues raised in this paper indicate a need for guidelines that would aid in minimizing CBS flaws that cause injury. Further, the development of analysis, design, and testing procedures in various environments could provide CBS developers with safety measures that can lessen the possibility of injury. There is also a need for continuing research involving the design of human-computer interfaces that would offer guidance in screen designs and commands that not only would lead users to safe outcomes but would also issue users forceful warnings when system limits are approached. It is important for system developers to make every feasible effort to ensure that all foreseeably affected parties are protected from the potential dangers of personal injury or property damage. Research that addresses these safety issues would certainly help alleviate problems relevant to CBS-caused injuries and any ensuing legal actions. For example, the use of audio and/or visual types of interfaces as well as designs that incorporate multimedia capabilities should be investigated. For the CBS developers, decisions regarding design and development could become clearer and more focused.

Researchers should also examine in greater detail the relationships between the legal environment and software quality. These include quality assurance, total quality management, and standards such as those advanced in the ISO 9000 registration and audit process. Since ISO 9000 requires development of quality assurance documentation, it could provide evidence of compliance with standards by CBS developers in the event of litigation. Following such standards, developers would have accurate records of identification and correction of defects, and they would also be able to substantiate any testing procedures.

We feel a final note is in order. Some CBS developers might suggest that, although some may be producing unsafe CBS that could lead to harm or injury to innocent persons with resulting legal calamity, it is not a problem for them because they are not a “bad vendor.” Unfortunately, this is a myopic approach to CBS development. As technology continues to advance and as more machinery, equipment, or systems become computer-based, the potential for harm or injury exists. “Good vendors” will become caught up in the potential entanglements too. For example, this would involve identification and possible imposition of standards by the courts and costs of defending the organization against legal action. We feel the time is now for CBS developers to become better informed of the potential liabilities they may incur and to take actions proactively, rather than waiting for the courts to impose their will upon them. Perhaps Leveson and Turner $[30]$ drive one of the points home quite well when they indicate that certification of software engineers is not yet required, but if CBS failures continue, such certification will surely become inevitable.

## APPENDIX A: Glossary of Legal Terms

Selected legal terms and definitions are listed below to provide clarification. Terms and definitions are provided by Gifis [19]. Additional examples where deemed necessary are also included and identified with an asterisk (\*).

Duty. The obligation owed by a person (actor) to another person, the breach of which results in liability to the actor. The term “legal duty” is that which the law requires be done by a determinate person. The breach of a legal duty owed another is an element of negligence and is the essence of most tort actions.

\* Generally, duty is considered to be a moral or legal obligation of a person to conduct him/herself in such a way as to exercise reasonable care for others' physical safety and the safety of their property.

Foreseeable. A concept used in various areas of the law to limit liability of a party for the consequences of his/her acts to consequences that are within the scope of risks that a person of ordinary prudence would reasonably expect might occur. Thus, in tort actions, in most cases a person's actions may be deemed negligent only where the injurious consequences of those actions were foreseeable.

Negligence. A failure to exercise that degree of care which a reasonable man [person] would exercise under the same circumstances. The term refers to conduct that falls below the standard established by law for the protection of others against unreasonable risk of harm. With regard to tort actions, there are degrees of negligence: slight (the failure to use great care), ordinary (the failure to use ordinary care), and gross (the failure to use even slight care).

\* Essentially, negligence rests on the duty of a person to exercise care in his/her dealings with others where there is a chance of injury.

Proximate Cause. Proximate cause is that which in natural and continuous sequence unbroken by any new independent cause produces an event, and without which the injury would not have occurred.

\* If a person purchases a new vehicle with defectively designed brakes, and, in the course of normal operation of the vehicle, the brakes fail, resulting in a collision with other vehicles, all injuries would be proximately caused by the brake failure. Even though the driver was in control of the vehicle, the courts would conclude that, because of the failure of the brakes, there was nothing that he/she could do to avoid the collision. Any injuries are obviously caused by the collision, but the courts look to any reasons why the collision even occurred in the first place, i.e., the defectively designed brakes would be the proximate cause.

Restatement (Second) of Torts. A restatement is an attempt by the American Law Institute “to present an orderly statement of the general common law of the United States, including in that term not only the law developed solely be judicial decision, but also the law that has grown from the application by the courts of statutes.” In developing the Second Restatement, the American Law Institute has taken into account factors such as modern trend of the law based on influential jurisdictions and well-thought-out opinions.

Stare Decisis. Literally, a Latin phrase meaning to stand by that which was decided; also, a rule by which common law courts are slow to interfere with principles announced in earlier decisions. Courts will follow and apply principles of law that have been adjudged in earlier cases to later cases that involve essentially the same facts. It is also similar to a precedent whereupon a case will serve as such for future cases.

Strict Liability. A tort action which basically means liability without fault. It often means that one who sells any product that has an inherent risk of injury is liable for all physical injuries proximately caused by the defective product, even without a showing of negligence. The concept applies to all who are involved in the manufacturing and selling of a product and is based on the proposition that when a manufacturer (i.e., CBS developer) presents products to the public for sale, it is understood that they are suitable for their intended use. Simply stated, the courts believe that if the product does cause physical harm or injury, the manufacturer will be held accountable for that injury, regardless of the care taken during the product's development.

Tort. A private or civil wrong or injury resulting from a breach of a legal duty that exists by virtue of society's expectations regarding interpersonal conduct. Tort actions are distinguished from contract or other private relationships. Essential elements of tort actions are the existence of a legal duty owed by a defendant to a plaintiff, a breach of that duty, and a causal relation between defendant's conduct and the resulting damage to the plaintiff.

APPENDIX B: Expanded Notes on Selected Legal Cases

Aetna Casualty and Surety Company v. Jeppesen & Company 642 F.2d 339 (1981)

United States Court of Appeals, Ninth Circuit. Argued and submitted November 10, 1980 and decided April 20, 1981.

Aetna sued Jeppesen in the United States District Court for the District of Nevada seeking indemnity for money paid in settlement of wrongful death actions filed by representatives of passengers killed in a plane crash. Jeppesen is a publisher of instrument approach charts designed to aid pilots in making instrument approaches to airports. Aetna contended that the chart in question was defective, and that the defect was the cause of the crash. The district court held for Aetna and apportioned damages between the airline (20% at fault) and Jeppesen (80% at fault). On an appeal by Jeppesen, the United States Court of Appeals held that, while the information contained in the particular approach chart was completely correct, the chart was defective in that there was a conflict between the information conveyed by words and numbers and the information conveyed by graphics.

Peter C. Halstead v. United States of America, Jeppesen & Co. and Katherine H. Saloomey.

Katherine H. Saloomey v. Jeppesen & Co. Katherine H. Saloomey v. United States of America. 535 F. Supp. 782 (1982).

United States District Court, D. Connecticut, March 10, 1982.

Wrongful death actions arose out of the crash of a small private plane in West Virginia. The deceased were residents of Connecticut, and actions were brought by the administratrix (Saloomey) and administrator (Halstead) of the estates. The court found, among other things, that the navigation chart produced by Jeppesen was classified as a product in that Jeppesen mass produced and distributed its charts. If suitable for mass marketing, the information was held to be in some sense a fungible good for which the manufacturer, placing it into the stream of commerce, must assume responsibility.

Shell Pipeline Corporation v. Coastal States Trading, Inc. 788 S.W.2d 837 (Tex.App.—Houston [1st Dist]. 1990)

Court of Appeals of Texas, Houston (1st Dist.). February 15, 1990 with a denial of a rehearing on May 10, 1990.

Shell appealed a decision by a Texas state court to award damages of \$1,762,350 to Coastal brought about by the misdelivery of 93,000 barrels of crude oil. Information received by Shell's personnel indicated that the 93,000 barrels of crude oil was resold by Coastal; Shell entered this information into its computer systems. Unfortunately, Shell's personnel entered the names of the parties to the reselling and trading incorrectly. Court records showed that Shell designed a computer system specifically to prevent the type of problems that arose in this case. The system failed. Shell assumed the responsibility of keeping its oil traders informed regarding trades, names, and any mismatches of names, a service upon which Coastal relied. Among other things, Shell was found negligent in that it: offered to implement “in-line transfers” when it knew or should have known that it did not have the ability to do so carefully and properly; failed to design a system for implementing “in-line transfers” that would prevent the possibility of misdeliveries; failed to warn the public, such as Coastal, that its system was susceptible to error; and that the design of the system depended on an inherently unreliable process. Shell’s liability was reduced to \$1,412,350 because Coastal had recovered \$700,000 from other sources.

Barbara A. Brocklesby, et al. v. United States of America and Jeppesen and Company 767 F.2d 1288 (1985)

United States Court of Appeals, Ninth Circuit. Argued and submitted December 11, 1984, decided February 15, 1985 and amended August 2, 1985.

On appeal Jeppesen, a publisher of instrument approach charts used by commercial and private aviation, sought to have a judgment against it for \$12,785,580.81 reversed; the judgment had been awarded to World Airways, the aircraft owner, and to the survivors of crew members of a plane that crashed in 1973. The defects in the approach chart prepared by Jeppesen stem from the Federal Aviation Administration's failure to establish a safe instrument approach procedure, i.e., Jeppesen received incorrect data from the FAA and incorporated that data into its instrument approach chart. Thus, Jeppesen failed to ensure that the information it received from the government was correct. Jeppesen was held strictly liable for its defective chart, and was also found to be negligent for failing in its duty to conduct reasonable tests and inspections to detect latent defects, and for failing to warn consumers of the defects of which, as manufacturer, it is or should be aware. On appeal the court stated that Jeppesen had both the ability to detect an error and a mechanism for seeking corrections, which it did not do in this instance.

## Rosa Marie Torres v. North American Van Lines, Inc. 638 P.2d 835 (Ariz.App. 1982)

Court of Appeals of Arizona, Division 2. December 1, 1982 with a denial of a rehearing on January 13, 1983.

Torres sued North American Van Lines and its driver in an Arizona district court for the wrongful death of her husband caused by North American's tractor and trailer rear-ending Torres' parked vehicle. The Arizona Superior Court found in favor of Torres, found the driver and North American grossly negligent, and awarded punitive damages of \$2.5 million. The main issue was North American's failure to verify "on duty" time, defined as actual driving and nondriving time of its drivers, the total of which cannot exceed 70 hours in an eight-day period. Drivers' log books contain the "on duty" time; however, North American did not adequately verify the entries. A computer expert testified that North American had a sophisticated data-processing system, along with the necessary equipment and personnel, to accomplish log book verification, and system modification would have been relatively easy. Expert testimony also revealed that North American had failed to correct the log verification problem over a two-decade period. The Court of Appeals affirmed the Superior Court's decision.

Dun & Bradstreet, Inc. v. Greenmoss Builders, Inc. 472 US 749, 86 L Ed 2d 593, 105 S Ct 2939

Argued March 21, 1984, Reargued October 3, 1984. Decided June 26, 1985.

Greenmoss sued Dun & Bradstreet (D&B) for defamation in a Vermont state court for circulating a false report that the contractor had filed for bankruptcy, an error which was found to have resulted from the mistaken attribution of a bankruptcy petition filed by a former employee of Greenmoss. Following a jury verdict which found for Greenmoss and awarded \$50,000 in compensatory damages and \$300,000 in punitive damages, the trial court ordered a new trial because of dissatisfaction with its instructions to the jury. The Vermont Supreme Court reversed the trial court's decision for a new trial. The U.S. Supreme Court affirmed the Vermont Supreme Court's decision. The case emphasizes the importance of properly verifying potentially damaging information. In this instance, the error occurred because one of D&B's employees, a 14-year-old boy paid to review Vermont bankruptcy proceedings, had erred and had inadvertently attributed the bankruptcy to Greenmoss. Although D&B's representative testified that it was routine practice to check the accuracy of these types of reports with the businesses themselves, it did not try to verify the information about Greenmoss before reporting it.
