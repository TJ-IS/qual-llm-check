# Key Prescriptions for Strategic Information Systems Planning

ALBERT L. LEDERER AND VIJAY SETHI

ALBERT L. LEDERER is Professor of MIS and Ashland Oil Research Fellow at the University of Kentucky. He holds a Ph.D. in industrial and systems engineering, and an M.S. in computer and information sciences from Ohio State University. He has over ten years of industry experience in MIS. His research in information systems management has appeared in the Journal of Management Information Systems, Information Systems Research, Communications of the ACM, MIS Quarterly, and elsewhere. He is Senior Editor of the Journal of Information Technology Management.

VIJAY SETHI earned his Ph.D. from the Joseph M. Katz Graduate School of Business at the University of Pittsburgh. His research is in the areas of strategic information systems planning, strategic information systems, and information technology productivity. He has published extensively in international journals including Journal of Management Information Systems, Management Science, MIS Quarterly, and Decision Sciences. Dr. Sethi worked on this article at the University of Oklahoma, College of Business Administration, as Associate Professor in the Division of Management. He is now on the faculty of Nanyang Technological University in Singapore.

ABSTRACT: Strategic information systems planning (SISP) has been defined as the process of identifying a portfolio of computer-based applications that will assist an organization in executing its business plans and realizing its business goals. SISP is an important activity for helping information executives and top management identify strategic applications and align IT with business needs. Previous researchers and practitioner observers have identified measures of successful SISP and have recommended many prescriptions for achieving success.

In this research, the SISP experiences of 105 planners contribute a new perspective on these prescriptions and the success of SISP. Planners extensively follow the prescriptions that promote the efficient management of the SISP study. Although they also extensively follow those prescriptions that lead to their own greater satisfaction, they do not so rigorously follow those that meet SISP objectives. However, the fit between information technology capabilities and the needs of the organization is important to them. Furthermore, plan implementation remains critical to meeting SISP objectives.

Combined, these generalizations suggest the central contribution of this research-planner's paradox: The planner must complete the SISP study rapidly to facilitate its implementation but in doing so risks compromising its fit to the organization and therefore reduces its chances of implementation. The planner must thus plan rapidly enough to produce the plan quickly but carefully enough to produce a relevant one.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: MIS implementation, organizational context of MIS, strategic planning for MIS.

STRATEGIC INFORMATION SYSTEMS PLANNING (SISP) HAS LONG BEEN RECOGNIZED for its ability to contribute substantially to organizations. This is largely because it can identify the most desirable information systems applications in which to invest [24]. It can also help an organization use its information systems to carry out its existing business strategies [23]. Moreover, it can help it define new business strategies [44] as well as technology policies and architectures [12].

On the other hand, the failure to effectively execute SISP can cause problems. It can cause lost opportunities, duplicated efforts, incompatible systems, and wasted resources. In fact, today's aggressively and turbulently competitive environment, with its rapidly changing information technology, may exacerbate the dangers of ineffective planning [19]. Hence, it is no surprise that both corporate general managers and information systems executives have viewed improved SISP as a key issue [5, 7, 40].

However, research has shown that many information systems executives find that successfully performing SISP is one of their top challenges $[35, 52]$ . For example, many applications recommended by an SISP study are often disregarded while many other applications, ignored by the study, are implemented $[12, 35]$ . Hence, the failure to execute the plan is a serious problem. In addition to implementation problems, resource and process obstacles can make SISP a challenging endeavor $[12]$ .

Observers have offered a number of prescriptions to make SISP more successful. For example, categorizations of practices have provided a strong conceptual focus on potential prescriptions $[3, 10]$ . Case studies have furnished rich insights from a data architecture perspective $[20, 21]$ . Descriptive surveys of planning practices have offered a broad look at SISP practices across large sets of companies in different countries $[9, 17, 18]$ . Survey research has also suggested specific problem areas to be addressed by practices during SISP $[35]$ .

The objective of this paper is to extend these studies through a comprehensive examination of the prescriptions most associated with successful SISP. The importance of SISP to both general and information systems management and its challenging nature make this research issue significant.

The research described in this paper is a comprehensive, empirical examination of prescriptions for successful SISP. The paper first defines SISP, identifies prescriptions, and discusses measures of its success. It then discusses a survey of practicing SISP planners who followed the prescriptions. Next, it identifies the extent to which SISP planners actually follow particular prescriptions, assesses the extent to which SISP is successful, elucidates the prescriptions associated with more successful and less successful SISP, and discusses whether planners follow the prescriptions associated with successful SISP. The paper concludes with implications for planners and researchers.

## Definition of SISP

SISP HAS BEEN DEFINED AS “THE PROCESS OF IDENTIFYING a portfolio of computer-based applications that will assist an organization in executing its business plans and realizing its business goals” [35, p. 446]. SISP also includes the specification of databases and systems to support those applications. SISP might embrace the selection of rather prosaic applications, from an existing list of possibilities, that would best fill the organization’s current and future needs [6, 30]. SISP might also entail the discovery of new applications with the potential to create an advantage over competitors [3, 8, 28, 37, 42, 44, 45].

To perform SISP, an organization usually carries out a major, intensive study. It follows one of several, similar well-defined and documented methodologies $[1, 6, 13, 26, 27, 30, 33, 36, 50, 55, 57]$ or it customizes its own. It forms committees of users and information systems specialists. It often uses the methodology's vendor for training and guidance. During the multistep study, it defines a portfolio of applications, their priorities, databases, data elements, and a network of computers and communications equipment to support them. The study also provides a schedule for their development and installation.

## Research on Prescriptions

THREE STREAMS OF RESEARCH CAN BE USED FOR DERIVING SISP prescriptions (where the term prescriptions is used to denote recommendations or guidelines, as in [54], for SISP success). First, surveys of SISP practices have used empirical data to examine various aspects of SISP. For example, Galliers' [18] data from 131 corporations in the United Kingdom showed that planners and consultants viewed several practices as critical to successful SISP. (Table 1 summarizes these along with the other studies in this section.) In an earlier survey of eighty Australian companies, Galliers [17] found that respondents had emphasized a similar set of practices. Conrath et al.'s [9] empirical study of 138 Canadian companies showed another set of activities to be highly emphasized.

The second stream of research used case studies and focused largely on data management. Thus, Goodhue et al.'s [20] case studies of data management practices in twenty organizations resulted in several recommendations. In another case study, Brancheau, Schuster, and March [4] identified key practices. A final case study by Goodhue et al. [21] suggested three important controls in SISP.

The third stream of research used conceptual studies to suggest categories of SISP practices. Boynton and Zmud [3] recommended that SISP should include an analysis of nine agendas and identified SISP behaviors that promote these analyses. Hoffer, Michaelle, and Carroll [25] provided a more succinct set of categories by defining five groups of SISP practices. Earl [12] suggested an even broader grouping of three components of SISP.

## The Derivation of Prescriptions

THE THREE STREAMS OF RESEARCH PERMITTED THE AUTHORS to derive a set of comprehensive but parsimonious SISP prescriptions. However, doing so required considering the item specificity or granularity [24] of each prescription. Item specificity (or granularity) refers to the level of abstractness of a prescription. In the case of overlapping prescriptions, the authors chose the more specific and precise one because it describes a more concrete action. For example, instead of the general prescription “link business planning with IS planning,” the authors chose specific linkage strategies such as “SISP planners participate in business planning” and “review the organization’s business plan.” This also helped ensure that the prescriptions have comparable granularity.

The derivation also required that the prescriptions represent all aspects of SISP and thus sample its entire domain. The authors accomplished this by explicitly confirming that the prescriptions tapped all the different categories proposed by such studies as Boynton and Zmud [3]. Moreover, because the prescriptions can be shown to originate from such previous frameworks, their underlying theoretical foundation is demonstrated.

Table 2 lists the SISP prescriptions derived from this literature after collation, summarization, and distillation by the authors from a much longer list. The derivation of the items from past writings, as indicated by the references in the table as well as the research streams, elucidates this foundation and provides preliminary evidence of content validity.

## Content Validity of the Prescriptions

THE CONTENT VALIDITY OF TABLE 2 IS FURTHER CONFIRMED by showing the correspondence of the prescriptions to theoretical frameworks for SISP. One such framework revolves around the notion of a planning system and has been called the input-process-output model [32, 35]. Such a system is driven by a set of inputs and, in turn, produces outputs.

Inputs can be broadly classified into three categories: business strategy, resources, and IS planning goals $[32]$ . Thus, subsets of input prescriptions in Table 2 focus individually on business strategy, resources, and planning goals.

The planning system “includes such things as the schedule of planning activities, the analyses that are performed, the organizational role of participants, and the meetings and reviews that are conducted” [32, p. 104]. Thus, the subsets of the process prescriptions in Table 2 deal with these issues.

Outputs of a planning system describe “the roles and strategies that have been chosen for IS” [32, p. 105]. Thus, subsets of the output prescriptions in Table 2 relate to the strategic role of IS, the applications and data architecture role, and implementation issues in carrying out the IS strategy.

In addition to the input-process-output model, the prescriptions also illustrate other theoretical categories of SISP practices. For example, they embody the nine planning agendas and eleven planning behaviors referred to in Table 1 [3]. This reinforces the comprehensiveness of the prescriptions. Tables 3 and 4 (on page 45) illustrate a prescription corresponding to each agenda and behavior to show that each category is represented. (This and subsequent tables use abbreviated prescriptions. One measure of planning success is the fulfillment of objectives $[58]$ . It has been used to gauge IS planning effectiveness $[46]$ . Table 5 (on page 46) provides a set of SISP objectives identified by previous research. Briefly, they include aligning IT with the business $[31]$ , gaining competitive advantage $[17]$ , identifying new and higher-payback applications $[18]$ , identifying strategic applications $[56]$ , increasing top management commitment $[12]$ , improving communications with users $[39]$ , forecasting IT resource requirements $[39]$ , allocating IT resources $[5]$ , developing an information architecture $[12]$ , and increasing the visibility of IT $[39]$ . The extent to which SISP fulfills these key objectives thus offers one means of assessing SISP success.

Another measure of SISP success that has been employed previously is planner satisfaction [16, 35, 39]. These studies assessed SISP success in terms of the planner's satisfaction with the study's resources, process, output, and implementation. Thus, although both measures—the fulfillment of objectives and planner satisfaction—might likely be correlated, they would still be distinct constructs [49, 58].

## Methodology

THE AUTHORS CONSTRUCTED A FOUR-PART QUESTIONNAIRE TO ANSWER these questions. In the first part, respondents expressed whether or not they had participated in an SISP study. If yes, they answered questions about the characteristics of the study, such as its duration, scope, planning horizon, initiator, and methodology. If no, they were instructed to return the survey to the authors.

In the second section, subjects identified the extent to which they had followed each of the prescriptions in Table 2 in their SISP study. They rated each prescription on a Likert-type scale of 1 to 5 where 1 meant they had followed it “not at all”; 2 meant “to a little extent”; 3 “to some extent”; 4 “to a great extent”; and 5 “to a very great extent.”

The third part of the survey included questions related to SISP success based on the literature reviewed in the Successful SISP section above. Subjects rated their satisfaction with the resources required for the study, process followed by the study, output of the study, and extent of implementation of the results. They also rated the extent to which the study had achieved each objective in Table 5.

The fourth and final part of the survey contained a number of questions related to respondent and organizational characteristics. These were adapted from McLean and Soden [39].

Five experienced strategic IS planners pilot-tested the questionnaire. Two of these subjects completed an earlier version of the survey in the presence of the authors and three completed it in other cities. The authors asked these pilot subjects to identify any omissions, errors, or inconsistencies in the survey. The authors interrupted the two present subjects occasionally with questions about if and when they paused, and why. The other three faxed their completed surveys to the authors along with their notes on it about omissions, errors, or inconsistencies. One of the authors discussed these notes with them by phone.

The pilot resulted in the incorporation of several small changes to the survey. These changes include the rewording of a few prescriptions and other questions to make them clearer, an adjustment to the scale for the extent to which the SISP study followed the prescriptions, and an alteration to the formatting of the instructions to make them easier to follow.

After the pilot, the sample was carefully developed by triangulating information from multiple sources so as to ensure data currency and validity. The 500 largest corporate IS users listed in Information Week were first supplemented with additional companies listed in the Corporate 1000 (a directory of the 1,000 largest manufacturing and service companies). From this set, the sample comprised only those 840 companies whose complete addresses were also listed in The Directory of Top Computer Executives.

Of the 840, 199 firms returned the completed survey for a response rate of 24 percent. One hundred and five (53 percent) of these firms had participated in an SISP study and thus provided usable data. A comparison of early and late respondents (those replying before and after reminders) was done because “significant differences between the two groups on relevant dimensions [would] indicate caution about the representativeness of the sample” [41, p. 267]. The comparison showed little disagreement in SISP experiences. In fact, 68 of the 71 prescriptions showed no significant differences. Such a finding is reasonable and enhances the generalizability of the results to the larger population. (The data do not permit an assessment of the detailed differences between respondents and nonrespondents.)

The response rate of 24 percent is encouraging considering that the questionnaire was seven pages long and fairly complex. Previous surveys of SISP have also been fairly detailed $[17, 22, 46]$ , with some as long as eight $[16, 35]$ to twelve pages $[9]$ . The response rate also exceeds that of other recent surveys of SISP $[16]$ . It thus attests to the fact that the respondents found the topic important.

While the content validity section in the literature review above explained the validation of the prescriptions, validation of the success measures used correlational analysis. The four satisfaction measures each correlated with a similarly scaled independent measure of the subjects' overall satisfaction with the SISP study. Correlation coefficients ranged from 0.58 to 0.85 and were all significant at the 0.001 level. Each of the ten objectives measures correlated with a similarly scaled independent measure of the extent to which SISP objectives were achieved. Correlation coefficients ranged from 0.35 to 0.63, and all were significant at the 0.001 level.

## Characteristics of Respondents and Studies

IN GENERAL, THE SUBJECTS WERE HIGHLY EXPERIENCED. They had worked in their organization for an average of 11.3 years and in IS for an average of 21 years. Their job titles showed them to be generally at fairly high levels in their organization. Although it would be desirable to survey a variety of users and IS professionals for each organization, the views of the subjects in this study are of interest. Moreover, it has been suggested that senior IS managers can provide a broader organizational perspective of IS activities than individual department heads, who might have more parochial views [46].

The organizations represented a wide variety of industries and were typically large. About 532 IS employees and 15,000 total employees worked for each. The annual revenue of the business unit was \$3.72 billion.

The average duration of each study was 6.9 months. Sixty-four (61 percent) of the studies covered the entire enterprise while twenty-seven (25.7 percent) covered the division and five (4.8 percent) covered a functional area. The average planning horizon of the studies was 4.8 years. Forty-one (39 percent) of the studies had a planning horizon of five years while eighteen had a planning horizon of three years. Forty-three (41 percent) of the studies were initiated by top management while fifty-five (42.4 percent) were initiated by MIS management.

These characteristics are fairly similar to those in other studies of SISP [35]. Hence the sample is probably representative.

## 1. Planners Are Very Concerned about the Efficient

## Management of the SISP Study

Five of the top ten most widely followed prescriptions are associated with the management of the SISP study as opposed to, say, analytic or implementation issues. Thus, “Key people on study start to finish” reflects concerns about both the wasted expertise resulting from replacing key people and the time-consuming efforts to educate their replacements. Adherence to this prescription facilitates the efficiency of the SISP study.

“Team members educated about scope, goals”; “Credible team members”; and “Team members chosen for competence” recommend a knowledgeable and competent planning team. Given the complexity of SISP and the volume of detail generated in planning $[21]$ , well-educated, credible, and insightful teams can be expected to work together well and sort through planning details to bring the study to its conclusion more rapidly. Efficiency is also enhanced if the study is narrow and focused, as recommended by “Keep scope manageable.”

Thus, there appears to be greater adherence to recommendations dealing with study management issues than to those related to the analytical or implementation aspects of SISP. In fact, with regard to implementation, “Avoid/dampen resistance” is the least practiced prescription while “Identify bases of resistance” is third least practiced.

## 2. Planners Are Not So Concerned about Meeting SISP Objectives

None of the ten most extensively practiced study management prescriptions is among the ten most highly correlated with meeting SISP objectives. Furthermore, the most practiced prescription, “Key people on study start to finish,” is the tenth-lowest correlate of the success measure of meeting objectives. At the same time, however, nine of the top ten prescriptions (in terms of correlation with the objectives) deal primarily with either implementation or analytical aspects of SISP. These observations intimate that planners are not quite so concerned about meeting SISP objectives.

This generalization, combined with the first one, suggests that efforts to expedite and better manage the SISP study may be necessary but not sufficient for SISP success. Previous results in the literature support this conclusion. The duration of the study is a major feature of unsuccessful SISP $[12]$ and a major problem $[16, 35]$ . Thus, efforts to expedite the study are necessary. However, observations that the duration of the study is not critical to SISP success $[11, 17, 18]$ indicate it is not sufficient. Instead, meeting the objectives of the study are still necessary.

## 3. Planners Follow Prescriptions That Promote Their Own Satisfaction

As mentioned earlier, the prescriptions by extent followed coincide a great deal with the prescriptions by correlation with satisfaction. Thus, the extensively followed “Team members chosen for competence” and “Credible team members” predict satisfaction. Moreover, they facilitate a harmonious planning environment. “Foster team member agreement,” the prescription with the highest correlation between extent practiced and satisfaction, likewise promotes such a collaborative environment. Coupled with the lack of similarity between the prescriptions by extent followed and prescriptions by correlation with objectives, this suggests that SISP may be such an arduous process that planners must place more emphasis on the actions that facilitate their own satisfaction than on those that help meet SISP objectives.

## 4. Planners Are Not Very Concerned with Data Architecture Issues

Data architecture-related prescriptions are neither followed nor associated with successful SISP regardless of how it is measured. Such prescriptions as “Use process diagrams,” “Use process-data matrices,” “Use data-flow diagrams,” “Use entity-relationship models,” and “Data modeling by experts” fall toward the bottom of Tables 6, 9, and 10. In fact, the practice of the specific prescription dealing with architecture implementation (“Specify actions for architecture”) ranks only fortieth as a correlate of the objectives and thirty-fourth as a correlate of satisfaction.

The relative unimportance of data architecture during SISP may be surprising in the light of evidence showing it is a top IS issue $[40]$ . However, data architecture processes and outputs are ambiguous $[21]$ . Thus, in terms of meeting organizational objectives, their benefits are uncertain. This uncertainty, coupled with the time-consuming nature and high cost of architecture activities $[21]$ , may further explain why architecture prescriptions are not followed very much during SISP. Nevertheless, the finding about the relative unimportance of architecture in SISP coincides with the suggestion that SISP now focuses increasingly on applications $[43]$ .

## 5. The Fit between IT Capabilities and the Organization Remains Important to Meeting Objectives

Prescriptions related to the analytical aspects to SISP in the top ten in terms of correlation with meeting objectives are “Assess IT strengths, weaknesses,” “Add value, reduce costs,” and “Examine trends.” Together, they indicate that a “fit” between IT capabilities and environmental and competitive conditions is necessary to realize SISP goals. Others have espoused this notion $[38, 46, 48]$ , and it remains important.

## 6. Plan Implementation Is Critical to Meeting SISP Objectives

The prescription most highly correlated with meeting objectives is “Prepare migration plan.” Two other implementation prescriptions are top correlates with the objectives “Identify actions to adopt plan” and “Identify resources for new tools.” Thus, the extent to which SISP meets its objectives is highly related to the formulation of the details of implementing new and key applications such as the identification of their priorities, resource requirements, and subsequent MIS actions. The reason may be that such specifics facilitate the transition from plans to action $[25]$ and therefore enable the development of key projects and the realization of organizational goals. These results also explain why “substantial further analysis” ranks as a top SISP problem [35].

Combined, the generalizations above suggest the planner's paradox: The planner must complete the SISP study rapidly to facilitate its implementation but in doing so risks compromising its fit to the organization and therefore reduces its chances of implementation. Such contradictory requirements are perhaps best explained by the fact that unless the SISP study is completed in a reasonable period of time, organizational, business, and technology changes can render the plan obsolete [21]. However, expediting the study may result in the identification of applications that do a poorer job of meeting SISP objectives and are thus less likely to be implemented. Perhaps the top two prescriptions in Table 9 illustrate the paradox most clearly: The first, "Prepare migration plan," addresses the details required for implementation, while the second, "Get approximate requirements," speaks to the need for a swift completion of the study.

## Conclusion: Implications for Planners and Researchers

IF BUSINESS AND INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY WERE STABLE rather than rapidly changing, and if large-scale information systems applications could be built swiftly at marginal cost and cobbled together into accessible architectures, then SISP would probably be unnecessary. However, because information technology is increasingly volatile and complex, and because business is fiercely competitive, more than ever planners need guidance for SISP and investigators need direction for future research. The study described here contributes such guidance and direction.

## Implications for Planners

For guidance to SISP planners, this paper contributes a comprehensive list of SISP prescriptions in Table 7. SISP planners can assess each prescription and determine which to follow in their particular situation. The research in this paper contributes some evidence about which prescriptions might work and which might not. However, planners should judge each on its merits with regard to their needs. Each may have a value.

For example, planners do not follow “Use external consultants” very much and even when they do, such use is favorably associated with neither the satisfaction nor the objectives success measures. Consultants, however, can have great value as a source of information about SISP because they have learned from many organizations. Thus, planners need to make careful judgments about when and how to use them.

Although judgment is necessary for each prescription, this research contributes the implication that, in general, some may deserve more attention than others. For example, the implementation and analysis items near the top of Table 9 probably need more emphasis. Likewise, “Top management educated about IT” from the top of Table 9 probably deserves more adherence than “Management approve project charter” from the bottom. Since implementation is critical and subtle resistance can prevent it, “Avoid/dampen resistance” probably deserves to be followed more.

In addition to considering specific prescriptions, this research contributes the recommendation that planners probably need to refocus their SISP efforts on meeting their objectives. Because SISP participants typically compete for scarce IS resources, it may be easier to minimize conflict and conduct a study that satisfies themselves rather than one that truly meets their organization's objectives. However, the need to meet objectives remains paramount.

Finally, this research contributes the notion of an SISP planner's paradox: Planners are challenged to complete their study quickly and inexpensively so as to maintain management interest and to finish the study before requirements change. However, in completing the study quickly, they risk developing a superficial plan that is not very valuable and may even be ignored. Thus, they should weigh carefully their need to complete their study quickly against the consequences of a superficial study. Faced with this planner's paradox, it is reasonable to carry out the study with alacrity, but at the same time it is essential to ensure that it provides a plan that is useful enough to guide the organization into the future. SISP planners face this paradox and the compromises it requires.

## Implications for Researchers

The current research has contributed to our understanding of the challenges of SISP. Likewise, it suggests several avenues for further research that might extend its findings.

For example, the current research has identified the prescriptions most highly correlated with successful SISP. Researchers could ask its central question—What activities facilitate successful SISP?—in different ways. One alternative would be to ask planners directly to assess how much each prescription contributed to their SISP efforts.

This research relied heavily on planners' perceptions. Future researchers might attempt to ask the central question using less perceptual measures. Alternative measures of successful SISP might be the proportion of completed projects from the plan or the return on investment of the IT applications from the plan.

Information systems department professionals might not be the only parties knowledgeable about SISP. Hence, future researchers might also ask the central question by considering the views of business managers and other participants from outside the MIS function.

The current research considered the correlation between practices and success and hence did not emphasize the difficult issue of causality. Thus, it opens the way for a more in-depth examination of SISP wherein causality might be better assessed. Future researchers might then ask, what causes successful SISP?

Future researchers might try to classify SISP studies as focusing on their own specific objectives and then consider whether some prescriptions are more associated with their success. For example, researchers might ask, if some SISP studies focus on gaining a competitive advantage, which prescriptions best serve it? This might also shed light on the answer to the central question.

On the other hand, the prescriptions, considered individually in this study, might be reduced to a more succinct set in future studies through factor-analytic techniques. The prescriptions were considered individually because the objectives of this study were to compile and examine them as such with regard to their practice and success. But, this done, the search for underlying themes might be undertaken. Data reduction would also be preferable from a statistical perspective because it allows the use of sophisticated multivariate analysis tools without violating the degrees of freedom constraint for a given sample size.

Contingency theory would be an appropriate means to study SISP. Future research could thus explore the effects of a variety of variables on successful SISP. For example, researchers might ask, how do company size, industry, planning methodology used, and the like affect the success of the prescriptions?

Future researchers might attempt to understand the conclusions about the prescriptions of this research through structured interviews in case studies of planners from ongoing or recently completed SISP projects. Researchers might ask subjects to explain why some practices are more popularly carried out and why others are more often associated with successful SISP.

Finally, this research helped elucidate the planner's paradox; however, future research is needed to enable planners to address it. Such research could result in guidance as to the duration and depth of an SISP study. This guidance might be a function of the organization's particular industry, IT usage, managerial experience, SISP objectives, or other variables. It might at last answer the question, how much time and effort should be expended on planning?
