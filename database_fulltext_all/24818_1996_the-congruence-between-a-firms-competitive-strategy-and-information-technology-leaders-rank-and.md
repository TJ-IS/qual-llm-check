RECENT TRENDS IN THE INTENSITY OF GLOBAL COMPETITION have made firms face (1) new competitive challenges, such as increasing product quality, decreasing time to market, and business process reengineering, and (2) new technological challenges, such as migrating to more cost-effective and flexible computing systems, understanding and implementing client–server technology, and dealing with tighter budgets and hidden costs [5, 32, 44]. These new challenges have forced firms to use their information technology (IT) resources for competitive reasons and to require new roles and skills from their IT leaders. $^{1}$

As more executives recognize the power of IT to reengineer their business and improve its effectiveness, many chief executive officers (CEOs) and line managers are looking for chief information systems officers (CIOs) who not only understand technology but also comprehend technology's potential to affect business strategy $[51, 52]$ . In particular, the single-threaded role that required IT leaders with hands-on technology management skills has now changed to a multidimensional role that is more linked to corporate business strategy and requires IT leaders with business knowledge and skills $[51, 52]$ . For the IT leaders, however, rising to such numerous, and often conflicting, challenges is not easy. Indeed, there is a higher than average corporate dismissal rate and shorter tenure for IT leaders compared with other top executives $^{2}$ $[52, 57, 67]$ . Recent surveys have shown that (1) CIO turnover has increased for the sixth straight year, and (2) over the past year, top technology positions were filled with people with business backgrounds twice as often as they were filled with people with computer backgrounds $[78]$ .

Previous research has also reported that (1) a firm's competitive strategy has a significant impact on the firm's IT strategic orientation and on the firm's IT use [10, 20, 26]; (2) the role and qualifications of the IT leader should reflect the firm's IT strategic orientation and the firm's IT use [12, 20, 79]; (3) the rank of the IT leader has significant linkages to the firm's IT strategic orientation [63, 64]; and (4) IT management decision-making strategies should be aligned with the firm's business strategies [9, 21]. Therefore, there are reasons to believe that a firm's competitive strategy may be related to or have an impact on the rank and role of its IT leader. In spite of the fact that evaluating the rank and role of the IT leaders has become an important IT management issue for both researchers and practitioners [2, 6, 11, 22, 24, 25, 56, 66], there are no empirical studies, to our knowledge, that have addressed the relationship between a firm's competitive strategy and the rank and the role of its IT leader. The need for studying such a relationship has also been advocated by Premkumar and King [61].

The important theoretical issue discussed in this paper is the extent to which a firm's competitive strategy affects the firm's IT leader's rank and role. Based on the responses from 213 IT managers, the results of this study indicate that, in the financial services industry, IT leaders' rank and role must be aligned with a firm's competitive strategy. A misalignment between a firm's competitive strategy and the rank and role of IT leaders may have an adverse effect on firms [3, 15, 28, 30, 45].

## Background

THE RESEARCH RELATED TO COMPETITIVE STRATEGY, “role of the IT leader,” and the “rank of the IT leader” forms the basis for this study.

## Competitive Strategy

Several appropriate typologies of firms in an industry have been set forth in the strategic management and organizational behavior literature $[1, 13, 47, 69]$ . The Miles and Snow $[49]$ typology, however, is unique because it views the firm as a complete and integrated system in dynamic interaction with its environment $[38]$ . A recent study by Doty et al. $[19]$ suggested that Miles and Snow's theory is a very powerful predictor of a firm's effectiveness. It has also been used in IT and management strategy literature (for a review, see $[86]$ ). Tavakolian $[80]$ , for example, used the Miles and Snow typology in describing the fit between a firm's strategy and IT structure.

Briefly, Miles and Snow postulated that competing firms within an industry exhibit patterns of behavior representative of four basic competitive strategy types: (1) defenders, (2) prospectors, (3) analyzers, and (4) reactors. The key dimension underlying this typology is the firm's response to changing environmental conditions; that is, the rate at which a firm changes its products or markets to maintain alignment with its environment. Each of these strategy types is richly described as a unique configuration of contextual, structural, and strategic factors. Miles and Snow posited that at least three of these ideal types—the prospector, analyzer, and defender—were effective forms of organization and that the relative effectiveness of the types varied with the context. Snow and Hrebiniak [73], for example, found that, in most environments, firms classified as analyzers were generally the most effective and that, in highly regulated environments, reactors were more effective than prospectors and defenders. ## Role of IT Leader

The evolution of the role of the IT leader in relation to recent developments in IT organizations and their functions has been extensively discussed in the IT literature $[2, 6, 8, 11, 20, 25, 66, 76]$ . In an earlier article, Rockart et al. $[66]$ predicted a “new” and evolving role for the CIO. More specifically, they predicted three emerging roles for CIOs: (1) decentralization of line responsibilities to divisions and departments, (2) staff orientation, and (3) corporate responsibility for information resource policy and strategy. In a followup study of twenty CIOs, Benjamin et al. $[6]$ confirmed that the above three predictions have been realized. Later, it was predicted that, in five years, virtually every major firm will have a CIO who is a peer to the CEO $[67]$ . To the contrary, more recently, some researchers and practitioners have predicted the end of the “new era” for the CIO $[39, 53]$ . They suggest that the CIO role will recede into the background as IT becomes more accessible $[38]$ . “Not all IS executives should expect to be invited into their company’s management inner circle now or in near future. . . . [Moreover] as the technology becomes more reliable and dependable, the strategic role of the CIO could go away,” says Max Hopper, IS vice president and director of American Airlines’ computerized reservation system, SABRE $[53]$ . Recently, based on studies of CIOs in sixty firms, Earl and Feeny $[21]$ concluded that the CIO’s ability to add value is the biggest single factor in determining whether a firm views IT as an asset or a liability. They further suggested that “the CIO adds value by building informed relationships with key executives, making sure that IT requirements become an integral component of business strategy.” The most significant change in recent years has been CIOs’ increased involvement and leadership role in the firms’ business process reengineering efforts $[35]$ .

Keen [44] suggested that too many firms create CIOs simply by promoting their data-processing managers. The role and qualifications of the IT leader should be radically changed so that it is consistent with the needs of the firm in identifying and exploiting the opportunities to use IT [12, 79]. Cash et al. [12] contended that, while

for some firms, [IT] activities represent an area of great strategic importance; for other firms they play, and appropriately will continue to play, a cost-effective, useful, but distinctly supportive role. It is inappropriate for firms with supportive IT to expect that the same amount of senior management strategic thinking should be devoted to the IT organization as in firms of the former type.

When IT served a strictly supportive function in firms, it was all right for the IT leader to be a technical expert and competent manager. In the “information era” of 1990s, however, he or she has to act as a link between IT and other firm executives [2, 85].

Earl [20] stated that IT leaders see themselves as corporate officers and general business managers. He suggested that they must be politically savvy and that their high profile places them in contention for top-line management jobs. Further, he pointed out four “leadership” qualities for the IT leaders, namely: (1) business leadership—for connecting the use of the IT with the business needs and strategy; (2) technology leadership—for shaping appropriate technology policies for the firm; (3) organizational leadership—for steering IT arrangements and making them work; and (4) functional leadership—for managing the function and its specialist groups. According to Earl [20], firms in each of the four cells of the McFarlan “strategic grid” [48] need different types of IT leaders to manage their IT functions. In a firm where IT has primarily a support role, it may be acceptable to have an IT leader who spends the lion’s share of his or her time in interacting with the employees within the IT organization. However, in firms where IT has a strategic orientation, and where IT is critical in achieving corporate objectives, IT leaders should have multidimensional roles. They should have business, strategic, and political skills, and a conceptual and visionary mind. A factory situation requires strong hands-on, performance-oriented IT leaders with technical and managerial skills. Turnaround firms should look for visionary, strong champions who have an excellent understanding of the business and are proactive to lead their IT. Support firms can tolerate a service-oriented technical leader with a hands-on internal style.

Applegate and Elam [2] indicated that the IT leader is becoming a member of the top management team and participates in a firm's strategy development. However, according to Thomas M. Lodahl, chairman of CogniTech services of Easton, “only about 30 percent of top IT managers are included in high-level strategic planning, and there is still a perception on the part of most CEOs that IT strategy can be developed independently of business strategy. It can’t” [51]. Other recent studies have also indicated new roles and responsibilities for the IT leader [2, 25, 36, 52]. These new responsibilities include: spending more time with business and in business training, building partnerships with business units and line management, focusing on improving business processes, and explaining IT cost in business terms. The results of these studies indicate that today’s IT leader is more a managerially oriented executive than a technical manager [29]. The problem for IT leaders is not one of acknowledging the needed change in their roles and responsibilities; it is one of adapting to it successfully [6].

## Rank of IT Leader

Ives and Olson's [41] study suggested that when IT leaders' reporting level is low, they tend to be involved in more activities per day, each of shorter duration, than leaders in positions of higher authority. Lower-ranking IT leaders tend to remain in their territory and spend considerably more time interacting with their organizational subordinates [77]. On the other hand, higher-ranking leaders spend more time interacting with those outside the IT unit or with groups having participants from IT and other areas [76]. In fact, firms with IT leaders having the title CIO spend significantly more time away from the firm. This was corroborated by a recent study of 500 CIOs conducted by Coopers and Lybrand/Datamation. The CIOs were asked: "How does a CIO spend his or her time?" Participants in the study said they spend 23 percent of their time—the largest percentage—working with business unit peers, while IT-specific planning consumed 21 percent of the CIO's time (the second most time-consuming item). The CIOs surveyed also spent an average of 16 percent of their time on administration, 13 percent on managing operations, and 10 percent on corporate strategic planning [11].

Previous research has reported that IT leaders who have two-way conversations with the CEO have a better understanding of the goals, objectives, and direction of the firm and they have specialized information that will benefit the CEO $[25, 31, 85]$ . A recent survey of 300 CIOs reported that 40 percent report to the firm's chief operating officer, only 10 percent of CIOs take part in strategic planning, and even fewer report to the CEO $[67]$ . In addition, IT leaders may not actually possess strategic influence with top management and they may lack operational and tactical influence with users $[11, 85]$ . Some specific problems may include higher than average corporate dismissal rates compared with other top executives, high expectations for new strategic systems that IT leaders may not be able to deliver, and lack of power bases due to the fact that CIOs are viewed as outsiders by other top executives $[67, 78]$ .

WE NOW ATTEMPT TO DEVELOP OUR HYPOTHESES to establish linkages between a firm's competitive strategy and the role and rank of IT leaders.

## Competitive Strategy and IT Leaders' Role

It has been argued that IT is a resource that must support the competitive strategy of a firm $[26]$ . Accordingly, firms must develop IT strategies that link the exploitation of IT to their competitive strategies $[61]$ . Previous research on the impact of competitive strategy on IT management strategies has shown that (1) a firm's competitive strategy is strongly related to the degree of centralization of IT structure $[80]$ , (2) multiple competitive strategies can cause multiple forms of IT adoption $[14, 40, 43, 60]$ , (3) competitive strategy has a significant impact on the correlation between IT adoption and business performance $[26, 72]$ , and (4) IT management decision-making strategies should be in alignment with a firm's business strategy $[9]$ . A misalignment between competitive strategy and IT management strategy would result in situations where IT becomes a “competitive burden” $[12, 84]$ and may result in decreased business performance $[26]$ .

Competitive strategy, therefore, is an important factor in predicting the nature of IT use and its structure, and in assessing IT strategic orientation in a firm and its IT management decision-making strategies. Firms that are heavily reliant on IT for conducting their value-chain activities and business strategies would be more likely to accord higher rank and a more influential role to their IT leaders. These, in turn, influence the IT leaders' roles in setting the IT management strategies. For the past several years, the alignment of IT management strategies with competitive strategies has consistently been a major concern of IT leaders $[55]$ . Thus, there are theoretical reasons to believe that competitive strategy is an important factor in assessing the IT leaders' role.

Prospectors tend to pursue an aggressive competitive strategy pioneering products and markets. They operate in an environment characterized by rapid and unpredictable changes. Understanding the changes and innovation in the industry and the uncertainty of the actions of competitors and customers will require firms' leaders to spend more time scanning their firm's environment [16, 17]. Prospectors are, therefore, expected to focus their scanning activities on opportunities for differentiation of products, markets, and services. The firm's IT should be designed to facilitate the acquisition and distribution of such information. Because of the firm's continuous dependence on new and multiple technologies for product and market development, the role of the IT leaders is, in turn, characterized by (1) spending more time scanning their firm's environment for new development in IT, (2) scrutinizing the internal environment for opportunities to use new IT innovations, (3) linking IT management strategies to their firm's business strategy, and (4) developing solid relationships with the senior management team.

Analyzers are a “unique combination of Prospector and Defender types” [49. p. 68]. Analyzers adopt some characteristics of prospectors and some characteristics of defenders, seeking effectiveness through both efficiency and new product and markets. Analyzers need to keep a balance in locating new market opportunities and maintaining their existing customer base. The IT leaders in these firms, in turn, have to maintain an equilibrium between conflicting demands for technological flexibility and for technological stability. Analyzers are expected to make choices typical of prospectors in their newer and more dynamic endeavors (such as spending time to scan their environment), while adopting a strategy typical to defenders in their traditional and stable lines of business [81].

The defender is a less dynamic form of firm strategy operating in an environment that is more stable and predictable than that of the prospector. This more stable environment allows defenders to engage in less environmental scanning. Defenders are expected (1) to depend on single-core technology for cost reduction, (2) to favor IT developed internally, (3) to form limited alliances with external sources for their technology, and (4) to overlook new market development in IT [81]. Hence, IT leaders in firms using these strategies are required to make continuous improvement in existing technology to maintain efficiency.

Reactors are firms with no distinct competitive strategic orientation. In a reactor firm, in the absence of a clear strategy, decisions are made in a reactive rather than a proactive mode. The need for environmental scanning, long-range forecasting, and planning is minimal because the environment will favorably support almost anything the firm does or does not do [19]. As a result, there is no reason to believe that reactors will follow a specific strategy to secure IT resources. In such firms, IT leaders are more likely to be required to run efficient data centers than to affect the firm's business strategy.

Both defenders and reactors are concerned about cost reduction and efficiency. Defenders, however, have a clear strategy to control cost by depending on the internal resources. Like prospectors, analyzers must also engage in environmental scanning. IT may play a more strategic role in these firms compared with defenders and reactors.

This leads us to our first research hypothesis:

H1: The role of the IT leader varies significantly according to a firm's competitive strategy.

## Competitive Strategy and IT Leaders' Rank

The rank of an individual in a firm reflects the formal organizational authority and power inherent in a position. That is, the higher the rank, the greater the authority and power to influence the firm's actions [33]. Cash et al. [12] pointed out that where IT is considered strategic, the IT manager's rank "should be very high," and where IT is supportive, the rank "can be low." In firms pursuing a prospector strategy, for example, a low-ranking IT leader would have difficulty in getting the necessary information from general management in the planning process. According to Cash et al. [12, p. 637], "If the corporate communication culture at the top is informal, this low status can be fatal, as the IT manager will be outside the key communication loop."

Raghunathan and Raghunathan [63], based on a study of 192 IT leaders, concluded that there are significant relationships between the rank of an IT leader and a firm's IT strategic orientation, and the importance given to IT planning. Further, in firms where the IT leader reports at the highest level, he or she plays an expanded role [64]. When the rank of the IT leader is very high, he or she would be able to have a close communication with the top executives through his or her membership on the firm's executive management team.

In the prospector and analyzer firms, IT leaders need to influence other executives toward recognizing the importance and value of information technology in the development of new products and markets. On the other hand, defender and reactor firms may not need IT leaders in higher ranks, because it is more likely that in these firms IT may not play a strategic role.

This leads to our second research hypothesis:

H2: The rank of the IT leader varies significantly according to a firm's competitive strategy.

Applegate and Elam [2] found that the rank of an IT leader in a firm is also dependent on his or her hiring status—that is, whether he or she is an internal or an external hire.

They classified IT leaders as internal hires if they had been with the firm for more than five years at the time they assumed the IT leadership position. Individuals were classified as external hires if they had been with the firm for five years or less. They found that external-hire IT leaders tend to assume their position at a higher rank in the firm than the internal-hire IT leaders.

Prospector and analyzer firms have a tendency to preclude the exclusive use of internal sources to meet their IT needs due to the diversity of information needs. Because of their continuing engagement in new business, prospectors, and to a lesser degree analyzers, may emphasize external sources over internal sources in acquiring IT $[81]$ . This implies that they may prefer externally hired over internally hired IT leaders. Defenders, however, are expected to favor the use of internal IT resources to develop IT internally in order to reduce cost. The emphasis on cost reduction is expected to motivate defenders, and to a lesser degree reactors, to favor internally hired over externally hired IT leaders. This leads to our third research hypothesis:

H3: The status of the IT leader varies significantly according to a firm's competitive strategy.

## Research Methodology

WE NOW DEAL WITH THE ITEMS USED TO MEASURE VARIOUS CONSTRUCTS and data collection procedures.

## Measurement of Strategic-Type Variables

As set forth by Snow and Hambrick [73], at least four alternative approaches are available for identifying and measuring strategy variables: (1) investigator inference, (2) self-typing, (3) external assessment, and (4) objective indicators. This study used the self-typing approach, whereby IT leaders assessed the strategies of their own firms using descriptions of the four strategies in the Miles and Snow typology.

The descriptions given in the appendix were originally developed by Miles and Snow [49] and have since been used in a number of other studies in this area [42, 47, 74, 80, 81]. The terms “Defender,” “Prospector,” “Analyzer,” and “Reactor” were not used on the questionnaire. Rather, each description was prefaced by a categorization of “Type 1,” “Type 2,” “Type 3,” and “Type 4,” each corresponding to the appropriate strategic type. Recently extensive reliability and validity studies were conducted by Shortell and Zajac [71] on the Miles and Snow strategy typologies with excellent results.

## Measures for IT Leaders' Role

The research literature related to the role of the IT leaders forms the rationale for the questions selected in this part. Rockart et al. [66] pointed out eight attributes that successful IT leaders should possess:

1. They see themselves as corporate officers.

2. They are seen by others as corporate officers.

3. They are general business managers, not IT specialists.

4. They are candidates for top-line management jobs.

5. They see the IT function as critical to company success.

6. They have a high-profile image in their firms.

7. They have political as well as rational perspectives.

8. They have a clear view of their own critical success factors.

The attributes identified above serve as job specifications implicit for successful IT leaders and are used to measure the role of the IT leaders in this study. These eight attributes formed the basis for eight questions in the questionnaire. Besides these eight questions, two more questions were added to measure the new responsibilities of the IT leaders: (1) spending more time outside the IT department focusing on the strategic and organizational aspects of IT; and (2) spending more time inside the IT department managing the function on a day-to-day basis $[2, 53]$ .

## Data Collection Procedure

The questionnaire sought responses using a five-point Likert-type scale that ranged from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). IT leaders were asked to indicate the extent of their role as IT leaders in their firm. In addition, they were asked to assess the competitive strategy of their firms using descriptions of the four types suggested by Miles and Snow. Finally, demographic data about the respondents and their firms were obtained.

The questionnaire was pretested, and several recommended changes made by a number of academics and practitioners were incorporated into the final instrument. Pretesting to identify construction defects was an integral component in the design of our questionnaire. Pilot tests of the questionnaire were conducted using financial services firms, drawn from the actual survey population, who were not included in the sample. They were asked to carefully scrutinize the survey and report any defects or inadequacies to the researchers conducting the study. In addition, the colleagues in our respective departments, who understood the study's purpose and hypotheses to be tested, thoroughly examined the questionnaire in terms of whether it would achieve the objectives of the study. Finally, additional insight was provided by several IT practitioners from local firms who were solicited to help pretest the questionnaire. They were asked their general impression of the questionnaire as well as the adequacy of individual questions and to identify problems that might otherwise be overlooked.

The questionnaires (and explanatory cover letters) were sent to approximately 1,035 randomly selected IT leaders in the financial service industry in the following eleven states: CA, FL, IN, MI, MN, NY, NJ, OH, PA, TX, and WI. $^{3}$ Of those mailed, 34 were postmarked undeliverable and 213 were completed and returned, yielding a response rate of 21.3 percent. This is a typical response rate for studies of this kind [46, 65, 82].

We performed two tests to check for nonrespondent bias. No statistically significant differences were found between responding and nonresponding firms using the categorical variables firm type and number of employees (chi square = 10.77 and 9.21, respectively, p > 0.05). There was little reason to suspect that IT leaders who did not respond to the questionnaire perceived their role much differently from the managers who did respond since the mix of the respondents included all the sectors within the financial services industry.

A top-level executive of the IT department responded to the survey. The use of a single respondent might be thought to create information bias. However, since the target respondent was a top-level IT executive, and since no other executive in a firm would have the necessary broad view to respond to our survey's inquiries, other approaches were found to be infeasible to accomplish the study's objectives. It was deemed important to have multiple respondents in each firm to classify the firm based on its competitive strategy. However, the limited research budget, combined with the demands of questionnaire-based research, restricted us from identifying other top executives in the firm to participate in the survey. A multiple respondents approach could be expected to increase the reliability of the strategy type data, since different executives may perceive a firm's strategy differently. However, it was not employed and this is acknowledged in the limitations section of the paper.

The financial services industry was selected primarily because it is an information-intensive industry in which IT is playing a strategic role $[4, 18, 27, 48, 54, 69, 83]$ , and it has been undergoing dramatic changes that have affected its entire structure; the industry is now faced with market conditions completely different from those prevailing less than a decade ago $[68]$ . In a recent study, to test the usefulness of the Miles and Snow strategic typology in the field of the marketing strategy, the financial services industry was also selected as representative of an industry responding to recent environmental changes $[47]$ .

## Analysis and Results

THREE BASIC ASSUMPTIONS IMPLICIT IN THE RELEVANT LITERATURE as well as subsequent empirical investigation of the Miles and Snow [49] typology [47, 74] are: (1) since reactors lack a consistent strategy and simply respond to environmental pressures when forced to do so, they were included in this discussion only as illustrations of extreme cases; (2) though there are three distinct strategic types, prospectors tend to be more similar to analyzers than to defenders, and (3) these strategic types are significant determinants of the IT leaders' rank and role when demographic factors, such as size (e.g., number of employees), and type (e.g., bank, insurance) are held constant.

To examine these assumptions, an analysis that is similar to one employed in a number of other studies of this type $[46, 73, 80]$ is used. There are four steps in this analysis. First, descriptive statistics on variables of interest are computed. Second, the reliability of the scale items designed to measure the IT leaders' role construct is determined. Third, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) comparison of means for the IT leaders' role construct is performed to test whether the importance of the role varies with the firm's competitive strategy and the combined rank and status of the IT leader. Fourth, a Duncan range test is performed to determine which of the means for IT leader's role construct differ for the strategy types and the combined rank and status of IT leader.

## Demographic Variables

The survey provides the data on the sectors within the financial services industry representing their annual sales, the rank of the CIO, the number of employees primarily involved in IT, the size of the IT budget per year, and personal information of the respondents. Table 2a shows the general information about the firms and personal information about the respondents. Most (82 percent) of the firms were banking and insurance firms. About 55 percent of the firms had more than 500 employees. Half these firms had sales of more than \$2.5 billion and have on average an annual IT budget of \$17.8 million. For these firms, the mean number of employees involved in the IT department or function was 129. Over half the respondents reported that they had an undergraduate degree. Typically, these respondents have been with their company for fourteen years and in their current position for about seven years. To ensure that the questionnaires were answered by the intended informants, that is, top-level executive of the IT department, the respondents were asked to indicate their job title at the end of the questionnaire. Table 2a also shows the tabulation and classification based on these titles. Thus, it appears that the respondents are top-level IT executives and can be expected to provide adequate answers to questions on their firm's competitive strategy as well as their roles.

From the 213 financial institutions responding to the survey, the self-typing approach in identifying the competitive strategy of firms yielded the following breakdown of strategic types: 60 defenders, 30 prospectors, 100 analyzers, 16 reactors, and 7 unclassified. Table 2b and figure 1 (on page 78) show the sample characteristics by the strategic and firm types. Table 2b indicates that the relationship between strategy type and number of employees is significant (p = 0.0014). This result is consistent with those obtained by Smith, Guthrie, and Chen [72], who found that the effectiveness of different categories of competitive strategy is contingent on firm size. The table suggests that all four strategies are pursued in the financial services industry with defenders, prospectors, and analyzers far outnumbering the reactors. This is because a reactor strategy is not a viable one for a firm to choose simply because the firm would be unable to sustain itself in a highly competitive market place for very long. Since, in general, reactors do not use IT in a strategic manner, as expected, they have smaller IT departments as compared with firms with defender, prospector, or analyzer competitive strategies. As shown in Table 2b, by partitioning the total chi square, it is possible to conclude that prospectors differ significantly from the other three strategic types with respect to the number of employees.

Figure 1 shows that the type of firm (i.e., banking versus insurance) is significantly related to the competitive strategy pursued $p = 0.00027$ . It shows that among the prospector firms, 43 percent are insurance companies (highest) and 20 percent banks (lowest). Until recently, banks were heavily regulated and had an extremely conservative business posture; on the other hand, insurance companies and brokerage firms have long been intruding in the traditional activities of banks, such as credit cards, ATM facilities, and personal loans [68]. With the deregulation and globalization of financial markets, banks are assuming a very aggressive posture. It is our belief that in the future the relationship between firm type and strategy may not hold. The partitioned chi square also revealed that prospector firms differed significantly from the other three strategic types with respect to the type of firm.

## IT Leader's Role

The IT leader's role construct was measured with multiple items aimed at tapping the underlying theoretical dimension of attributes that successful IT leaders should possess. The eight attributes identified by Rockart et al. [66], and two additional attributes derived from the literature [2, 53], comprised our construct to measure the IT leader's role. After the removal of two items based on small corrected item total correlations, the IT leader's role construct consisted of eight items and had a reliability of 0.7345 (see Table 3).

## Competitive Strategy and Role of IT Leader

The next step in the analysis was to determine whether the role of the IT leader would vary with a firm's competitive strategy. This was accomplished through a univariate analysis of variance procedure for differences in the mean values of the IT leader's role across the four strategy types. If there is a significant difference in the mean values for the IT leader's role, then at least one of the competitive strategy types differs from the others. Consequently, we can conclude that firms with different strategic types differ with respect to the role of their IT leaders.

## IT Leaders' Rank and Status

Table 5 shows the relationship between a firm's competitive strategy and the rank of the IT leader. As shown in Table 5, although the competitive strategy is not significantly related to the IT leader's rank, it is, however, significantly related to the IT leader's rank and status combined. This implies that our hypothesis (H2) can be supported by data only when the status of the IT leader is taken into account. Applegate and Elam [2] suggested that external hires tend to assume the IT leader position at a higher rank as compared with internal hires. Table 5 indicates that the percentage of the sample reporting directly to the CEO or one or two levels below the CEO for analyzer firms in both external-hire and internal-hire categories are approximately the same—that is, 84 percent (51 out of 61) and 82 percent (32 out of 39), respectively. Our data do not corroborate Applegate and Elam's observation. One explanation, albeit weak, could be that Applegate and Elam collected their data from January 1986 to January 1989 (some eight years ago) and their sample size was relatively small (81 firms).

Table 6 shows that competitive strategy is not related to the status of the IT leader and therefore H3 is not supported. Although the chi-square test of the relationship between strategy and status is not significant, Table 6 confirms the previous results found by Applegate and Elam [2], that in the financial services industry most of the IT leaders are external hires (61 percent versus 39 percent). This implies that, in this industry, firms prefer to hire IT leaders from outside. This may be due to the fact that firms such as banks are going through a major transformation as a result of deregulation. Firms in the process of transformation and change often find the requirements for leadership significantly different from before [59]. Moreover, individuals with such requirements may not be readily available within the firm since the need for such skills did not previously exist.

We compared the rank of the internal hires for the IT leader's role. In particular, we would expect the means of the IT leader's role to differ depending on the rank. The mean value of the roles for the internal-hire leaders, who are closer in rank below the CEO, should be higher than internal-hire leaders who are, for example, three or more levels below the CEO. Based on the statistically significant results of a one-way ANOVA, we conclude that at least two of the three groups of internal hires are associated with different means for the IT leader's role ( $F = 3.2288$ ; prob. = 0.0450; d.f. = 2, 77). The results of the Duncan range test, as shown in Table 7, imply that the internal-hire IT leaders who are one level (G1) below the CEO have significantly higher mean roles than the internal-hire IT leaders who are three or more ranks below the CEO (G3).

## Discussion and Implications

THE PURPOSE OF THIS PAPER WAS TO EXAMINE THE RELATIONSHIP between a firm's competitive strategy and the role and rank of its IT leader. The study shows that a firm's competitive strategy must be aligned with the role and rank of its IT leader. In addition, in financial institutions, the vast majority of IT leaders are external hires (61 percent). This may be due to the fact that the financial services industry is being presented with new opportunities or problems at an escalating rate. Concomitantly, the requirements for IT leadership are changing very rapidly, leading to an instability in leadership [51, 52, 59]. In spite of the fact that having an IT leader spend an extended length of time in the saddle may lead to stagnation and organizational myopia [50], high levels of turnover can have a dysfunctional impact on organizational effectiveness [15]. This is especially so in the financial services industry, where IT plays a strategic role [4, 18, 27, 48, 54, 69, 83]. We therefore offer the following recommendations.

First, CEOs charged with the responsibility of hiring IT leaders should examine with extreme rigor the current and future needs of the firm and align them with the qualification and experience of the prospective candidates. Misalignment may result in role conflict and role ambiguity, which, in turn, may result in lower job satisfaction and organizational commitment, and eventually lower than expected performance and dismissal. High levels of turnover pose a challenge for CEOs to sensitize the new IT leader about the existing culture of the firm $[37, 70, 73]$ . Ideally, firms should look for an individual with strong business, technical, and organizational experience $[2, 52]$ . Firms, however, may find it difficult to find an ideal candidate. It may, therefore, be necessary in such situations for a CEO (1) to make the CIO a true member of top management team and to ensure that CIOs receive peer acceptance of their role as a member of top management team, and (2) to develop mentoring programs to provide a broad knowledge of the firm and access to a broad network of organizational contacts to strengthen the new IT leader's business understanding and to supplement any areas where his or her knowledge may be insufficient. The CEO and IT leader should develop a partnership that allows them two-way communication. This partnership is necessary and beneficial as they need to utilize IT as a strategic resource and to align the role of the CIO and the IS organization with the firm's changing competitive strategies. These can be instrumental in educating the CIO as well as challenging the continuing validity of the CIO's business thinking.

Second, our results indicate that the combined rank and status of an IT leader is related to his or her role in the firm. This means that IT leaders who aspire to higher positions and have not yet broadened their knowledge, skills, and experiences in business operations, strategy, and management should immediately embark on personal career development programs to acquire these insights [2].

Our study also has a number of implications for the university community. As suggested before, firms are faced with a number of competitive and technological challenges and these challenges have an impact on the rank and role of IT leaders. These new challenges must also transform IT research and teaching. Ives [39] argued this succinctly by stating:

we do our undergraduate IS majors no favors if we train them for the COBOL/CICS jobs that industry is moving away from and for which there is already an excess supply of experienced talent. We must prepare them for the IT world of the future, not the world of the past. Not only must we reshape our curriculum, but we must take a very hard look at the market we are serving.

The university community must design graduate and executive programs for existing IT leaders. These programs must provide the IT leader with an understanding of how (1) to add value and develop his or her critical success factors based on the firm's competitive strategy, (2) to employ new technologies for emerging business needs by developing new product and services, (3) to be a technology specialist by discovering how new technologies, such as client–server applications, can be applied to existing business needs, (4) to be a change agent by using his or her knowledge of technology and change-management skills to lead or guide the redesign of business processes, and (5) to employ IT resources in the most cost-effective manner by creating standard base technologies and using organizational skills that will yield greater flexibility and shorter system development times.

Future research should address the relationship between the competitive strategy and other components of information management, such as IT planning, IT control, IT integration, IT organization, and IT performance evaluation.

## Limitations

WHILE THIS PAPER HAS ESTABLISHED THE EXISTENCE OF SIGNIFICANT linkages among a firm's competitive strategy and the firm's IT leader role and the combined rank and status, our results should be interpreted with the following limitations in mind.

As pointed out by Snow and Hambrick [73], the self-typing approach is not without shortcomings. First, many managers are reluctant to classify their firms. In this study, however, the number of respondents who did not self-type their firms constituted less than 4 percent. A second limitation of self-typing is possible variance among managers' perceptions of strategy within the same firm. Snow and Hrebiniak [74], in a study of eighty-eight companies, found "substantial agreement on strategy among top managers within a given firm." Because of the similarity in questioning between this study and the Snow and Hrebiniak [74] study, we believe that a response from only one individual within the firm, in this case the IT leader, would be sufficient. A third limitation of self-typing noted by Snow and Hambrick [73] is that executives may tend to report their firm's intended rather than emergent or realized strategies. If there is no intended strategy, an executive may even create one for the benefit of the researcher. As noted by Nisbett and Wilson [56], this tendency is a common problem in the social sciences. A fourth limitation of the self-typing approach is the lack of external confirmation of the respondent's answers. While that is true, the use of the self-typing approach enabled us to collect data from a large number of financial institutions. However, because of the size and nature of our sample, we could not obtain external confirmation of the self-typing done by the IT leader. A key assumption was that, as IT leaders of these firms, these individuals had an accurate perception of the overall firm's competitive strategies and their own roles. We constructed the questionnaire and the hypothesis carefully, taking into account the limitations.

Because of the special characteristics of the financial services industry, where the data were gathered, additional studies should be conducted in other industries before we can determine whether or not these results apply to other IT contexts.

## APPENDIX

Now we would like to assess the Competitive Strategy of your organization. Please circle one of the following types of competitive strategies that best describe your organization.

1. (Defender) An organization with this type of strategy attempts to locate and maintain a secure niche in a relatively stable product or service area. The organization tends to offer a more limited range of products or services than its competitors, and it tries to protect its domain by offering higher quality, superior service, lower prices, and so forth. Often an organization with this type of strategy is not at the forefront of developments in the industry—it tends to ignore industry changes that have no direct influence on current areas of operations and concentrates instead on doing the best job possible in a limited area.

2. (Prospector) An organization with this type of strategy typically operates within a broad product-market domain that undergoes periodic redefinition. The organization values “first in” in new product and market areas even if some of these efforts prove not to be highly profitable. The organization responds rapidly to early signals concerning areas of productivity, and these responses often lead to a new round of competitive actions. However, an organization with this type of strategy may not maintain market strength in all areas it enters.

3. (Analyzer) An organization with this type of strategy attempts to maintain a stable, limited line of products or services, while at the same time moving out quickly to follow a carefully selected set of the more promising new developments in the industry. The organization is seldom a major competitor in areas compatible with its stable product–market base, the organization can frequently be “second-in” with a more cost-efficient product or service.

4. (Reactor) An organization with this type of strategy does not appear to have a consistent product–market orientation. The organization is usually not as aggressive in maintaining established products and markets as some of its competitors, nor is it willing to take as many risks as other competitors. Rather, the organization responds in those areas where it is forced to by environmental pressures.
