# An Empirical Study of Factors Affecting Software Package Selection

ALI R. MONTAZEMI, DAVID A. CAMERON, AND KALYAN MOY GUPTA

ALI R. MONTAZEMI is Associate Professor of Information Systems at Michael G. Degroote School of Business, McMaster University. He received his M.Sc. in management science from the University of Southampton, England, and his Ph.D. from the University of Waterloo, Canada. His current research interests are information system planning, application of artificial intelligence in business and education, and measuring the effectiveness of decision support tools. His articles have appeared in the Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics, International Journal of Man-Machine Studies, INFOR, European Journal of Operational Research, Journal of Operational Research Society, Journal of Educational Computing Research, and others.

DAVID A. CAMERON is a project administrator with the financial department at Defasco Incorporated. He has more than eight years' experience in software engineering, systems analysis, project management, and office automation software training. His current responsibilities include providing administrative systems for treasury operations and participating in a corporate logistic system rebuild project. He holds a B.Sc. in mathematics and computer science from the University of Western Ontario and an M.B.A. from McMaster University.

KALYAN MOY GUPTA is a doctoral candidate majoring in management science and information systems at Michael G. Degroote School of Business, McMaster University. Prior to joining the doctoral program, he received an M.Tech. in industrial management from the Indian Institute of Technology, Kharagpur, India. He has several years of industrial experience in the design and development of a variety of information systems. His research interests include application of case-based reasoning to business problems and information systems planning and design. He has presented and published in conferences of the American Management Association and the Canadian Operations Research Society.

ABSTRACT: Evidence suggests that information centers (ICs) have significantly more interest in evaluating software packages and assisting in the selection of software packages than end users have. However, the selection of software packages by the information center product specialists (ICPSs) can compromise their usage. Ease of use and usefulness are believed to be fundamental predictors of usage. The question of whether ICPSs are able to correctly evaluate ease of use and usefulness of software packages for end users is posed. An insight into this issue could enhance end-user computing (EUC) policy and lead to a more effective partnership between end users and information systems (IS) professionals. A search for this insight provided the motivation for our empirical investigation of the perception of ICPSs and end users in assessing the ease of use and usefulness of thirty different software packages. Our investigation was performed in an organization with an IC that had evolved to the formalization stage. The findings show that the perceptions of the end users and ICPSs were similar in terms of assessing the ease of use of software packages. However, end users found the software packages less useful than did IC product specialists. Therefore, in sophisticated environments, end users should be empowered to develop their own user groups and suggest to IC personnel which useful software packages to acquire. Otherwise, selection of software packages without end-user participation could have an adverse effect on their usage.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: end-user computing, information center, software selection criteria.

THE SUCCESS AND FAILURE OF END-USER COMPUTING (EUC) within an organization ultimately depends on how effectively software packages are used $[57]$ . A software package can undoubtedly offer solutions to business problems, but often only as these are perceived by the software vendor who may not even be aware of the total information requirements of each end user. Therefore, selection of the “right” software package to meet end-user information requirements is critical to the success of management information systems (MIS) $[62]$ .

Selection of the “right” software package for end users is not the only demand on the MIS function. A typical MIS department, in an average organization, must still cope with a backlog for needed applications, which can often range from two to six years $[58]$ . Most often, this backlog is attributed to a shortage of programmers and systems analysts; however, this shortage seems to exist, regardless of the organizational context. Thus, selecting software packages for end users further adds to the workload of an already busy MIS department. Large backlogs of applications awaiting development, cost overruns, MIS departments that do not meet user requirements, and the short supply of MIS professionals have resulted in a shift of computing responsibilities toward the user community $[4, 5, 36, 55]$ .

The information center (IC) represents an important response to the need to support end-user computing. In general, the IC's role is to train and assist end users in acquiring, developing, using, and controlling their computer resources [53, 64]. With the growth of information centers, end-user computing, and decentralized organization, the question arises: Who should assess the ease of use and usefulness of software packages? This question is especially important for ICs in the formalization and maturity stage of their life cycle. At this stage, users have a wide background and a relatively high degree of computer skills [38]. To explore the implications of these factors, an empirical investigation was conducted to compare the perceptions of IC product specialists (ICPSs) and end users when assessing the ease of use and usefulness of software packages.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: the next section provides an overview of EUC policies and end users' requirements, followed by a presentation of the research objectives. Afterward, we describe the methodology and results of the study and close with a discussion.

## EUC Policies and Requirements

END-USER COMPUTING IS AN ALTERNATIVE TO TRADITIONAL SOURCES of acquiring information. Numerous studies track the growth of EUC [4, 29, 40, 55]. However, some organizations are finding that without appropriate policies and mechanisms, the cost of EUC can overshadow its benefits [23]. To improve the management of EUC, a variety of control strategies have been proposed [3, 18, 24, 45, 52].

End-user computing policy has been defined as [18]:

A set of statements, reflecting an organization's strategy for user-directed computing, that identifies the responsibilities of both information systems personnel and users, limits the range of acceptable choices and establishes specific control practices to be followed by all users who perform their own data processing and system development tasks. [p. 3]

Generally, EUC policy content areas include hardware, software, data security/privacy, and others $[40]$ . Based on a survey of MIS executives from 107 Fortune 1,000 companies, Galleta and Hufnagel $[18]$ found that the five policy areas mentioned most frequently involve acquisition approval for hardware and software. Interestingly, compliance with these policies is perceived by MIS executives to be relatively high. The question is whether the policies adopted by organizations can successfully provide an appropriate infrastructure for supporting the requirements of end users. To this end, Rainer and Carr $[52]$ surveyed 126 ICs to determine their responsiveness to end-user needs. Their findings suggest that, although ICs typically offer a broad variety of services, differences are apparent in the services most frequently offered by ICs and services most requested by end users. For example, ICs assume significantly more responsibility for offering software package evaluation services and for assisting in software package selection than required by the end users $[52]$ . Rainer and Carr $[52]$ have provided two possible explanations for this finding. The first explanation is that these services are considered fundamental to ICs but perhaps not to end users, who take them for granted or see them implicitly included in other services. The second possible explanation is that, as end users become more familiar with software packages over time, they have less need for the services of ICs. If the second explanation is true, then appropriate EUC policy should be adopted by the organizations to take advantage of savings that can be realized by reduction of services.

Effective use of software packages is believed to be dependent on ease of use and usefulness $[1, 15, 16, 57]$ . Perceived ease of use is the degree to which a person believes that using a particular software package is free of effort; perceived usefulness is the degree to which a person believes that using a particular software package could enhance his or her job performance. Research findings show that usefulness is related to actual hands-on usage of a software package, and ease of use is a significant determinant of intention to use a system [16].

To assume that cooperation between end users and IS professionals would be necessary to assess the usefulness and ease of use of a software package is not unreasonable. The rationale could be based on the assumption that, on the one hand, end users know their own information requirements, but lack technical skills (e.g., use of software packages) [47], and, on the other hand, that IS professionals have the technical knowledge but lack the knowledge of business needs [47]. However, this differentiation may not be necessary: the pertinent knowledge of end users and IS professionals may, in fact, become more similar as the IS environment in organizations evolves [40].

## Objectives

LET US ACCEPT, AS SOME OTHERS ALREADY HAVE (e.g., [38, 48]), that IS goes through the four stages of initiation, expansion, formalization, and maturity. At the initiation stage, it is reasonable to suggest that end users have less knowledge of software packages than do IS professionals. It is also reasonable to assume that end users become more knowledgeable in IS-related skills, such as evaluating a software package, as the IS in an organization evolves to the formalization and maturity stage (see [38] for a detailed description of these stages). As ICs evolve, they are increasingly faced with a proliferation of control mechanisms, initiation of standards, and decentralization of IC operations to user departments. Let us assume that end users and IS professionals become much more alike in organizations as they evolve through these stages. This assumption is based on two conjectures. The first is that the assessment of ease of use (i.e., intention to use) of a software package by the end users is similar to assessments made by IS professionals; the second postulates that the assessment of the usefulness (i.e., usage) of the software packages by IS professionals is similar to assessments by end users. If either of these two conjectures is not true, then reliance on IS professionals to select software packages can be dysfunctional—they may select useless software packages. If the first conjecture is true, however, then it is reasonable to empower end users to assess both usefulness and ease of use of software packages. The following two null hypotheses were postulated to assess the above conjectures:

H1: There is no difference in the perceived ease of use of a software package between the ICPS and end users.

H2: There is no difference in the perceived usefulness of a software package between the ICPS and end users.

Research findings suggest that personal factors can influence use of a software package (see Table 1). Therefore, to control the extraneous variance due to personal factors, the dependent variables should be partialed out (i.e., ease of use and usefulness). If the variance of the individual differences is not isolated, it becomes part of the error term and this leads to a less precise analysis of the stated hypotheses.

## Methodology

THE ENVIRONMENT FOR TESTING THE STATED HYPOTHESES SHOULD INCLUDE organizations with ICs that have evolved to the formalization or maturity stage. To this end, the definition of Magal et al. [38] was adopted in our search for the optimal test environment. With this as a basis for selection, the following environment was chosen to test the stated hypotheses.

## Environment

The MIS within a large, Canadian integrated-steel company provided the means to test our hypotheses. The company has approximately 8,700 employees, of which 180 work in the MIS department. This department has been in existence for twenty-five years. It evolved from supporting mainframe transaction processing systems into developing microcomputer-based artificial intelligence applications. Recently, the organization identified information technology as critical to its effort to gain competitive advantage in the 1990s and beyond. This prompted the organization to develop a plan of action for a distributed information system with an open architecture. As a result, during the past three years, the company has been involved in an in-depth information requirements analysis of its end users.

EUC, in this firm, is widely used in different functional areas. To meet the growing requirements of end users, the MIS department has ICPSs to support the IC personnel serving various user departments. In recognition of the sophistication of the EUC environment in this firm, we can conclude that its IC has evolved to the formalization stage. It can be argued that, after carrying out the new IS strategic plan, in the form of distributed information systems with an open architecture, the IC will further evolve to the maturity stage $[59]$ .

## Software Packages

Structured questionnaires were used to evaluate thirty software packages for perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness. Twenty-nine microcomputer application packages and one mainframe software package were included in the study. The mix of applications was as follows: 30.0 percent programming tools, 26.6 percent project management software, 23.3 percent graphics and design, 13.3 percent financial applications, and 6.6 percent other (e.g., expert system shell and word processing).

## Instruments

Three questionnaires were completed by the subjects. The first questionnaire included demographic questions such as: the subject's gender, educational background, and computer experience. The second was used to assess the subjects' computer anxiety, and the third to assess the subjects' perceived ease of use and usefulness of the software package(s). This last questionnaire also included an item to assess the subjects' perceived expertise in using the software package that they were appraising. The following sections elaborate on the instruments used in this investigation.

## Computer Experience

The two measures used to appraise the subjects' computer experience consisted of: (1) the number of years of computer experience, and (2) the level of computer literacy [55] (see appendix A).

## Computer Anxiety

Fourteen items identified by Kernan and Howard [31] to measure computer anxiety were adopted to reflect a fear of using computers (see appendix B).

## Perceived Ease of Use and Usefulness

The questionnaire developed by Davis [15] was adopted to assess perceived ease of use and usefulness. Assessment of each item for each construct was based on a seven-point Likert-type scale. The ease of use and usefulness questionnaire also included one criterion to measure the expertise of subjects in using each software package they appraised. This criterion was rated on a seven-point Likert-type scale that had the same verbiage anchors as the usefulness and ease of use questions (see appendix C).

## Subjects

The MIS department in the company under study was divided into several divisions, including an IC. Usually, an ICPS employed in this division conducts an initial assessment of software packages on behalf of other members of the organization. If the ICPS finds the software package useful and easy to use, then it is passed on to interested users. These users include MIS personnel in other divisions and end users in other departments, such as accounting, marketing, and production. A typical ICPS has a degree in computer science (or an equivalent degree) with in-depth experience of current information technologies and user information requirements.

Subjects who participated in this investigation were selected as follows: Twenty-four ICPSs were selected at random from a pool of forty-three product specialists in the MIS department. Also, a list of end users from six departments (accounting, marketing, sales, production, personnel, and research and development) was used to select four users from each department. Those who eventually agreed to participate in the study included twenty-two ICPSs and twenty-two end users. The descriptive statistics for these forty-four subjects are presented in Table 2.

## Test

To start, a list of software packages was circulated among the twenty-two ICPSs and twenty-two end users. They were allowed to select those that they were most interested in assessing. In addition, each subject completed the demographic and computer anxiety instruments. Next, each of these forty-four subjects was requested to assess his or her software package. After familiarizing themselves with each software package for at least one hour, subjects were required to respond to the ease of use and usefulness questionnaires. Some of these forty-four subjects assessed more than one software package. A total of 125 assessments resulted. All software packages were assessed by at least one end user and one ICPS.

## Reliability and Validity of the Questionnaires

Cronbach's alpha test was used to measure the reliability of the questionnaires used in our study [13, 60]. The scores for the questionnaires ranged from 0.837 to 0.987 (see Table 3). This indicates that the questionnaires were acceptable instruments because little variance in response was due to measurement error [49].

A measure of construct validity is the extent to which each item of a questionnaire correlates with the total score [30]. Table 3 shows the correlation between the score for each item (question) and the total score of the pertinent questionnaire (all significant at p < 0.001). Based on these findings regarding validity and reliability, the overall scores of ease of use, usefulness, and computer anxiety, and the score for each item of the questionnaires can be used as a meaningful measure of these constructs.

## Experimental Design

A one-way ANCOVA with randomized block procedure was used to test the two stated hypotheses (see Table 4). The factor, user type, had two levels (treatments): (1) ICPS, and (2) end user. Perceived ease of use and the perceived usefulness of the software packages were assigned as the response variable. However, the responses of subjects could have been influenced by variations in the software packages. Therefore, a software package was introduced as the blocking variable to account for its effect on response variables. As noted, the effect of variance due to personal factors on the response variables has to be accounted for in the analysis of the stated hypotheses. This was done, in our statistical model, by including the personal factors as covariates. In this research, the covariates consisted of gender, educational background, level of computer literacy, years of computer experience, level of expertise in using the appraised software package, and computer anxiety. Our experimental design was unbalanced because the number of replications per cell was unequal. One-way ANCOVA methodology, using the general linear model (GLM), was utilized to perform the analysis. Effective use of GLM requires (1) test of homogeneity of variance among the two populations (i.e., end users and ICPSs), and (2) assessment of multicollinearity among the independent variables [25, 51]. Since the analysis of variance is based on the assumption of equal variances, a test for homogeneity of variances on the sample was performed. The result of this test confirmed that the analysis of variance was valid. The Cochran's C and Bartlett-Box tests of our sample data verified that our two populations had equal variances (see Table 4); thereby demonstrating the appropriateness of using ANCOVA for testing the stated hypotheses.

Problems arising from correlation among independent variables are generally discussed under the heading of multicollinearity. Multicollinearity refers to the absence of orthogonality in a set of independent variables. When two variables are orthogonal, they are independent of each other, and the correlation between them is zero [51]. It is possible to detect the presence of multicollinearity by assessing the tolerance of each variable in a model. When the tolerance is low, the multiple correlation is high, and the possibility of multicollinearity increases [9]. The SPSS statistical package was used to do the analysis in this study. The tolerance for our independent variables ranged from 0.95 to 0.99, which suggested that multicollinearity was unlikely. Therefore, based on the above analysis, GLM was a valid technique for testing the stated hypotheses.

## Analysis

## Test of the Hypotheses

THE FIRST NULL HYPOTHESIS, H1, POSTULATES THAT THERE IS NO difference in the perceived ease of use of a software package between the ICPSs and the end users. The results of the analysis indicate that the null hypothesis is not rejected (significance level p = 0.3700) (see Table 5). The mean total perceived ease of use scores was 34.46 for the ICPSs and 34.65 for the end users.

The second null hypothesis, H2, postulates that there is no difference in the perceived usefulness of software packages between the ICPSs and end users. The results of the analysis indicate that the null hypothesis should be rejected because of a difference between the perceived usefulness scores among the two factor levels (see Table 6 on page 100) (significance level p = 0.0280). The mean total perceived usefulness score was 44.59 for the ICPSs and 23.47 for the end users. This implies that the ICPSs perceived the software packages to be more useful in comparison to the perception of the end users.

## Discussion

THE GROWTH OF END-USER COMPUTING AND THE TREND TOWARD the use of packaged software have focused attention on issues related to user support. A varied user population needs customized support, and yet vendor-supported software with a generic interface is increasingly being used $[39, 61]$ . As a result, the need for MIS to develop means for supporting end users is growing. Today, support for end-user computing includes a wide range of services such as consulting, technical/product support, user hotlines, help desks, training, and user groups $[35, 38, 54, 61]$ . To foster effective utilization of information resources within an organization, the partnership of end users and IS professionals is needed to determine appropriate policies, procedures, and standards $[7, 34, 40]$ . In the 1980s, such partnerships were based on the complementary nature of the skill sets and perspectives brought to the relationship by the two groups $[40]$ . McLean et al. $[40]$ claim that in the 1990s end users and IS professionals are becoming more aware of one another and, in some ways, more alike. At first glance, this contention seems to be inconsistent with the findings of Nelson $[47]$ . However, our findings provide an insight into the relevance of both viewpoints.

In this research, we investigated whether any perceptual differences exist between ICPSs and end users when involved in an assessment of the ease of use and usefulness of a variety of software packages. Our empirical investigation was performed in an organization with an IC that had evolved to the formalization stage. In this environment, we found no significant difference between the perceptions of end users and ICPSs when assessing ease of use. On the one hand, this finding supports the proposition made by McLean et al. [40] that “end users and IS professional are becoming, in some ways, more alike." End users seem to have as much technical ability as IS professionals when it comes to assessing the ease of use of software packages. On the other hand, our results may not be applicable to IC environments that are in the initiation or expansion stage. The findings of Nelson [47] could become binding in these environments. The reason is that users' training needs change as they become more experienced and desire to utilize advanced software capabilities [38, 42]. This evolving phenomenon hypothesis should be investigated in future studies.

Although the perceptions of end users and ICPSs in assessing the ease of use of software packages were similar, they differed when the usefulness of software was assessed: End users perceived the software packages to be less useful to their job. This could be because ICPSs lack “organizational knowledge” [47]. Therefore, selection of software packages by the ICPSs without the cooperation of the end users can be dysfunctional—end users will not find them useful.

The evolution of IC toward maturity consumes significant resources. However, a recent survey of information system executives $[40]$ suggests that they are more concerned with evaluation of value and benefits than with the cost of EUC. To this end, the evaluation of an IC should include $[37]$ : user self-sufficiency, quality of IC services, and quality of user-developed applications. End-user self-sufficiency is a state where users are able to develop applications with the least reliance on IS professionals. This ability presumes knowledge and understanding of the tools and techniques needed for developing applications. The result of our investigation suggests that, when an IC has evolved to the formalization stage, end users have as much knowledge as ICPSs when it comes to assessing the ease of use of software packages. Consequently, by adopting appropriate policies to empower end users in such an environment, organizations could expect to be more successful in implementing software packages $[36]$ and could experience improved communication between end users, IS professionals, and the vendors of the software packages $[36, 61]$ . Furthermore, a more enriched form of peer support can be expected to flourish $[10, 14]$ . Thus, exploration of the relevant organizational information policies and communication channels for supporting end users and IS specialists are among the more challenging areas of current research.

## Categories to Describe the Level of Computer Literacy

1. None: Those who have never used computer systems either directly or indirectly.

2. Intermediate: Those who have never used computer systems directly, but who receive required information through an intermediary, such as a data processing analyst.

3. Nonprogramming: Those whose only access to computer-stored data is through software provided by others. Access to computerized data is through a limited menu-driven environment or through a strictly followed set of procedures.

4. Command level: Those who access data on their own terms. This type of user understands the available database(s) and is able to specify, access, and manipulate information. Most often, command-level users utilize report generators and/or a limited set of commands from languages such as FOCUS, SQL, SAS, or LOTUS 1–2–3.

5. End-user programmers: Those who utilize both command and procedural languages directly for their personal information needs. These users develop their own applications, some of which are used by other end users. This latter facility is an incidental byproduct of what is essentially analytic programming performed on a “personal basis” by quantitatively oriented actuaries, planners, financial analysts, and engineers.

6. Professional programmers/analysts: Those who design and/or develop information systems in a data processing environment.
