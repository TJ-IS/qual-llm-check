# Participation on Electronic Bulletin Board Systems: An Empirical Analysis of Work Value Congruency

JOHN TILLQUIST

JOHN TILLQUIST is an Assistant Professor in the Faculty of Commerce and Business Administration at the University of British Columbia. He has published works in Communications of the ACM, Leadership Quarterly, Journal of Management Information Systems, and in several conference proceedings. He received his Ph.D. in information and computer science from the University of California, Irvine. His research interests are the organizational, institutional, and societal analysis of information technologies, computer-supported cooperative work, and IT-enabled transformations of work.

ABSTRACT: In an empirical study, perceptions of work and the workplace are compared with information systems (IS) support for those values. It is proposed that the congruence between the work values held by an individual and the perception of support for those values by an information system will affect the decision to use the system. Using a modified version of Elizur's Work Value Questionnaire, the work values and perceptions of IS support for those values are examined. Three factors that relate to the subjects' orientation to the world—societal, organizational, and individual—are isolated. The organizational orientation is found to be the most influential of the three in determining voluntary user participation, explaining 47 percent of the variation among subjects' usage patterns of a bulletin board system. These findings support the contention that when users became convinced of the efficacy of using an IS in mediating organizationally bestowed values, and when they perceive value in these organizational relationships, the system is used.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: adoption of technology, information technology use, technology assimilation, work values, workplace vision.

This paper proposes a research model for studying this rift between developer intentions and actual use. A key relationship of the model—that IT usage decisions are influenced by personal work preferences—is empirically tested. The findings suggest that people use computerized communication systems when they are convinced that the systems effectively support personally valued aspects of their work. This study is not intended to celebrate the efficacy of users over technology, or to lament the twisting of technology for personal gain. Instead, it simply seeks a means by which to understand how IT differs between conceptualization and implementation—that is, between design and use.

The focus here is limited to the use of electronic bulletin board systems (BBS). BBS are characteristic of a larger cadre of organizational work-augmentation systems. These are the “work smarter, not harder” systems—automated information-gathering and -filtering systems, computer-supported collaborative work systems, decision support systems, skill-augmentation systems (CAD/CAM, CASE, and process control systems), and computer-mediated communication systems—that have traditionally promised substantial productivity gains along with new and exciting value-added services. This research specifically concentrates on how computer-mediated communication technologies are affected by held values about work.

The next section frames the question of the influence of user preferences and values in the research literature. I then detail the user-developer model and propose two hypotheses about the nature of the relationship between work values and IT participation based on the model. The following section presents the empirical study of a voluntary-use communication system, followed by the analyses of the results. The final section discusses the findings and concludes with implications for the larger organizational domain.

## The Role of Work Values

TECHNOLOGICAL ARTIFACTS CARRY MANY CLUES ABOUT THEIR USE. For information technologies, how systems facilitate and constrain certain modes of communication, coordination, and work activities clue the user in to how the system was intended to be used. The ability to communicate with some work groups and not others, for instance, or the flexibility of manipulating facts and figures with relative ease, or the presence of technical support for one work activity over another all serve to communicate intended computer use. Some of these clues are picked up by the users. Others are misconstrued, overlooked, or ignored. Some of the constraints and facilitated work modes are adapted without change; others are misapplied or violated. The implementation of information technologies (IT) often happens in ways and in degrees that are unanticipated, sometimes even counter to the original design intention.

Unfortunately, existing descriptions of organizational IT in the research literature usually portray a singular metaphor of IT in the workplace, depicting implementation in terms of rational actors or self-interested economic agents. These characterizations understate the significance of the individual in defining and shaping IT use, tacitly ignoring the roles played by both the instigators and the recipients of the technologies. They tend to depict work as a rational end-product of calculated strategies, and workplace participants as undifferentiated and largely compliant agents of managerial directives.

In contrast, this research finds individual differences and personal values playing a significant role in shaping the use of computerized resources. User preferences and values are found to be expressed in choices about how information technologies are used. These choices about appropriate IT use are seen as central to implementing computerized work systems.

At one level of analysis, design of organizational IT instantiates high-level strategic choices. These design decisions emphasize organization-wide concerns—capitalizing on efficiencies of automated work design, garnering increased control and coordination over the work process, or responding to competitive pressures to adopt the latest technology $[28, 30, 42]$ . However, below the operational level of analysis, the detailed implementation decisions are left to the discretion of the users of the system. How these low-level choices are made can have a considerable impact on shaping the implementation environment $[22, 25, 32, 54]$ . These decisions include which manual processes to mechanize, what level of work monitoring and supervisory control is appropriate, what the intended role of the user should be, and how much local autonomy and creativity should be supported.

It is proposed here that users interpret computer-mediated communication systems in terms of their work value systems. Participation in these systems is affected to the extent that these interpretations support or conflict with the users' own value systems. This proposal comes from a relatively simplistic observation: When a system does not support work considered important by the users, users tend not to use the system.

## Shaping Implementation: The Expression of Work Values

DIFFERENT ORGANIZATIONAL GROUPS SHAPE INFORMATION TECHNOLOGIES through how they envision IT in relation to their own particular roles and responsibilities. Some will have greater influence during the early conceptualization and development of the technologies, while others have more during operation and use. Here, these two phases of IT implementation—(1) design and development and (2) adaptation and use—are considered independently (see figure 1). The differences between the two are significant. Processes and conceptualizations that occur in the up-front design and development of IT rarely mesh well with the downstream adoption and use of the computerized systems. It is the differences between design and use that hold the key to many of the problems of productivity and issues of appropriate use of information technologies in the workplace. This is why computer implementations are often, as one manager puts it, “bloody.”

## Design and Development

## Workplace Visions

The design and development of IT implementations are characterized by two key concepts. The first is the influence of workplace visions held by the managerial strategists and IT developers responsible for IT deployment [24]. In design and development, the idealized conceptualizations of work held by managerial strategists play a key role in guiding the construction of the IT architecture. These conceptualizations are idealized visions of workplace organization. They include an organization's perceptions and held beliefs as they relate to an external world, such as a particular organizational orientation to the external market, compliance with industrial and legislated standards, or corporate standing in society [34]. These visions also include normative and professional beliefs about how the organization should be managed internally. These normative and professional beliefs guide decisions regarding the centralization of power, authority, and responsibility; preferences regarding workplace control; the means of production; standards of professionalism; or adherence to an espoused organizational culture or professional code of conduct [10, 25, 26]. In any given organization, a number of these idealizations about work exist among strategic decision makers but only a few relatively stable forms of work conceptualization dominate the organizational discourse. Unsurprisingly, these dominant work schemas typically represent the institutionalized positions of the more powerful political players [32]. These abstract work schemata organize and focus managerial attention during the design, development, and deployment of IT.

## IT Affordances

Another set of influences on the deployment of IT resources refers to the affordances of IT. Information technologies hold particular efficiencies of speed and accuracy, economies of scope, flexibilities for mass customization, and time and distance compression capabilities that create and define the potential domain of computerized work. These enable and organize an arena in which automated work processes are made manifest, defining the features and the opportunities from which automated processes may be constructed.

Information technologies provide a wide range of affordances for workplace organization. The locus of control over the work process may be relocated between workers, as with commitment tracking systems such as the Coordinator [17]; within the automated process, as with many transaction and manufacturing production systems (see Zuboff's Tiger Creek pulp-processing plant [54]); among independent organizations, as evidenced by Rosenbluth's federation of travel agents [34]; or across levels of authority, as exemplified by Attewell's electronic "Big Brother" [2].

Automated systems may alter the division of labor within an organization. Routinized and proceduralized job tasks may be compartmentalized and then distributed or replicated among many workers $[7, 36]$ . Conversely, tasks may be automated and subsumed under higher skill demand positions, thereby increasing job scope and responsibility $[49]$ . Computerized systems can change the relative autonomy of workers and work groups by extending opportunities for distanced work $[8, 21]$ , providing “canned” expertise for project or personal management $[41]$ , or by enabling extended access to diverse information, knowledge, and support resources $[34]$ . Finally, use of computer-based communication systems, such as e-mail and electronic bulletin boards, can alter patterns of interdependency and enable different opportunities for team building, task integration, and work coordination $[7, 28, 53]$ .

Taken together, the workplace vision of the strategic innovators molds the enabling affordances of IT to create an IT architecture in two ways. First, the workplace vision constrains the available automation opportunities to fit a particular strategic intention. Second, the workplace vision further serves to bind potential technical application functions and features to the final IT configuration. In short, workplace visions restrict the technological options available for solving a particular business problem and influence the final form of the computerized solution. The result is a technological configuration that embodies conceptualizations about how work is to be done and how the particular artifact or configuration ought to fit into the work process.

## Adaptation and Use

As much as the workplace vision and the unique nature of information technologies shape the conceptualization and development of the IT environment, this is not enough to fully characterize an IT implementation. Orlikowski [37] and Orlikowski and Robey [38] describe a dialectic nature of IT, in which IT acts both as product and as medium of human activities. Generally speaking, they propose that the designer's conceptualizations of what constitutes a computer are reflected in its artifactual form, and this form facilitates and constrains subsequent actions when put to use.

To what extent does the relative congruity (or conflict) between the user's work preferences and those embodied by a computer artifact affect use? While support in the literature of a direct causal relationship between value congruence and overt behavior is somewhat sparse, there is a rich research history suggesting at least an indirect link between value congruence and elicited behavior as mediated by attitude. Relationships have been suggested between the degree of value congruence and attitude formation [3, 27, 33, 35, 46, 47] and between attitudes and overt behavior [9, 11, 16, 31, 45]. This tends to support the notion that value congruencies between personally held values and those exhibited by an external influencing agent (such as a computer-mediated group communication system) are potentially strong determinants of overt usage behavior.

This system/user value interaction relationship is particularly prominent when the user has a choice in appropriating a specific system feature. This is most apparent when the information system conflicts strongly with existing user values. Grudin [18] cites such an example, where a problem tracking system was modified so that problem reports were forwarded directly to the chief executive officer. Concerned with the high visibility and the negative implications of being associated with the report, workers stopped reporting problems. In another case, Malone et al. [29] describe a conflict over workplace privacy. Workers became reluctant to use an electronic mail system when they found that their messages were being monitored by their supervisors. Again, an incongruence with users' work values led to dysfunctional system use.

It appears from this anecdotal evidence that the extent to which an information system implementation represents value systems that are congruent or conflicting with the value systems of the intended users can have a pronounced effect on the user's decision about how the computer-supported work processes are appropriated (or not appropriated) into the user's work situation. This is the first hypothesis:

H1: The degree of congruence between a user's values and those values perceived as supported by a voluntary-use information system will influence the user's decision to participate in the system.

Work done by Elizur and his colleagues [6, 12, 13, 14] has identified twenty-four work values that are broadly applicable to many work situations, which they have organized into three major categories: cognitive, affective, and instrumental (see Table 1). Elizur's work values instrument has been used across organizational settings and national cultures in several comparative and value congruence studies $[1, 3, 6, 14, 44]$ , making it a good fit for the research undertaken here. Research in value systems has also indicated that values have a significant structural component and a hierarchical organization $[1, 6]$ . It may be that these categories of work values have a collective influential effect on the decision to adopt specific system features and functions. The second hypothesis makes this proposition:

H2: Work values have differential effects on the user's decision to participate in a voluntary-use information system.

## Method

THE STUDY DESIGN USED TO INVESTIGATE THE INDIVIDUAL-LEVEL influence of held work values and computer support was a cross-sectional survey of two cohort samples. A sample of seventy-three upper-class graduate computer science students acted as voluntary subjects for the study. Subjects were asked to rate themselves on each of Elizur's twenty-four work values from Elizur's work value questionnaire (WVQ). They were then asked to rate the support for these work values, as envisioned in a current or future work situation, offered by electronic bulletin board systems (BBS). The subject survey results were cross-indexed with electronic bulletin board participation scores that served as the dependent variable in this study.

The electronic BBS systems were semiprivate, interactive forums for discussion created specifically for the students. Access was available through on-campus computer laboratories, dial-up facilities, or Telnet. The BBSs were not moderated. Students could sign on locally or remotely, scan previously posted messages, reply to discussion threads, and compose new discussion topics. The issues discussed included computer privacy, computer hacking and viruses, government regulation and the Clipper chip, and other computer-related controversies.

Values are deeply held beliefs that vary little across social setting and change only slowly $[6, 14, 35]$ . This contrasts with attitudes and intentions for behavior, which, although tied to more permanent underlying value systems, are themselves only temporary and context-specific $[9, 19]$ . The relative stability of value systems supports a generalizability of the findings here to the larger organizational unit, even though the question remains open to empirical support.

It is not clear from the literature whether students hold a different set of work values from experienced computer professionals (see $[1, 15, 48]$ ). Although some difference is probable, the use of students in this case was not seen as substantially limiting. While differences in work values may have an effect on the absolute level and range of computer use, the focus here is on the relative relationship of values vis-à-vis computerized support. It is hypothesized that, within a given absolute range of computer use for a cohort of users, it is the relative congruency between work values and the perceived computer support for those values that affects an individual's level of use. For this reason, subjects were not required to hold any particular set of absolute work preferences for this study. Care should be exercised, however, until these relative

Subjects were experienced users with values that were to some extent supported or contradicted by the use of an electronic bulletin board system. There is ongoing debate over the use of ranking versus rating instruments in the measurement of values $[5, 23, 43, 44]$ . While rank measures provide a relative ordering of values, these measures cannot capture subtleties in degrees of importance between the relative rankings. The variance between a given value and the next is unknown, preventing the use of interval statistical analysis techniques. On the other hand, rating scales mask rank order effects. Subjects tend to rate every value highly, again obscuring statistical effect. To counter this problem, Alwin and Krosnick $[1]$ suggest a hybrid approach of the two measurement schemes. For this study, just such a forced rating measure was used. The administrative instructions called for subjects to rate three value items as “very important” and three values as “very unimportant.” This created an “anchor and adjust” relative relationship $[50]$ that ensured both an interval spread of the value ratings and an aggregate ranking preference.

The dependent variable, bulletin board participation, was measured by rating each message posted to the bulletin boards for contribution of the content. A three-point scale was used, with a range from 1 = little contribution to 3 = high contribution. Two referees independently rated the scores. Interrated reliability was greater than 0.9, with both referees agreeing without exception after discussing disparate scores. Scores for participation over multiple postings were summed for each subject. A zero score indicated no postings by a particular subject; higher scores reflect higher substantive contribution. There was no upper limit to the participation scores. Raw participation scores ranged from a minimum of zero to a maximum of 84 (average = 18.66; std. = 11.46). These scores were standardized to facilitate interpretation. (See Table 3.)

## Analysis

THE FIRST STEP IN THE ANALYSIS PROCESS WAS AN ATTEMPT to confirm Elizur's cognitive, affective, and normative values model. This consisted of a factor analysis of the WVQ data using maximum likelihood. Because the factor model proposed by Elizur suggested three unrelated factors, an orthogonal (varimax) rotation was chosen. Elizur's factor model failed to replicate, as have other attempts to confirm the cognitive, affective, and instrumental factor model (Borg [6] offers several alternative structures and hierarchies). Instead, three factors surfaced that differentiate individual, organizational, and societal loci of work values. Stated differently, an optimal three-factor solution suggested that individuals perceive the object of value preference in varying degrees of externality to themselves, a factor structure supporting Liedtka's [27] and Ravlin and Meglino's [43] formulations of work values. The work values loading on the individual locus factor, such as Achievement in Work, Independence, Esteem, and Recognition, closely parallel Rokeach's [47] description of the personal values “sense of accomplishment,” “freedom,” “self-respect,” and “social recognition.” Values loading on the organizational factor included organizational level effects (e.g., “to be employed by a company of which you are proud” and “influence in the organization”) and extrinsic motivators (Pay, Benefits, Security [19, 51]). The single work value loading on the societal factor was Contribution to Society.

Since the effect of interest was the interaction between the work values held by the subject and the perceived support for those values offered by the bulletin board system, the independent variables used in the model were cross-products of the individually held work values and the perceived support for the work values by the bulletin board system. The resulting second-order terms (work values by perceived BBS support) model congruency between relative significance of a work value and perception of corresponding BBS support. Formally, for each unit increase (decrease) in one of the terms in the cross-product (holding the other constant), a proportional increase (decrease) in the observed use of the BBS can be established. Thus, for a given relative rating of a particular work value, participation in the BBS should vary in accordance with the perceived support offered by the system for that work value. Increased participation is expected when significance and/or perceived support are increased; lessened participation is expected when significance and/or perceived support are increased; lessened participation is expected when significance and/or perceived support are low (hypothesis 1).

## Discussion

## Factor Analysis Interpretation

THE RESULTS FROM THE FACTOR ANALYSIS SUGGEST that there is more than one factor $X^{2}=0.0002$ and no more than three factors $X^{2}=0.6440$ underlying the reported work values. A scree plot analysis supported the three-factor solution. In addition, each work value tended to load on a unique factor, with the slight exception of Pay and Benefits, which showed some spillover. Taken together, this evidence lends support for the three-factor solution.

## Regression Model Interpretation

Because bulletin board participation frequencies were standardized, the beta values of the final regression model represent standard deviations of mean usage frequencies for all students multiplied by 100. Hence, a beta value of -100 is one standard deviation lower than the overall student mean bulletin board system (BBS) participation level.

The three-factor final model proved to be significant $p < 0.01$ , explaining 47 percent of the observed variance in bulletin board participation. Examination of the root MSE indicates that 87 percent of the observed values are expected to occur within $\pm$ one standard deviation from the predicted mean participation level (alpha = 0.05). Independent t-tests showed that the effects for the variables Job Status $p < 0.04$ and Pride in Company $p < 0.02$ were significant below the a priori 0.10 alpha level. Advancement came close to statistical significance at p < 0.12.

Unfortunately, direct interpretation of the $\beta_{0}$ (intercept) value does not provide any significant information because the effects of the other beta factors cannot be negated.

The interaction effect of Job Status and computerized supported by bulletin board systems was significant and positive. This indicates that, holding all other effects constant, the higher one values job status and the more the individual perceives the system as supporting that value, the more the system will be used. By contrast, if either the individual does not consider job status important or if the system is perceived as not supporting status in the job, then the system will reflect this in a lack of use. The lowest level of participation effect occurred when both the value of job status and the perceived support by the system for job status were low.

The beta coefficient for Pride in Company was negative. This finding was at first rather surprising. One possible explanation would be that having pride in the company means not having to use an electronic BBS. Subjects may equate computer system use with routinized, socially alienating job positions $[4, 39, 40]$ , undesirable characteristics that dampen pride in a company. Woodward $[52]$ and Hulin and Roznowski $[20]$ suggest that the rigidity associated with certain technical systems may produce or invite interpersonal strains in organizational relations, again depreciating the view of organizations where computer use is higher.

The beta coefficient for the remaining variable, Advancement, was significant and positive. This indicates that when an individual places low importance on advancing in the organization, or when the person perceives little support for advancement by using the system, participation on the system is decreased. Participation is increased when the information system is seen to support organizational advancement, especially when advancement is valued highly.

## Implications

The final regression model, which matched Pride in Company, Job Status, and Advancement to computerized support offered by BBSs, proved to be a significant predictor of system use. Hypothesis 1 was therefore supported.

Work values also appear to have a structural component. Individuals perceive the object of value preference in varying degrees of externality to themselves, through individual, organizational, or societal references. However, only one of these constructions, the Organizational Model, proved to be a significant predictor of system use. As a result, work values can be said to have differential effects on the usage decision, but only a few of the work values appear to be significant in this process. Hypothesis 2 was therefore only partially supported.

That the work values with an organizational locus would be the only significant predictor of system appropriation was unexpected, especially given that four of the five work values rated as most important loaded on the individual locus factor (Job Interest, Esteem, Achievement in Work, and Use of Ability and Knowledge). $^{3}$ For this sample, the degree to which the electronic bulletin board systems were consistent with personally or socially centered work values had little effect on the decision to participate on the BBS.

That congruency of work values with an organizational locus was a strong predictor of BBS participation suggests that BBSs are seen as instrumental in achieving extrinsic rewards afforded by the organization, such as having the opportunity to advance or having a job that carries some organizational status. This suggests that even while work values that are more inherently individual in nature are rated higher in perceived importance, participation in BBS support only the work values that are extrinsically bestowed upon the individual by the organization.

Looking at the work values in terms of relative importance, we see that Job Interest, Advancement, Esteem, Achievement in Work, and Use of Ability and Knowledge were consistently rated highly, while at the bottom of the ratings were Influence in the Organization, Job Status, Independence in Work, Contribution to Society, and Convenient Hours of Work. These scores suggest that respondents tended to rate items of job content and merit as important work values, while values that reflect social standing and contribution were seen as least important.

The top five work values supported by electronic bulletin boards, on the other hand, were Achievement in Work, Use of Ability and Knowledge, Job Interest, Feedback, and Independence in Work; the bottom five were Benefits, Pride in Company, Supervisor, Contribution to Society, and Interesting Coworkers. These value rankings suggest that BBSs are viewed as tools for supporting the individual work process and performance. The low-end scores appear to be mostly related to characteristics of the company and of the work setting, indicating that while BBSs are perceived as instrumental to the work process, they are not necessarily seen as valuable in facilitating relationships between coworkers or with the company itself. That bulletin board systems are rated low as a source of company pride appears to support the regression analysis interpretation that computers are viewed as tools for work rather than sources of company esteem.

It appears, then, that the best predictors of BBS participation were those work values that were rated as both relatively unimportant and relatively unsupported by BBSs. This apparent contradiction provides a valuable insight into the relationship between value congruence and the usage participation behavior. While most subjects saw these organizationally centered work values as both unimportant and unsupported, those who perceived BBSs as valuable in organizational relationships tended to use the electronic bulletin boards more. Put another way, it appears that the average subject did not see BBSs contributing much in facilitating relationships with others within the organization, but the few who did find these values important were those who participated to a greater degree on the BBS.

## Limitations

An analysis of the mean value of the participation ratings for subjects indicated that the sample of students who participated in the survey had BBS participation levels about 13 percent higher than nonrespondents. Since much of the student sample was taken from a nonrequisite follow-on course, this difference could be due to a natural selection process. The subjects from the follow-on course were typically those who had enjoyed the prerequisite course using bulletin boards and, consequently, had used bulletin boards more often. This was not unduly alarming, but could limit the generalizability of the findings.

Second, because the results of the factor analysis depend upon the value sets of the student sample, caution must be exercised in assuming that individual, organizational, and social structuring of values exist in other groups. Several of the factor loadings, while relatively high on a single factor, were lower than the 0.5 target. Further research is necessary to determine the stability of these factor structures.

Finally, while this study investigated the psychological aspects of value congruity on computer use, the organizational implications of the findings remain untested. Can the work values of student subjects be extrapolated to the organizational context? The evidence provided here on this point is based on current IS theory. Building from the empirical works of other studies, there appears to be a growing body of research indicating that social, political, and institutional factors do play a part in the organization of work and work behaviors. Much of this work is qualitative in nature, producing analytical concepts and relationships that provide explanation of many organizational phenomena unavailable to statistical analysis. These theoretical underpinnings bridge quasi-experimental field study with organizational analysis.

## Conclusion

Work values appear to play an important role in the intended users' decision to participate in computer-mediated communication. In this paper, the congruence between personally held work values and the extent to which those values are supported by the system were useful predictors in estimating the use of electronically mediated communication systems. The degree to which users are convinced of the utility of the system in supporting work values that are considered important can be an important determinant of user participation.

These findings indicate that elements and features of the IT environment are appropriated and used in line with local user interests. In the case of this study, when users became convinced of the efficacy of using electronic bulletin boards in mediating organizationally bestowed values, and when they perceived value in these organizational relationships, then these BBSs became important channels in organizational connectivity.

The intended strategic initiatives that go into IT design and development are largely susceptible to renegotiation, reinterpretation, and recharacterization once situated in the workplace and made available to users. Elements of the social, political, and institutional user setting greatly influence the way in which IT-supported managerial initiatives are received by users. As a result, these factors play a larger role than has been previously recognized in shaping the emergent work order.

Studying intended work changes in the context of the implementation environment reveals the social dynamics and systems of shared beliefs about work that can otherwise wreak havoc on purely rational implementation designs. Continuing investigation of the breach between IT-as-designed and IT-as-used will provide further insights into questions about workplace productivity, computer use, and information system design.
