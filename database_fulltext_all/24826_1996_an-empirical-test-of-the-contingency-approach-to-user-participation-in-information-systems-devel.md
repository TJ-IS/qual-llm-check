# An Empirical Test of the Contingency Approach to User Participation in Information Systems Development

NAVEED SALEEM

NAVEED SALEEM is Associate Professor of Information Systems at the University of Houston–Clearlake. He earned his Ph.D. in management science and information systems from the University of Texas at Austin. His research interests include system development and implementation strategies, application of expert systems, and application of CASE tools. His research papers have been published in Management of End-User Computing, Computer Personnel, Software Development Strategies, EDP Auditing, Computers in Math and Science Teaching, and Computers in Nursing.

ABSTRACT: User participation in information systems development is considered the key to system success in organizations. The empirical evidence, however, does not support this. A review of the literature suggests that one critical weakness in empirical investigations is inadequacy of operational measures of participation to gauge user influence on system design. Furthermore, there is also a growing consensus that the contradictory evidence may be due to a contingent, rather than a direct, relationship between participation and system success. This conception asserts that the outcome of user participation may depend on various contextual variables.

One variable in particular—users' system-related functional expertise—is believed to moderate the outcome of participation. This paper derives the contingent effect of user expertise and reports the results of a controlled laboratory experiment and a field survey conducted to test it. The data suggest that users who perceive themselves as functional experts, relative to others, are unlikely to accept a system unless they exerted a substantive influence on its design. On the other hand, users who perceive themselves as functional nonexperts, relative to others, are likely to accept a system regardless of the extent of their influence on its design. This finding suggests user expertise as a useful criterion for selecting participants to serve on design teams and for determining the appropriate extent of a participant user's influence on system design.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: design teams, system acceptance, system design, system development, system success, user participation.

IT IS INTUITIVELY APPEALING THAT USER PARTICIPATION leads to system success. However, the empirical evidence on the merits of user participation has as much clarity as would a law of gravity stating that a falling object may sometimes come down, occasionally go up, and periodically drift to the side.

Two major explanations have been proposed for the ambiguity of user participation research. First, Ives and Olson [19] attribute it to the conceptual, methodological, and measurement flaws present in much of this body of research. They point out that (1) most of the studies are exploratory in nature and poorly grounded in theory; (2) survey studies rarely obtain a random sample of respondents or control for the type of system; (3) the participation measures rarely reflect actual user influence on the physical development of the system; and (4) objective indicators of system success are rarely employed. Hence, the ambiguous results and the emphasis on rigor in the future research.

Second, a number of researchers (e.g., [3, 12, 49, 50]) maintain that a contingent, rather than a direct, relationship links user participation and system success. Accordingly, the outcome of participation depends upon various contextual factors, and relating participation to system success without accounting for contextual factors, as normally done in user participation studies, can provide confounding results.

One factor, users' system-related functional expertise, is believed to be a particularly important contingent determinant of the participation outcome [7, 12, 19, 33, 37, 39]. This contingency, however, is usually explained in terms of system quality as the desired outcome of participation. Specifically, participation of expert users is expected to result in a better-quality system, through integration of employee expertise, better understanding of users' information requirements, and superior evaluation of the system.

The focus on system quality stands to logic. However, research suggests that lack of system acceptance, rather than intrinsic technical system problems, is the primary reason for the failure of information systems $[13, 35, 39]$ . To date, however, contingent significance of user expertise in terms of system acceptance has been neither derived nor empirically tested. Specifically, whether participation of functionally expert users is imperative for system acceptance remains unaddressed. And that is the focus of this research.

This paper derives the contingent effect of users' functional expertise on system acceptance and then reports the results of a controlled laboratory experiment and a field survey conducted to test it. Given the rampant system failures in organizations [12, 50], such efforts are indeed warranted.

## Literature Review

THIS SECTION EXAMINES THE PARTICIPATION CONSTRUCT AS TYPICALLY defined in the IS literature, and reviews the arguments frequently advanced for the significance of users' functional expertise as a contingent determinant of the outcome of user participation.

## User Participation Construct

User participation is typically defined as the behaviors and activities performed by target users or their representatives during the system design process [4]. Participation varies in degree, that is, in the extent of user influence on the system design [19]. This variation may be conceived as a continuum. On the low end of this continuum, user input is not solicited or is ignored; and, on the high end, user input forms the basis of system requirements [19, 37]. Thus, participation and influence are not synonymous: a participant user may or may not have any influence on the system development.

Gauging user influence is imperative, because the outcome of participation depends on the amount of user influence on system design. User participation is unlikely to induce system acceptance if participant users have little or no influence on system development $[25, 36]$ . In fact, participation with little or no influence may prove to be counterproductive, as users may consider it manipulative $[10, 16, 30]$ . Unfortunately, the empirical investigations rarely incorporate this dimension of user participation into the operational measures of participation. Some notable exceptions, however, include Tait and Vessey $[50]$ and Doll and Torkzadeh $[12]$ .

Tait and Vessey [50] related degree of participation to system success, but failed to find a significant relationship. Degree of participation in this study was operationalized in terms of the participation categories compiled by Ives and Olson [19]. (The participation categories supposedly reflect increasing degree of participation and include the following: [1] no participation: users are not invited to participate; [2] symbolic participation: user input is sought but ignored; [3] participation by advice: users are consulted; [4] participation by weak control: users may have sign-off responsibility; [5] participation by doing: users are members of design team; and [6] participation by strong control: users may pay for the system development.) The lingering problem in this study was that the categories of participation do not reflect actual user influence on system design. For instance, users may sign off without really knowing what they are signing off on [37], and being a member of the design team does not necessarily mean that the users actually influenced the system development [46].

Doll and Torkzadeh [12], on the other hand, found a significant relationship between users' perceived influence and system success. However, noting the ex-post design of the study, they remarked that the user perceptions may change in response to actual success or failure of the system. Consequently, they suggested further studies that gauge user influence during the system development process.

## Users' Functional Expertise as a Contingent Factor

Various factors such as users' system-related functional expertise [19, 39], system complexity [50], desired user participation [12], and stages of system development [13] are believed to affect the outcome of participation. However, as the following discussion suggests, users' system-related functional expertise is considered particularly critical.

The argument generally advanced in support of user participation suggests that development of an information system requires a blending of two types of expertise: the users' system-related functional expertise and the designers' technical expertise [7, 19, 37]. This argument is further refined in terms of the level of users' functional expertise and the appropriate degree of their participation in system development.

Accordingly, a low degree of user participation is deemed adequate when designing standard application systems such as a payroll system $[40]$ . A high degree of user participation is recommended when designers lack the functional expertise required for developing the system, and the users possess such expertise $[19]$ , or when the task uncertainty is high $[11]$ .

This recommendation appears logical with respect to system quality as the desired outcome of user participation. However, its impact with respect to system acceptance as the intended goal is not understood. Specifically, it is unclear if the degree of user participation required to achieve system acceptance depends on a user's system-related functional expertise.

## Theory and Hypotheses

PARTICIPATIVE DECISION-MAKING THEORY PROVIDES THE THEORETICAL BASIS for the hypothesized relationship between user participation and system acceptance. Status congruence theory provides the rationale for deriving the contingent effect of users' functional expertise on this relationship.

## Participative Decision Making (PDM)

Participative decision making refers to group decision making in which employees who are to execute the decisions or who are affected by the decisions participate in the decision-making process. Participation varies in degree—that is, in the degree of employee influence in decision making [34]. PDM research provides significant empirical evidence suggesting a positive relationship between employee's degree of participation in decision making and decision acceptance (e.g., [17, 27, 29, 38, 47, 52]). The intervening mechanisms believed to account for this relationship include factors such as decision understanding, feelings of decision ownership, trust in management, sense of control, and integration of knowledge [34].

## Hypothesis Relating Participation and System Acceptance

PDM has been suggested as an appropriate paradigm for investigating user participation $[12, 19, 50]$ . Ives and Olson $[19]$ maintain that user participation is a special case of PDM in which the users and designers substitute for employees and managers. This would suggest that there is a positive relationship between degree of user participation and system acceptance through intervening mechanisms parallel to the ones in the PDM context (such as feelings of system ownership, sense of control, better understanding of system objectives and capabilities, etc.). Accordingly, it is hypothesized that

H1: Users with a high degree of participation in system development will demonstrate greater system acceptance than the users with a low degree of participation.

## Status Congruence Theory

This theory is based on the premise that the members of a group may be differentiated along several different dimensions defined by an attribute (e.g., sex, race, and age) or an achieved characteristic (e.g., education, occupation, and income). The status of an individual member along some dimension is defined as the relative position of that member compared with the positions of the other members of the group along that dimension [6].

The status of an individual along one dimension need not match that individual's status along another dimension. The status congruence is defined as the extent to which an individual occupies comparable positions on different relevant dimensions (i.e., dimensions that are expected to go together). Thus, the same position, high or low, on two (or more) relevant dimensions indicates perfect status congruence (e.g., an uneducated janitor, a corporate president with an M.B.A.). Exactly opposite positions along two relevant dimensions illustrate perfect incongruence (e.g., a janitor with a Ph.D., an illiterate corporate president). Thus, the status incongruence may be defined as the extent to which an individual occupies noncomparable positions on different relevant dimensions. An incongruent individual is believed to strive for compatibility among his or her ranks on different relevant dimensions toward the highest rank [45].

Status incongruence is claimed to create frustration, tension, and problematic attitudes and behavior in individuals $[20, 23, 31, 32]$ ; and mutual distrust, conflict, and hostility in the group $[8, 15]$ . Effects on individuals are believed to occur through factors such as a natural tendency toward congruence, unpleasant or frustrating social experiences, feelings of injustice, and discrepancy between the underlying expectations and occurrence of ranks on different dimensions. Likewise, effects on groups are believed to occur through factors such as violation of generally accepted norms, inability to gain congruence because of the other members of the group, and the conflicting expectations of the incongruent and others' behavior $[45]$ .

Status congruence theory has direct implications for participative decision making and thereby for user participation. Specifically, status incongruence in the decision-making process can trigger mechanisms that are opposite in nature to the mechanisms believed to induce decision or system acceptance. For example, instead of triggering sense of control, feelings of decision or system ownership, decision or system understanding, integration of knowledge, and trust in management, the status incongruence is likely to trigger mechanisms such as frustration, feelings of unfair treatment, hostility, and distrust of management.

Considering these combinations from the PDM perspective, we would expect high-participation users (groups I and III) to display greater system acceptance than low-participation users (groups II and IV).

Groups I (high expertise, high participation) and IV (low expertise, low participation) are congruent with respect to their status on the two dimensions under consideration; groups II (high expertise, low participation) and III (low expertise, high participation) are incongruent. From a status congruence perspective, we expect user groups I and IV not to suffer any undesirable individual or group effects that may in turn lead to negative feelings toward the system or the designer.

Because the group II users (high expertise, low participation) are incongruent, they are likely to undergo undesirable effects of incongruence and thereby develop negative feelings toward the system and the designer. This, combined with their low degree of participation in the system development, makes them even less likely to accept the system: Both PDM and status congruence theories will predict them unlikely to accept the system.

The group III (low expertise, high participation) users are also incongruent. However, these users are in an advantageous position: Their rank on the degree of participation dimension is higher than they may expect based on their rank on the expertise dimension. Therefore, these users are unlikely to experience feelings of frustration, unfair treatment, or distrust of the designers. Consequently, they are unlikely to develop any negative feelings toward the system or the designer. (Conceivably, at some point, the difference in expertise and participation ranks may induce feelings of incompetence and discomfort. This scenario, however, may not apply if users can make meaningful contributions to system development in spite of their perceived functional inferiority.)

In short, we expect the low-participation users to be less likely to accept the system than the high-participation users. This likelihood, however, is further weakened if these users are also incongruent with respect to their expertise and their participation. ## Method

THIS SECTION DESCRIBES THE USER AND DESIGNER PARTICIPANTS, gives an overview of the experiment conducted, and defines the independent variables and dependent variables measured.

## User and Designer Participants

Sixty students in an introductory undergraduate statistics course participated as system users in the experimental part of this study; four graduate and two senior undergraduate students participated as system designers.

Prior to their participation in the experiment, all user participants had acquired, through classroom instructions and homework assignments, a thorough familiarity with the following statistical techniques: mean, median, geometric mean, standard deviation, coefficient of variation, maximax rule, maximin rule, maximum likelihood rule, and Bayes's rule. They were required to review these topics before their participation in the experiment and were briefly quizzed to assure their understanding of these techniques when they arrived to participate in the experiment (two users were dropped from the experiment because they were unprepared).

None of these participants had more than six hours of computing or related courses, so none was an expert in information technology or information system development. Participation in the experiment was handled as a class project, worth 10 percent of the overall course grade, to motivate users to do their best in completing the experimental task. They were informed that the evaluation of their performance would be based on the quality of their analysis of the investment case.

All students who played the role of a system designer were volunteers and had a working expertise in SAS [48].

## Experiment Overview

The experiment took place in three stages, and each stage lasted about seventy-five minutes. The first stage entailed user introduction to the experiment and development of an SAS-based [48] prototype system (this system assisted a user in comparing past and predicted performance indicators of different companies using various statistical techniques such as coefficient of variation, Bayes's rule, maximax, maximin, etc.); the second stage involved user training to use the system; and the third stage comprised user analysis and comparison of the performance data of a set of companies.

Upon arrival, each user was informed that the user and the designer were to develop the system together as a team, and that the following constraints were to be observed in developing the system: the system could support only (1) four statistical techniques, (2) four performance variables, (3) one past data set, (4) one future data set, and (5) one report format (i.e., the reports could display either the best company based on a statistical technique or all the companies). (Various data sets and performance variables available for the companies under consideration are listed and defined in appendix A.) Thus, developing this prototype system essentially required determining the most appropriate statistical techniques, performance variables, data sets, and display format. Users' perceptions of their system-related functional expertise, relative to designers, and their influence on the system development were manipulated.

In the second stage, the designers trained the users in system use. First, the designer demonstrated the system, and then the user exercised the system use. The designer made sure that the user had no difficulty interacting with the system.

Finally, in the third stage, the users completed an investment analysis task that required them to compare the performance of a given set of companies and to recommend one for investment purposes (a sample task is given in appendix B). The users had the freedom to conduct this comparison using the system fully, partially, or not at all. Thus, system use was completely voluntary. The users could access as many technique-variable combinations as they desired in carrying out their analyses.

User interaction with the system was automatically saved into a log file. A printout of this file was used to count the number of system queries utilized by the participant. Since users were free to use techniques and variables that were not supported by the system (non-system queries), they were instructed to submit all the paperwork with their final recommendations. This paperwork was examined to check and count the non-system queries utilized by a user. Thus, various indicators of the users' acceptance of the prototype system were measured.

Research Variables

## Independent Variables

User expertise was defined as possession of system-related functional expertise. Each user participant possessed one of the two levels of expertise: high or low. Users with the high level of expertise perceived themselves as having superior system-related functional expertise than the designers. To induce this perception, these users were informed that the system designer was an undergraduate student with SAS programming experience but very limited expertise in statistics and its application to business decision making. The users with the low level of expertise perceived themselves as having inferior system-related functional expertise than the designers. These users were informed that the system designer was a graduate student with SAS programming experience and a very strong background in statistics and its application to business decision making. The designer's academic level was deliberately mentioned to reinforce user perceptions in the likelihood of designer's expertise or non-expertise.

The degree of participation variable in this research was operationalized along the definition employed by Robey, Farrow, and Franz [43]. The degree of participation reflected the extent or amount of user influence on the system development at two levels: high and low. This variation in influence was induced by controlling the opportunity of making system-related choices (i.e., format and content of reports, variables and techniques supported by the system, and the data sets included in the system).

Each user with a high degree of participation him- or herself made all these choices and therefore created a different system. In the case of a user with a low degree of participation, the designer imposed the choices. Actually, the choices the designer imposed were the ones made by a user in the corresponding “high participation” cell; however, the “low participation” user was not aware of that. The designer pretended to make all the choices pertaining to the system and gave some canned explanation for the preferences if asked by the user. (All SAS programs required for the system were written in advance. Consequently, the system development during the experiment comprised making a few necessary changes in these programs to accommodate the choices made by high-participation users.)

The choices by a “high expertise, high participation” user were imposed upon the next “high expertise, low participation” user. Thus, this pair of high-expertise users utilized and evaluated the same system. In other words, the quality of the system remained constant between these two users. Consequently, thirty high-expertise users evaluated fifteen systems in total. Likewise, the choices of the “low expertise, high participation” users were imposed upon the next “low expertise, low participation” users, and the pair rated the same system. Consequently, thirty low-expertise users evaluated fifteen systems.

This strategy of imposing “high participation” user choices on “low participation” users was followed to control system quality as a constant between the two participation levels at a given expertise level and, thereby, to enhance the reliability of simple main effects at that expertise level. Random assignments of users to different cells and random order in which they participated in the experiment minimized the possibility of any bias in this strategy.

The experimental design was balanced with fifteen users assigned to each cell. Each designer participated at one expertise level and worked with an equal number of users in the two cells at that level. Figure 3 exhibits the $2 \times 2$ experimental design and summarizes the treatment manipulations.

## Dependent Variables

The extent of system use reflected the extent to which a user utilized the system in completing the experimental task and was operationalized as the total number of task-related system queries made by the user. System query implied interrogating the system to compute and display information on some technique/variable combination, supported by the system, concerning the businesses under consideration. For example, a user could ask for expected net profit for the next year or net profit variance during the past eight years. User interaction with the system was automatically saved by the system into a log file. A printout of the log file was used to measure this variable.

As noted before, the system use was completely voluntary. This strategy was important because system use is not a valid indicator of system acceptance if the use is mandatory; earlier studies, as Ives and Olson [19] pointed out, did not maintain this distinction.

Resistance to system use indicates the extent to which a user underutilized the system and relied on non-system queries in completing the experimental task. Non-system query implied use of a criterion or a variable that was not supported by the system. Users derived the values of variables employed manually or with a calculator. The resistance to system use index was computed as the sum of (1) the number of non-system queries used, and (2) the number of possible system queries not applied by the user in completing the task. User resistance was set to zero, if (1) the users utilized the non-system queries in addition to full utilization of the system, or (2) non-system queries were not applied even when some system queries were left unutilized. User analysis of the task and printout of the log file were examined to measure user resistance to system use.

User satisfaction was based on the user satisfaction questionnaire developed by Jenkins and Ricketts $[21]$ and included five items (see appendix C). The Cronbach alpha measure of reliability for this instrument was 0.87. The corrected item total correlations, a measure of validity as suggested by Kerlinger $[24]$ , ranged from 0.65 to 0.77, and all were significant at the 0.0001 level.

## Data Analysis and Results

To verify that randomization resulted in groups with similar averages for various student measures, the groups were compared for the following demographic characteristics: total hours of coursework completed, hours of computing courses taken, GPA, and gender. Analysis of variance indicated no significant differences among the four groups.

More important, a chi-square test of medians suggested that the two manipulations, degree of participation and user expertise, were quite successful (degree of participation, chi sq. = 17.1, p < 0.0001; user expertise, chi sq. = 52.3, p < 0.0001). Thus, users who made the system choices felt significantly more influential than the users who were denied that opportunity. Likewise, the high- and low-expertise users clearly differed in their perceived functional expertise relative to the designers' (appendix D lists the questionnaire used to assess these two manipulations).

The extent of system use and resistance to system use data for the four groups violated the assumptions of normality and equal variances. However, the balanced design of the experiment allowed use of parametric techniques for testing hypotheses involving these variables $[14, 44, 55]$ . The user satisfaction data, measured at the ordinal level, for the four treatment groups were individually tested for normality. The values of the Shapiro-Wilk statistic W for the four distributions ranged from 0.944 to 0.884, and suggested that the four distributions were samples from a normal distribution. Therefore, parametric techniques were applied to this variable as suggested by Tinsley and Weiss $[51]$ .

The research hypotheses were tested using a two-step procedure suggested by Hummel and Sligo [18]. First, the following MANOVA model was tested to identify the independent variables that had significant overall effect on the dependent variables.

The MANOVA results indicated that only three factors—degree of user participation, user expertise, and their interaction—exerted a significant effect on the overall variation in the dependent variables. Second, the tests of overall effects were followed by the tests of hypothesized effects of the three factors on the individual dependent variables. The results of these ANOVA tests are presented next.

The data confirmed the main effect hypothesis and provided a significant evidence for a relationship between user participation and system acceptance (extent of system use, F = 9.82, p = 0.0027; resistance to system use, F = 8.87, p = 0.0043; user satisfaction, F = 5.58, p = 0.0217). This implies that the participant users are unlikely to accept a system unless they exerted influence on its development.

The data also provided statistically significant evidence of an interaction effect of user expertise and degree of participation on system acceptance (extent of system use, F = 8.30, p = 0.0056; resistance to system use, F = 8.39; p = 0.0054; and user satisfaction, F = 15.83, p = 0.0002). To clarify this interaction, analysis of simple main effects of degree of participation for the two levels of expertise were conducted [14, 44]. This analysis indicated that the two low-expertise user groups were alike in their system acceptance (extent of system use, F = 0.14, p = 0.7145; resistance to system use, F = 0.02, p = 0.8943; and user satisfaction, F = 1.70, p = 0.20). This suggests that the users with a low perceived functional expertise, relative to designers, are likely to accept the system even if they had little influence on its development.

The two high-expertise user groups, on the contrary, displayed significantly different levels of system acceptance (extent of system use, F = 10.25, p = 0.0034; resistance to system use, F = 9.54, p = 0.0045; and user satisfaction, F = 16.31, p = 0.0004). Evidence suggests that the users with a high perceived functional expertise, relative to designers, are likely to accept the system only if they had a substantive influence on its development.

In summary, the experimental data strongly support the hypothesis that user influence on system development increased the likelihood of system acceptance by the participant users. Alternatively, lack of user influence amplified the likelihood of user resistance to the system. The support for the interaction hypotheses, however, suggests that user influence becomes imperative for system acceptance when users perceive themselves as experts in the system domain. These results validate the contingent significance of the users' system-related functional expertise in the participation process.

## A Field Test of Hypotheses

THE RESEARCH HYPOTHESES WERE TESTED IN THE FIELD SETTINGS AS WELL, to compensate for the simulated environment of the laboratory setting. To accomplish this, the system development activities in sixty-four organizations were surveyed.

Initially, the IS managers in these organizations identified systems that were (1) developed through user participation as members of design team, and (2) implemented during the past year. The design team criterion was employed to match the participation mechanism between the experiment and the survey. The recency of systems was expected to help users recall their perceptions and experiences during the system development process. The identified systems represented a cross-section of information systems applications.

IS managers also identified the participant users. These users were requested to express their perceptions and experiences during system development and were assured of strict confidentiality. Although the organizations and users were select, the large numbers of organizations and users enhanced the generalizability of the findings. User perceptions of degree of participation and expertise were measured using the questionnaire in appendix E. Users were also interviewed in person or by phone to confirm their participation in system development.

User responses were utilized to assign users to one of the four “degree of participation-expertise” combinations. These combinations were based on two levels of degree of participation and two expertise levels, much like the experiment. The first item determined the level of user participation—high or low—and the next three questions determined the perceived user expertise—high or low. Notably, the users with average participation (item 1) were placed in the high-participation category. This approach seemed logical because if users did not perceive their influence as small, then their input was viewed as significant, although others had the same amount of influence. (This option, however, was placed in the questionnaire to help users respond precisely to this item.) Likewise, equally expert users were placed in the high-expertise category. This classification scheme provided the following category sizes: HE,HP = 178, HE,LP = 130, LE,HP = 142, and LE,LP = 147. To convert these numbers into a balanced design equivalent to the experimental part of the study, observations were randomly dropped to attain a balanced design with 130 observations in each category. The test results reported next are based on this set of observations. (It is worth noting that statistical analysis with original, unequal category sizes provided similar results.)

The questionnaire used to measure user satisfaction is again based on Milton and Jenkins's instrument (see appendix F). The Cronbach alpha measure of reliability for this instrument is 0.90. The corrected item total correlations measure of validity ranges from 0.70 to 0.74, and all were significant at the 0.0001 level. Mean user satisfaction for the four participation-expertise combinations is listed in Tables 1 and 2.

The results supported the two hypotheses (H1: F = 22.34, p = 0.0001; H2: F = 18.89, p = 0.0001). Support for the first hypothesis provided further evidence that influential user participation enhances the chances of system success. Simple effects analysis confirmed that user influence becomes critical for system acceptance, when users perceive themselves as experts in the application domain relative to other members of the design team (F = 5.64, p = 0.0001). However, this influence becomes less significant when users perceive themselves as non-experts (F = 0.32, p = 0.75). Thus, the survey data also supported the contingent significance of a user's system-related functional expertise during the participation process.

## Discussion and Conclusion

THIS RESEARCH EXPLORED THE FOLLOWING TWO ASPECTS OF USER PARTICIPATION: (1) merits of user participation as a strategy to ensure system success, and (2) the contingent effect of users' functional expertise on the outcome of participation.

This study, based on a review of user participation and participative decision-making research, hypothesized that system acceptance depended on the degree of influence participant users exerted on system development. The research data supported this hypothesis. This result suggests that effective user participation entails substantive, rather than superficial, user contribution to system development. Furthermore, this finding implies that failure of participation measures to gauge user influence on system development could have contributed to the equivocal nature of user participation research.

It must be noted that, in both the experiment and the field survey, users exercised influence on the system design. This influence, according to participative decision-making theory, gave users a sense of contribution and control, feelings of system ownership, better understanding of system capabilities, and the like, and thus resulted in system acceptance by users. Consequently, in applying this finding, designers must make sure that user influence takes the form of user contribution to system design.

This research also hypothesized that users' system-related functional expertise modifies the relationship between user participation and system acceptance. The data supported this hypothesis and showed that the user influence on system development becomes imperative for system acceptance when users are perceived to possess greater system-related functional expertise than other members of the design team. However, this influence becomes less critical when users are perceived to possess less expertise than those who influenced the system design.

The status congruence theory explains this difference between high-expertise and low-expertise users. Accordingly, users expect congruence between their system-related functional expertise and their influence on system design. Consequently, users with high expertise and low influence, being incongruent, experience feelings of frustration, unfair treatment, and distrust of designers and other members, resulting in stronger negative attitudes toward the system. On the other hand, users with low expertise and low influence, being congruent, do not develop negative attitudes toward the system. Furthermore, with their limited contributions, they may not develop strong feelings of system ownership or sense of control, but they will understand system capabilities better and develop confidence in the system. Thus, they are likely to accept the system.

This finding has important implications. On one hand, it identifies a situation where user influence on system development is a must for system acceptance. On the other hand, it specifies a situation where users may accept a system regardless of their influence on its development. Thus, this research finds that influential participation of functionally expert users is important not only from a system quality perspective, as frequently suggested in the literature (e.g., [7, 19, 36]), but from that of system acceptance as well.

Despite the emphasis on quality user–designer interaction [3], empirical research provides little insight into what constitutes quality interaction. This research fills in some gaps in this respect. The support for correspondence between “user expertise” and “user influence on system design” presents an important benchmark to systems personnel to monitor and improve the quality of user–designer interaction. Specifically, this interaction must facilitate user contribution to system design and also maintain correspondence between user expertise and user contribution to system design.

The research results also furnish a critical criterion for selecting users to participate in system design. The criteria advanced in the literature include: personality traits $[22]$ ; functional technical and communication skills $[9, 19, 26, 42]$ ; and computing background $[53]$ . However, empirical investigations of these criteria are limited and confusing. Consequently, it is not surprising that system development directors express frustration that functional managers are unwilling to assign qualified users to design teams $[2, 41, 54]$ , and, on the other hand, system designers themselves are criticized for talking to the wrong users during system development $[22, 28, 53]$ .

On the basis of the theoretical analysis and empirical evidence, this study suggests that users' functional expertise should be employed as the primary criterion for selecting users to serve on design teams. Other criteria, such as users' communication skills, computing backgrounds, and personality traits should be given secondary consideration. This guideline should be observed when system designers are deliberating which users to approach for participation in system development, or when functional managers are deciding which users to assign to the design team. Furthermore, given that time and budgetary constraints affect the extent of user participation and system scope, this study suggests relying on users' functional expertise in determining system scope and in resolving conflicts related to system design and scope.

The research findings also posit a potential strategy to introduce a system to the larger, or geographically dispersed, user community, after developing the system through participation by a few, representative users. The research data unequivocally suggest that systems developed through substantive contribution by expert users will likely be accepted by other participant users. Extrapolating this result to nonparticipant users suggests that communicating the criteria employed in selecting participants and their contribution to system design should enhance the overall chances of system success.

## Limitations of the Study

THE RESULTS OF THE STUDY SHOULD BE INTERPRETED and accepted with caution, since both the experiment and the survey segments of the study have certain inherent limitations.

The laboratory experiment obviously lacks the dynamics of the real world. The student users may not be true representatives of organizational users in their attitudes and reactions. Moreover, student users may react in a way they think experimenters want them to react (demand effect). Besides, the experiment results are limited to the type of application used in the experiment. Also, since the experiment involved a one-to-one interaction between users and designers, its findings may not hold for organizational design efforts with larger design teams. Furthermore, in the experiment, both the amount of participation and the opportunity to demonstrate the results of participation are essentially limited, which raises internal validity concerns.

While survey data overcome the simulated environment of the experiment, they suffer from some limitations of their own. Since survey data represent ex-post user perceptions, user responses may have been influenced by the ultimate success or failure of the system. Users may unconsciously upgrade their perceptions of participation if they like the system, and downgrade their perceptions if they dislike the system.

One weakness in both the experiment and the survey data is the lack of longitudinal measurement of system acceptance. Furthermore, single-item scales used to gauge users' perceptions of their relative expertise and influences limit the findings.

The limitations in the design and execution of the experiment and survey certainly raise concerns about reliability and generalizability of the findings of this study. However, realizing similar results from multiple methods enhances the confidence in the results obtained. Consequently, this study makes a contribution to the understanding and effective implementation of user participation.
