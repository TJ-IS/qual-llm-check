# Organizational Learning during Advanced System Development: Opportunities and Obstacles

ERIC W. STEIN AND BETTY VANDENBOSCH

ERIC W. STEIN is Associate Professor of Management Science and Information Systems at The Pennsylvania State University, and teaches in the Master of Management program at the Great Valley campus in Malvern, PA. Dr. Stein holds a B.A. from Amherst College and received his Ph.D. in 1989 from The Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania. His research interests are organizational memory and learning, human expertise, and expert systems. His papers have appeared in several refereed journals including Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, and Expert Systems: Research and Applications. He has presented research at the Academy of Management, the International Conference on Information Systems (ICIS), the Hawaii International Conference on Systems Science (HICSS), and specialized conferences in artificial intelligence and business.

BETTY VANDENBOSCH is the Lewis-Progressive Assistant Professor in the Management Information and Decision Systems Department of the Weatherhead School of Management at Case Western Reserve University. She holds a Ph.D. from the Western Business School at the University of Western Ontario. Dr. Vandenbosch has presented papers at various national and international conferences and has published in Accounting, Management and Information Technologies, Information Systems Journal, and Journal of Management Information Systems. She also has several years experience as a management consultant and continues to consult to several organizations on management information issues. Her current research interests focus on executive information and the impact of technology on individual and organizational learning.

ABSTRACT: Higher-order organizational learning occurs when a company adopts new principles, assumptions, and paradigms, which often turn into competitive advantage. Systems development and implementation offer an opportunity for higher-order organizational learning that is rarely exploited. Advanced information systems, in particular expert systems (ES) and executive information systems (EIS), provide ample opportunities for higher-order organizational learning if the development process is structured in certain ways. This work includes an analysis of three organizations in terms of project outcomes, organizational learning outcomes, and organizational performance. On the basis of these assessments, five critical success factors are identified that may contribute to organizational learning during advanced system development. The relationships between these factors and organizational outcomes are summarized in a preliminary model that can form the basis for future research. The work closes with some recommendations for ways information systems managers can encourage higher-order organizational learning during advanced system development.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: advanced information systems, executive support systems, expert systems, information systems development, organizational learning, sociotechnical design.

MUCH OF THE MANAGEMENT LITERATURE DIVIDES ORGANIZATIONAL LEARNING into two types. For example, Argyris and Schon [3] divide learning into single-loop and double-loop learning. Senge [40] distinguishes between adaptive learning (learning to cope) and generative learning (learning to create). March [31] characterizes learning as the exploitation of old certainties and the exploration of new possibilities. He believes that “maintaining an appropriate balance between exploration and exploitation is a primary factor in . . . survival and prosperity” (p. 71).

Theories about learning in organizations state that, while both types of learning are evident, single-loop learning (adaptive learning or exploitation) is the more likely behavior $[19, 29, 35]$ . Single-loop learning implies that basic routines remain appropriate. In comparison, double-loop learning is much riskier and its benefits more remote. New ideas, markets, and products have much longer time horizons, more uncertain outcomes, and more indirect results than the exploitation of existing ideas, markets, and products $[31]$ . Moreover, frameworks and criteria for their evaluation are lacking. Yet, without double-loop learning, stagnation begins and the organization ultimately fails $[20]$ . Indeed, competitive sustainability requires that organizations both explore the unknown and exploit the known $[31]$ . Examples abound illustrating how double-loop learning has the capability to enhance organizational performance and profitability significantly. Elkington and Burke $[14]$ found that managers who were forced to respond to environmental issues inadvertently increased profits because more efficient production methods were found when the status quo was challenged. Anecdotally, the successes of Dell Computer, American Express Travel-Related Services, 3M, Wal-Mart, American Hospital Supply, and many other companies offer examples of firms that have reconceptualized competitive recipes $[45]$ , competitive environments, and products and services $[21]$ because their executives engaged in double-loop learning.

Information technology has been associated with learning (e.g., [8, 24, 28, 34, 39, 44]), although not always in a favorable light. Several researchers have argued that information technology inhibits double-loop learning. Proponents of this view believe that most information systems promote rigidity rather than flexibility because they define a narrow view of the world by implicitly stipulating assumptions, environmental characteristics, “relevant” information, objectives, units of analysis, and so forth [23]. After a system is implemented, information is typically filtered in a specific way or decisions are reached using a specific formula. As a consequence, the technology often reduces discretion and limits intuition [22, 43], or, as Argyris [2] writes, “Most organizations, often without realizing it, create systems of learning that suppress double-loop inquiry and make it very difficult for even a well-designed information system to be effective” (p. 114).

Nevertheless, the development and implementation of information systems can be a catalyst for higher-order learning because of interrelationships between technical and social aspects of systems development [7, 30, 32, 36]. When people are forced to reflect on how they undertake their work in order to explain how to automate it, they have the opportunity to modify their understanding of how their work processes can be improved [13]. Unfortunately, many development methods are not explicit about the benefits of this sort of reflection and, consequently, do not ensure that it will take place. For example, IBM's Business Systems Planning process requires an understanding of the major problems encountered in achieving organizational purposes [53]; the popular critical success factors approach [12, 38] requires an understanding of the critical success factors of the organization in order to develop a system to support them; and Wetherbe's [53] ends/means analysis requires an understanding of the “ends” that the organization wants to achieve and the means to get there. In all these methods, it is possible to learn a great deal about the organization and to do things differently as a result of that learning. In most cases, however, rather than struggling with learning, development is little more than a codification of current requirements. Success is usually defined in terms of system completion rather than transformation of the organization.

Many researchers and practitioners have recognized these and many other limitations of traditional development methodologies. Certainly, developers of advanced information technologies such as expert systems (ES) and executive information systems (EIS) use techniques such as prototyping to mitigate the rigidities inherent in systems development. Systems employing these technologies are typically developed, used, and refined as their developers and users learn more about them and about the use to which they will be put. In theory, this iterative process enables users to learn about what they need and want and enables developers to build and modify systems according to users' changing requirements. However, according to Cooprider and Henderson, "there is a danger in prototyping that the system builder and user will come to agreement on a solution before they have fully evaluated all of the system requirements" [11, p. 73]. Once again, while organizational learning is a possibility, it is not guaranteed and it does not always materialize.

We believe that organizations rarely capitalize on the potential for higher-order organizational learning inherent in the development and implementation of advanced information technologies. The purpose of this paper is to examine the opportunities for and obstacles to organizational learning during the life cycle of advanced information systems. We use the term “advanced information technologies” in a way that is consistent with Huber $[26]$ , although we choose to focus on what he refers to as computer-assisted decision-aiding technologies—for example, expert systems, decision support systems, and executive information systems. We begin with a framework for analyzing three different cases of advanced system development in terms of project outcomes, learning outcomes, and organizational outcomes. We then identify some of the factors that contribute to higher-order learning throughout the system development life cycle in the context of the three case examples. Next, we discuss ways to research learning outcomes empirically in the context of systems development, as well as providing some general recommendations for managers. We close with the lessons we learned from these assessments.

## Theoretical Justification for Organizational Learning during Development

SUPPORT FOR OUR CONTENTION THAT SYSTEM DEVELOPMENT can be an opportunity for higher-order organizational learning comes from a variety of sources. For example, Boland [7] proposed a framework for guiding the process of system development that provides opportunities for organizational learning. His theory is based on the belief that designers have “selectively ignored their own biases . . . and have underestimated the ambiguity and dialectical quality of social reality” (pp. 259–260). While he does not refer specifically to learning, he does support the notion that, for the most part, development is based on a static model of the organization that is not affected by the environment or by the action of managers. Boland believes that information systems help to construct an “organizational reality through the symbolic interaction of its participants” (p. 262). He proposes that what is required is an “action-based” systems development approach that creates rather than codifies an understanding of the organizational situation. This notion is based on Churchman’s [9] proposition that underlying assumptions must be challenged in information systems design.

These ideas find additional support in current literature that identifies interactions between information technology (IT) and the organization $[36]$ . Their model is based on the theory of structuration articulated by Giddens $[16, 17, 18]$ , who suggested that human actions simultaneously condition and are conditioned by institutional properties in social contexts. The primary modalities for mutual interaction are interpretive schemes, resources, and norms. Interpretive schemes are vehicles for the communication of meaning. Resources are allocated by human agents, thus becoming the basis for individual power. Once legitimized, they contribute to structures of domination. Norms are rules and conventions that constrain behavior within acceptable limits. Norms emerge from patterns of recurrent interaction between human agents based on personal notions of what is sanctioned. Mutual interaction via the three modalities between the realm of human action and the institutional realm constitutes the process of structuration.

It may be observed that two of the modalities for structuration (i.e., interpretive schemes and norms) are also critical to learning in organizations. For instance, single-loop learning occurs as members detect deviations from prevailing norms and take action to minimize those variances. Double-loop learning occurs when members question underlying assumptions and norms and modify their shared maps and theories in use—that is, their interpretive schemes. Organizational learning, thus, represents a subset of interactions that may be said to contribute to structuration. Robey and Orlikowski [36] suggest that technology in general and IT in particular are central to the processes of structuration. Information technology as an artifact represents the values, norms, and meanings adopted by developers and users. Furthermore, the process of designing these artifacts and using them mediates human action, which in turn can lead to the development of institutional properties. Viewed in this way, the possibilities for organizational learning via system development are opened.

While the preceding literature supports the notion that higher-order learning is possible (even inevitable) during the system development life cycle, actual changes in method and procedure have not been documented outside of a few works. Avison and Wood-Harper [4] illustrate the application of sociotechnical system-design methods to system development, which provide an environment more receptive to organizational learning. Salaway [39] developed and studied the impact of an “organization learning” methodology on user–analyst interactions. She found that the new methodology generated more valid information with fewer errors remaining undetected throughout the development process. The goal of our work is to move our understanding along this path by identifying specific factors that contribute to higher-order learning during advanced system development. We accomplish this goal through an analysis of case material, common practices, and extant theory, resulting in a preliminary model of factors that coproduce organizational learning.

## Three Case Examples with Analysis

IT IS INSTRUCTIVE TO BEGIN WITH A FRAMEWORK FOR ANALYZING different outcomes that occur during system development. The system development life cycle, or SDLC, is usually parsed into several phases that may be grouped into development (systems analysis, design, and implementation) and postimplementation (operation and maintenance) [54]. During these phases, three outcomes are of interest: project outcomes, organizational learning behavior outcomes, and organizational performance, all of which can assume different values. These outcome states are summarized in Table 1.

For example, a project may succeed or fail in terms of long-term completion (or it may experience mixed results whereby features are scaled down or the system dies a premature death). Development may result in codification of existing procedures, may improve on them (OL1), or may result in a completely new way of conducting business (OL2). In addition, the organization may experience positive or negative changes in performance (or no changes) that may be attributed to the system development project. Each system timeline will have a unique profile according to these attributes. Ideally, an organization will seek opportunities for organizational learning during system development, although in practice, project outcomes (e.g., completion success) often dominate the agenda. We examine three different cases of advanced system development arising from our intervention experiences that illustrate some of the possible learning and organizational outcomes associated with system development and implementation.

## EIS Case 1

Company A is a \$4.4 billion financial institution with 13,000 employees that prides itself on its technical sophistication. The organization embraced the use of electronic mail earlier than most of its competitors, and most employees had work stations on their desks. The EIS, when proposed, was considered “another tool in the competitive arsenal.” Initial development of the system was driven by the Information Center (IC).

Company A paid fairly close attention to typical steps to EIS success (e.g., [5, 51]). At the outset, the CEO was a powerful sponsor. He was extremely supportive because the IC had convinced him that the EIS would provide executives who were not comfortable analyzing raw data tables with much better access to the information they needed to make decisions. He believed that this analytical capability was critical to the success of his organization. Product areas were responsible for the quality of information and for implementing new applications. Twenty people throughout the organization were trained to create value-added applications, and it was believed that the applications would be of strategic importance to the business. MIS expertise was used throughout. Development was the responsibility of four developers who worked full time on the project. Data availability was assured, since no development stepped outside the bounds of the existing information infrastructure. The prototype was developed and enhanced over the initial two-year period in an iterative manner. Reports were modified according to user requests. Finally, throughout the two-year period, the role of the system was communicated to overcome resistance. The system was given a catchy name, and mouse pads and coffee mugs were appropriately emblazoned. In addition, regular E-mail broadcasts heralded improvements and future plans.

Unfortunately, by the end of the second year, the effort lost steam. The head of the IC still considered himself the focal point for EIS development. He felt the system continued to be an immature product and that it was “still in the infancy stage.” He thought it would become successful as soon as the organization found a few “killer” reports. He believed, however, that these reports were elusive and that finding them would be serendipitous. He also believed that his main audience—the regional vice presidents—did not know what they needed to run the business, and it was up to the EIS developers to help them figure that out. For the most part, development of the EIS had not progressed beyond providing reports that had previously been provided on paper in a more timely and flexible manner.

Users were skeptical of the IC's ability to provide them with anything useful. Many felt the pressure of having to provide reports to be put up on the EIS but also felt that putting information on screens would not improve performance. For example, the CFO said: “The reports can only have value if I have a use for them . . . something has to be presold to me. I tended not to drive early applications onto [the EIS] because its features are irrelevant or of minor value.” By this time, the president’s initial enthusiasm had waned as well: “So far, I am still not convinced that the value is there. Another report doesn’t do anything for me. We’ve got to have people that are creating those systems that are thinking all the way through and say, ‘Now, how does this affect cost or revenue?’” He also indicated that he did not have the time or the inclination to determine what information would be helpful. Rather, he wanted to evaluate what was put in front of him or conduct “back-of-the-envelope” analyses when he needed to dig deeper.

## An Expert System Case

At the K corporation, inspection training was perceived as a problem because it relied heavily on a flow of temporary help owing to the cyclical nature of its business (for a full description of this case, see Stein and Evans [48]). To address this problem, an expert system was developed to train and assist inspection workers on its assembly lines. The inspection expert system trained technicians to discriminate between parts that were acceptable and those that were not. The main benefit expected from the system was a reduction in the amount of time two experts spent in their roles as trainers, which was achieved. Once implemented in prototype form, the expert system saved about 66 percent of the experts' time spent training and advising new assembly technicians. The typical training time of eighteen hours over a period of twelve weeks was reduced to only six hours to train new assembly technicians when utilizing the system. Thus, by all accounts, the system was a success. The expert system's impact on the organization, however, turned out to be more significant.

The expert system was used to track the number of products that were deemed acceptable over a period of four months. As a result of the system's data-collection capabilities, it was determined that only 15 percent of the manufactured products were suitable and the total labor cost per product exceeded twenty dollars. Although excessive costs during the early stages of a product's life cycle are common, a member of the organization who helped design the expert system was alarmed by these figures and by what he learned about the organization in the process of system development. Further analysis motivated the production team to redesign the manufacturing system in order to improve capacity and yield. The redesign increased production levels forty to fifty times and reduced labor costs to about \$0.10 per unit. Furthermore, the defect rate dropped to a very small percentage, inspection no longer required a dedicated staff, and the expert system was not needed. The investment in the redesign was paid back within sixteen months. Based on these and other data, the key outcomes for K corporation are given in Table 3.

In this case, development and implementation led to conditions for double-loop organizational learning, which resulted in performance improvements in the organization.

## EIS Case 2

Company B is a multi-billion-dollar mining company with 9,000 employees. When EIS development was contemplated, the organization was not particularly computer-literate. Development of the EIS was initiated because the senior executive responsible for international sales felt a need to provide his organization with management information about sales in any form that might be relevant. Neither he nor those who helped with the initial conception of the system knew what they wanted. A consultant to the project described the situation as follows:

From day 1, they knew that while they were replacing the base transactional systems, the ultimate intent was to be able to provide management information. They didn't know what it was, they didn't know what they needed, they had no idea this kind of system, that kind of system, but they knew that if they had good base systems with data integrity that they could rely on, that they would, in fact, be able to do something with them, even though they couldn't define what that something was.

At every step in development, questions were raised: What do we use? How and how often do we use it? Is it valid the way it is? There was little concern about strategic necessity. “If we can get a benefit out of something, it doesn’t matter if it’s not strategic.” The head of the international sales organization was very comfortable with the notion of an evolving system conducive and responsive to learning. He was a proponent of developing regular expectations against which to judge reality: “Every time reality’s different from expectations, that’s an indication you have to look for more, you have to understand more.” He felt that the factor that made a difference was attitudinal, clearly showing a concern for social rather than technical issues. His job was to encourage his subordinates to think of the system as a learning opportunity rather than a system that enabled them to answer the questions that were asked of them, much as a school child would do. Development was clearly a process, rather than just an outcome. He also felt that development was not over, nor would it ever be complete:

The environment changes and the information that was meaningful for the old kind of market is not meaningful for the next kind of market. So we're doing a bit of experimenting. What's happening is that it's like the process of innovation . . . but what happens is when you have a system and it provides you with information you didn't have before, then that very act causes new ideas. And these new ideas then feed back into looking at the information differently and that will generate a new strategy. So, it does generate new strategy . . . not in the system, but in the attempt to get the most use out of the system.

Based on these and other data, the key outcomes for company B are given in Table 4. System changes were driven by the market. Executives regularly reflected on the type of information they were using to make decisions. When they found themselves focusing on different factors, the system was revised to incorporate them. Here it is clear that higher-order learning guided the ongoing development process. In addition, the organization took a contextual view of the expertise the users of the systems had and were developing. The system was revised as a result of the experiences of its users in the context of their day-to-day work.

Company B (and the senior vice president in charge of international marketing in particular) was comfortable with the notion of “mucking around” to find out what was required and changing it when it no longer fit. Development was an opportunity to learn, and expectations about what that learning would entail were not specific. On the other hand, despite a lack of clear direction, the system was extremely successful. It was credited with substantially improving bottom-line results in the two years following its inception. It seems to us that the explicit attention the organization paid to process and to the learning that took place was fundamental to the ongoing success of this particular EIS.

## System Development as an Opportunity for Higher-Order Learning

WE CONTEND THAT SYSTEM DEVELOPMENT AND IMPLEMENTATION are an opportunity for organizational learning, especially double-loop learning. System development is a time when both users and developers are in the position to apply and to question dominant values and norms. As such, the development effort can lead to changes in the way things are done, regardless of the actual delivery of the system. ## Factors That Favor Learning during Advanced System Development

In companies K and B, we observed several instances of single- and double-loop organizational learning instead of mere codification. From these observations, we suggest that there are a number of critical success factors (CSFs) that favor higher-order organizational learning (OL2) in the context of expert system and EIS development (we might note that the first three CSFs we have identified and, to a lesser extent, the last one may apply to conventional systems as well). These factors are outlined in Table 5 and contrasted with frequently used and common practices.

The first critical success factor we have identified is the organizational and managerial (vs. the technical) orientation of the developer, whom we see playing a key role in determining the learning outcomes of a project. For example, in the expert systems case, the knowledge engineer had an advanced degree in management in addition to a degree in engineering, which made him sensitive to organizational issues; he also was being groomed for upper management. More commonly, expert system developers view system development in technical rather than organizational terms. This is in part due to their training, as reflected in development textbooks. A simple content analysis of a few of the most popular expert systems textbooks reveals that typically only about 10 percent of the text is devoted to organizational issues. This lack of recognition of organizational issues tends to produce knowledge engineers who fail to see the relationships between artifact creation and organizational structuring. We suggest that projects under technically oriented developers will experience fewer organizational learning outcomes than those under persons with an organizational orientation. Company A's EIS bears this out. The IC manager considered the system a technical creation rather than a set of organizational activities. On the other hand, the outcomes associated with the second EIS case suggest that projects developed with high levels of participation by developer/managers who are eager to change the way business is conducted will produce more opportunities for higher-order organizational learning.

Development focus is another critical success factor. By development focus, we mean: To what extent does the project favor the process of development rather than the product? In the first EIS case, there was a high level of focus on the product, as evidenced by adherence to Barrow's [5] EIS method. Despite this, the project had little effect on the organization, and organizational learning was not a key outcome. Why? We believe the developers neglected the process of development. Walls, Widmeyer, and El Sawy [52] observe that design, which is a component phase of development, is both a product and a process. Development texts tend to favor descriptions of system requirements, specifications, and testing, but not the development process. Although the action steps built into Barrow's method (e.g., "identify," "plan," and "communicate") do not preclude learning, they do not encourage experimentation or the kind of "Let's think about whether we are doing this the best way" philosophy that is essential to higher-order learning. In the expert systems case example, the knowledge engineer used a methodology that incorporated into the development process management assessments such as those described in Turban and Liebowitz [50]. We believe this factor helped to promote organizational learning throughout the process. Unfortunately, this sensitivity is not typical. A simple analysis of three widely used expert systems texts reveals that about 80 percent of the text is typically devoted to product development, while about 20 percent is devoted to construction procedures. Developers schooled in conventional methods tend to be less sensitive to process issues during development and are likely to overlook opportunities for organizational learning.

Another critical success factor we have identified is the paradigm that is the foundation for all phases of development, especially analysis and design. Development methods are built, either implicitly or explicitly, on “kernel” theories borrowed from the natural and social sciences [52]. Design theories built on natural-science paradigms tend to underplay or ignore learning behaviors or the limitations of human “rationality,” just as early theories of management influenced by engineering science (e.g., Taylor) tended to view organizations as machines. Robey, Wishart, and Rodriguez-Diaz [37] provide an excellent critique of business reengineering based on overreliance on the engineering metaphor and suggest that we move instead toward paradigms that embrace learning. Most texts on expert system development adopt kernel theories rooted in engineering science or simply avoid theoretical commitment of any kind (e.g., “knowledge acquisition is an art”). The weak affinity for kernel theories from the social or biological sciences has resulted in methods that tend to ignore the role of learning during system development. In the second EIS case, the theory-in-use of one of the managers closely aligned with the project was rooted in biological and social metaphors of adaptation: “The environment changes and the information that was meaningful for the old kind of market is not meaningful for the next kind of market. So we’re doing a bit of experimenting.” We believe that this viewpoint helped create opportunities for higher-order learning in that organization. The same kinds of views were evident at K corporation, where the expert system was developed.

The next CSF we have identified is the view of expertise generally held by the developers. We believe that developers and managers who view expertise in its organizational context are more likely to be open to higher-order learning than those who view expertise in isolation. The dominant schools of thought in cognitive science, psychology, and artificial intelligence view expertise as a function of individual characteristics such as mental models $[15]$ , memory and pattern matching mechanisms $[6]$ , and levels of competence $[49]$ . More recent works on the subject have tried to situate the expert in his or her social context (e.g., $[1, 41, 42, 46, 47]$ ). A contextual view of expertise encourages the system developer to analyze the social structures that support flows of knowledge within the organization (see, e.g., $[46]$ ). By analyzing social structures using a technique such as network analysis, the developer and management can more easily examine the existing assumptions and paradigms upon which the organization is built. Such reflection opens the door to higher-order learning. We saw evidence of this viewpoint in the second EIS case. The most senior executive in this organization interspersed his personal reflection about the system and the information it provided with conversations with other users, in order both to check his own expectations and understandings and to build on the accumulation of ideas that resulted. Unfortunately, expertise, which is the foundation for all knowledge-based systems, is seldom viewed in this way.

The last CSF is the type of interaction that occurs between the developer and the domain experts and users who help build the system. For example, when one is building expert systems, knowledge acquisition (KA) processes typically reinforce communication patterns that impede higher-order learning. Argyris and Schon [3] identify these behavioral patterns as Model I behaviors. In brief, Model I behaviors impede higher-order learning because individuals seek unilateral control, maximize winning and losing, maximize rationality, and minimize the expression of negative feelings (see the appendix for a complete explanation of Model I and its impact during advanced systems development). Among the negative consequences of Model I are miscommunication, mistrust, protectiveness, self-fulfilling prophecies, and escalating error. At best, under this model, developers build systems that support the dominant values and norms of the organization. At worst, systems built under this model will have functional error built in and suffer from implementation delays as suggested by Salaway [39]. The alternative to Model I is Model II. Model II behaviors reinforce the free flow of information combined with public testing (see the section “Advice for System Developers and Managers” below for a more complete description of Model II). We see evidence in the second EIS case that the organization operated closer to Model II assumptions than Model I. For example, statements to the effect of, “Every time reality is different from expectations, that’s an indication you have to look for more, you have to understand more,” derive more from Model II than Model I. Developers who embrace Model II assumptions are more likely to help their organizations achieve higher-order learning than those who do not.

## Future Research

WHILE IT IS OUTSIDE THE SCOPE OF THIS PAPER TO TEST OUR CLAIMS empirically, we wish to articulate an emerging model that can be made the basis for future research. We encourage others to frame appropriate research designs in order to understand the relationships between advanced system development and organizational learning.

The success factors and their relationships vis-à-vis learning that we have identified are summarized in the following model (see figure 1). We propose that each of the critical success factors (on the left side of the figure) will contribute positively to an overall learning environment, leading to instances of organizational learning. We identify the need for suitable instruments to measure developer orientation (organizational vs. technical); development focus (process vs. product); development paradigm (learning vs. engineering); view of expertise (in context vs. isolation); and expert-developer interaction (Model II vs. Model I). We also assume that organizations that experience more instances of higher-order organizational learning will be rewarded with positive organizational performance, although this is an untested assumption both here and in the literature at large. The challenge with research of this nature is to develop a design that meaningfully compares alternatives. Conducting additional case studies will provide instances of the various learning/outcome combinations, and we encourage such research. On the other hand, case studies alone will not result in validity or generalizability. Toward that end, a more fruitful approach might be to compare the development processes for similar systems in different organizations, or two or more systems being developed in a single organization. Researchers would observe the development processes, paying particular attention to the methods and outcomes of each phase. Such descriptive data would provide clues to the strengths of the proposed relationships. Alternatively, one could compare the results of projects where active intervention was used to encourage adherence to our recommendations. The outcomes of these “treatment” groups would be compared with the “control” groups in which conventional methods were employed. We use the notions of “control” and “treatment” loosely and do not imply the use of strict experimental designs. Given the complexities of the interrelationships among the social and technical aspects of development, it is not possible to conduct such studies in artificial settings; real managers and real developers creating real systems will be necessary. However, a program tailored for one or more organizations might enable the provision of the appropriate conditions.

## Advice for System Developers and Managers

We close with some practical advice for organizations that develop advanced information systems in-house (for organizations that outsource development, we suggest that this advice become the basis for the selection of an appropriate outsourcing agent). In essence, systems development can only develop into an opportunity for organizational learning if there is a shift in the ways developers and managers view the process itself. Based on our synthesis of the literature and the cases, we offer the following five recommendations:

## Recommendation 1: Frame the problem as a sociotechnical problem.

Although employing a sociotechnical perspective [33] or applying the concepts of neohumanism [25] are not new ideas, system development is still construed as simply a technical problem. According to sociotechnical design methodology, joint optimization of the social and technical dimensions of the problem needs to be realized. Several excellent examples of systems developed using sociotechnical principles can be found in the work of Avison and Wood-Harper [4]. Advanced technology development should pay appropriate attention to the organizational aspects of development, including the implications for organizational learning. This suggestion finds support in a recent work by Conrath and Sharma [10], who advocate a sociotechnical framework for expert systems quality. They propose that one measure of quality be based on the degree to which the organization learned as a result of the use of the expert system. We accept and agree with this proposition.

## Recommendation 2: Adopt a process orientation during development.

System development is both a source of product and a process. Most textbooks on knowledge engineering favor product development over construction procedures. Knowledge engineers and EIS developers should consider the process of development as well as the integrity of the end-product. The quality of interaction between developers and end users should be reviewed to encourage organizational learning.

Recommendation 3: Incorporate organizational learning theories into the development method.

Walls et al. [52] observe that kernel theories borrowed from the social and natural sciences are essential to the product and process of design. A kernel theory serves as a theoretical foundation for a development method, which can influence and structure the procedures governing artifact construction, including the kind of communication interaction that occurs among users, managers, and developers. By explicitly incorporating theories of organizational learning into theories of development, organizations may be able to create opportunities for organizational learning. An example of how organizational learning theory can be incorporated into the development process is provided in recommendation 5.

## Recommendation 4: Adopt a contextual view of expertise.

As long as developers view human expertise as a localized resource that can be “extracted,” opportunities for organizational learning will be rare. This perspective minimizes the relationship between the expert and the organization and encourages developers to engage in Model I communication strategies that minimize learning. Seen in context, human experts are viewed as resources that serve organizational constituencies, which validate the knowledge of their experts. Thus, an expert embodies the assumptions and values of the organization. System developers may stimulate the expert and the members of the organization to reexamine these assumptions throughout the development process rather than blindly codifying them. Unfortunately, current prescriptions typically do not follow these recommendations and provide advice of their own that seems to inhibit learning.

## Recommendation 5: Restructure developer interaction.

Knowledge engineers and developers need to change their interaction patterns with the experts and users who contribute to their systems. One model to be considered is that of Argyris and Schon [3]. By adopting Argyris and Schon's theory of Model II learning as a kernel theory, an organization can move away from the problems associated with Model I learning behavior as noted earlier. ## Summary and Conclusions

WE HAVE ARGUED THAT THE DEVELOPMENT OF ADVANCED information systems provides a potential opportunity for organizational learning, although that potential is seldom realized for several reasons. On the other hand, we observed that some organizations do produce organizational learning outcomes when expert systems and executive information systems are built. In these organizations, different norms and values existed that helped to structure the development processes in terms of learning outcomes. We identified what we believed to be the major factors most conducive to organizational learning during advanced SDLC. However, more research is required to understand the precise nature of the relationships between different levels of learning and organizational outcomes, and we encourage others to address these important research questions. Finally, we offered some preliminary practical suggestions for encouraging organizational learning during system development: adopting a sociotechnical design perspective, adopting a process orientation, incorporating theories of organizational learning into the development method, adopting a contextual view of expertise, and adopting Model II types of behavior during development.

The knowledge acquisition phase of development is also one that can be extremely frustrating as well as time consuming. Some have termed it the bottleneck of expert systems development. In this passage, the author acknowledges the difficulty of dealing with the human aspects of design and notes that knowledge acquisition is often the major development bottleneck.

[B]ased upon the assumption that a human is now performing the task that is to be performed in the future by an expert system, our first step is to identify that person (or persons). Once this person has been identified, the next step is to set up an initial meeting with the alleged domain expert. This meeting should be informal because you most decidedly want the inaugural meeting with this person to take place in a relaxed atmosphere . . . .This first meeting establishes some rather critical first impressions. And first impressions in an atmosphere of tension and excessive formality are not likely to be favorable.

There are several purposes for the initial meeting. First, as mentioned, we wish to relax the individual. Second, we should attempt to explain (simply and clearly) to the individual just precisely what it is that we intend to accomplish. Typically, one emphasizes that our purpose (i.e., as knowledge engineers) is not to use and then discard the expert, but rather it is to provide him or her with a computerized assistant so that he or she can pursue more interesting work. Here, we are addressing one of the most obvious fears of the expert. Third, we should lay out a proposed schedule for follow-on meetings, and describe their general form and purpose ....

... It should be stressed that each and every meeting with the designated expert should be carefully considered beforehand, and that a formal agenda should be drawn up. Any mistakes made during such meetings have the potential for destroying the often fragile working relationship between the knowledge engineers and the expert, and any time that is perceived to be wasted will not be well received by most busy experts. [pp. 112–113]

Thus, the author lays the groundwork for Model I behavior. The knowledge engineer establishes his or her goals for the initial session while assuming unilateral control. A high degree of protectiveness is also evident, as is minimization of negative feelings, which can lead to miscommunication and error.

There is yet another purpose to the initial meeting, as well as those that follow, that should not be openly discussed. Specifically, we should use this meeting, as well as follow-on meetings, to attempt to evaluate the true extent of the expertise of our expert. As mentioned before, all too often one is designated an expert as a result of simply getting the job done, even when the job is not being performed all that well. Cloning the expertise of such individuals will result in nothing more than a perpetuation of this less-than-desirable performance, and this, in fact, is precisely all that a number of existing expert systems accomplish. However, if one is inclined toward cynicism, it may also be noted that few of the users of such expert systems actually appreciate this problem. That is, they suffer the same illusions about the expert system as they did about the human expert.

If and when you encounter a domain expert in whom you have no confidence, there are a number of alternatives that should be considered. First, and most obviously, you might try to find a replacement—someone else in the organization who seems reasonably competent in the domain under consideration. This option, of course, requires a certain amount of diplomacy. Second, you might consider learning enough about the problem at hand so that you can act as the domain expert. [pp. 113–114]

Here, the author urges the knowledge engineer to maximize winning and control in order to save face.

Returning to the primary point of our discussion, as long as we feel confident that the expert systems approach is indeed the most appropriate, and that the domain expert is reasonably competent, we may continue with the knowledge acquisition process. Thus, following our initial informal meeting with the domain expert, we should conduct a series of formal meetings designed to extract as much information, as painlessly as possible. [p. 114]

The literature in the field of artificial intelligence and expert systems is replete with references to “knowledge extraction,” presenting an imperialistic view of human resources. Again, the primary governing variables are ones of control and rationality.

There should hopefully develop, during the knowledge acquisition sessions with the domain expert, a sense of mutual respect. Ideally, the knowledge engineers should have a genuine respect for the domain expert, for the job that he or she does, and for the heuristics that have been developed through real-world experience. Even more important, the domain expert must develop a genuine respect for the knowledge engineers. Thus, while the knowledge engineers are obviously novices with respect to the specific domain of interest, it must be apparent that they have an ability to solve real problems and are knowledgeable and experienced in their own profession. One of the best ways in which to gain the respect of the domain expert is to make an all out attempt—well before the initial meeting—to understand the problem, the environment in which it exists, and the specific terminology and jargon employed by the expert. This last point cannot be emphasized enough. Contrast, if you will, the example of the American tourist in a foreign country who has made an effort to learn at least a few common words and phrases in the language of that country with the tourist who keeps asking “Why don’t these ignorant people speak English?”—and I think you can guess how each will be perceived by the locals. [p. 115]

The governing variables in the passage above are saving face, maximizing winning, and minimizing negative feelings. The author suggests means for the knowledge engineer (KE) to “engineer” the impressions of the expert—in short, the KE is in control to win.

One must always keep in mind that the purpose of these meetings is to extract the knowledge base of the expert. While this sounds obvious, it has been observed that the discussions in some knowledge acquisition meetings meander off onto tangents as the discussants pursue points that have little if any bearing on the knowledge base. For example, one particular meeting that we attended quickly degenerated into a gossip session when the expert began to complain about the work habits of a fellow employee. These work habits, in turn, were so bizarre as to virtually end further discussion (for that session) of the original problem under consideration. While one cannot always avoid such excursions, and while one should obviously never abruptly change the subject, it is necessary to at least attempt to maintain the focus of the meeting. [p. 115]

This passage suggests active ways to avoid higher-level learning. Through the governing variable of unilateral control, the KE defeats any of the expert's attempts to surface interpersonal problems that may be symptomatic of deeper organizational problems. The KE who follows this advice misses a key opportunity to help the organization learn.

At the conclusion of each session, the knowledge engineers will typically try to restate the responses of the expert in the production rule format. Thus, after a few sessions, it should be possible to develop a simple, prototype expert system. Once a prototype is available, we may use it to extract additional knowledge from the expert. That is, once the expert is introduced to the prototype, a fuller appreciation of the concept and purpose of an expert system is obtained. Further, the prototype provides a tangible basis for evaluation. In particular, the expert can note inconsistencies, limitations, and deficiencies of the prototype, which in turn may be used to refine and enhance the rule base.

A word of caution with regard to the prototype and the domain expert is in order. The domain expert should be warned that any conclusions reached by a prototype, in its earliest forms, are likely to be both naive in nature and limited in scope. We usually explain that the evolution of a prototype expert system is somewhat akin to, and can be just as dramatic as, the evolution of a human—from infancy to adulthood. Initially, the prototype is the proverbial empty slate. It knows nothing, including the fact that it does not even know what it does not know. As more and more heuristic rules are entered into the knowledge base, and as more demonstration examples are encountered, the intelligence of the prototype will grow—first slowly, then faster and faster until a reliable decision analysis assistant has evolved. The domain expert must be assured that, with patience and training, the prototype expert system will prove its worth. [p. 116]

Thus, the author provides more advice to control and dominate the interaction. Further opportunities for knowledge extraction are identified and the author closes with more advice on how to save face in light of the limited functionality provided by the expert system prototype.
