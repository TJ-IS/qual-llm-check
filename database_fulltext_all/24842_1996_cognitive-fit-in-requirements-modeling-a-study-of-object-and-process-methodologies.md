# Cognitive Fit in Requirements Modeling: A Study of Object and Process Methodologies

RITU AGARWAL, ATISH P. SINHA, AND MOHAN TANNIRU

Professor Agarwal's articles have appeared in Journal of Management Information Systems, Information and Management, OMEGA, Decision Support Systems, Knowledge-Based Systems, International Journal of Man–Machine Studies, Behavior and Information Technology, Knowledge Acquisition, among other publications, and she has presented papers at several national and international meetings. His current research interests are case-based reasoning, expert systems, object-oriented modeling, and human factors in software engineering.

His current research interests are systems analysis and design, decision support systems, and expert systems.

ABSTRACT: Requirements modeling constitutes one of the most important phases of the systems development life cycle. Despite the proliferation of methodologies and models for requirements analysis, empirical work examining their relative efficacy is limited. This paper presents an empirical examination of object-oriented and process-oriented methodologies as applied to object-oriented and process-oriented tasks. The conceptual basis of the research model is derived from the theory of cognitive fit, which posits that superior problem-solving performance will result when the problem-solving task and the problem-solving tool emphasize the same type of information. Two groups of subjects participated in an experiment that required them to construct solutions to two requirements-modeling tasks, one process-oriented and the other object-oriented. One group employed the object-oriented tool while the other used the process-oriented tool. As predicted by the theory of cognitive fit, superior performance was observed when the process-oriented tool was applied to the process-oriented task. For the object-oriented task, however, the performance effects of cognitive fit require further investigation since there was no difference in subject performance across the two tools.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: cognitive fit, human factors, object-oriented analysis, process-oriented analysis, requirements modeling.

THE SUCCESS OF ANY INFORMATION SYSTEMS DEVELOPMENT EFFORT is critically contingent on the quality of the front-end phases of the systems development life cycle $[5, 13]$ . Systems development is an inherently complex activity involving multiple stages and multiple actors. Several methodologies have been proposed to manage this complexity; some of the more prominent among them are structured techniques $[19]$ , data-oriented methods (e.g., $[21]$ ), and the more recent object-oriented development methods $[6, 7, 9]$ . These methodologies all prescribe formal representational notations as well as specific steps to be followed during the systems development activity.

Successful system implementation requires a high-quality design, which, in turn, depends on how well the requirements have been modeled. As noted by DeMarco [13] and by Vessey and Conger [39, 40], requirements modeling constitutes the most consequential phase of the system's life cycle. It is critical here that the analyst develop a correct and complete set of requirement specifications to form the basis for system design. Despite the proliferation of methodologies and models for requirements analysis, empirical work examining the relative efficacy of alternative techniques is limited.

Process-based methodologies, such as structured techniques, have gained considerable favor in the past three decades and have emerged as the means of choice for most systems development efforts $[17]$ . More recently, object-oriented techniques have become popular. The object-oriented methodology has been proposed for systems analysis $[7]$ , as well as for systems design $[9]$ . Widely claimed benefits of the object-oriented approach include its expressive power, the data abstraction and encapsulation it provides, the reusability of the resultant software, and the inherently modular nature of the modeling technique, which facilitates extensibility and maintainability. Desiring these benefits, several organizations are actively exploring ways to incorporate object-oriented tools and techniques into their systems development efforts.

In general, all methodologies provide for the modeling of an invariant set of constructs—such as those for specifying objects (data stores), attributes (data elements), processes, sequencing of processes, and relationships—relevant to the task of systems development $[13, 30]$ ; however, the notation and formal constructs are usually idiosyncratic to a given methodology. Also, differences could arise depending on the method of decomposition used. For example, in structured approaches, the primary unit of decomposition is the process while, in the object-based approaches, it is the object $[17]$ . It has been suggested that the different emphases of the methodologies focus the analyst's attention on different aspects of the target application [29]; in fact, proponents of the OO approach contend that OO is more natural for the analyst.

Prior work comparing performance with alternative requirements analysis methodologies includes studies by Yadav, Bravoco, Chatfield, and Rajkumar $[45]$ and by Vessey and Conger $[39, 40]$ . In an experimental study comparing data flow diagrams (DFDs) and the Integrated Definition Method (IDEF0), Yadav et al. $[45]$ found that, in general, DFDs were easier to learn and use although no definitive statements could be made about the quality of the model produced by both techniques. Vessey and Conger $[40]$ compared process, data, and object-oriented methodologies for requirements specification using process-tracing methods with a small sample. Their results suggest that novice systems analysts find the process-oriented methodology easiest to apply and the object-oriented methodology the most difficult. In a related study examining the nature of the interrelationships between prior knowledge about an application domain and knowledge about the requirements modeling methodology, Vessey and Conger $[39]$ found that both categories of knowledge are important for the task of requirements specification.

Research examining the effectiveness of systems development methodologies has not investigated whether the nature of the application itself has any effects on performance in systems analysis tasks, though such effects have been studied for other phases of the development life cycle, such as design and coding $[31, 42]$ . Applications can range across a broad spectrum. DeMarco $[14]$ suggests that applications could be function strong—that is, they contain significant amounts of processing—or that applications could be data strong—that is, they have a significant proportion of data requirements. There is substantial evidence outside the systems development area suggesting that problem solvers employ different processing strategies for tasks with different characteristics $[15, 32, 37]$ . Thus, a methodology that facilitates the solution of one type of task may inhibit solution of another type. The efficacy of a systems development methodology, therefore, should be judged in the context of the task to which it is applied. As Vessey and Conger $[39, p. 199]$ rightly point out, “although a range of methodologies exist, little attention has been paid to characterizing applications such that methodology can be matched to application.” Their study, however, characterized applications as “familiar” or “unfamiliar” and did not distinguish between the nature of the applications.

In this paper, we report the results of an experiment examining the nature of the interaction between the type of methodology used and the intrinsic characteristics of the task to which it is applied. Forty-three subjects applied both object- and process-oriented methodologies to two types of tasks: one inherently object-oriented, the other inherently process-oriented. The theoretical framework underlying the study is that of cognitive fit [38], which posits that synergistic problem solving will take place when there is a match between the different problem-solving elements, such as the task and the tool used.

Our research accomplishes the following objectives: First, from a theoretical perspective, our study extends the paradigm of cognitive fit to a new domain, that of systems analysis and design. Second, it examines alternative systems analysis methodologies. From a practical standpoint, this is important because it can guide requirements analysts in selecting an efficacious methodology for a given systems analysis task. Finally, the study provides insights into the relative abilities of novice systems analysts in applying two different systems development methodologies.

Theoretical Background and the Research Model

## Theoretical Background

REQUIREMENTS MODELING IS A HUMAN PROBLEM-SOLVING ACTIVITY in an unstructured domain (see, e.g., [12, 45]). Human problem solving and its complex underlying cognitive processes have been extensively examined by researchers in cognitive psychology. In their seminal work, Newell and Simon [24] proposed a theory in which humans were considered information-processing systems. The theory posits a set of cognitive processes or mechanisms that produce the behavior of a human problem solver. Problem solving takes place within a problem space, which is the internal representation of the task environment used by the human subject. According to the theory, the task instructions and previous experience in solving similar tasks contribute significantly to the determination of the problem space. The task instructions not only define the task but also provide a specific representation that helps define an initial problem space.

Building upon Newell and Simon's work, human factors research explored the influence of the nature of the task and the way it is represented on problem-solving performance. An important notion generated from this stream of work is that of "cognitive fit" [38]. The basic model of cognitive fit views problem solving as the outcome of the relationship between the problem (or external) representation and the problem-solving task, which are both characterized by the type of information they emphasize. Processes act on the information in the problem representation and the problem-solving task to produce a mental representation, which is the internal representation of the problem in human working memory [20]. Processes then act on the mental representation to produce the solution to the problem. According to the cognitive fit theory, when the types of information emphasized in the problem-solving elements (problem representation and task) match, the problem solver can employ processes (and formulate a mental representation) that also emphasize the same type of information. Cognitive fit exists because the cognitive processes used to act on the problem representation and the cognitive processes used to complete the task both match; this synergy results in superior problem-solving performance. Conversely, when a mismatch occurs between problem representation and task, cognitive fit will not result and problem-solving performance will deteriorate. The idea of cognitive fit has gained empirical support in diverse domains, which suggests that it does indeed provide a general and powerful framework for investigating problem-solving performance.

## Determinants of Problem-Solving Performance

Related research in human problem solving has examined factors other than task and representation that could affect problem-solving performance. Sinha and Vessey [31] tested an extended model of cognitive fit that included “problem-solving tool” as an additional determinant of problem-solving performance. They argued that matching the type of information provided by the tool to that in the task and the problem representation would lead to effective and efficient problem-solving performance (see also [42]). Recursive programming tasks were represented both recursively and iteratively, and subjects were required to use either recursive or iterative constructs to solve the tasks. Two programming languages, LISP and PASCAL, were utilized with two groups of subjects. The study found that the effects of a match between task and problem-solving tool (programming language) outweighed the match between problem representation and task. Significant effects were also observed when subjects used their preferred programming constructs, although those effects were weaker than those due to cognitive fit.

In another study, Vessey and Galletta [41] examined the effects of the match between three elements—problem-solving skill, problem representation, and problem-solving task—on problem-solving performance. The problem-solving tasks they used were spatial and symbolic tasks, while the problem representation dimension included graphs and tables. Both spatial and symbolic subject skills were measured. From their results, the authors concluded that the effectiveness of a problem representation varied with the type of task to be solved. They also found that performance improved when subject skills matched either the task or both the problem representation and the task. No performance improvements were noted when skills matched the problem representation alone.

In the domain of programming, researchers have explored other types of cognitive fit. Soloway and his colleagues $[1, 33, 34, 35, 36]$ focused on the cognitive fit between the most naturally chosen strategies and the looping constructs provided by the programming language in use. They found that subjects, while planning looping solutions independent of any programming language, overwhelmingly preferred a READ/PROCESS strategy to a PROCESS/READ strategy. They also observed that program accuracy improved when it was written in a language that facilitates the “cognitively preferred” looping strategy for the problem. Wu and Anderson $[44]$ corroborated these results. They found that most PASCAL programmers were quite sensitive to the nature of the problems being solved. In an effort to identify which among two strategies—while-do (W) and repeat-until (R)—is cognitively preferred in planning looping constructs independent of any programming language, they found that, while the W-strategy was dominant for W-problems and the R-strategy was dominant for R-problems, neither strategy was dominant for neutral problems. Also, when subjects were forced to use a nonpreferred strategy, their performance deteriorated.

In summary, on the basis of the research literature, we find that different problem-solving elements—such as problem-solving task, problem representation, and problem-solving tool—can emphasize different types of information. As posited by the theory of cognitive fit, a match between the information emphasized in the problem-solving elements will result in improved problem-solving performance.

## The Research Model

In this study, we build upon prior work by empirically examining the notion of cognitive fit in the domain of systems analysis and design—more specifically, requirements modeling. Our focus is on the performance of inexperienced modelers on two types of systems analysis and design tasks—process-oriented (PO) and object-oriented (OO)—using two types of modeling tools—process-oriented and object-oriented. The research model, shown in figure 1, includes problem-solving task and problem-solving tool as determinants of problem-solving performance. Cognitive processes involved in problem solving are represented by the directed flows linking pairs of elements in the model. Problem solvers construct a mental representation using internal cognitive processes that were activated by the information emphasized by the tool and the task. The mental representation is then manipulated by cognitive processes to produce the solution to the problem.

## The Tool Dimension: Object- and Process-Oriented Methodologies

The design of a business information-processing application typically requires the specification of two components: the data underlying the application and the processes used to manipulate the data. As an example, consider the case of a payroll system: A complete design for a payroll system would include a description of the data needed to support the system (such as employee attributes, time cards, etc.), a specification of the processes underlying the payroll system (such as the creation of paychecks, the creation of income reporting forms, etc.), and an order representing the sequence of those processes. A complete description of both data and processing constitutes a model of the target application.

Systems analysis and design methodologies typically prescribe the use of a set of procedures and documentation tools to understand and represent a complex target system $[13, 19]$ and utilize the principle of decomposition as the primary method of reducing complexity. Although several methodologies now exist, structured systems techniques have been the predominant choice for over two decades $[18]$ . The fundamental unit of decomposition in the structured techniques is a process; the system is represented as a series of processes that transform data. Processes are linked via data flows, which help establish the sequencing of those processes. A complete structured requirements specification consists of a data flow diagram representing data flows and processes, a data dictionary, and a set of detailed process specifications $[13]$ . As Vessey and Conger $[40]$ note, the emphasis of the structured techniques is on processes; such techniques constitute a “process-oriented methodology.”

The object-oriented methodology, on the other hand, uses the concept of an object as the primary unit of decomposition [6, 26, 27]. An object is a collection of data 忠诚度 (attributes) and processes (services) that manipulate the data. An object implements a key concept in systems development, that of encapsulation and information hiding [6, 17, 18, 30]. Services are invoked via messages between objects. Though represented differently, services are similar to processes used in the structured techniques. In addition to object and service specifications, an object-oriented model includes structural relationships that exist among objects, such as a classification hierarchy, which allows lower-level objects to inherit the attributes and services of higher-level objects.

When comparing the process-oriented methodology with the object-oriented methodology, beyond the surface differences between the constructs and terms used by the techniques are other fundamental differences. The distinction that we focus on is process- versus data-orientation. As noted by many researchers (see, e.g., [17, 18, 40]), the process-oriented methodology places greater emphasis on processes, whereas the object-oriented methodology tends to focus more on the data and structural relationships. In their comparison of process-, data-, and object-oriented methodologies, Fichman and Kemerer [17, p. 23] underscore this point by observing that “Process-oriented methodologies focus attention away from the inherent properties of objects during the modeling process and lead to a model of the problem domain that is orthogonal to the three essential principles of object orientation: encapsulation, classification of objects, and inheritance.”

Constantine [10] succinctly summarizes the essence of the difference between process- and object-oriented methodologies:

In the functional paradigm, function and procedure are primary, data are only secondary. Functions and related data are either conceived of as independent, or data are associated with or attached to the functional components.

The antithesis of this viewpoint is the object-oriented paradigm. In this paradigm, data are considered primary and procedures are secondary; functions are associated with related data.

## The Task Dimension

Consistent with the fundamental constructs used to model any information systems application, we distinguish between systems analysis and design tasks on the basis of their inherent traits. They are classified as “process-oriented” if their emphasis is on processes and sequencing of those processes, rather than on data and structural relationships. Alternatively, tasks are classified as “object-oriented” if their description highlights data and structural relationships (such as classification) among the data. DeMarco [14] alludes to this distinction by recognizing that a system could be function-strong—when it can be conceptualized almost entirely in terms of the operations it performs—or data-strong—when it can be specified predominantly in terms of the data upon which it acts. Note that this distinction could possibly result from how the problem is represented; that is, the description of the problem could mask details associated with either the data that need to be manipulated or the operations that need to be performed.

At a high level of analysis, tasks may be characterized as process- or object-oriented depending on their dominant features. However, this characterization only focuses on the dominant features. A systems analysis and design task is never fully process-oriented or object-oriented, since both processes and data must be modeled. We alluded to this characterization earlier in describing a hypothetical payroll system. Solving a task entails solving the structure subtask (the representation and organization of data) and the behavior subtask (specification and control of processes) [7, 9]. Thus, in our taxonomy of tasks, a task is considered inherently process-oriented (object-oriented) if its behavioral (structural) features dominate its structural (behavioral) features. Classification of tasks is done through a feature analysis, as explained subsequently. Each task, however, necessarily contains subtasks of both structure and behavior, albeit to differing degrees.

## Cognitive Fit in Requirements Analysis

As discussed above, a systems development methodology or tool, and the task to which it is applied, could emphasize process or data aspects, thereby focusing the attention of the analyst on those aspects of the problem. Cognitive fit will occur when the task and the tool match—that is, they emphasize the same type of information. Two matches are possible: (1) both task and tool are process-oriented, and (2) both are object-oriented. The two possible mismatches occur when (1) the task is process-oriented but the tool is object-oriented, and (2) the task is object-oriented but the tool is process-oriented. We examine the effects of the interrelationship between task and tool at two levels of task granularity: (1) the task level (OO and PO) and (2) the subtask level (structure and behavior). We expect each task-related proposition to have a corresponding subtask-related proposition. More specifically, propositions dealing with the PO (OO) task would have corresponding propositions for the behavior (structure) subtask. The subtask-related propositions are applicable to both the PO and the OO task, because both are composed of structure and behavior subtasks.

As suggested by the theory of cognitive fit, a match between task and tool should yield positive performance effects. For the PO task, analysts are readily able to formulate a mental representation if they use the PO tool, because both the task and the tool emphasize processes and sequencing. On the other hand, if they use the OO tool, they would experience difficulty in representing the task internally because of the mismatch between task and tool. We, therefore, test the following proposition:

## Research Design and Methodology

## Research Design

A 2×2 FACTORIAL DESIGN WITH PROBLEM-SOLVING TASK AND PROBLEM-SOLVING TOOL was used for the experiment. One group of subjects (G1) used the OO tool, while the second group of subjects (G2) used the PO tool. Both groups consisted of business students enrolled in an information systems course at a university. The subjects were selected so that they had limited prior knowledge of both object-oriented and process-oriented modeling (i.e., they had not taken any systems analysis and design courses as part of their formal education) and no experience in on-the-job application of these concepts. There were twenty-four subjects in G1 and nineteen subjects in G2 (see figure 2). Subject demographics indicated that there were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of their level of process-oriented modeling experience and their object-oriented modeling experience. Although all subjects indicated that they had some amount of prior computer programming knowledge, the overall level of self-rated programming knowledge was fairly low (2.6 on a five-point scale where 1 indicates no knowledge at all, 3 indicates some knowledge, and 5 suggests a lot of knowledge); and there were no significant differences between the two groups. The use of novice subjects was appropriate because it ensured that the research results were not confounded by modeling experience and related biases [40].

The two groups, G1 and G2, were trained (a total of six hours, split into two three-hour sessions) on OO and PO methodologies for requirements modeling, respectively. The purpose of these sessions was to teach the subjects the invariant modeling constructs and how they are represented in each approach, and to give them some practical experience in applying those concepts. Training for the object-oriented and the process-oriented groups was conducted separately. The same instructor was utilized for both groups to eliminate bias and the training materials were constructed so as to present identical concepts in the two modeling approaches. At the end of the training session, both groups were asked to solve three example problems; those problems helped reinforce the concepts learned earlier and provided the students with an opportunity for hands-on experience. The methodology used for OO training was adapted from the one suggested by Coad and Yourdon [7, 9], while that used for PO training was adapted from DeMarco [13].

## Experimental Tasks and Feature Analysis

Two experimental tasks—narrative descriptions of business information-processing problems—were utilized. G1 subjects were required to develop object-oriented models for both tasks, while G2 subjects were required to develop process-oriented models for the tasks. Task T1 was an application where the problem description emphasized the operations in the target domain, that is, the task was function-strong or process-oriented in nature. Task T2 was one where the problem description emphasized data and structural relationships, that is, the task was data-strong or object-oriented. Tasks were selected in the following way. Eight business information-processing applications of roughly equal complexity were scored independently by two evaluators on the basis of the features highlighted in their descriptions. The applications came from two sources: (1) a collection of cases developed from actual systems analysis and design projects conducted by students with industry, and (2) applications created by the researchers.

The features of a task were evaluated along two dimensions: structure and behavior. The features considered for the structure dimension were data intensity (the relative number of objects and attributes identified in the problem description and the extent to which they dominated the narrative), structural relationships (such as classification), and polymorphism/inheritance. Thus, in addition to being data-oriented, we characterized an object-oriented task as one that emphasizes structural relationships, polymorphism, and inheritance, all recognized as essential principles of object-orientation [17, 22, 30]. The features considered for the behavior dimension were process intensity (the relative number of processes in the problem description and the extent to which they dominated the narrative), and sequencing/control of the processes. These features constituted a list of characteristics to look for in a given problem description. Each problem was scored by two independent evaluators on each feature on a scale of 1 to 5. A score of 5 for a particular feature indicated the feature's strong presence in the problem description, while a score of 1 indicated that the feature was present to a very small degree. A task was classified as inherently process-oriented (object-oriented) if the score on its behavior (structure) dimension greatly exceeded the score on its structure (behavior) dimension. The features used for task classification are consistent with the primitive components of an application as identified by DeMarco [14].

The average scores on the structure and behavior dimensions were determined by the evaluators. The problem selected as the inherently PO task had a significantly higher behavior score (4.75) than the structure score (2.00). Similarly, the problem selected as the inherently OO task had a significantly higher structure score (4.34) than the behavior score (2.50). The reported scores are averages of scores assigned by the two raters; the Pearson correlation coefficient for the evaluator scores on the eight cases was 0.83, significant at p < 0.01.

The selected PO task was an “Accounts Payable System” (T1) at a fictitious corporation; the OO task was an “Employee Benefits System” (T2) at a fictitious university (see appendix A). Each problem description occupied approximately one page; T1 was described in 328 words, T2 in 404 words. T1 contained a significant use of the word “then” (suggesting sequencing of activities), while T2 was characterized by a natural generalization/specialization hierarchy among employees of the university. The order of task presentation was counterbalanced for the experiment so as to eliminate any confounding learning effects. Subjects were provided with an incentive to complete the task as quickly as possible; a maximum time limit of one and a half hours was allotted for each problem.

## Training Material and Expected Outcomes from Subject Solutions

The main issue that has to be resolved prior to the design of the training is a precise definition of what constitutes a requirements modeling methodology and what techniques are fair to compare in an experimental situation. Different modeling methodologies provide specifications at varying levels of detail, and care needs to be exercised to ensure that they have the capabilities to model the same set of real-world elements. For example, process-modeling techniques require that a separate data dictionary be constructed to provide explicitly for the specification of data elements $[13]$ . Object-oriented techniques, on the other hand, incorporate data within the graphical representation and provide explicit notations for depicting data elements; also, they can display additional semantics such as inheritance and overriding. However, the object-oriented techniques we adopted $[7, 9]$ do not graphically depict the control or sequencing of processes through a notation such as a sequence diagram, or do not implicitly enforce a sequence as in the process model.

For the group using the OO tool, subjects had to identify the objects and their attributes, identify the structures (classification and assembly), define the services of each object (i.e., the activities an object performs), and specify the messages that need to be passed between objects. Subjects were asked to conform to the format of the examples provided in the training materials. Subjects in the group using the PO tool were required to construct a level-one data flow diagram, to provide detailed process specifications using structured English, and to construct a data dictionary identifying the data stores and elements. Classification hierarchies were to be captured through the specification of the data elements in the data stores. The explicit delineation of what was required of a subject solution helped circumvent the problem of comparing techniques that do not identically model a set of real-world phenomena.

## Operationalization of Research Variables

The primary outcome of interest was the performance of subjects for each task and subtask. Performance was operationalized through three different dependent variables and evaluated on the basis of a scoring scheme, which specified rules for determining the scores on each facet (such as processes, data stores, objects, attributes, and services). The facet scores were then combined into scores on two dimensions: structure and behavior (see details later). The researchers developed a model “correct” solution for each task, providing a benchmark for evaluating the subjects’ solutions. Each subject’s response was evaluated by two independent evaluators and rated using the scoring scheme. Multiple correct solutions were allowed as long as the relevant facets were recognized and included in the solution. For example, the problem statement for T2 described a faculty member of a university as an assistant, associate, or full professor. Two alternative and correct representations using the object-oriented methodology are possible. One is to model FACULTY as an object class with a rank attribute; the second option is to model ASST\_PROF, ASSOC\_PROF, and FULL\_PROF as subclasses of the FACULTY class. Similarly, for T1, subjects may choose to combine the processes SIGN CHECK and MAIL CHECK into one higher-level process PROCESS CHECK. Both representations were treated as correct, as long as the two subprocesses were included in the minispecification for the higher-level process. This type of variation in subject solution was allowed and explicitly recognized in the scoring scheme. The overall quality of a solution was assessed using two metrics: a subjective score between 0 and 10 assigned by the evaluators, and Jaccard's similarity coefficient [2, 16], which provided a more objective assessment.

The problem of achieving scoring consensus and objectivity has persisted in the research literature for studies of this nature (e.g., [3]). Nevertheless, every attempt was made in the development of the scoring scheme to ensure that no systematic bias was introduced by the evaluators. In the OO model, a score on the structure subtask is comprised of scores on three features: objects, structural relationships, and the degree to which attributes are identified and associated with appropriate objects (attribute presence and match). In the PO model, these features correspond to data stores, the relationships among data elements in the stores, and the appropriate association of data elements with data stores. The features, therefore, can be treated as invariant modeling constructs. Those features were used for assigning a score on the structure subtask (see appendix B). The method of computing the score is identical for both models. Objects (data stores) and structural relationships were classified as major and minor, depending on their relative importance in characterizing the problem. Scores for objects (data stores) and relationships were assigned on the basis of whether they were present or absent in the solution, while “attribute (data element) presence and match” was assigned a discrete score between 0 and 10. Table 1 shows how each feature was scored and how the individual feature scores were weighted and combined to generate the final structure score.

For the object-oriented methodology, a score on the behavior subtask is comprised of scores on the following features: services, processing, quality of sequencing, and service-object match. For the process-oriented methodology, the corresponding features are: processes, detailed process specification, quality of sequencing, and process-data store match. Services (processes) were classified as major or minor and scored according to whether they were present or absent in the solution. Appendix B also lists the features used to assign a score on the behavior subtask. Processing and “quality of sequencing” scores were assigned on the basis of the presence of the required computations and of the degree of sequencing in the solution. The “service-object (process-data store) match” score depended on the association (match) of services with appropriate objects. For processing, quality of sequencing, and service-object match, discrete scores between 0 and 10 were assigned. Table 1 provides details of how the different features were scored and how they were combined to generate the final behavior score.

Interrater agreement of the evaluators' identifications of objects and services, as measured by Cohen's kappa [4], was 0.73 and 0.79 for T1 using the PO and the OO methodology, respectively, and 0.90 and 0.94 for T2, using the PO and the OO methodology, respectively. All the kappa scores were within the 99 percent confidence interval. Disagreements were resolved through discussion; in most instances, disagreements arose from an oversight on the part of an evaluator.

The Jaccard's similarity coefficient was computed by dividing the difference between the total number of objects, services, and structural relationships in a "correct" solution and the number correctly identified in a subject solution, by the total number of objects, services, and structural relationships. For the other measure, namely, “overall quality,” a score between 0 and 10 was assigned by each evaluator. This score was considered important because it evaluates the notion of correctness on a holistic basis—what Yadav et al. [45] call the semantic correctness of a model. While the similarity measure provides insights into the elemental components of the model (i.e., invariant modeling constructs) that subjects were able to identify correctly, this score attempts to establish how well subjects were able to combine the structure and behavior components to develop an overall model of the target application. Interrater reliability as measured through the Pearson correlation coefficient [23] was 0.87 for the PO task and 0.84 for the OO task, both significant at p<0.01; reliability coefficients (Cronbach's alpha [11]) were 0.92 for the PO task and 0.90 for the OO task. The significantly high levels of reliability for both the classification and the continuous scores indicate the validity of the scoring scheme.

## Results

To explore subtask-level propositions, two separate two-way ANOVA procedures with subtask and tool as the two treatments were run independently for the two tasks. In order to gain more detailed insights into subject performance, we tried to assess the differences between the two groups qualitatively. We found that, for both tasks, subjects failed to identify services correctly more with the OO tool than with the PO tool. Using the OO tool for the OO task (T2), many subjects missed all three calculate services (calc\_pen, calc\_life, and calc\_health) in their solutions. On the other hand, using the PO tool, very few subjects missed all those services. A possible explanation is that the OO tool did not facilitate the specification of those primitive services the same way as the PO tool did. For the PO task (T1), very few subjects missed any of the services using the PO tool, though quite a few subjects failed to identify some of the services using the OO tool. It was apparent from the subjects' solutions that the PO tool was much more effective than the OO tool for specifying services. We also found a major difference in the subjects' ability to specify structural relationships (classification and assembly) using the two tools. Using the PO tool, all subjects missed all three minor relationships for the PO task. Using the OO tool, on the other hand, many of the subjects identified those minor relationships; in fact, nineteen of the twenty-four subjects identified the XYZ—MAIN+BRANCH assembly relationship correctly. Also, for the OO task, subjects did much better in specifying the major classification relationships with the OO tool than with the PO tool. The difference in performance may be attributed to the fact that the OO tool supports a graphical representation of relationships, as opposed to the nongraphical data definition allowed in the PO tool.

In summary, those propositions related to the process-oriented task and the behavior subtask were supported, while those related to the object-oriented task and the corresponding structure subtask were not. The lack of support for the latter two propositions is surprising, given that the propositions are strongly grounded in theory that has been empirically tested and confirmed in a variety of other domains.

A possible explanation, consistent with prior empirical examinations of cognitive fit (e.g., [31]), is that improvements in performance due to a match between certain problem-solving factors (in this case, task and tool) may be dominated by a regression in performance caused by a mismatch between other factors. There is evidence to suggest that human problem solvers have an innate tendency to apply procedural or process-oriented approaches to problem solving (e.g., [25, 28, 43]). Vessey and Conger [40] found subject performance with the process-oriented methodology superior to performance with the object-oriented methodology. Fichman and Kemerer [17] characterize object-oriented approaches as a radical departure from procedural approaches. The nonexistence of cognitive fit effects for the OO approach may be attributed to this: The radical difference between the OO approach and the way individuals naturally solve problems could have offset cognitive fit effects.

A second, related explanation for the lack of significant results for object-oriented tasks and structure subtasks is that structure is inherently easier to model. If that is the case, the cognitive burden involved in solving structure-oriented tasks would be small; hence, the effects of a match between the tool and the task would not be discernible as improvement in performance. In other words, because solving structure problems is relatively easy, the information emphasized by the tool does not make a big difference. This explanation, however, needs further empirical confirmation, perhaps in another experiment with varying levels of task complexity.

The study described here has some limitations that need to be acknowledged and considered in the interpretation of the results. We selected our experimental tasks on the basis of features indicative of process-orientation or object-orientation. Insofar as this was a subjective assessment, there is a chance of misclassification. On the other hand, the use of multiple raters and an objective classification scheme for each feature helped address this limitation. We examined subject performance on only two tasks. The generalizability of our results across a wider range of applications would require an examination of performance across a larger set of tasks. Nevertheless, the study does provide insights and a foundation that could be further expanded through future research. The measurement procedures required an assessment of subject performance on various tasks. Again, the potential for error was minimized through the use of a comprehensive scoring scheme, the use of multiple evaluators, and high levels of interrater reliability.

## Discussion and Implications

THIS STUDY SOUGHT TO ACHIEVE THREE OBJECTIVES: First, to extend the external validity of the theory of cognitive fit by testing it in a different domain and with a different set of subjects; second, to gain insights into the relative efficacy of object and process methodologies for modeling the requirements of different types of tasks; and, finally, to ascertain which methodology is easier for novice subjects to apply.

Our results are suggestive of the general predictive ability of the theory of cognitive fit. As hypothesized, the theory was instrumental in explaining subject behavior with two different tools for tasks that are primarily process- or behavior-oriented. A match between the information emphasized by the tool and by the task resulted in superior problem solving. Although this was not true for tasks that are primarily object- or structure-oriented, that may not be a limitation of the theory per se but, rather, an outcome of the problem-solving elements manipulated and measured in the experiment. As noted before, the proclivity of human problem solvers to reason procedurally may have rendered the cognitive fit effects indiscernible.

The theoretical implication of this result is that cognitive fit does provide a powerful paradigm for examining problem solving. However, the results also suggest that, from the perspective of theory advancement, it is important to develop a comprehensive taxonomy of elements relevant to problem solving so that the true effects of a match between pairs of problem-solving elements can be distinguished. Prior research in cognitive fit has included additional elements such as skill and problem representation. Additional work is required to identify what other factors might be relevant in problem solving so that experimental research can control for the factors that are not of interest or else directly measure them.

The current trend in industry to shift to OO approaches for all phases of systems development might require reevaluation. Perhaps object-orientation is a superior design method because of the encapsulation and information hiding that it provides. But is it also a superior analysis method? Our results leave this question open. Subjects using the OO methodology performed no better than those using the PO methodology for one type of task and performed worse for the other. Therefore, before jumping onto the object-oriented bandwagon, serious consideration should be given to the ability of the OO approach to provide a powerful modeling formalism. Recall that the emphasis of object-orientation is on systems design and programming and that the paradigm has evolved in a bottom-up fashion [22]. To present a viable alternative to process-oriented methods, object-oriented methods need to be refined and extended to provide some of the analysis capabilities offered by process-oriented methods. In fact, extensions such as service charts [8] are a step in that direction.

Insofar as superior performance may be used as a surrogate for ease of learning and ease of use, our results suggest that novice subjects find process-oriented methodologies easier to apply than object-oriented approaches. Although consistent with prior work $[40]$ , this result is nevertheless disturbing. To the extent that the behavior of novice analysts can be extrapolated to practicing analysts, the result does not bode well for the current crisis confronting the software-development community: that of training analysts in object-oriented development methods. Clearly, it will take significant investment in training to help analysts make the transition from process- to object-oriented approaches. In fact, one might speculate that experienced analysts will find the change more difficult to handle than novices because of the prior biases for a particular worldview of modeling they have acquired through experience.

Our results also have implications for instruction in systems development methodologies in general. The results clearly indicate that, for process-oriented tasks, inexperienced modelers perform best when the task characteristics emphasize the same type of information as the problem-solving tool. Since the learning curve associated with any new methodology is fairly steep, care should be taken in selecting the right set of tasks for training purposes, since inadvertent selection of training tasks could inhibit effective learning and create negative attitudes. On the other hand, selection of training tasks that match the tool would help reinforce the salient concepts of the methodology and would lead to synergistic problem solving.

## APPENDIX A: Experimental Tasks

## T1: Process-Oriented Task

XYZ Company, Accounts Payable System

XYZ company is a retail jewelry organization consisting of a main store and three branch stores. The company sells products such as watches, gold chains, diamond rings, and other precious stones. The company deals with over 120 vendors to supply its four store outlets. The financial record keeping and check-payment activities of this company are the responsibility of two individuals who reside in the main office.

Merchandise from vendors is received at the branch stores, and all associated vendor invoices are forwarded to the main store. The bookkeeper checks all invoices for the salesperson's signature to verify that the shipment was received. The invoice information is then entered simultaneously (using carbon paper) onto the purchase journal and vendor ledger card. The purchase journal is organized by branch store, the ledger cards by vendor. The invoices are then filed into an outstanding invoice file.

Approximately every ten days, the bookkeeper searches the outstanding invoice file and removes any invoices with pending due dates within the next ten days. These invoices are placed in the pending payment file. Checks are prepared and recorded in the checkbook register for those that need payment. The matching paid invoice numbers are written on the checks and the check numbers are written onto the vendor ledger. The bookkeeper submits the prepared checks to Mr. Wilson to be signed. Once the checks are signed, the payment amount is recorded in the vendor ledger and the checks are mailed. The corresponding paid invoices are attached to the carbon copy of the check and filed in the paid invoice file.

When monthly statements are received from a vendor, the bookkeeper compares them with the outstanding invoice file and paid invoice file. Completely reconciled statements are discarded. Statements that are not completely reconciled by this comparison are placed in the “troublesome statement” file. These statements are then reconciled by contacting the branch stores to locate missing invoices or verify receipt of merchandise.

## T2: Object-Oriented Task

## Riverside University, Benefits Systems

Several persons are associated with Riverside University, which offers undergraduate and graduate programs in different areas. The faculty and staff members constitute the employees of the University. The faculty body is comprised of Assistant Professors, Associate Professors, and Professors. Secretaries, clerks, and officers constitute the staff. The student body is comprised of undergraduate and graduate students. Some graduate students are employed as Graduate Assistants; they get a monthly stipend and a full tuition waiver in return for their duties.

The Human Resources and Benefits (HRB) department maintains pertinent information for all employees of the school. Periodically, it issues different reports to employees, keeping them informed on pension, health insurance, and life insurance benefits. All employees are eligible for health insurance benefits. Graduate assistants, however, are not eligible for pension and life insurance benefits.

Reports on retirement pension funds are issued to employees quarterly; these reports contain information about the employee's contribution, the school's contribution, and the total accumulation of funds in the employee account. Employees are required to contribute a fixed percentage of their salaries; the school contributes an equal amount. While the twelve-month salary is used as the basis for computing pension contributions for a staff member, the nine-month salary is used as the basis for faculty members. Any extra money earned by a faculty during the three-month summer term is not considered in computing pension contributions. Since employee contributions toward pensions are tax-deferred, the HRB department issues a report to the payroll office annually, listing the names and contributions of all employees.

The HRB department issues a life insurance report annually to each employee; this report contains information on the employee's life insurance amount and insurance premium. The life insurance amount is determined from the employee's salary; premiums, which depend on this amount and the employee's age, are paid in full by the school.

During the Open Enrollment period, held once a year, employees have the option of making changes to their current health insurance benefits by filling out a form at the HRB office. If an employee does not submit this form, his or her benefits continue to be the same as the current benefits. Health insurance premiums are shared by the employee and the school. The HRB department issues a report to the payroll department listing the names of all employees signed up for health insurance, along with the amounts to be withheld from their salaries for premium payments.
