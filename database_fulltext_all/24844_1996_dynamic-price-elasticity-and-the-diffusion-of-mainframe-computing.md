# Dynamic Price Elasticity and the Diffusion of Mainframe Computing

KAR YAN TAM

KAR YAN TAM is Associate Professor and a Weilun Fellow at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (HKUST). Before joining HKUST, he was a faculty member at the University of Texas in Austin. He has also held a Research Scientist position at EDS, where he worked in software engineering methodologies and tools. His research interests include information technology diffusion, electronic commerce, and applied artificial intelligence. He has published extensively on these topics in major management science and information system journals, including Management Science, Information Systems Research, Journal of Management Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, and European Journal of Operational Research. Dr. Tam has organized many international conferences, including the Third International Conference on Decision Support Systems in 1995 and the First Asia Pacific DSI Conference in 1996. He is currently on the Editorial Board of Decision Support Systems and International Journal of Computational Intelligence and Organizations. Dr. Tam has extensive consulting experience with major companies, including IBM China/Hong Kong, Merrill Lynch (Asia Pacific), Hongkong Telecom, Tele Danmark, and Hong Kong Industrial Technology Centre Corporation.

ABSTRACT: The organizational adoption of information technology (IT) innovations has been an area of increasing research activity in the last two decades. While much attention has focused on the relationship between attributes of the innovation and the adoption decision, little has been done to study the adopters' reactions to changes in these attributes over time. An important innovation attribute is price, which is commonly used as a proxy for adoption cost. In this paper, we investigate how organizations in the United States react to mainframe price changes over a period of thirty years, from 1955 to 1984. A parsimonious diffusion model that integrates both diffusion and pricing effects is developed. The model is empirically tested using annual adoption and pricing data of mainframes. Quality-adjusted price index is used to account for any changes in quality improvement over time. Our findings indicate that (1) price is an important factor in the diffusion process, (2) organizations' reactions to price changes (i.e., price elasticity) are not constant, and (3) elasticity dynamics can serve as an innovation attribute that provides a continuous characterization of adoption behavior over the life cycle of an innovation. Implications to IS research and opportunities for future work are also discussed.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: innovation diffusion, information technology adoption, price elasticity.

THE ORGANIZATIONAL ADOPTION OF INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY (IT) innovations has been an area of increasing research activity in the last two decades. A number of IT innovation studies have been conducted, and a variety of attributes are reported to have an impact on the adoption decision $[9, 14, 35, 40, 57, 58, 59, 60]$ . However, there is little consensus regarding the importance of these attributes. Despite broad interest in the subject, our understanding of the innovative behavior of organizations remains relatively undeveloped $[51, 56]$ . Information on how innovation attributes affect the diffusion process over time will help us better understand the dynamics of organizational adoption of IT. Unfortunately, changes in innovation attributes and the corresponding adopters' reaction have received little attention in the information system (IS) literature so far. This study attempts to fill this gap by studying the impact of price changes on the adoption decision in the context of mainframe computer diffusion.

The inconclusiveness of many IT innovation studies can be attributed to the ambiguity and inconsistency concerning the various stages in the diffusion process in which they are conducted [56]. Very few studies present a clear reference to the time frame of the innovation's life cycle, which is essential in interpreting the findings. Because many are cross-sectional studies [51], they are limited in revealing the time-dependent behavior of adopters. The limitations of cross-sectional studies are magnified by changes in innovation attributes over time. It is only legitimate to compare cross-sectional findings with different time frames if adopters are homogeneous and time-invariant in their perceptions. However, these assumptions are not realistic and often lead to conclusions contrary to the observed diffusion pattern. Indeed, the social theory of innovation diffusion suggests that adopters are not homogeneous [48, 53]. The lack of a theory that clearly describes changes in adopters' preferences and behavior is the root of the problem. Thus, research in characterizing the changes of adopter behavior over time is both warranted and worthwhile.

To investigate whether perceptions of an attribute change and, if so, how they change over time requires a longitudinal approach. Mainframe computers are used in the current study for the following reasons: First, mainframes have already gone through a significant portion of their life cycle; this enables us to obtain reliable estimates of model parameters in our empirical analysis. Second, a considerable number of organizations still rely on mainframes as the core processing platform, even though their role may have changed over the last two decades. Third, reliable secondary data regarding adoption and price trends are available for mainframes over a long period of time. Fourth, the impact of mainframes, measured by both depth and breath, has been universal and substantial.

Price is selected as the attribute of interest because there is strong evidence that price is the driving force behind the rapid growth of computer spending in the last few decades $[24]$ . It is also a good proxy for cost, which Downs and Mohr $[17]$ suggested as a primary innovation attribute. Primary attributes such as cost and size can be relatively easy to measure without referring to the specific circumstances of the adopting organizations.

In economic terms, adopters' sensitivity to price changes is referred to as price elasticity. The current work represents a pioneering effort to study the impact of price elasticity on the adoption of mainframe computers in the United States. In terms of modeling methodology, it improves on previous work in three major directions. First, it overcomes the shortcomings of previous work by taking into account the rapid technological improvement of mainframes using a quality-adjusted price index. Second, the adoption pattern of mainframes is based on a well-established parsimonious diffusion model adjusted for pricing effects. Third, the hypothesis of constant price elasticity is tested against a number of alternative forms of elasticity using a nested model approach. This work also supplements an earlier study by Chow [13] on the demand for computers. Surprisingly little has been done in this area since Chow's work. The findings of the current study also provide insights for managers devising marketing strategy for IT products.

## Model Formulation

## Price Elasticity

THERE IS EVIDENCE THAT ADOPTERS AT DIFFERENT STAGES of the diffusion process exhibit different degrees of sensitivity to price changes. In comparing a number of consumer durables, Parker [42] concludes that the dynamic behavior of price elasticity is not identical for all products. In a meta-analysis of econometric studies on price elasticity, Tellis [52] finds elasticity changes over the life cycle of a product. A dynamic price elasticity indicates that the demand curve will shift over time. Figure 1 illustrates the case of increasing price elasticity. An incremental change in price $\Delta P$ will cause a change in demand by $\Delta Q_{1}$ at time $t_1$ . At a later time $t_2$ , the same $\Delta P$ will trigger a larger response in demand $\Delta Q_{2}$ such that $\Delta Q_{2} > \Delta Q_{1}$ . On the other hand, if price elasticity is decreasing, then for the same $\Delta P$ , $\Delta Q_{2} < \Delta Q_{1}$ .

The relationship between demand and price is generally given by the following demand function:

$$
Q = P ^ {\alpha},\tag{1}
$$

where $\alpha$ is the price elasticity coefficient. To model dynamic elasticity, $\alpha$ can be replaced by $\alpha(t)$ , which is a function of time. Tellis's finding of dynamic elasticity concurs with social studies of innovation diffusion [48] that posit that early adopters are more innovative and less cost-conscious than adopters in later stages. A plausible explanation is that the reservation price of early adopters is higher than the market price. These adopters are typically large organizations with extra resources to experiment with new ideas.

Information on the existence and the magnitude of elasticity dynamics provides a number of insights into the adoption pattern of an IT innovation. First, one can infer the perceived necessity of an innovation from estimates of its price elasticity. A low elasticity indicates a strong perceived necessity, and vice versa. Second, the trend of elasticity sheds light on the behavior of adopters in different stages of the diffusion process. This has important implications for setting pricing strategy for IT products.

## Modeling the Diffusion Pattern

The impact of price elasticity on the adoption pattern of mainframes can best be empirically assessed in the context of a diffusion model. Because of their parsimonious nature, diffusion models have started to become a popular tool among IS researchers for addressing diffusion- and adoption-related hypotheses $[36]$ . For example, Brancheau and Wetherbe $[8]$ found evidence that diffusion of IT follows an S-curve pattern. Gurbaxani and Mendelson $[24]$ developed an integrated growth model of IS spending. They showed that spending follows an S-curve initially and then grows exponentially. The adoption of BITNET over time is also studied in the context of a diffusion framework $[23]$ .

Many diffusion models have been developed to investigate the aggregate adoption pattern of an innovation over its life cycle. The Bass model $[5]$ is selected as the underlying diffusion model of mainframes in the current study because of its parsimony and wide acceptance by diffusion researchers. The model is robust for analyzing aggregated adoption data at the industry level. Its applications have recently started to appear in the IS literature. For example, Loh and Venkatraman $[36]$ adopted the Bass model to study the growth of outsourcing projects in the United States. A similar approach is adopted by Dos Santos and Peffers $[18]$ to study the aggregate adoption of ATMs. While the original Bass model is limited, many extensions and variants have been introduced in the last two decades. For example, it has been extended to include behavioral implications such as cannibalization and leapfrogging behavior of adopters $[36]$ . While applications of the Bass model and its variants lie mainly in sales forecasting, new product development, and marketing strategy formulation, use of diffusion models for descriptive purposes is increasing $[37]$ . Descriptive uses of diffusion models provide an explanatory mode to test diffusion-related hypotheses. In the current study, we apply the Bass model to study the price elasticity dynamics of adopters.

The original Bass model is given by:

$$
\dot {X} (t) = \left[ a + b \frac {X (t)}{M} \right] [ M - X (t) ],\tag{2}
$$

where $X(t)$ is the cumulative adoptions up to but not including t, $X(t)$ is the adoptions in time t, M is the market potential, and a and b are parameters capturing external and internal effects on adoption. Equation (1) describes the diffusion pattern of an innovation over its life cycle. A plot of $X(t)$ depicts a symmetrical S curve.

An alternative formulation of the Bass model is given by:

$$
f (t) = [ a + b F (t) ] [ 1 - F (t) ],\tag{3}
$$

where $f(t)$ and $F(t)$ denote the probability and distribution functions of adoption at time t, respectively. By solving the differential equation (3), $F(t)$ can be expressed as a function of t as follows:

$$
F (t) = \frac {1 - e ^ {- (a + b) t}}{1 + \frac {b}{a} e ^ {- (a + b) t}}.\tag{4}
$$

The probability that an organization will adopt in $[t_{i-1}, t_{i}]$ given that it has not adopted by time $t_{i-1}$ is $[F(t_{i}) - F(t_{i-1})]/[1 - F(t_{i-1})]$ . Thus, the demand for mainframes in the ith period, $X(t)$ , can be given by

$$
\dot {X} (t) = \frac {[ M - X (t _ {i - 1}) ] \cdot [ F (t _ {i}) - F (t _ {i - 1}) ]}{1 - F (t _ {i - 1})},\tag{5}
$$

where $X(t_{i-1})$ is the cumulative number of adoptions by $t_{i-1}$ .

## An Integrated Model of Dynamic Price Elasticity and Diffusion

The rapid decline of computing price has been recognized as a major factor driving the continuous growth of computing spending in the last few decades. The pricing factor can affect the diffusion pattern in two ways. It can alter the diffusion rate by increasing or decreasing the number of adopters in each period while the market potential remains unchanged [6, 47]. Or it can affect the pool of potential adopters directly [16, 30]. For innovations such as mainframes that have a high knowledge barrier and steep learning curve [3], price mainly influences the number of potential adopters. Complementary products such as application software require a long time to develop. A drop in price in the short run will not increase the number of adopters drastically even when the drop is substantial. In other words, price has a long-term effect on demand. This is consistent with Chow's work [13] with regard to the demand for computers. He suggested that a lower price will place the computers within the budget constraints of a larger number of potential adopters.

To model the dynamic price elasticity of mainframes, we follow the spirit of Parker [42] by assuming a relation between demand and price $P(t)$ at time t as

$$
Q (t) = \dot {X} (t) \cdot P (t) ^ {\alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} t + \alpha_ {2} t ^ {2}},\tag{6}
$$

where $Q(t)$ is the demand for mainframes; $P(t)$ is the quality-adjusted price level at time $t$ , and $\alpha_0, \alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_2$ are dynamic elasticity parameters. By substituting (5) into (6), we get

$$
Q (t _ {i}) = [ M - X (t _ {i - 1}) ] \cdot \frac {[ F (t _ {i}) - F (t _ {i - 1}) ]}{1 - F (t _ {i - 1})} \cdot P (t _ {i}) ^ {\alpha_ {0} + \alpha_ {1} t + \alpha_ {2} t ^ {2}}\tag{7}
$$

One can interpret equation (7) as a function of two multiplicative terms: (1) changing market potential $[M - X(t_{i-1})] \cdot P(t)^{\alpha_{0} + \alpha_{1}t + \alpha_{2}t^{2}}$ , and (2) the conditional probability of adoption $[F(t_{i}) - F(t_{i-1})]/[1 - F(t_{i-1})]$ driven by the diffusion effect. The model assumes that the demand curve will shift over time. It is closely related to previous formulations by Robinson and Lakhani [47] and Bass [6]. Although the time path of elasticity dynamics can take on any form, a quadratic form is used because it consists of most of the patterns suggested in previous work [38, 43, 49]. Depending on the magnitude and the sign of $\alpha_{0}$ , $\alpha_{1}$ , and $\alpha_{2}$ , different patterns (i.e., constant, linear, and quadratic) can be represented. Indeed, all combinations can be represented by seven nested specifications and the full model of equation (7) as shown in Table 1.

The research question of interest here is framed by the following hypothesis:

## H1: Price elasticity of mainframe demand is not constant over the life cycle.

In terms of modeling approach, the proposed integrated model is an improvement on the original Bass model. It represents a general diffusion model adjusted for dynamic price elasticity effect. Its use need not be restricted to the study of mainframes but can be applied to other IT innovations. There are two reasons for applying it to mainframes: First, mainframe computers have been in existence for decades, providing a long-term view of the diffusion process. Kamakura and Balasubramanian $[31]$ argue that a long-term view of the diffusion process provides more insights into the effect of variables perceived to affect the diffusion process. Second, a long series reduces the instability of parameter estimations normally found when only a few observations are available [5]. Heeler and Hustad [27] and Srinivasan and Mason [50] report that a minimum of ten years of data is needed to provide reasonable estimates for equation (1).

## Data and Empirical Analysis

THE DATA SET USED HERE IS BASED ON THE STUDIES BY PHISTER [44] and Einstein and Franklin [19]. It covers annual domestic sales of mainframes from 1955 to 1984. The two sources of data have been used in a number of empirical studies, especially in the construction of price indices for computers [7, 12, 20, 39]. The work of Gurbaxani and Mendelson [24] is also based on data provided in Phister [44]. The two data sources are well researched and reliable.

A plot of the number of mainframes purchased in each year from 1955 to 1984 is shown in figure 2. It is worth noting that the mainframes purchased per year are not identical; they represent a class of computers with similar functions, prices, and distribution channels. As the figure shows, the first sales peak occurred in 1967, thirteen years after the launch of mainframes. After that, the number of adoptions flattened and fluctuated between 6,000 and 10,000 for the next fifteen years. Previous studies indicate that more stable estimates can be obtained if the series contains the first adoption peak. A second peak occurred in 1973. As shown, this series does contain the first peak and thus should provide good estimates.

## Quality-Adjusted Price Index for Computers

While many researchers recognize the importance of economic variables in the diffusion process, few studies have explicitly incorporated the pricing factor. The effect of price is largely ignored by IS researchers in the study of IT adoption. One problem in empirical work in this area is the lack of reliable pricing information. Unlike other durable items, regular statistics on price (e.g., price index) for computers have been unavailable until recently. For more than three decades, computers have been lumped in the general category of OCAM which includes other office machinery as well.

Using macro deflators (e.g., GNP deflator) to adjust computer prices over time may be problematic because these deflators do not take into account quality improvement. Tellis [52] reports that the omission of quality information will have a positive bias on price elasticity. If the price index is not adjusted for quality improvement, estimates will be less elastic. Thus, market analysts may have the impression that the demand for mainframes is less elastic than it actually is. Pricing strategy so derived may overestimate the number of adopters if quality is not taken into account.

In 1985, the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) developed a quality-adjusted index for computers. Although the BEA index has been used in recent IT productivity studies [11, 28, 33, 34], it is a composite index and not useful in our study. The deflator for mainframes used here is derived from two series of indices reported in Gordon [21, table 6.7]. Both series are derived using hedonic regression. The first series is based on the Phister data set [44], which covers mainframe models introduced between 1951 and 1979. The second series is derived from reports in Computerworld, which contain data covering 1977 to 1984. The two series are combined with an overlapping year in 1979. Missing values are estimated by interpolation. The annualized price decline is 20.56 percent from 1954 to 1984, which is in the range of estimates obtained from previous empirical studies [7, 12, 20, 39]. The quality-adjusted price index as shown in figure 3 provides a more accurate picture of the price trend of mainframes. Although the overall trend is decreasing, it is interesting that price did go up in some years (e.g., 1958 and 1967). Unlike previous empirical works on IT diffusion that assume a constant rate of price decline, this study has revealed a more refined pricing trend that more accurately reflects the temporal changes in the market for the specified period. The price index is used to deflate mainframe price in this analysis.

The next step is to identify the most appropriate model among all specifications using the log-likelihood test. The test works as follow: Let model A be a special case of model B by setting some restrictions on the value of B's parameters. The log-likelihood obtained for model $A\left(LL_{A}\right)$ and that for model $B\left(LL_{B}\right)$ is used to calculate the statistic $-2(LL_{A}-LL_{B})$ , which has a chi-square distribution. The degree of freedom of the statistic depends on the number of restrictions on B [22]. The null hypothesis that the nested model is appropriate (i.e., A) is rejected if the statistic is larger than the critical value at a certain level of significance. ## Discussion

THE EMPIRICAL RESULTS OF THIS STUDY INDICATE THAT THE HYPOTHESIS of dynamic price elasticity is supported. As shown in the first row of Table 3, log-likelihood tests conclude that model 1 (i.e., price has no effect) should be rejected. Furthermore, it is clear that the specification with constant price elasticity (model 2) or linearly declining price elasticity (models 3 and 5) are inadequate to model the diffusion of mainframe computers over the specified period. By testing a number of nested specifications, the log-likelihood test indicates that models 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 are rejected in favor of the full model. It turns out that the best models are the two that specify a quadratic function of time. The findings suggest a number of implications about the study of IT innovation and adoption.

## The Time-Dependent Effect of Price on Adoption

Existing IT innovation studies have either ignored the price factor or assume a fixed rate of price decline with a constant elasticity. Even though it may be that elasticity of demand is constant for aggregated spending [24], the two assumptions are challenged at a finer level of analysis (e.g., hardware spending). At a broader level, price elasticity may no longer be constant, especially when factors such as product life cycle, monopolisticity, and substitution/complementary effects brought about by new generations of computers (e.g., minis and PCs) are taken into account. By relaxing the assumption of price elasticity, we can garner more insights.

The time-dependent effect of price elasticity seems to provide a better explanation of the diffusion pattern. As shown in figure 3, the quality-adjusted price index for mainframe computers indicates that, although price declined from 1955 to 1984, there were short periods when price actually went up and periods when price was relatively stable, for example, from 1955 to 1958. Despite the price increase, the number of adopters increased four times during the same period. One explanation is that early adopters are less risk-averse and are also less sensitive to the cost of innovations [48]. This is consistent with estimates of our models.

As figure 3 shows, there are three apparent price drops in 1966–67, 1972–73, and 1981–82. It is interesting that the first two price drops are coincident with the sharp increase in the demand for mainframes in 1967 and 1973. On the other hand, the effect of the 1981–82 drop is less apparent. Such a difference could well be explained by combining both pricing and diffusion effects. With 1984 as the base year, 23 percent of the market had adopted mainframes in 1967 and 50 percent in 1973. These are the periods when the growth rate $dX(t)/d(t)$ was positive. Because of the diffusion effect, the number of adopters continued to grow even when price was held constant. At the same time, price elasticity increased steadily. Consequently, a large drop in price accelerated the adoptions, as reflected in a surge of purchases in 1966–67 and 1972–73. On the other hand, almost 90 percent of the organizations had adopted mainframes by 1981. Although our models indicate that price elasticity had increased considerably at this stage, the effect of a price drop had less impact as the growth rate was negative and $M-X(t)$ was declining rapidly. The latter implies that the number of potential adopting organizations that acted in response to the price drop in 1981–82 was much smaller than those in 1966–67 and 1972–73.

## Paradigm Shift In Organizational Computing

Our findings offer a new perspective to assess the importance of mainframe computers in organizational computing by inspecting the trend and magnitude of their price elasticity. Compared with estimates for other durable items $[42, 52]$ , price elasticity for mainframes, though growing rapidly, was quite low after twenty years into the life cycle. For instance, using estimates from model 7, price elasticity was virtually zero in the first decade. The low elasticity and the rapid growth of adoptions indicate that the mainframe was an essential asset in many organizations from 1955 to 1975. In retrospect, there was no real alternative to mainframes in the 1970s, and many organizations had not completed their process of computerization. However, dependence on mainframes declined steadily as elasticity increased after 1975, which coincides with the introduction of alternative platforms for corporate computing. The paradigm shift from centralized processing to departmental computing in the late

1970s is evidenced by a drop in the price elasticity of mainframes. The current study provides a new perspective to investigate the trend of organizational computing by comparing elasticity dynamics of alternative computing platforms. As an analytical framework, the model can be used to study recent development, such as client-server computing, by comparing elasticity of workstations and personal computers. Its should not be restricted to analyzing hardware innovations but can be applied to other IT innovations with price as a major attribute.

## Price Elasticity as an Innovation Attribute to Characterize Adopter Behavior

A large body of literature has accumulated over the years attempting to identify attributes that can be used to differentiate between early and late adopters of IT innovations. Among the many investigated attributes, size (measured in terms of either market capitalization or number of employees) has been correlated with the timing of innovation adoption $[26, 32, 57, 60]$ . For many innovations, early adopters are dominated by big organizations. Many have offered explanations to the size effect. One common view is that large organizations have slack resources that allow them to be less risk-averse and more receptive to new ideas $[15]$ . Another view suggests that large organizations have better channels for receiving new ideas and have a better mechanism to implement innovations, and thus stand a higher chance of success $[55]$ . Size as a predictor of adoption, however, is of limited use in explaining changes in adoption behavior. What is much needed is the identification and characterization of an innovation attribute that can be used to describe adopter behavior over time.

Our findings suggest that early adopters of IT could be distinguished from later adopters on the basis of their price sensitivity. This is a new characterization of adopters in the diffusion process. The elasticity dynamics can be expressed as a function of time that lends itself to empirical analysis. Unlike characteristics such as size, diversity, and slack resources, which are useful only in the early stage of the diffusion, the dynamic behavior of price elasticity provides valuable information over the entire life cycle of an innovation. When combined with Rogers's classification of adopters under the bell-shape diffusion curve $[48]$ , one can obtain a richer adopter profile in each category. This study indicates that the results of cross-sectional IS innovation studies must be interpreted in the context of the specific time frame in which the innovation was adopted because the effects of some innovation attributes (price in this case) on the adoption decision change over time and may lead to conflicting results if not taken into account.

## Price Elasticity and Innovation Suppliers

Whether price elasticity is dynamic or constant will have important implications on the supply side of an innovation. For the suppliers, a constant price elasticity implies that early and late adopters are homogeneous in their reaction to price changes. If other factors are constant, this implies that a pricing strategy will produce the same effect at different stages of the life cycle. On the other hand, dynamic price elasticity suggests that a change in price level will trigger different levels of demand depending on the point when the change takes place. Timing is important and should be included as a parameter in developing the optimal pricing strategy $[49, 52]$ . Depending on the dynamics and other factors such as discount rate and effects of learning on cost, the optimal pricing strategy can be penetration pricing (increasing), skimming (decreasing), or increasing then decreasing prices. Simon $[49]$ suggests that penetration pricing is usually optimal when elasticity decreases in the early stage of the life cycle. In this stage, market competition is low, and suppliers may adopt penetration pricing strategy. Our findings are consistent with Simon's analytical work where he observed a low elasticity in the early years and a surge in demand for mainframes, even though price remained relatively stable during 1955–58. There is evidence that penetration pricing may have been adopted by mainframe suppliers in the early years.

## Price Elasticity and Learning Effects

Learning effects play a significant role in the adoption of IT innovations. For complex technological innovations such as mainframes, it may take several years for an organization to master the technology effectively $[2, 4]$ . The learning effects are a function of the firm's characteristics, such as culture, beliefs, and processes $[29]$ . The learning effects were substantial in the early years for mainframes. For many organizations, the cost of learning and the risk involved may well have exceeded the perceived benefits of adoption. Many adopted a wait-and-see attitude, hoping to learn from others' experiences. In the presence of learning effects, early adopters are less influenced by price change resulting in a low price elasticity. In fact, government units normally act as technology champions and were among the early adopters of mainframes. The experience gained in the government sector had spillover effects in the commercial sector. As the number of mainframe installations increased, the cost of learning became less of a problem to potential adopters. Concern was then directed toward the cost-effectiveness of the technology. Potential adopters became better informed and were able to assess the cost-effectiveness of models from an increasing number of vendors and alternative technologies. The result was an increase in price elasticity that is consistent with our estimates.

## Limitations and Future Research

LIKE ANY STUDY EMPLOYING SECONDARY DATA [25], THE SCOPE of the current study is limited by the availability of data. The period 1955 to 1984 is the only period when both reliable price-adjusted quality index and adoption data are available. It would be interesting to extend the data set to include recent years to study the changing role of mainframes. Unfortunately, the current data set cannot support this kind of analysis.

The work reported here considers adopters' sensitivity to changes in prices rather than in the cost of adoption. It is known that the purchase price represents only a portion of the lifetime cost of adopting an innovation. As one reviewer suggested, it may be that computer suppliers will provide incentives to early adopters by treating price and adoption costs contemporaneously. The use of price, though not a perfect substitute for the cost of adoption, provides a feasible vehicle to study the dynamic behavior of adopters at an aggregated level. As Tornatzky and Klein [54] suggest, adoption cost could be perceptually based and may not be easily measured. One extension of the current work is to study how price elasticity changes correlate with increases in perceive network externalities provided by major mainframe vendors such as IBM.

The Bass model is generally applied to study first purchases of an innovation. Here, we do not differentiate first and repeat purchases because repeat purchases assume that the product purchased again is identical to the one purchased the first time. This is not justified, however, because of the rapid technological advancement of mainframe computing. To be consistent with the Bass model, repeat adoptions are interpreted as initial adoptions of mainframes having a new set of capabilities. To account for replacement purchases will be a subject of future research.

The limitations of the current study suggest need for future research in this area. Two extensions of the current work that examine in more detail the factors affecting the dynamic price elasticity of IT adopters are examined below.

## Replacement Purchases

An extension of the current study would be to differentiate among categories of adopters according to their adoption timing. More and more studies are devoted to the modeling of heterogeneity of adopters in the diffusion process. An important issue addressed by these studies is replacement purchases $[10, 41, 46]$ . Replacement purchases are made by adopters who have previously adopted the innovation, who for performance and wearout reasons decide to acquire it again at a later stage. With replacement purchase, the traditional S-shaped curve may not provide an accurate account of the diffusion process since sales will continue even at the later stage of the cycle. In the case of mainframes, we expect price elasticity of repeat purchases to be less than that of initial purchases over the life cycle.

## Multigeneration Diffusion Model

The current study investigates a single class of innovations and defines successive generations of IT innovations as classes of innovations with distinctive performance, characteristics, and markets. For example, mainframes are mainly used for central corporate data processing, while minicomputers are for departmental or small-business computing. Such a difference is illustrated by previous work in constructing price indices for computers. In developing price indices for computers, Gordon [21] reported that estimation results became unstable if mainframes and minicomputers were pooled in estimating the hedonic regression models. Therefore, it is important to differentiate new generations of innovations from continuous improvement in quality and performance. The decision to adopt a number of minicomputers rather than a large mainframe entails changes in the organizational structure and procedures that are likely to be different from those involved in using a mainframe. Thus, by examining the magnitude of substitution and complementary effects among different generations of computing platforms (i.e., mainframes, minis, and PCs), researchers can obtain valuable insights into the driving forces of recent phenomena such as downsizing, outsourcing, and open systems. For example, if there is significant overlap between the markets for mainframes and minicomputers, then one should expect substitution effects in the period when the two life cycles overlap. We hypothesize that price elasticity of mainframes positively correlates with the adoption rates of minicomputers and PCs.

## Conclusion

THE IMPORTANCE OF PRICE IN INNOVATION DIFFUSION IS RECOGNIZED by many IS researchers, but little has been done to assess its impacts on the adoption decision over time empirically. The current study represents a pioneering effort to address the dynamic behavior of adopters in the context of mainframe adoption. Its findings suggest that the assumption of constant price elasticity is not appropriate in the study of IT adoption. Adopters are less sensitive to price changes in the early stage of the diffusion process, but price elasticity subsequently increases rapidly. The current work contributes to the theory of IS innovation and adoption by presenting a model that integrates macro diffusion pattern and micro adopter behavior. It provides evidence that price elasticity dynamics as an innovation attribute can provide a much needed characterization of adopter behavior over the life cycle of the innovation. Such a characterization is important in understanding the behavioral fundamentals of technology adoption. On the application side, the elasticity dynamics of adopters provide valuable information for devising pricing strategy and for inferring the dependence on a particular technology over time. The paper also contributes to the methodological aspect of IS research by adopting a longitudinal approach in analyzing both adoption and pricing data with price elasticity assumptions relaxed. A nest specification test is introduced to test for alternative elasticity patterns. More work is needed to study adoption behavior and its relation to the diffusion process of an innovation. The proposed modeling framework should not be restricted to mainframe computing but is general enough to be applied in other IT innovations with price as a major attribute. To assess and compare the dynamics of these innovations will be the subject of future research.
