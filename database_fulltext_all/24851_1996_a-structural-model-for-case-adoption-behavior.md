# A Structural Model for CASE Adoption Behavior

ARUN RAI AND RAVI PATNAYAKUNI

ARUN RAI is an Associate Professor of MIS in the Department of Management at Southern Illinois University at Carbondale. His research interests include management of the systems delivery process, diffusion of advanced information technologies, IT-based organizational transformation, management of strategic alliances in the IT industry, and integration of information systems and management science for decision making. Dr. Rai has published articles on these subjects in Communications of the ACM, Decision Sciences, European Journal of Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, Computers and Operations Research, Omega, Long Range Planning, Accounting, Management and Information Technologies, and several other journals. Dr. Rai has received awards for both outstanding research and teaching contributions. He is on the editorial board of the Information Resources Management Journal and is a member of the Association for Information Systems, Decision Sciences Institute, INFORMS, Beta Gamma Sigma, and Phi Kappa Phi.

RAVI PATNAYAKUNI is a Research Fellow in the Department of Information Systems at the University of Melbourne. His research interests include diffusion and implementation of information technology, systems delivery processes in organizations, IT and business value, and the impact of emerging electronic infrastructures such as the Internet. He has published several articles in proceedings of national conferences and recently in Omega and Communications of the ACM. Mr. Patnayakuni is a member of ACM, INFORMS, the Association for Information Systems, and Decision Sciences Institute.

ABSTRACT: The adoption rate of computer-aided software engineering (CASE) technology continues to be low among information systems departments (ISDs). Some ISDs have reported significant hurdles in propagating CASE usage, while documenting the advantages of the technology. We construct and empirically test a theoretical model to explain CASE adoption behavior. Factors considered include need pull (environmental instability of the ISD and performance gap of the ISD), technology push (internal experimentation and learning from external information sources), and the adoption context (top-management support for the IS function, CASE championship, training availability, and job/role rotation). A national survey of 2,700 ISDs resulted in 405 usable responses for the data analysis.

Our analysis suggests a reasonable fit between the model and the data. The results indicate that the need-pull factors do not directly promote CASE adoption behavior. Performance deficit promotes CASE championship behavior while negatively affecting other elements of the adoption context. The instability of ISDs, where the very existence of the ISD may be in question, negatively affects all elements of the adoption

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: CASE technology, information technology diffusion, information systems implementation, software engineering innovations.

COMPUTER-AIDED SOFTWARE ENGINEERING (CASE) TECHNOLOGY has been advocated as a possible means to address the performance problems of the development process. Anecdotal evidence, surveys, and case studies support the view that CASE technology can bring about productivity gains, reduce costs, and cut down lead times for systems development $[12, 30, 34]$ . At the same time, CASE tools are likely to involve learning costs and possible slippage in performance levels during the initial phases of transition to CASE-based development $[26]$ . Furthermore, the acceptance of CASE as a panacea or even as a significant important factor for system development problems is hardly universal. Studies have reported failed implementation efforts and significant sunk costs $[33]$ . Rai and Howard $[39]$ observed that substantial changes often need to be made in the systems development process when CASE technology is adopted, which has in turn inhibited its adoption by many ISDs. Our purpose here is to examine why organizations differ in their CASE adoption behavior, rather than to evaluate the outcome of such adoption.

Some researchers have conceptualized the diffusion of information technologies as a stage process $[17, 32, 38, 50, 52]$ , clearly differentiating between information technology adoption and implementation. Adoption gauges the degree to which a technology has been introduced into the organization, while implementation assesses the degree of usage of the technology that has been achieved. We limit our attention to understanding what leads to differences in CASE adoption level within information system departments (ISDs).

## The Conceptual Framework

THE ASSOCIATION BETWEEN CONTEXTUAL FACTORS AND THE INNOVATION diffusion process has been examined by researchers [39, 48]. Zmud [52] examined the moderating effect of selected contextual factors on the relationship between push and pull factors and the use of modern software practices. However, the impact of push and pull factors on the organizational context itself has not been examined. This is important: The forces of need pull and technology push not only may influence manifest adoption behavior but could also shape elements of the organizational context which, in turn, may affect adoption behavior. By examining the factors and the organizational context for CASE adoption, we approach the study from an organizational imperative. $^{1}$ Figure 1 shows the conceptual model for our study.

## Adoption of CASE Technology

The International Workshop on Computer-Aided Software Engineering defines CASE as “tools and methods to support an engineering approach to software development at all stages of the process” [15]. From interviews with several IS executives, Rai and Howard [40] identified thirteen important system development functions to which CASE technology can be applied. These functions represent front-end systems work, back-end systems work, and project management. We limit our attention here to the technical core of systems work and focus on front-end and back-end system development functions that can be supported by CASE tools (Table 1). Front-end activities are relatively unstructured, and CASE tools essentially support systems personnel in the execution of these tasks. Front-end systems development includes activities such as drawing data-flow and entity-relationship diagrams, creating and maintaining data dictionaries and repositories, and designing screens. Back-end systems work tends to be procedural and is easily supported or performed by CASE tools. Back-end systems development consists of activities such as code generation, analyzing program and database structures, and testing. Integrated CASE tools provide the most comprehensive coverage by supporting both front- and back-end development tasks.

Classical diffusion theory provides an inadequate theoretical basis for measuring the adoption of software engineering technologies [4]. These technologies are composites of several technology fragments. Classical diffusion theory treats technology adoption as binary and fails to recognize adoption of only selected fragments. However, partial adoptions are typical of composite technologies such as CASE. Bayer and Melone [4] emphasize the need to modify diffusion theory in this regard and suggest the use of multi-item measures to capture partial adoptions. We use the term “CASE adoption” for the proportion of system development function for which CASE tools have been introduced into the ISD (without regard to usage level). This definition enables us to distinguish between nonadopters, partial adopters, and complete adopters of CASE technology.

## Need Pull for CASE

We consider two need-pull factors for CASE adoption—namely, ISD environmental instability and ISD performance gap. When present development technologies are unable to meet expected performance levels, it becomes necessary to examine possible alternatives $[40]$ . Substantial performance shortfalls, among other factors, may lead organizations to question the long-term viability of their internal IS departments. Some organizations have, in fact, eliminated internal ISDs and outsourced all IS work to external providers. IS departments operating in unstable environments where their very existence is in question are under intense pressure to improve performance in key processes, often within short time frames. Turning around from high levels of instability often requires substantial changes in core systems-delivery strategies, creating a need pull for technologies like CASE.

## Technology Push for CASE

We include two technology push factors: The degree of learning about CASE from external information sources, and the degree to which ISDs dedicate resources for exploring advances in development technologies. Regarding the former, the literature on diffusion of innovations and IT implementation suggests that external information sources facilitate learning about the potential of new technologies [38, 42, 50]. Regarding the latter, such internal experimentation allows for an in-situ understanding within the organization of a technology's capability and limitations [3]. Together, these two factors represent mechanisms that create awareness about the opportunities and challenges that a technology, if adopted, presents to the organization.

## Adoption Context for CASE

Past research emphasizes that the adoption and implementation of innovations are not driven purely by technical, economic, or rational considerations $[52]$ . The social and organizational context can have a significant influence on the diffusion process $[4, 13, 36]$ . We examine four contextual factors that we believe are likely to influence CASE adoption. First, top-management support for IS is almost consistently recognized as critical for the success of IS initiatives $[45, 48, 52]$ . Second, the information systems and organizational science literatures have examined the importance of championing innovations $[5, 45]$ . Third, switching to CASE technology from traditional methods of systems development can require significant changes in people's skill sets $[31, 48]$ . Providing CASE training is likely to be an important means to instill appropriate skills, reduce knowledge barriers, and alleviate other concerns regarding the new technology. Finally, CASE tools can facilitate coordination and integration of development tasks $[9]$ . The diffusion of such task-integrating technologies is likely to be facilitated if job roles are blended or development personnel are rotated among tasks in the ISD. Job rotation is the fourth element of the adoption context considered in this study.

## Research Hypotheses

## Need Pull for CASE

## Environmental Instability of the Information Systems Department

ISDs undertake a primary role in systems delivery for different user groups within an organization. In a few firms, line managers are taking the lead in initiating systems development and have established a strong partnership with the IS function $[41]$ . In other organizations, the ISD is alienated from mainstream business and some face possible elimination. CASE adoption requires significant political and resource support that is unlikely to be forthcoming in such environments $[40]$ . Furthermore, ISDs under threat of elimination are unlikely to have top-management support or resources for the training required for new technologies.

Organizations tend to become inwardly focused and resistant to change when operating in turbulent and resource-constrained environments $[44]$ . It may also be argued that the instability of the IS function will lead some IS personnel to examine how key process such as systems development can be significantly improved. This may foster the championship of promising technologies such as CASE. However, by the time ISDs find themselves in such situations, it may be too late to be proactive! While it is unclear whether such instability promotes or inhibits CASE championship, we posit a positive relationship between environmental instability and CASE championship.

Job rotation can be used as a means to exchange information and knowledge among personnel $[23]$ . Moving people to new roles implies learning costs and a possible reduction in short-term efficiencies, and IS departments operating in unstable environments are unlikely to be able to afford such losses. They may, in fact, embrace job designs that emphasize narrow specialization designed to improve short-term efficiencies.

On the basis of the foregoing discussion, we hypothesize the following relationships:

H1: The environmental instability faced by an ISD has a direct (negative) effect on top-management support for IS.

H2: The environmental instability faced by an ISD has a direct (negative) effect on job/role rotation of IS personnel.

H3: The environmental instability faced by an ISD has a direct (positive) effect on CASE championship.

H4: The environmental instability faced by an ISD has a direct (negative) effect on CASE training availability.

H5: The environmental instability faced by an ISD has a direct (negative) effect on CASE adoption behavior.

## Performance Gap of the Information Systems Department

A performance gap can result from rising output targets for an organizational unit, declining performance $[18]$ , changes in the technological environment, or increasing pressure from competitors $[49]$ . It has been suggested that the rate of innovation is likely to increase when changes in the environment make existing procedures unsatisfactory $[28]$ . ISDs facing performance problems such as substantial resource consumption for systems maintenance, long lead times for systems delivery, and a dissatisfied user base clearly need to examine alternative approaches for systems development. Some IS personnel may see in CASE technology a possible solution for uch performance gaps, but significant performance shortfalls are likely to diminish top-management support for the IS function. The tight resource conditions created by deficiencies in performance should also negatively influence the availability of resources for CASE training programs. Also, ISDs facing large performance gaps are unlikely to be willing to absorb the learning costs required to establish job rotation as a means to carry out systems development.

H6: The performance gap of ISD has a direct (negative) effect on top-management support for IS.

H7: The performance gap of ISD has a direct (negative) effect on job/role rotation of IS personnel.

H8: The performance gap of ISD has a direct (positive) effect on CASE championship.

H9: The performance gap of ISD has a direct (negative) effect on CASE training availability.

H10: The performance gap of ISD has a direct (positive) effect on CASE adoption behavior.

Technology Push for CASE

## Internal Experimentation by Information Systems Department

Past empirical research supports the notion that the creation of interest groups stimulates the exploration of innovations $[11, 27]$ . Huber $[23]$ suggests that internal experimentation increases the adaptive capability of an organization and, consequently, its ability to adopt innovations. Groups for standards, testing, methodology and metrics evaluation, and technology exploration are examples of specialized units within the ISD. Such groups enable members to focus their attention on improving technologies and procedures applied in systems work. This should, in turn, stimulate the exploration and adoption of technologies such as CASE.

The reservoir of knowledge created during the exploration process should be beneficial to overcome the knowledge barriers that inhibit the diffusion of CASE technology. Internal experimentation can be instrumental in educating IS personnel about the relative advantage of new technologies such as CASE and alternative job designs that could be used given the capabilities of new technologies. Increased awareness of the potential of CASE is likely to promote CASE championship. Furthermore, the very act of exploring new development technologies (which is the technical core of systems work) should suggest to top management that the IS department is committed to improving its development infrastructure. This should, in turn, positively influence top-management support for the IS function.

H11: Resource support for internal experimentation of technologies by the IS department has a direct (positive) effect on top-management support for IS.

H12: Resource support for internal experimentation of technologies by the IS department has a direct (positive) effect on job/role rotation of IS personnel.

H13: Resource support for internal experimentation of technologies by the IS

department has a direct (positive) effect on CASE championship.

H14: Resource support for internal experimentation of technologies by the IS department has a direct (positive) effect on CASE training availability.

H15: Resource support for internal experimentation of technologies by the IS department has a direct (positive) effect on adoption of CASE technology.

## Learning from External Information Sources

Managing the flow of technical and economic information triggers people over their action thresholds to pay attention to new ideas and opportunities $[45]$ . Empirical evidence supports the critical role of external information in the organizational innovation process $[32, 51]$ . Awareness about the strategic potential of CASE should increase support from top management for IS and should promote championship of the technology. Furthermore, an influx of information about CASE is likely to emphasize the importance of effectively integrating various phases of the development process. This should then challenge traditional designs of segregating programming and analysis and foster blended job roles. Increased information flow about CASE should make managers more aware of the criticality of training for CASE implementation and, therefore, should positively influence training availability.

H16: Learning about CASE from external information sources has a direct (positive) effect on top-management support for IS.

H17: Learning about CASE from external information sources has a direct (positive) effect on job/role rotation of IS personnel.

H18: Learning about CASE from external information sources has a direct (positive) effect on CASE championship.

H19: Learning about CASE from external information sources has a direct (positive) effect on CASE training availability.

H20: Learning about CASE from external information sources has a direct (positive) effect on CASE adoption behavior.

## Adoption Context

## Top-Management Support for the IS Function

The importance of top-management support for the acquisition and successful implementation of innovations is well accepted $[19, 36, 52]$ . Implementing CASE invariably requires resources that will be forthcoming only with the active support of top management. Moreover, implementing CASE technology represents a substantial departure from conventional system development approaches and calls for careful change management. Strong leadership helps overcome the behavioral and technical inertia that accompanies significant changes in everyday practices and job roles. Thus, top-management support for the IS function is likely to exert a positive influence on CASE adoption behavior.

H21: Top-management support for the IS function has a direct (positive) effect on CASE adoption behavior.

## Job/Role Rotation of Development Personnel

Some programmers and analysts may look upon CASE as a deskilling technology $[36]$ . The technical programmer often thinks that his or her importance will be diminished in the face of increased emphasis on logical aspects and automated generation of program code. Programmers and analysts might find their present skills obsolete because of a restructuring in tasks, technology, and work flows. Huber $[23]$ suggests that job rotation can be useful to broaden awareness and facilitate knowledge sharing. Rai and Howard $[39]$ observe that the rotation of development personnel among different job functions enhances their understanding of the relationships between steps in the development process. Blending the job roles of analysis and programming and rotating personnel among different job functions should lead to a better understanding and appreciation of task-integrating technologies such as CASE. Hence, job/role rotation of IS personnel is expected to have a positive effect on CASE adoption behavior.

H22: The degree of job/role rotation has a direct (positive) effect on CASE adoption behavior.

## Champions of CASE Technology

Van de Ven [45] expresses a popular view shared by researchers that an innovative idea without a champion will not progress. The champion initially convinces higher-level management that a new product or process is feasible, beneficial, and worthy of significant investment [5, 6]. The resources released to promote usage of a new technology should influence the nature of programs used to achieve desired behaviors. Through informal channels and influence mechanisms, champions can also help overcome possible resistance to new development technologies such as CASE. Thus, enthusiastic championing of CASE should have a positive impact on CASE adoption behavior.

H23: CASE championship has a direct (positive) effect on CASE adoption behavior.

## Training Availability

Past empirical evidence suggests that training imparts necessary technical skills and removes unfounded fears about the technology. Popper [37] has shown that providing appropriate methodology training to programmers and analysts increases their usage of structured methods. In a study of Ada adoption by sixty-six aerospace and defense industry software firms, Melone and Bayer [31] found that groups that had achieved high degrees of implementation had allocated considerable resources to in-house training. It is reasonable to expect that CASE tool and methodology training should impart the necessary skills and allay fears associated with the adoption of the technology. The availability of CASE training should therefore create a supportive context for adopting more sophisticated, and a greater number of, CASE technology features.

H24: CASE training availability has a direct (positive) effect on CASE adoption behavior.

The Empirical Study

## Survey Instrument Development

THE PROCESS OF DESIGNING THE SURVEY IS NOTABLY INFLUENCED BY CHURCHILL'S [8] recommendations for developing reliable and valid measures. Open-ended interviews were used in the initial stages of instrument development. Thirteen senior IS managers from different organizations in the Midwest were interviewed to ensure the study variables' face validity. Discussions with the IS managers on each variable helped in developing operational measures.

Upon completion of the interviews, a draft survey was developed. A pretest was conducted in which twenty-one senior IS managers were asked individually to evaluate the instrument. Of these, thirteen managers had participated in earlier interviews. The executives were asked to comment on the clarity of instructions and understandability of individual items. Fourteen managers responded and, based on the feedback received, minor revisions were made to the instrument. The measurement items used are presented in the appendix.

## Data Collection

A cross-sectional field survey of ISDs in the United States was conducted. A directory of senior IS executives $^{2}$ compiled by a market research firm was used as the sampling frame. This directory consists of organizations that met at least one of the following three criteria: (1) they were members of the Fortune 500 and the six nonindustrial sectors, (2) their MIS budget exceeded \$250,000, and (3) they reported annual sales greater than \$50 million. The list is updated twice a year to ensure its accuracy and currency. In case of multiple individuals per site, only the most senior executive's name was retained. While using a single source from each organization has its limitations, these are overcome to some degree by identifying senior IS managers as executives most “informed” about CASE usage and associated variables within each organization. A similar use of the “key informant” approach has been suggested for survey research and has been adopted by several IS researchers (see [43]). Surveys were sent to senior IS executives in 2,700 randomly selected organizations. Four hundred and five usable responses were received, providing a response rate of 15 percent.

## Measures

## Adoption Level of CASE Technology

Most studies based on classical diffusion theory treat technology adoption as binary: adoption versus nonadoption [4]. However, when an innovation consists of several technology fragments, an organization may not adopt all the fragments. Multi-item measures to capture partial adoption of innovations become necessary in such contexts. Accordingly, a multi-item scale was developed to assess the usage of CASE tools for systems development. Twelve important systems development functions were listed along one dimension. These functions cover the front-end and back-end aspects of systems work discussed earlier. Usage levels for CASE tool(s) were listed along the second dimension. The usage levels were categorized as: 0 = CASE tool not used; 1 = few people/projects experiment with the tool; 2 = a few people/projects use tool regularly; 3 = a lot of people/projects use tool regularly; 4 = most people/projects use tool regularly; and 5 = CASE tool used on a routine basis. The two dimensions together form a response matrix on which respondents indicated CASE tool usage (see the appendix). A similar categorization of usage was employed by Zmud [50, 52] and Nilakanta and Scamell [32] in their respective studies on diffusion of modern software practices and database technology.

The operationalization of CASE adoption is consistent with similar past studies in the IT diffusion literature $[32, 50, 52]$ . Whether or not a CASE tool is being used for a systems development function (regardless of its level of usage) provides the basis for computing the operational measures of CASE adoption. Specifically,

1. An ISD is considered to have adopted CASE for a specific systems development function if the indicated usage level is at or beyond the experimentation level (usage level = 1). The purchase of a CASE tool that is not being experimented with by the ISD does not constitute adoption.

2. The proportion of system development functions for which CASE tools are being used at or beyond the experimentation level is used as an operational measure of CASE adoption. The level of adoption is computed as:

$$
A d o p t i o n = \frac {\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {1 2} f _ {i}}{1 2}
$$

where $f_{i}=0$ if CASE tool is not initiated for system development function I else $f_{i}=1$ . The adoption measure thus has a range of 0 to 1.

3. An ISD can represent no adoption, partial adoption, or full adoption of CASE technology. No adoption implies that CASE tools are not used at or beyond the experimentation level for any of the twelve system development functions. Of the 405 respondents, 113 ISDs had not adopted CASE technology. Five ISDs were using CASE tools for all twelve system development functions and had fully adopted CASE technology. The other 287 ISDs represented partial adoption of CASE technology as CASE tools were adopted for some, but not all, system development functions.

## Size

ISD size was used as a control variable in the analysis. One objective item was used to assess the number of full-time employees as a measure of ISD size. Respondents were asked to indicate the size of their ISDs by selecting from one of ten possible indicated ranges (from 1 to 10 to >350 employees). The natural logarithm of the midpoint of the specified range was used as the measure for ISD size. Similar measures have been used by Zmud [50], Nilakanta and Scamell [32], Fuller and Swanson [16], and Rai and Howard [39].

## Need Pull for CASE Technology

Six items were used to assess the need pull for CASE technology. A principal components analysis followed by a varimax rotation resulted in an expected two-factor solution The uncertainty of the ISD's future in the corporation/organization and the threat of being disbanded loaded on the first factor, and the two items together represent environmental instability of the ISD ( $\alpha = 0.76$ ). The second factor represents a subjective assessment of the overall performance gap of the ISD ( $\alpha = 0.70$ ). The items that loaded heavily on this factor were development backlogs, user satisfaction with the ISD, user satisfaction with the applications portfolio, and pressure on the ISD to improve its performance.

## Technology Push for CASE

Technology push for CASE was assessed using nine items. A factor analysis of these items resulted in the expected two-factor structure. Specific groups involved in R&D, experimentation, and technology exploration and the degree of resource commitment for technology exploration loaded on the first factor and together represent organizational support for internal experimentation ( $\alpha = 0.60$ ). The second factor represents learning about CASE from external information sources ( $\alpha = 0.84$ ). Information sources considered were seminars and product shows, consultants, trade publications, vendor representatives, video/audio tapes, text and reference books, and contacts with programmer/analysts in other organizations. These information sources were adapted from the list of external communication sources used by Nilakanta and Scamell [32] in their study on diffusion of database technology. The organizational innovation literature also recognizes them as key sources of technical and economic information about innovations [45].

## Adoption Context for CASE

Fourteen items were used to assess the adoption context for CASE. Factor analysis of these items resulted in the expected four-factor structure. The first factor consists of items that assess championship of IT innovations by top management, top-management involvement in leading the IS function, top-management participation in the activities of the ISD and information systems, and participation and involvement of top management in establishing clear goals for the IS function. These together represent top-management support for the IS function ( $\alpha = 0.80$ ). Rotation of development personnel among job roles and the degree to which job responsibilities are changed among programmer/analysts loaded heavily on the second factor, representing job/role rotation of programmer/analysts in the ISD ( $\alpha = 0.72$ ). A third factor represented CASE championship, and the four items that loaded on this factor together gauge the advocacy and enthusiasm for CASE by members in the organization ( $\alpha = 0.91$ ). Availability of training in CASE tools, methodologies, and structured development approaches loaded on the last factor. These four items together represent CASE training availability in the ISD ( $\alpha = 0.84$ ).

## Summary of Psychometric Properties

Venkatraman and Grant [46] recommend that survey instruments used for research should use scales (1) with multiple higher-level items rather than single, nominal items, that (2) are internally consistent, and that (3) are valid. Interviews with practicing senior managers and the subsequent pre-tests ensured the appropriateness of measurement items employed for each of the constructs. Each of the constructs was measured using multiple items. ## Results

Researchers suggest that multiple measures should be used to assess the fit of a model to the observed pattern of correlations in the data $[14, 22, 25]$ . The commonly used measures of model fit, based on results from analysis of the structural model, are summarized in Table 5. The $\chi^{2}$ statistic ( $\chi^{2} = 47$ ) indicates that the model does not show a good fit with the observed data. However, the $\chi^{2}$ statistic is sensitive to large sample sizes, and $\chi^{2}$ divided by the degrees of freedom is used as a measure of acceptable fit. The computed value of 4.7 for this measure is below the cutoff value of 5 suggested in $[47]$ . The goodness of fit and the adjusted goodness of fit indices are 0.977 and 0.872. These indices are well above generally accepted values and indicate a good fit of the model. A value of 0.047 for the root mean square residual is at an acceptable level, providing further evidence of good fit. The standardized residual, which is the fitted residual divided by its asymptotic standard error, is considered “large” if it exceeds 2.58. Of the forty-five correlations estimated, only six exceed this value.

Each path in a model represents an explicit relationship under study. LISREL estimates the path coefficients for each of these paths. A path is considered significant if its T statistic (path coefficient divided by the standard error estimate) is significant [22]. Thus, a significant path indicates the presence of a significant relationship. Twenty-one of the proposed twenty-five paths were significant. The number of significant paths compared with the expected theoretical relationships indicates the overall validity of the research model.

## Need Pull for CASE

Performance gap of the ISD did not have a direct effect on CASE adoption behavior but did affect the contextual variables and, therefore, had an indirect effect on CASE adoption behavior. Top-management support, training availability, and job rotation were negatively influenced by performance gaps. CASE championship, on the other hand, was spurred by deficits in performance. The erosion of top-management support and the lack of training availability are understandable. Deficits in performance are likely to lead to a deterioration in top-management confidence in the IS department, and such ISDs are likely to be under a resource crunch to set up training programs for new technologies such as CASE. Blended job roles and job rotation represent changes to traditional segmented job designs of programming and analysis. Under environments characterized by significant performance pitfalls, it may be difficult to move toward new job designs that represent significantly new skills, investments, and learning times for development personnel.

Environmental instability of the ISD negatively affected CASE adoption behavior. Thus, ISDs whose very existence in the organization was in question were less likely to adopt CASE. Furthermore, such ISDs did not have a supportive context, and therefore the level of CASE adoption were negatively affected in such environments. Our results show that top-management support, CASE championship, and training availability were negatively affected by environmental instability, while job rotation was not influenced by environmental instability.

In our hypothesis development, it was difficult to say definitively whether CASE championship would be promoted or inhibited in highly unstable environments. We argued that championship would be spurred since these ISDs were in dire need of radical change, and that CASE championship that advocated this technology as a turnaround strategy might emerge. Our findings did not support the hypothesis. Championship was, in fact, inhibited in such environments. Performance gaps triggered championship for CASE, but such championship was likely to be eroded if the ISD's very existence was in question.

## Technology Push for CASE

Learning from external information sources about CASE has a direct positive effect on CASE adoption behavior. It also directly affects all four adoption context variables. Our findings are consistent with Attewell [3], who points out that it is critical to alleviate knowledge barriers for the diffusion of complex information technologies. This has implications for both ISDs and the vendor community. ISDs should attempt to integrate more closely with external information sources on CASE technology. The vendor community should also attempt to inform their customer base about the associated challenges and benefits of CASE technology.

Internal ISD experimentation with new technologies does not have a direct effect on CASE adoption behavior. It does, however, influence all elements of the adoption context. It is important to emphasize here that this factor does not assess experimentation with CASE specifically but assesses the allocation of IS personnel and budgets for technology exploration. There has been some discussion in the recent literature on the “learning organization” [23]. Together, both technology-push factors represent learning-oriented behavior, and our results suggest that engaging in such learning promotes a favorable context for CASE adoption. Our results suggest that greater support for the IS function from top management should be forthcoming if the ISD undertakes to improve its core processes on an ongoing basis. Increased awareness about the CASE capabilities should lead to increased championship of the technology. Learning about CASE technology, either internally or from external sources, should highlight the importance of evolving the skill sets of development personnel in tandem with the acquisition of the technology. Such learning should also underscore the importance of integrating job designs and developing an overall understanding of the development process through approaches such as job/role rotation.

## The Adoption Context for CASE

## Top-Management Support

Not surprisingly, the environmental instability and performance gap of the ISD negatively affect top-management support for the IS function. This offers an interesting challenge to IS managers. While the need-pull factors suggest the inadequacies of core IS technologies and processes, they also erode top-management support. The adoption of new technologies such as CASE is likely to take place in a difficult organizational context—without top-management support for the unit. The situation, naturally, will be unfavorable for units facing the greatest need pull.

How then do these units regain top-management support? And is such support needed for CASE diffusion? Our study provides some interesting insights that should help managers confront this problem and regain top-management support. Both technology-push factors have a positive effect on top-management support for IS. Internal experimentation with promising technologies signals a commitment to continuous improvement. IS departments operate in the face of dramatic technological change. Integration with external information sources is clearly critical to the alleviation of knowledge barriers and remaining current. Both factors collectively suggest a commitment to learning and improvement—something that should win support from top management. This is consistent with Adler and Shenbar [1], who observed that maintaining standards to meet current real business needs is inadequate. Rather, units should strive to upgrade their technology infrastructures so that they can deliver products and services that meet the challenges of tomorrow.

Our results showed that top-management support for IS does not have a direct effect on CASE adoption. However, it is likely that top-management support is more critical during the post-adoption stages of the diffusion process. Rai and Howard [40] also found that top-management support was positively related to aggregate CASE usage in ISDs. Past research suggested that top-management support was particularly important for implementation since significant resistance to new technologies often surfaces only during the later stages of the diffusion process.

## CASE Championship

As expected, CASE championship had a positive effect on adoption behavior. This is consistent with the literature recognizing the importance of champions in the diffusion process $[5, 6, 45]$ . Both technology-push factors contributed to CASE championship. This seems reasonable, since increased awareness about the potential of CASE technology should stimulate individuals to promote the technology with their colleagues and management. ISD performance gaps tended to promote CASE championship, suggesting that CASE champions were more likely to be found in environments where existing technologies were considered inadequate. However, environmental instability negatively affected CASE championship. An ISD whose very existence is in question is unlikely to provide the climate for individuals to suggest radical changes, which entail steep learning curves and require significant resources.

## Training Availability

CASE training availability positively affected the level of CASE adoption. Our findings were consistent with the IT literature on this issue $[7, 31, 37]$ . The cumulative evidence provided by past research shows that training imparts requisite skills to utilize the new technology, creates positive attitudes, and allays unfounded fears. Training should alleviate knowledge barriers among IS personnel and reduce the perils of adoption failure. If resources for training are available, organizations are more likely to move forward in their adoption of sophisticated CASE products that impose high knowledge barriers.

The need-pull factors, environmental instability and ISD performance gap, negatively affected CASE training availability. The very need for change imposes restrictions on training availability, possibly because of resource issues. However, both technology-push factors positively affected CASE training availability. The technology-push factors informed ISDs not only about the potential of CASE technology, but also about the importance of training. As ISDs become more and more knowledgeable about the capabilities and challenges of CASE technology, it should become clear that venturing into CASE without a training plan is not advisable. Our findings support Huff [24], who underscored the importance of training to ISDs planning CASE adoption.

## Job Rotation

Our study showed that an ISD performance gap negatively affected job rotation. A move away from segmented job designs of analysis and programming to blended job roles can call for significant organizational change. Large performance gaps may constrict the ISD's ability to carry out such structural changes in existing core processes such as development. The technology-push factors promote increased job rotation and blended job designs of programming and analysis. Such job designs are consistent with the “process thinking” suggested in the business process reengineering literature. Conceptual papers on business process reengineering have suggested a move away from narrow specialization and a myopic view of selected tasks to an emphasis on overall awareness of the process and task interrelationships $[10, 20]$ . CASE technology integrates various steps in the development process. The ability to utilize CASE capabilities is likely to be greater if development personnel have a good understanding of the overall development process. Based on their learning either from external information sources or internal experimentation, ISDs may rotate personnel into different roles and design blended job roles to share knowledge and create this better overall understanding. The positive direct effect between job rotation and CASE adoption behavior is evidence that rotating and blending jobs are likely to support the adoption of increased CASE capabilities.

## Conclusions

OUR STUDY SHOWS THAT THERE IS A CLEAR TENSION BETWEEN THE EFFECTS of need-pull and technology-push factors on the CASE adoption context. Of the need-pull factors, environmental instability negatively affects top-management support for IS, CASE championship, and CASE training availability. Unstable ISDs, threatened with elimination and facing a very unsupportive context, are going to find it extremely difficult to adopt CASE technology as a turnaround strategy. Interestingly, no direct effect is observed between performance gap and CASE adoption behavior. Performance gap negatively affects top-management support, job rotation, and CASE training availability. However, it does trigger CASE championship behavior. Where resources or radical change are required, performance gap acts as an inhibitor.

IS managers then face the challenge of creating a favorable adoption context. This is difficult because the very need for the change also creates an unfavorable adoption context. Our study suggests that technology-push factors help in cultivating a supportive context. Investments in learning about CASE from external information sources and internal experimentation should pay off. The learning should not only address CASE's technological capabilities but should also transcend related issues of job design and training requirements. The benefits of information technology are likely to emerge with a reconfiguration of existing processes and skill upgrading of personnel. Realization of the organizational value that CASE can bring about should promote CASE championship behavior.

The IS literature has advocated the importance of top-management support for the IS unit. It is important to ask how top-management support can be cultivated. Top-management support is unlikely to be forthcoming when there are significant gaps in ISD performance. It cannot, and perhaps should not, be demanded. It has to be nurtured and earned by demonstrating a capability to understand and to learn new technologies and a commitment to improving service.

Learning about developments in core development technologies such as CASE, either from external sources or internal experimentation, should affect the nature of the CASE adoption context. A supportive adoption context is critical for the absorption of powerful CASE capabilities. Training, championship, and job redesign are important elements that positively affect the absorptive capacity for complex information technologies such as CASE.

The results of this study should be generalized with caution. CASE products are evolving, and, as vendors enhance or modify their offerings, the issues involved in successfully translating CASE products to software development process innovations could conceivably be different. The present study was also limited to the use of CASE in a technical process. The constituencies (project managers, team leaders, IS managers, etc.) that use administrative tools and techniques for the management of systems projects are different from those that use CASE for actual systems work. Future research should attempt to contrast the issues involved in implementing CASE for administrative and technical processes.

We have limited our attention here to understanding CASE adoption behavior, focusing on a subset of variables that should influence this behavior. We recognize that other variables—for example, the existing adoption base of CASE technology, the costs of CASE tools, and the stability of CASE technology—are likely to influence CASE adoption behavior. Other elements that determine the absorptive capacity of the ISD should be determined as they are likely to influence CASE adoption behavior. The relationship between development team and individual characteristics and the diffusion of CASE is another interesting area of exploration.

A broad definition of CASE was provided early in the paper. Competing development technologies are available, and we need to improve our understanding of their relative strengths and weaknesses. We also need to address issues pertaining to evaluation and selection of a development technology. When should organizations discard a development approach that is being used or has been used for several years, and try to embrace something different? When is it appropriate to embark on radical change, as opposed to an incremental adaptation to the present development approach? Research in these directions should move us toward a framework that will enable us to evaluate changes to development technology in a given organizational context.

At the beginning of this paper, we drew a distinction between adoption and implementation. Adoption gauges the level to which CASE technology has been introduced into the ISD, and implementation assesses the level of usage achieved. While we have limited our attention to understanding CASE adoption behavior, it is important that future research provide us with a good understanding on how to differentially manage the adoption and implementation processes for CASE technology. We are currently executing two studies in this area. While understanding the CASE diffusion process is useful, it is even more important to assess the actual impact of CASE tools on performance of the software-delivery process.
