# Lotus Notes® and Collaboration: Plus ça change...

BETTY VANDENBOSCH AND MICHAEL J. GINZBERG

BETTY VANDENBOSCH is the Lewis-Progressive Assistant Professor of Information and Decision Systems at the Weatherhead School of Management at Case Western Reserve University. She has several years' experience as a management consultant and continues to consult with leading organizations on management information issues. Her research focuses on executive information, mental models, and the impact of technology on learning and ideas. It has appeared in Accounting, Management and Information Technologies, Information Systems Research, Information Systems Journal, and Journal of Management Information Systems.

MICHAEL J. GINZBERG is Professor Management Information and Decision Systems and Associate Dean for Professional and International Programs at the Weatherhead School of Management at Case Western Reserve University. He has previously served on the business or management faculties of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, Columbia University, New York University, and Erasmus Universiteit. Dr. Ginzberg's research, teaching, and consulting interests focus on managing information services activities, development of decision support systems, implementing new information systems and organizational arrangements, the management of technical professionals, and the international deployment of information technology. His articles on these topics have been published in MIS Quarterly, Communications of the ACM, Management Science, OMEGA, European Journal of Operational Research, International Journal of Technology Management, Interfaces, and the Sloan Management Review.

ABSTRACT: Work in organizations is becoming increasingly focused on collaborative work in groups. Groupware is widely touted as the information technology that can support this new mode of work by fostering collaboration. In a study of Lotus Notes $^{®}$ , a popular groupware product, implemented throughout the professional staff of a large American insurance company, we found the impact of groupware to be somewhat different from certain common expectations. While almost everyone was quite pleased with the Notes $^{®}$ implementation and its perceived impact, there was no evidence of a change in the degree of collaboration among organization members. Two key themes are explored as possible explanations for this result: fit of the technology to the organization, and limited training in how best to use this new technology.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: collaboration technology, groupware, information systems implementation.

IT IS WIDELY BELIEVED THAT WORK IN ORGANIZATIONS IS INCREASINGLY becoming centered on collaborative work in groups. Marshall [23] suggests that collaboration "is a principle-based process of working together that produces trust, integrity and break-through results by building true consensus, ownership and alignment" (p. 15). For Marshall, collaboration requires culture, leadership, vision, team processes, and information support systems. Schrage [32] defines collaboration as "the process of shared creation: two or more individuals with complementary skills interacting to create a shared understanding" (p. 40). He believes that shared space is a fundamental requirement for the creation of shared understanding. That space then becomes a frame of reference for the collaboration and provides an environment in which collaboration can occur.

Traditional computer-based systems offer little that would create the environment needed to support collaboration. However, recent software developments have begun to address this need. “Groupware” has been touted as an exciting new class of information technology that will provide the higher levels of coordination and cooperation needed to support individuals working together in organizations. Indeed, it has been suggested that groupware will lead to increased collaboration among individuals in organizations, in part through the creation of networks of shared spaces that facilitate common understanding and are fundamental to enabling people to collectively grasp key concepts and issues $[19]$ .

The term groupware covers a wide range of technologies $[14]$ . The SIM working group on groupware defines it as “a collection of hardware, software and networks that uniquely enables organizations to communicate, to collaborate and to coordinate business processes. It allows businesses to create inter- and intraorganziational memory and to share knowledge and expertise across time and geography from both structured and unstructured information sources” $[3]$ .

Though much has been written about the potential for groupware technologies to improve organizational functioning, there has been, so far, relatively little evaluation of the actual impact of these technologies, especially in real organizational settings. This paper focuses on the impact of an organization-wide implementation of one particular group support technology, Lotus Notes $^{®}$ , on process and outcome variables in that organization.

Wide-scale implementation of group support technologies in an organization has been touted as an appropriate mechanism for promoting beneficial change within that organization (e.g., [43]). After reviewing some previous evaluations of similar technology introductions, we describe Lotus Notes $^{®}$ , its introduction into the target organization, and our assessment of its impact on collaboration and work outcomes within the organization. Consistent with the findings of others who have begun to study these technologies (e.g., [27]), the data seem to indicate that the impact of Notes $^{®}$ , introduction may not be as dramatic as some of its proponents would suggest.

## Prior research on the impact of Notes®

THE TERM GROUPWARE COVERS A DIVERSE ASSORTMENT OF SOFTWARE PRODUCTS providing many different services to their users. Although Notes $^{®}$ covers only a small part of that range, it is widely used and has been hailed as the standard-setter for this class of technology [12, 29]. It supports loosely defined discussion databases, on the one hand, and, on the other, serves as a systems development platform on which highly structured database or workflow applications can be built. Our focus here is only on the use of Notes $^{®}$ databases to facilitate group discussions. In this form, Notes $^{®}$ has been touted as providing organizations with a technology to support and encourage collaboration [16]. The product is “a combination document creator and indexer, database generator and manager, and messaging platform. It enables asynchronous collaboration by introducing a measure of structure that passively facilitates the process of sharing, organizing and navigating information through an interactive electronic space that serves as a common repository for contributions” [19]. “Notes $^{®}$ lets users transform normally unstructured textual documents into databases, incorporating fields that can be searched and indexed, without the usual constraints of field and record length associated with normal databases” [31, p. 24].

Beyond these database features and the fact that it is easy to use, an attraction of Notes $^{®}$ is the integration of document databases with e-mail: E-mail messages can be forwarded onto the document databases, and information from document databases can be forwarded as e-mail messages. This provides an infrastructure that supports a fluid interaction between individuals and within groups. To organize entries in a database, Notes $^{®}$ supports a hierarchical categorization scheme based on topics. A discussion topic can also have subtopics, resulting in headings and subheadings in the database. Underneath each heading, the titles of the various messages are listed together with the author's name. Another structuring mechanism offered by Notes $^{®}$ is the classification of entries into a main topic and a response. A response is a document that is “attached” to the entry to which it refers. These structures help people distinguish the important from the unimportant and offer them ways to process information without having to read messages in their entirety.

Every message in a Lotus Notes $^{®}$ database has a time stamp and reveals the identity of the author. Depending on how the read/write privileges are set up, documents on a database may or may not be edited by people other than their author. This means that a conversation among a group of people can be held within one document. People can, for instance, choose a color or font that differentiates them from others.

A great deal of practitioner-oriented literature describes the collaborative potential of products such as Notes $^{®}$ (see, for example, the March 1, 1996 issue of CIO magazine). Much of this literature applauds Notes $^{®}$ as a technology that supports the building of a collaborative organization by enabling the creation of community knowledge bases that may pull organizations toward a more open sharing of ideas [19].

Orlikowski [27] conducted a rigorous study of the adoption of Notes $^{®}$ in a large, geographically dispersed professional-services firm. Her findings indicate that the organization's culture and the users' understanding of the technology can have a marked impact on the degree to which a technology such as Notes $^{®}$ will affect collaboration among group members. She suggests that the lack of an appropriate framework for understanding how the Notes $^{®}$ technology differs from other, more familiar technologies (e.g., spreadsheets or e-mail) leads to an adoption that fails to take advantage of the new technology's true potential. Further, she points out that the basically competitive nature of the organization's culture limited interest in a technology to support collaboration. Kiely [16] summarized observations from several field studies of Notes $^{\textregistered}$ , including Orlikowski's, and concluded that, while Notes $^{\textregistered}$ is able to enhance collaboration in organizations that have an inherently collaborative culture, such organizations are relatively rare. Davenport [4] is not as positive. He has “yet to find a company that believed Notes $^{\textregistered}$ transformed how groups worked. Work is not done differently, nor are decisions made differently” (p. 24).

Several other studies of communication technology also inform our understanding of how Notes $^{®}$ might affect interactions among organizational members, and possibly collaboration. Schrage [32] identifies several themes that appear to be consistent among successful collaborations. These include a shared, understood goal; mutual respect, tolerance, and trust; creation and manipulation of shared spaces; continuous communication; and clear lines of responsibility but no restrictive boundaries. Hiltz and Turoff [10, 11] found that the social connectivity of users who adopt a computer-mediated communication system increases about tenfold. In conjunction with this, they found a strong tendency toward more equal participation, and that more opinions tend to be asked for and offered. This finding seems to express several of the themes identified by Schrage. In contrast, Weisband et al. [41] found that people tend to maintain their relative status regardless of the medium through which they communicate.

In summary, the research evidence about the impact of computer-mediated communication systems on collaboration in organizations does not entirely support the claims made for these technologies in the less rigorous, practice-oriented literature. While some studies indicate some apparent impact of these technologies on collaboration in the adopting organizations, most studies have not found substantive effects. Taken together, these studies seem to suggest that the implementation of such technologies will enhance collaboration in an organization when: (1) organization members have a need to collaborate; (2) users understand the technology and how it can support collaboration; (3) the organization provides appropriate support for the adoption, implementation, and continued use of the technology; and (4) the organizational culture supports collaboration. We have observed, however, that many organizations believe that introducing Notes $^{®}$ and similar technologies will impact collaboration and problem-solving behavior no matter what, and they act accordingly.

## The Study

THIS STUDY FOLLOWS THE LARGE-SCALE INTRODUCTION OF Lotus Notes $^{®}$ in an organization in an attempt to increase our understanding of the impact Notes $^{®}$ (or similar groupware products) might have on organizations that adopt it. Given the focus of much of the practice-oriented literature that appears to be influential with potential adopting organizations, our focus was on the change in the extent of collaboration, as well as on such outcome variables as efficiency, effectiveness, and satisfaction. We studied the rollout of Notes $^{®}$ at a large American insurance company. Over a ten-month period, we collected usage statistics, conducted a series of interviews, and performed two surveys of users. This design provided us with information about the nature of the implementation itself and enabled us to develop an understanding of the effect of Lotus Notes $^{®}$ on the organization and how that effect changed over time.

The company is a major insurance company headquartered in the Midwest with major operations from coast to coast. In 1993, it had premiums in force of \$1.8 billion and 5,800 employees. The company has a long history of very successful innovative use of information technology. The company originally adopted Lotus Notes® to allow geographically dispersed members of the organization to participate in a corporate reengineering effort. Historically, the organization consisted of highly autonomous units distributed throughout the United States. In 1992, the company instituted several initiatives aimed at centralizing control over its key activities. It built a campus to house the divisions headquartered in its home state at a single location. It undertook a set of Process Management Initiatives (PMIs) intended to standardize the company's key activities across its eleven regional divisions. These processes included claim service, premium quoting, premium billing, customer service, customer retention, rate revision, and distribution.

One of the division presidents took responsibility for each of these process initiatives. This responsibility included the formulation of a vision statement, a measurement system, and plans for improving the process. Lotus Notes $^{®}$ was regarded as a key tool in supporting this reengineering effort as it allowed people who were geographically dispersed to participate in discussions about process change. In addition, Notes $^{®}$ provided those not directly linked to the initiatives with a mechanism for staying informed. This was intended to ensure visibility of the PMIs and to give people with innovative ideas a chance to be heard by those in charge of redesigning the processes. Using the Lotus Notes $^{®}$ databases as discussion forums opened up the decision-making process to everyone.

Senior management viewed the experience of using Notes $^{®}$ to support these process redesigns as a great success. The number of people participating in these redesign efforts and the extent of their interaction and participation significantly exceeded the results of other major initiatives undertaken by the organization. Consequently, it was decided to expand the use of Notes $^{®}$ from the 200 or so people involved in the PMIs to a much wider group. This expansion, it was believed, would alter the nature of communication in the company, resulting in a much more collaborative organization.

At the outset of our study, 337 people in the organization had Lotus Notes $^{®}$ on their desktops, and about 29 gigabytes of disk space were used to store their mail folders and 300 databases. By the time of our first survey, the technology was in the hands of over 1,200 users. By the time of our final survey, about 2,200 people had Notes $^{®}$ and close to 60 gigabytes of storage were used for their mail folders and more than 2,000 databases. The organization used Notes $^{®}$ exclusively for electronic mail and document databases.

## Research Design

DATA FOR THIS STUDY WERE COLLECTED THROUGH A SERIES of initial interviews followed by two surveys. The interviews took place after it was decided to expand

Notes $^{®}$ availability throughout the organization but prior to completion of the rollout. Survey data were collected approximately three and nine months after the rollout.

## Interviews

Semistructured interviews were conducted at the outset of our project. We interviewed seventeen senior managers and thirty other employees at various levels of the organization to assess their perceptions of the objectives for the Notes $^{®}$ implementation and to understand their perspectives and expectations toward the technology. Both face-to-face and telephone interviews were conducted. Each lasted from 30 to 45 minutes and was tape-recorded and transcribed for subsequent analysis. We asked interviewees not about collaboration specifically, but rather about their use and opinions of the technology and the difference it made (or would make) to their work and to the organization.

## Surveys

The interviews provided a glimpse into how Notes $^{®}$ was being received by the organization, but interviews with fewer than fifty employees could not provide an adequate picture of how Notes $^{®}$ was affecting the broad segments of the organization implementing this new technology. In order to capture this broader picture, we surveyed a much larger sample of employees three months after we conducted the interviews, and again six months later. The first survey was administered to a random selection of 450 employees. Of these, twenty did not have access to Notes $^{®}$ at the time and another five had been included in the pretest; this resulted in a true sample of 425. A total of 290 usable surveys were returned, for a response rate of 68 percent. The second survey, which contained the same questions as the first, was administered to the same employees six months later. This time, 245 completed the survey, yielding a response rate of 54 percent. One hundred and eighty-six employees completed both surveys, resulting in a final response rate of 41 percent. The paired responses of this group were used for all subsequent analyses.

T-tests were run to determine if the demographics of the people who only responded to the first survey $n = 98$ differed substantially from those who responded to both surveys $n = 186$ . We found no statistically significant differences in tenure with the company, tenure in the job, or age. These results satisfied our concern about the possibility of a systematic bias in the final sample.

The group of respondents that answered both questionnaires had varying levels of experience with Notes $^{®}$ . At the time of the first survey, 45.6 percent had had access to Notes $^{®}$ for three months or less, another 16.3 percent for between three and five months, and the remainder for between five months and two and a half years. On average, they used Notes $^{®}$ for 1.6 hours per day at the time of the first survey and 1.9 hours per day at the time of the second survey. They ranged in age from 22 to 57, had been with the company from less than a year to more than 22 years, with a mean of 7 years. They had, on average, been in their current jobs for 3 years.

In order to develop an understanding of how Notes $^{®}$ affected collaboration, we asked respondents to complete scales on degree of collaboration and individual and organizational impact. The appendix shows the wording of the items that make up these scales.

## Degree of Collaboration

There are several ways to determine the level of collaboration in a group. Social psychologists typically locate individuals in their roles with respect to one another and the group. They have identified various roles such as isolate, group member, liaison, and boundary spanner. Other measures are used to describe the degree of collaboration within social relations; these include degree of centrality or integrativeness, information exchange, expertise exchange, friendship, and status. All of these dimensions can be combined to determine the degree of collaboration and group effectiveness within an organization $[39]$ . Thompson $[36]$ focuses on the nature of the relationship among individuals and groups, defining pooled, sequential, and reciprocal interdependence as reflecting increasing levels of need for communication and collaboration. Shea and Guzzo $[33]$ believe that task interdependence, outcome interdependence, and potency are especially important in determining group effectiveness. They define task interdependence as the degree to which task determines interactions among group members, outcome interdependence as the extent to which task results affect all members of the group, and potency as the belief of group members that they can be effective as a group.

Following Moch, Feather, and Fitzgibbons [25], we use a scale that taps into the myriad potential interdependencies and collaborations found in organizations. Those authors developed a scale to measure degree of interdependence within a work group, between the work group and other groups in the division, and between the work group and other divisions in the organization. The scale asks for assessments of need for assistance, need for information or advice, and interdependence of work. Moch et al. found this scale highly correlated with more traditional measures such as network diagrams, providing evidence for its validity as a measure of work-flow interdependence. We used their scale to measure the degree of collaboration within the organization we studied. We divided it into three subscales: departmental, divisional, and corporate collaboration.

## Impact

A fundamental issue facing all MIS researchers is the selection of a dependent construct to measure system impact $[6, 15, 34]$ . The ultimate aim of most systems is to improve organizational performance. However, because of the many confounding factors, alternate explanations, and time lags associated with the measurement of performance, researchers frequently develop surrogate measures. User information satisfaction (UIS) and system use are two of the most common $[15]$ , even though these measures are not ideal and their appropriateness is often dependent on the situation under investigation [13]. For example, high use may be an indication of a successful system, of a system that is very easy to use, or of compliance with a mandate for use from management [20, ch. 5]. High use of a system may also result when the system is the only source for necessary information or provides a channel of communication not otherwise available.

Given the difficulty of interpreting data on use and/or satisfaction, we chose, following DeLone and McLean [6], to measure both individual and organizational impact.

## Individual Impact

DeLone and McLean [6] define individual impact as “an indication that an information system has given the user a better understanding of the decision context, has improved his or her decision-making productivity, has produced a change in user activity, or has changed the decision maker’s perception of the importance or usefulness of the information system” (p. 69). Davis [5] followed a careful process to develop a reliable and valid scale to measure perceived usefulness. In two separate studies, the scale attained exceptionally high reliability scores (Cronbach’s alphas were 0.97 and 0.98) and high scores on measures of convergent and discriminant validity. Davis’s final questions were incorporated into our study questionnaire to measure perceived usefulness.

## Organizational Impact

There is general agreement that organizational performance is multidimensional $[40]$ , although there is some disagreement about what the dimensions actually are. Barnard $[1]$ divides performance into efficiency and effectiveness. In partial agreement, Lawrence and Dyer $[18]$ believe performance hinges on the appropriate balance of efficiency and innovation. Steers $[35]$ believes that organizational effectiveness is best understood in terms of productivity, flexibility, and the absence of intraorganizational strain.

In simple terms, performance can be divided into two components: doing things right (efficiency or production) and doing the right things (effectiveness, innovation, flexibility). Efficiency and effectiveness have often been used as surrogates for performance in management research. Recent examples can be found in the strategy $[30]$ , organizational behavior $[42]$ , and MIS $[22]$ literatures. Vandenbosch $[38]$ developed scales to measure perceptions of efficiency and effectiveness related to executive support systems. Those scales proved to be both reliable and valid in that context, so we modified them and used them to assess performance in the survey.

Items included in the scales to assess the perceived individual usefulness of Notes $^{®}$ as well as its perceived impact on organizational efficiency are included in the appendix.

## Results

## Interviews

THE INITIAL INTERVIEWS CLEARLY INDICATED THAT NO ONE in the organization had spent time thinking about how Notes $^{®}$ would change the organization. One division president complained,

No one has said, "Here is the vision. Here is the ultimate end objective that we are trying to create."

In addition, there was concern on the part of some that the organization was not making full use of the technology.

It is nothing but a big expense.

When one reads about Notes $^{®}$ in other organizations, one gets the perception that we are relatively underwhelming in our use of it.

I know cheaper ways to make people feel good.

I think that the company has a long way to go in terms of understanding the power of these tools and how to use them. I think a lot of people are still kind of looking at Lotus Notes $^{®}$ as fancy e-mail. And it can be so much more than that, and we are just not doing it.

Nevertheless, most people we interviewed were very supportive of the technology and believed it would improve decision making in the organization.

Better decisions, faster decisions.

And so all that stuff about making better decisions faster, I think it is really all true.

So we got a lot more input, and I believe that leads to better decisions.

It is an efficient way of exchanging information. And updating each other on what is going on. And communicating.

Many people also talked about the potential that Notes $^{®}$ had to improve collaboration and consultative decision making.

I suspect it has probably made me a little more collaborative, if you will, than I have been in the past. Because again, it is just so easy to speak with somebody.

And I think that standardization will help a lot of people work together better.

We have spent four or five years fighting to impose consultative decision making, so there will be more. I guarantee there is more all the time. And Notes $^{®}$ is not the reason for it, but it may facilitate it.

The benefit of Notes $^{®}$ is that it supports our consultative management style. It is a much simpler and more direct way to handle things.

At the same time, some people felt that the benefits were not substantive. One executive was particularly eloquent on this matter.

Communication will be more efficient. But it can make us more efficient in bad communication. When we introduced PROFS, we ended up underestimating the amount of hardware we needed to support it. Because communication went up dramatically. But we did not communicate any better. And that is my theory around this We are going to make a huge investment in this thing and we are not going to communicate any better. We are just going to communicate more efficiently.

## Surveys

The interview results pointed to widely varying technological frames of reference $[28]$ but did not provide a clear picture of how Notes $^{®}$ was affecting decision-making behavior and collaboration. Survey data, collected later and from a broader sample of the organization, were used to clarify our understanding.

Two types of analysis were undertaken to investigate collaboration using the survey data. In the first, repeated-measures MANOVAs were employed to determine if there were any changes in perceptions between the time of the first and second surveys. In the second, correlations among the constructs were calculated to determine if collaboration was related to perceptions of impact regarding Notes $^{®}$ .

The differences between perceptions at the time of the first survey and at the time of the second were calculated using MANOVA. A repeated-measures design with multiple dependent variables was specified with time as the single factor for each of the six constructs of interest. Table 1 shows the results of these analyses. The means and standard deviations of the dependent variables comprising each construct are also shown. Each of these constructs was scored on a five-point scale (1 = low, 5 = high).

These results indicate that there were no significant changes in the amount of departmental, divisional, or corporate collaboration between the two surveys. However, perceived usefulness, efficiency, and effectiveness all had at least one variable that differed significantly from the first survey to the second. Biases associated with a potential Hawthorne effect were ruled out because of the differing outcomes. Biases associated with recall were not considered likely given the length of time between the two surveys.

To evaluate the changes in perceived usefulness, efficiency, and effectiveness more precisely, a post-hoc Bonferroni procedure was used to control the Type I error rate. Perceived usefulness had six dependent variables in the analysis, so an overall significance level of p < 0.05 was maintained with individual significance of $p \leq 0.008$ . All of the individual variables had changed significantly at this level between the first and second surveys. Perceived efficiency and perceived effectiveness both had five dependent variables. Hence, the significance level required to maintain an overall significance level of $p \leq 0.05$ was p < 0.01. One variable in the efficiency construct (speeds decisions) and two in the effectiveness construct (organizational effectiveness and innovation) failed this post-hoc test.

To evaluate the relationship between collaboration and impact, zero-order Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated. Because of the high Cronbach's alphas achieved for each construct, the means of the items comprising each were calculated and used in the correlation analyses. ## Discussion and Conclusions

IN THE ORGANIZATION STUDIED, THE INTRODUCTION OF Notes $^{®}$ resulted in general satisfaction but no increase in collaboration. Our initial interviews indicated a general belief that Notes $^{®}$ would improve communications efficiency within the organization. The two surveys taken three and nine months later showed an increase in perceived usefulness of the software product itself as well as in perceived organizational efficiency and effectiveness. Perceived collaboration, however, at all levels—department, division, and corporation—remained unchanged over the period of this study. There was, nonetheless, a significant correlation between an individual’s perception of collaboration and his or her perception of usefulness, efficiency and effectiveness: That is, those individuals who indicated that they collaborated most with others gave Notes $^{®}$ the highest marks.

What do these results tell us about the implementation of Lotus Notes $^{®}$ ? The view of Notes $^{®}$ (and similar systems) promulgated in the popular and trade press is that its implementation in an organization will result in an increased level of collaboration among the people in that organization. The results of this study, consistent with earlier studies [23], suggest that the process is not so straightforward. While general satisfaction with the outcome was expressed, overall, we detected no change in the level of collaboration within this organization following the implementation of Notes $^{®}$ . There was a range in levels of collaboration across the individuals in the organization prior to the introduction of Notes $^{®}$ , and that range was maintained following its introduction. Those who collaborated most before (the “collaborators”) continued to do so.

Those who had not collaborated before (the “loners”) continued not to do so. Further, the “collaborators,” those whose existing style of working best fit the model implicit in Notes $^{®}$ , indicated the greatest degree of satisfaction with Notes $^{®}$ and its impact. In considering these results, we should keep in mind that the introduction of Notes $^{®}$ in this organization was not accompanied by any redesign of work processes or rigorous training program that might lead organization members to adopt a new mode of working. The technology was given to everyone, minimal training was provided, and it was assumed that people would learn to use the new technology in a manner that would support their working environment and enhance collaboration in their work relationships. This is in direct contrast to recommendations by Orlikowski [27] that training is critical if Notes $^{®}$ or similar technologies are to have a significant impact on work practices.

We suggested earlier that the existing literature points to several prerequisites if the introduction of a collaboration technology is indeed to foster collaboration. First, a need to collaborate must exist. In the organization's initial, limited use of Notes® for process redesign initiatives, the task and the need for collaboration in that task were clear. When Notes® was implemented more broadly, there was no clearly defined task in mind; thus, the need for collaboration was, at best, mixed. Second, users must understand the technology and how it can support collaboration. The limited training and the apparent lack of a clear vision suggest strongly that this requirement was not met, at least not for the larger group. The third requirement was that the organization provide appropriate support for the introduction and ongoing use of the technology. Again, the lack of training suggests this requirement was not met. Finally, the fourth requirement was a culture supportive of collaboration. The survey data suggest wide variations on this dimension across the organization. When we view the data through this lens, it is not surprising that the introduction of Notes® did not result in marked increases in collaboration.

We must not generalize from a single case study in one organization. The results of this study are, however, quite consistent with previous studies of system implementation generally and Notes $^{®}$ implementation in particular. Bjørn-Andersen, Eason, and Robey [2] conducted a multicountry study of multiple computer-based systems and concluded that these systems more often reinforce existing structures and practices than they introduce new ones. The notion of information technology as the spearhead for change in organizations is, in most cases, just not applicable. Orlikowski [27], too, in her study of Notes $^{®}$ implementation, found that it did not necessarily lead to the changes in work practices and increases in collaboration that its champions had hoped it would. She points out the need both for an appropriate framework for understanding the technology and for a fit between the technology’s underlying premise (e.g., collaboration) and the organization’s structure, culture, and policies. It is possible that insufficient time had elapsed during our study for changes in collaboration to take effect. The findings of Tyre and Orlikowski [37], however, that system-usage patterns congeal in about three to four months, diminish the strength of this argument.

The results of this case study warrant consideration for another reason. The organization studied here is generally considered a leader in the use of information technology. It has a documented history of success in effectively deploying IT to meet organizational objectives. If this organization assumed that simply introducing Notes $^{®}$ would be an effective strategy for enhancing collaboration, it is likely that many other organizations would pursue a similar thought process and strategy.

Notes $^{®}$ (and similar systems) will, most likely, develop into an important component of groupware technology. However, it will not mysteriously transform organizations

from collections of highly competitive loners to well-integrated, cooperative groups of collaborators. Without careful planning for its introduction and the changes that this will entail, the impact of Notes $^{®}$ is likely to be quite limited. This message is not new and has long been a part of the prescription for successful information system implementation [8]. Nonetheless, the evidence both from this study and in the popular press suggests that the message bears repeating. Successful implementation of Notes $^{®}$ or other collaboration technologies will require both a careful assessment of the fit of the technology to the organization and a well-designed training program to introduce this new technology and its potential to the organization members.

The organizational assessment must address the question of fit between the technology and the key features of the organization—structure, culture, policies, and reward system. Where potential conflict is found, a decision must be made. Can the technology provide sufficient benefit if it is implemented in the organization as it stands? Or will it be necessary to change the technology, the organization, or both, in order for implementation to be a success? Recent work on the implementation of advanced manufacturing systems $[24]$ reiterates the importance of changing the culture first in order to obtain maximum benefit from these sophisticated systems.

Finally, we turn to training. Notes $^{®}$ , and groupware more generally, is meant to support organizational processes that are likely to be different from those in place in the organizations that adopt it. The implicit model behind these technologies is different from the model behind other software tools with which organization members are familiar. Instructing new Notes $^{®}$ users in the mechanics of its operations will not be adequate. They will learn to operate the new technology, but they will fit that operation into their own framework for understanding technologies. That is, they will view it, for example, as a more sophisticated e-mail system, and will use it as such. Without training aimed specifically at developing a new framework for understanding the technology and its potential, it is unlikely that the full benefit can ever be reached.

Groupware offers the potential to transform organizational functioning. The accumulating evidence from this study and others indicates that achieving that transformation will require significant effort. The guidelines presented here are very consistent with prior research on IS implementation and should provide a direction for those organizations seeking to utilize these technologies most effectively.
