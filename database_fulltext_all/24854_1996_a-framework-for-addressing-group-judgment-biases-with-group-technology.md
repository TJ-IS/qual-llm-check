# A Framework for Addressing Group Judgment Biases with Group Technology

LAI-HUAT LIM AND IZAK BENBASAT

LAI-HUAT LIM is a Lecturer in the Department of Information Systems and Computer Science, National University of Singapore. Dr. Lim received his bachelor's degree in electrical engineering and master's degree in MIS from the National University of Singapore, and his Ph.D. from the University of British Columbia. His current research interests include negotiation support systems, group support systems, and managerial issues pertaining to collaborative technology.

IZAK BENBASAT is CANFOR Professor of Management Information Systems at the Faculty of Commerce and Business Administration, University of British Columbia. He received his Ph.D. from the University of Minnesota. His research interests include evaluating human-computer interfaces, investigating the influence of decision support tools on problem-solving strategies, the use of explanations in expert systems, and comparing methods for conducting information systems research.

ABSTRACT: Systematic biases have been found in both individual and group judgments, calling for research into debiasing approaches. Although individual debiasing has been studied to some extent, no such effort exists for group debiasing. This paper advocates the use of group support systems (GSS) for group debiasing and presents a theoretical perspective on how this debiasing may be achieved. Special attention is paid to two important judgment biases: representativeness bias and availability bias. A research model is developed from which propositions are derived.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: availability bias, debiasing, decision-making bias, group support systems, representativeness bias.

THE SEMINAL WORK OF AMOS TVERSKY AND DANIEL KAHNEMAN [60] has shown that human judgments contain systematic biases as a result of the use of heuristics. Decision makers employ heuristics so that complex inference tasks can be reduced to manageable proportions. Two important heuristics are representativeness and availability [34, 35, 59, 60]. Representativeness bias refers to the bias incurred in posterior-probability estimations by not properly utilizing information sources such as base rates. Availability bias refers to the estimation of the frequency or probability of events by the ease with which instances or associations come to mind. Group judgments, an

important facet of organizational activities, are also prone to these biases (e.g., [2, 54]). Although debiasing methods and effectiveness have been examined in contexts involving individuals making judgments (e.g., [23, 64]), no parallel effort seems to exist for group judgments.

Group support systems (GSS) have been designed to support group decision making and deal with process losses, such as air-time fragmentation, conformance pressure, and others. Although GSS have been evaluated using various criteria, if and how well they would serve as a debiasing tool remains unexplored. Possibly because of this neglect, both the design and evaluation of GSS have been characterized by a tilted emphasis toward the communication perspective and fall relatively short in terms of supporting the cognitive aspect of judgment and decision-making processes. For example, the meta-analysis conducted by Benbasat and Lim [6] shows that empirical work in GSS has been generally more concerned with collective and group communication phenomena than with cognitive and information-processing aspects. However, as emphasized by DeSanctis and Gallupe [17], more sophisticated GSS designs should incorporate not just communication but modeling support as well.

This paper seeks to provide a theoretical perspective for the investigation of the impact of GSS on group judgment biases (with specific attention on representativeness bias and availability bias) by identifying the match between new or existing GSS group debiasing tools and judgment tasks. We believe that this research will fill an important gap in both the GSS literature and the literature in judgment heuristics and biases. More important, it will serve to open up a new direction for GSS research that is more closely associated with the investigation of traditional decision theories.

## Two-Dimensional Information Integration Perspective

THIS RESEARCH EMPLOYS A TOP-DOWN APPROACH IN ATTEMPTING to theorize the effects of GSS on judgment biases. At the highest level, it uses a two-dimensional information integration perspective (TIIP), depicted in figure 1. According to this perspective, the effect of GSS use on group judgments and choices is produced via two integration processes. Along the interpersonal dimension, GSS brings together the views, opinions, judgments, and choices of individual members. The means by which the interpersonal aspect of information integration is accomplished is communication, which has been the traditional focus of GSS design and evaluation. Left unsupported, group processes are often plagued by “process losses,” which refer to aspects of the meeting process that impair outcomes relative to the efforts of the same individuals working by themselves or of groups that do not experience them [57]. An example of process loss is “air-time fragmentation,” which is the need to partition speaking time among speakers during a verbal meeting [45]. By incorporating electronic communication, GSS helps to reduce process losses such as air-time fragmentation by providing features such as parallel inputting and forges a group judgment that takes into account a broader range of possibilities and considerations.

Although much of the GSS research has concentrated on the communication perspective, an equally important mechanism through which GSS may be useful in judgment and decision-making situations has to do with the more direct linkage between GSS and judgment tasks. This aspect involves cognitive support, dealing with the intrapersonal dimension of information integration. It is a well-known and undisputed fact that humans are limited in terms of cognitive processing capabilities. For this reason, decision aids should always be welcomed $[31]$ . This aspect of GSS support has been relatively unexplored, although there are exceptions (e.g., $[52]$ ).

For the sake of clarity, the two dimensions are conceptualized here as though they were independent and orthogonal; in practice, however, these dimensions may be intertwined; one activity may trigger the other, and vice versa. For example, through sharing of information and views, individuals may obtain new information and thus trigger a new round of intrapersonal information integration. Similarly, after an individual has combined different information sources and arrived at a judgment, he or she might share it with the group members, thus affecting the interpersonal information integration process.

In sum, two types of support can be provided by GSS to address the two aspects of information integration encountered in group judgment situations (see figure 2). On the one hand, GSS provides features that improve communication among group members, thus making the group more efficient. The use of an efficient group (i.e., one that is devoid of “groupthink” and other deficiencies) has been proposed as a method in and of itself for improving judgments (e.g., [33, 37]). GSS addresses communication problems by providing parallel communication and a communication channel that invites fewer social and political cues. On the other hand, GSS may also provide features that aim directly at augmenting the information-processing capacity and capability of human decision makers, thus replacing the less reliable heuristics.

## Components of GSS

ALTHOUGH DIFFERENT IMPLEMENTATIONS OF GSS MAY CONTAIN DIFFERENT decision-making and communication aids, some generic GSS tools may be defined conceptually. Nunamaker et al. [45] identify four categories of tools that support the different group activities: (1) exploration and idea generation, which includes electronic discussion besides brainstorming; (2) idea organization, for organizing ideas into specific categories; (3) prioritizing, which support the evaluation of multiple items; and (4) tools that provide formal methodologies to support policy development and evaluation (e.g., stakeholder analysis). While this categorization is useful for many decision-making situations, we propose that for judgment situations, which conceptually precede the choice phase, $^{1}$ a more specific set of tools can be defined. Building on Nunamaker et al.'s taxonomy, we propose that the following categories of GSS tools are needed to support judgment activities: (1) information exchange, which primarily means electronic discussion; (2) idea generation, which specifically means electronic brainstorming; and (3) problem-representation tools, aimed specifically at debiasing and supporting a certain class of judgment tasks. The separation of idea generation from information exchange is also supported by Nagasundaram and Dennis [43], who contend that:

[A] large part of idea-generation behavior in electronic brainstorming (EBS) can be explained by viewing EBS as an individual, cognitive (rather than a social) phenomenon from the human information processing (IPS) perspective. EBS incorporates a set of structuring mechanisms meant to overcome the limitations of the human IPS. Consequently, a group using an EBS outperforms both verbal brainstorming and nominal groups by operating not as a group but as a collection of individuals who interact with an evolving set of ideas rather than with individuals. [p. 463]

Among the GSS components we propose for supporting judgment, electronic communication is perhaps the most familiar concept in GSS research and has therefore received the most attention. Problem representation is a relatively new concept in GSS research, although it has been studied in the context of supporting individual judgments and debiasing.

A Match of GSS Tools and Judgment Tasks

## Judgment Tasks: On-line versus Memory-based

FROM THE PERSPECTIVE OF THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN MEMORY AND JUDGMENT, Hastie and Park [28] propose the taxonomy of “on-line” versus “memory-based” judgment tasks. According to this taxonomy, the key distinction concerns the source of inputs to the judgment operators. In on-line tasks, information for the operator follows a path from the stimulus environment external to the subject into working memory and directly to the judgment operator. An important example in this group is the representativeness task (associated with the representativeness bias). On the other hand, in memory-based tasks, judgment is necessarily memory-based; the subject must rely on the retrieval of relatively concrete evidence from memory in order to reach a judgment. Therefore, the availability task (associated with the availability bias) belongs to this category. The pattern of findings that emerges from the overview of research in group judgment lends some support to this taxonomy by showing clearly different results between the representativeness and availability tasks (i.e., groups showed greater representativeness bias and slightly lower availability bias than individuals). Although simple, Hastie and Park’s taxonomy is adequate for the purposes of an initial effort to identify types of GSS tools for debiasing judgments.

It would be superficial to assume that only two types of “generic” GSS tools will be needed to deal with the biases associated with the two categories of judgment tasks, primarily because each category (especially on-line) may not be homogeneous. Therefore, instead of trying to find a generic set of tools for each category of judgment tasks, we shall attempt to identify the appropriate tools for the most important member task in each category—that is, representativeness task and availability task.

## Representativeness Bias

When the representativeness heuristic is used, the probability that X came from Y is evaluated on the basis of how much X resembles Y. In other words, class membership of an object is judged by its similarity to the stereotypical class member. The use of this heuristic leads to several systematic biases in probability estimation. These include insensitivity to prior probabilities (i.e., the base-rate fallacy), insensitivity to sample size, misconceptions of chance, insensitivity to predictability, illusion of validity, and misconceptions of regression $[60]$ . The base-rate fallacy, of particular concern to this research, refers to people's tendency to ignore base rates in favor of individuating or specific information, rather than integrating the two $[3]$ . Among the biases resulting from the use of the representativeness heuristic, the base-rate fallacy has probably been most researched empirically (see [4] for a review).

Normative rules prescribe that a prior probability associated with a belief in a hypothesis remains relevant when some new or more specific evidence is received. Bayes's model dictates that $p(H \mid E)$ , the probability of a hypothesis $H$ , given the information about the base rate or prior $p(H)$ , evidence $E$ , and the diagnosticity of the evidence $p(E \mid H)$ , can be estimated with:

$$
p (H \mid E) = [ p (E \mid H) \times p (H) ] / [ p (E \mid H) \times p (H) + p (E \mid \text { not } H) \times p (\text { not } H) ].
$$

When asked for $p(H \mid E)$ , the probability of the hypothesis given the base rate and the evidence, subjects often answer with $p(E \mid H)$ ; that is, they favor the diagnostic information at the expense of the base rate. To illustrate, let us consider the following “cab problem” [61], one of the best-known problems in base-rate fallacy research:

A cab was involved in a hit-and-run accident at night. Two cab companies, the Green and the Blue, operate in the city. You are given the following data:

(i) 85 percent of the cabs in the city are Blue and 15 percent are Green.

(ii) A witness identified the cab as Green. The court tested the reliability of the witness under nighttime visibility conditions and concluded that the witness correctly identified each one of the two colors 80 percent of the time and erred 20 percent of the time.

What is the probability that the cab involved in the accident was Green rather than Blue?

The correct answer is 41 percent, but the most common answer is 80 percent due to subjects' overreliance on the diagnostic information (referring to the formula stated, $p(H)$ is 0.15, $p(\text{not } H)$ is 0.85, $p(E \mid H)$ is 0.8, and $p(E \mid \text{not } H)$ is 0.2).

The representativeness bias is found not only in individual but in group judgments as well. For example, Argote, Seabright, and Dyer $[2]$ found the bias more severe in group judgments than in individual judgments, suggesting an obvious need for some type of support. Argote, Devadas, and Melone $[1]$ found that groups tended to be more ignorant about base rates than individuals when the individuating or diagnostic information is informative rather than vague. Consistent with these findings, Brightman et al. $[8]$ reported that nominal groups behave more like Bayesian information processors than interacting groups. Interested in the group consensus process, Stasson et al. $[56]$ used the social decision scheme approach to study two representativeness tasks. For one task, the group consensus process can be described with a model suggesting that groups are only slightly better than individuals; for the other task, the best-fitting models correspond to indications that groups have a higher proportion of biased decisions than people working individually. In general, empirical studies have found more severe biases in group than in individual judgments.

## Availability Bias

When a decision maker uses the availability heuristic, the frequency or probability of an event's occurrence is judged by the ease with which similar events are brought to mind. For example, one may assess the risk of heart attack among middle-aged people by recalling such occurrences among one's acquaintances. This results in biases due to retrievability of instances. Similarly, one may evaluate the probability that a given business venture will succeed or fail by imagining various difficulties it might encounter. This results in biases due to imaginability. Biases due to retrievability of instances and biases due to imaginability may have important consequences for business judgments. When we judge the probability of events we have previously experienced, sensational and vivid events are more easily remembered. Overestimation of the probability of these events and underestimation of less spectacular events often result. In fact, selective bias due to the use of the availability heuristic has been specifically pointed out by Janis [32] as a major defect in decision making.

An example of the problems used in availability research is asking subjects to estimate the relative frequencies that a given letter will appear (say, “R”) in the first and third positions in English words $[59]$ . Despite the fact that the tested letters are more frequently found in the third position, a large majority of subjects judged the first position more likely because the corresponding words were more available to recall.

Following Lichtenstein et al. [38], who studied availability bias in an individual context; Sniezek and Henry [54] asked their subjects, in individual and group settings, to estimate the frequencies of some causes of death. The researchers found groups' judgments more accurate than individual judgments, although bias was still present in group judgments. These results were replicated by Sniezek and Henry [55] with a comparable task. Stasson et al. [56] examined the group consensus process in an availability task. The researchers used the classical task in availability research [59] by asking subjects to estimate the relative frequencies of the letter "R" in the first position of a word versus the third. The best-fitting model indicates that groups outperform individuals working alone, although, again, both display judgment bias. The findings suggest that availability bias observed in individuals can be only partially corrected using groups.

## Cognitive Support Tools for Judgment Tasks

Different types of cognitive support are needed for the two judgment tasks we have described. In the individual judgment context, representativeness bias has been traced to ineffective representations of the problem situation as employed by decision makers. Correspondingly, along the intrapersonal dimension of the information integration process, the bias can be attenuated with GSS by providing some sort of problem representation tool. On the other hand, availability bias is primarily rooted from an inefficient information search and thus can be addressed using the electronic brainstorming tool. In comparison, the electronic brainstorming tool is a more generic GSS tool than the problem representation tool, which has to be tailored to specific classes of problems.

## Using Problem Representation to Alleviate Representative Bias

Previous research has pointed out the effect of problem representation on the neglect of base rates (e.g., [26]). Roy [51] went a step further in identifying the underlying cognitive processes that may explain such an effect. Based on empirical data, these researchers concluded that causal representations dominate information processing in base-rate problems. In such representations, for example, a causal link is formed between the event “the cab was green” and the event “the witness says the cab was green,” such that the former causes the latter. Related to this internal representation, semantic confusion of different types of conditional probabilities has been observed [14, 22]. For example, when questions are posed in an inverse-to-causal order (e.g., if the witness says the cab was green, what is the probability that it was in fact green?), people tend to reinterpret the question to fit the causal order (i.e., if the cab was green, what is the probability that the witness says it was green?). However, when no causal links are present in the representation, judgments are more accurate or are closer to the normative answer [3, 51]. Because no causal theory can be derived, people resort to feature similarity for categorization. With the cab problem [61], for example, the representation would involve the following categories: Green cab identified (by witness) as green; green cab identified as blue; blue cab identified as blue; blue cab identified as green.

Following from the above, a promising approach to improve judgments in base-rate fallacy-generating contexts would be to help people build noncausal representations. Previous results have shown that representing the problem situation in terms of the population distribution and visualizing the characteristics of the various components facilitate judgments of probabilities $[9, 47]$ . In particular, tables and graphics have been tested as representational aids (e.g., $[11]$ ). Roy $[51]$ concludes that a table is not sufficient to change the initial causal representation of the problem and that graphs present the interaction of object features more clearly. Roy further recommends graphical representational aids as critical elements in decision support systems (DSS) design for probabilistic reasoning and found empirical support in individual judgment contexts.

Part of the current research represents a further extension of the study of problem representation as a debiasing tool by incorporating into GSS a graphical problem-representation tool known as the “probability map” [10, 11, 51] to improve group judgments (see figure 3). $^{2}$ This augmentation could be realized in one of two ways: In the first form, the tool is associated with each member’s “private” system; correspondingly, an individual member can interact with the tool at his or her own pace and form a judgment on his or her own, even before public discussion with other group members. In the second form, the tool is accessible to the group as a whole and appears in some “public” screen. In this implementation, the group forms a judgment out of its common experience with the tool. Which realization is superior remains an open empirical question.

The map consists of a total of 100 boxes that are differentiated in terms of both base rate and diagnostic information. For example, figure 3 shows a situation where the base rate is 85 percent lawyers and 15 percent engineers, and the diagnostic information is such that correct identifications based on personality profiles are made 80 percent of the time (thus, errors occur 20 percent of the time). Therefore, 85 of the 100 boxes are white and the other 15 boxes are shaded. Of the 85 boxes representing lawyers, 17 contain a cross, denoting that 20 percent of the 85 are (erroneously) identified as engineers. Also, of the 15 boxes representing engineers, 12 are crossed since 80 percent of the 15 are (correctly) identified as engineers. Consequently, if one is interested in the probability that a certain personality profile has been correctly identified as engineer, such probability can be easily derived by differentiating among the different types of boxes.

## Using Electronic Brainstorming to Alleviate Availability Bias

Availability biases are caused by, among other things, limited information search sets. The GSS tool necessary to provide cognitive support is therefore the idea generation, or electronic brainstorming, tool.

Determining and improving the effectiveness of the brainstorming process in groups has been a major research concern. Using a prescriptive approach, Osborn [46] introduced a systematic technique, in the form of a set of rules, to increase the effectiveness of the brainstorming process in groups. The rules are intended to overcome major social and motivational factors that could inhibit the generation of ideas. For example, one of the rules prohibits criticism of ideas during the brainstorming process. The technique was found to increase the number of ideas produced [36].

The performance of nominal groups (i.e., noninteracting individuals) has also been compared with and found to be superior to verbal brainstorming groups [20, 36, 42, 58].

Diehl and Stroebe [18] tested three major interpretations to account for brainstorming productivity losses: production blocking, evaluation apprehension, and free riding [12, 36, 63]; they concluded that production blocking explained an overwhelming proportion of the variance in performance between verbally brainstorming and nominal groups. In a followup study to determine the causes of production blocking, Diehl and Stroebe [19] found that the longer a brainstorming participant had to wait to verbalize an idea, the greater was the productivity loss; the need to listen to others' ideas while rehearsing one's own ideas further impaired the productivity of groups interacting verbally. The authors [19] concluded that production blocking occurs for one or more of three reasons, consistent with the three forms of production blocking described in [45]. "Attenuation blocking" occurs when group members who are prevented from contributing comments as they occur forget or suppress them later in the meeting because they seem less original, relevant, or important. With "concentration blocking," fewer comments are made because members concentrate on remembering comments, rather than thinking of new ones, until they can contribute them, as a result of short-term memory limitations. "Attention blocking" occurs when new comments are not generated because group members must constantly listen to others speak and cannot pause to think; in other words, exposure to others' ideas is distracting or interferes with their thinking.

There is evidence that electronic brainstorming can effectively address the process losses mentioned above. In several recent studies, groups that brainstormed electronically have been found to outperform verbally brainstorming and nonelectronic nominal groups in the number of ideas generated $[15, 24, 25, 62]$ .

The general principle underlying many idea-generation methods is to create sources of variety in a participant's environment—the greater the variety in the sources (or stimuli) of ideas, the greater the potential variety of ideas generated [30]. In unaided brainstorming situations, however, the cognitive limitations of humans as an information-processing system (IPS) [44] severely restrict the technique's productivity. By providing cognitive assistance to the IPS, electronic brainstorming has been found to be more effective than either verbal brainstorming [25] or nominal group idea generation where individuals work separately without communicating [15, 62].

An electronic brainstorming system includes three structuring mechanisms—parallel input, collective memory, and serially retrievable output—that together work around the limitations of the IPS [43]. The parallel input mechanism allows participants to contribute ideas as soon as they are generated. Thus, participants need not rehearse their ideas in short-term memory indefinitely, and their short-term memory can be freed up for processing additional ideas. Nonetheless, ideas generated simultaneously by multiple participants cannot all be attended to and committed to memory by participants because of the serial nature of the IPS. To address this limitation, the collective memory mechanism allows these ideas that have been generated simultaneously to be stored and later retrieved from this external memory by a participant at will. The serially retrievable output mechanism permits one idea at a time to be accessed in any sequence unrelated to the sequence in which the ideas were generated. Furthermore, the collective memory helps decouple the parallel input from the serial output and introduces at least one level of indirection of communication among group members. These three mechanisms together create the necessary and sufficient conditions for production “unblocking” [43]. In “production blocking,” ideas and comments are suppressed because of cognitive limitations; the concept may be further differentiated as attenuation blocking, concentration blocking, and attention blocking [45]. Attenuation blocking occurs when group members who are prevented from contributing comments as they occur forget or suppress them later in the meeting because they seem less original, relevant, or important. Concentration blocking describes how fewer comments are made because group members concentrate on remembering comments (rather than thinking of new ones) until they can contribute them. Attention blocking occurs when new comments are not generated because group members must constantly listen to others speak and cannot pause to think. Each of these kinds of blocking can be effectively dealt with using the structuring mechanisms of the electronic brainstorming system. Several recent studies indicated that groups that brainstormed electronically outperformed verbal brainstorming and nonelectronic nominal groups in the number of ideas generated [15, 24, 25, 62].

The debiasing power of the brainstorming tool can best be realized if the tool is used during the early moments of a group's deliberation, perhaps even before alternative solutions are designed. This would expose group members to a wider scope of relevant preliminary information that is inherently related to the availability question. In the absence of criticism and evaluation, users interact with an evolving set of ideas pertaining to the topic of interest and later invoke electronic communication (discussed in the next section) to form specific, alternate judgments based on such exploration.

## Electronic Communication

It should be obvious that the GSS tools identified above are meant to support the intrapersonal aspect of the information integration process. $^{3}$ A key difference separating group decision and judgment process from its individual counterpart is the fact that group decision and judgment occur as a result of interpersonal communication—the exchange of information among members [17]. The group decision process is revealed in the production and reproduction of positions regarding group action, which are directed toward the convergence of members on a final judgment or choice [17]. The communication activities exhibited in a decision- or judgment-related meeting include expressions of preference, argumentation, socializing, information seeking, information giving, and solution development [5, 48]. With GSS, these activities may be supported with electronic communication.

The difference between electronic communication and verbal communication has been viewed from different perspectives $[40]$ . For example, Lim and Benbasat $[39]$ point out that the two communication channels differ in terms of “paths” and “contents.” The electronic communication channel takes a somewhat indirect and longer path than the verbal channel, since any group member's input has to go through the computer system before it reaches the target recipient. However, given the very high transmission speed of electronic communication, this difference is transparent at the user level. A second difference has to do with the fact that electronic communication contains fewer social and political cues and is therefore relatively task-oriented. In comparison, a verbal communication channel supports socially oriented communication better by accommodating verbal cues such as timing, pauses, accentuations, and tonal inflection. This distinction is supported by empirical findings [29, 49] and is consistent with media richness theory [13].

Two distinctions between electronic communication and verbal communication are particularly relevant to this research. First, the “social presence” of the electronic communication channel is smaller than that of the verbal communication channel $[50]$ . “Social presence” is the degree to which a medium is perceived as conveying the actual physical presence of the communicating participants $[53]$ . This social presence depends not only on the words conveyed during communication, but also on a range of nonverbal cues, including facial expression, direction of gaze, posture, attire, physical distance, and many verbal cues (timing, pauses, accentuations, inflection) $[7]$ . Because of the smaller social presence in groups communicating electronically, group members need not be as sensitive or wary in making suggestions that seem to oppose those made by others. Indeed, electronic communication is capable of facilitating free expression of views and opinions, a desirable element of group process $[16, 27]$ .

Second, the parallel communication made possible by the electronic communication channel allows group members to raise their thoughts and comments simultaneously without having to wait for another person to finish speaking $[65]$ . As a result, process losses such as air-time fragmentation are reduced and interpersonal information integration is improved.

Several important differences between electronic communication and electronic brainstorming ought to be noted (see Table 1). First, the intent and design of electronic brainstorming has a distinct cognitive focus [43]; in other words, individuals are constantly interacting with an evolving list of ideas rather than other group members. On the other hand, the focus of electronic communication is mainly interpersonal. Second, the electronic brainstorming tool is designed to encourage divergence of ideas, but the primary purpose of the electronic communication tool is to help the convergence of views and opinions. Third, while the parallel input and serial output in electronic brainstorming are decoupled by the collective memory mechanism, which introduces a level of indirection of communication among group members, communication using the electronic communication tool has to be synchronized. Indeed, Easton, George, Nunamaker, and Pendergast [21] found, when comparing electronic discussion tool to electronic brainstorming tool, that the number of ideas generated is significantly lower with electronic discussion. They concluded that each tool worked best on the task for which it was designed; this supports the premise that there should be a match between the GSS tool and the aspect of the information integration process to be supported.

## Research Model

THE IMPACT OF GSS SHOULD BE EXAMINED IN TERMS OF BOTH judgment processes and judgment outcomes (see figure 4). Exploration of the judgment processes offers insight into why the outcomes occur as they do. The detailed variables vary according to the judgment tasks so as to cater to the specific aspects of process and outcome pertaining to each task.

## Representativeness Bias

The cognitive support in this case is realized in the form of the problem representation tool. The impact of GSS on the judgment process can be measured by five variables, aimed basically at understanding the integrating process of the two relevant pieces of information, namely, diagnostic information and base rate. “References to the diagnostic information” indicates how frequently the group refers to the diagnostic information. Similarly, “references to the base rate” provides information on the group’s access of the base rate. “References to the use of single information source” reflects the group’s resolution involving only one piece of information, which is obviously biased. On the other hand, “references to the use of dual information sources” refers to the group’s use of two pieces of information, while they may or may not be properly combined. “References to the use of the problem representation tool” is a measure of how often the group refers to the support tool and thus is only meaningful within groups provided with the tool. Judgment bias, an outcome measure, is assessed as the difference between the group’s response and the correct solution given by the Bayesian model.

## Availability Bias

The cognitive support in this case is realized in the form of the electronic brainstorming tool. Four basic process variables, aimed basically at understanding how the information search scope is affected, are included. “Number of ideas generated” refers to the total count of ideas mentioned. “Number of ideational connections with ideas generated during electronic brainstorming” measures the degree to which ideas generated during electronic brainstorming are used during subsequent discussion. “Number of proposed estimates” measures the total number of times an estimate of the frequency or probability of occurrence of the issue at hand is proposed; this variable is useful for the availability task since the availability bias is often encountered in situations requiring the estimating of the frequency of occurrence of events. In close relation to the previous variable, “range of proposed estimates” measures the difference between the highest and the lowest estimates, thus providing a sense of the width of exploration for alternate judgments.

## Propositions

WE PRESENT HERE TWO MAJOR SETS OF PROPOSITIONS concerning the impact of GSS on judgment biases and the processes through which bias reduction is achieved. Detailed hypotheses may be derived from these propositions.

Proposition 1a: In availability tasks, judgment biases will be reduced in groups provided with cognitive support (vs. no cognitive support) or electronic communication (vs. verbal communication).

Proposition 1b: In representativeness tasks, judgment biases will be reduced in groups provided with cognitive support (vs. no cognitive support).

The relative effectiveness of the two major GSS components discussed in this paper depends on the differential responsiveness of the particular type of judgment being addressed to the two dimensions of information integration—that is, the interpersonal versus the intrapersonal. For example, electronic communication is useful when the judgment task is constrained by both intrapersonal and interpersonal aspects of information integration and can be helped by improving either of the aspects. Availability task, a memory-based task, falls in this category because availability bias is primarily associated with limitations in the information search scope, which can be expanded either through providing cognitive support to individual group members or via information sharing among group members. Electronic communication aids the latter. On the other hand, when the judgment task is restricted mainly by the informationprocessing capacity of group members, and benefits little from information exchange among them, the usefulness of electronic communication is limited. This is the case with the representativeness task. In fact, the representativeness bias has been found to be greater in (unaided) group judgments than in individual judgments $[1, 2, 8, 56]$ , indicating that increased communication among group members does not contribute to effectiveness.

Proposition 2: GSS will achieve the desired bias reduction through influencing the judgment process in a particular manner, depending on the judgment tasks.

To illustrate, consider the use of the problem-representation tool (cognitive support) in addressing the representativeness bias. This tool helps in two ways. First, base-rate fallacy has been caused by excessive reliance on the diagnostic information (versus base rate) in both individual and group judgment situations $[1, 3]$ . The problem-representation tool offers a radically different way of viewing the information sources, thus highlighting the saliency of the base rate to group members. Consequently, we expect groups provided with the tool to make fewer references to the diagnostic information and more to the base rate than groups not provided with the tool.

Second, from an information-integration perspective, the base-rate fallacy is a direct result of groups not properly combining the relevant information sources. In extreme situations (including the base-rate fallacy), only one piece of information is utilized (incorrectly) to generate the posterior probability. Because the problem-representation tool produces a representation that takes into account both the base rate and the diagnostic information, groups provided with the tool are expected to make fewer references to the use of a single information source than those not so equipped.

Next, consider the case of the availability bias, which results from the limitations of the human information-processing system. Events that are more available to the memory are also judged as more important or as occurring more frequently. The primary function served by the electronic brainstorming tool in this situation is to enlarge the information search scope of group members and the group, thereby increasing the total number of ideas generated and the connections of discussion to these ideas. The net result is a better and more thorough information search and integration, leading to reduced availability bias.

## Concluding Remarks

THIS PAPER HAS PRESENTED A FRAMEWORK FOR ADDRESSING GROUP BIASES using GSS and discussed the ideas in terms of two major judgment biases. An important basis within this framework concerns viewing the effects of GSS use in terms of cognitive and information-exchange aspects. This concept parallels that of understanding the effects of negotiation support systems in terms of decision and communication aids [40]. Indeed, besides bias-based and negotiation tasks, this conceptualization may prove useful in situations involving other group tasks described in McGrath's [41] taxonomy. For example, in a task requiring one to decide issues with no right answer (a "type 4" task in the taxonomy), each group member can employ a certain form of cognitive tool to help him or her realize and ascertain his or her preferences while using the communication tool to coordinate confirmed preferences with other members. Empirical studies are called for to expand and refine our understanding of the two information-integration dimensions.
