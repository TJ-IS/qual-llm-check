# Consensus and Perceived Satisfaction Levels: A Cross-Cultural Comparison of GSS and Non-GSS Outcomes within and between the United States and Mexico

ROBERTO J. MEJIAS, MORGAN M. SHEPHERD, DOUGLAS R. VOGEL, AND LITVA LAZANEO

ROBERTO J. MEJIAS is an Assistant Professor of MIS in the Division of Management at the University of Oklahoma. He has over ten years experience in the business sector, with IBM, and the public sector. His interests include the international applications and cultural implications of information technology, particularly group support systems (GSS) technology. He received his M.S. in production management and his Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Arizona.

MORGAN M. SHEPHERD is an Assistant Professor in the Information Systems Department at the University of Colorado at Colorado Springs. His research interests include IT support for distributed groups, applications for technology in the learning environment, and evaluating factors that affect the diffusion of IT in organizations. He received his Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Arizona and his B.S. degree in mechanical engineering from the University of Virginia.

DOUGLAS R. VOGEL is an Associate Professor of MIS at the University of Arizona. His interests bridge the business and academic communities and address the impact of IT on interpersonal communications, group problem solving, organizational productivity, and the international applications of GSS. He received his Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Minnesota and his M.S. in computer science from UCLA.

LITVA LAZANEO is the Associate Director of the Centro de Estudios Estratégicos at the Monterrey campus of the Instituto Tecnológico y de Estudios Superióres de Monterrey (ITESM) in Nuevo León, Mexico. Her interests include the applications of GSS for strategic planning within diverse work groups. She received her M.S. in business administration and her B.S. in industrial and systems engineering from ITESM.

ABSTRACT: Few studies of group support systems (GSS) have considered cultural dimensions in their analysis of GSS applications, and even fewer have used empirical data to test their hypotheses. This cross-cultural field study was an exploratory investigation of the effects of “national culture” (U.S. and Mexican) upon group consensus levels and perceptions of participation equity and satisfaction within GSS environments and non-GSS (manual) environments. Within both the U.S. and Mexican cultures, results indicate no significant differences in consensus levels between GSS and manual treatments. However, both U.S and Mexican groups generated significantly greater relative changes in consensus levels using GSS technology versus manual technology. Comparison between cultures shows that Mexican groups generated higher levels of consensus than U.S. groups with main effects due to culture and experimental treatment (i.e., GSS support).

While U.S. group participants reported no differences in satisfaction among experimental treatments, Mexican participants reported higher satisfaction levels using GSS-supported environments. In a similar pattern, U.S. participants reported no differences in perceived participation equity between treatments, while Mexican GSS participants reported higher participation equity than Mexican manual participants. A comparison study between cultures found that Mexican participants across all treatments perceived higher levels of satisfaction and participation equity than U.S. participants with main effects due to culture.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: consensus in group support systems, cross-cultural use of group support systems, group support systems.

MODERN ORGANIZATIONS AND CORPORATE EXECUTIVES ARE INCREASINGLY confronted by a competitive and often hostile business environment. Managers, particularly those from multinational corporations (MNCs), often face the complex task of integrating their operations, frequently across nationally and culturally diverse environments $[25, 31]$ . Managers and employees spend a considerable portion of their work day (30–80 percent) attending a broad spectrum of meetings and working with a wide range of cross-functional and multinational work groups $[20]$ . Making the most effective and efficient use of these group meetings has understandably become a high managerial priority. Organizations have increasingly looked to information technology (IT), particularly electronic support systems such as group support systems (GSS), to maximize the effectiveness and quality of group meeting outcomes.

Globally, the use of IT by both industrialized nations and, to a lesser degree, developing nations to support numerous decision-making activities continues to grow $[25]$ . However, the conceptual and theoretical foundations supporting the applicability of current GSS designs have been based upon traditional North American concepts of “desirable” group goals and “desirable” group behavior $[35]$ . The vast majority of empirical research regarding GSS meeting technology and its effects on group decision making has been confined to U.S. subjects within U.S. organizations and environments $[35]$ . Few GSS studies have considered cultural dimensions in their analyses of electronic support systems; even fewer have employed empirical data to test their hypotheses. In view of the significant developments in IT and the global proliferation of telecommunication systems, this shortcoming in IT research is clearly disappointing.

This cross-cultural field study was an exploratory investigation into the effects of national culture (U.S. and Mexican) upon consensus levels and satisfaction levels in environments using computer-mediated support (i.e., GSS) and non-GSS, or traditional, “manual” environments. The study was conducted at two research sites: the University of Arizona, in Tucson, representing the U.S. national culture, and the

Instituto Tecnológico y de Estudios Superióres de Monterrey (ITESM) in Monterrey, Nuevo León, Mexico, representing the Mexican national culture. The study examined consensus and satisfaction levels both within and between cultures by exposing groups to three experimental study treatments: GSS-Aonymous, GSS-Identified, and Manual-Identified.

## Background and GSS Research

IT LITERATURE HAS HISTORICALLY BEEN DIRECTED AT UNDERSTANDING how new information technologies are diffused within and across U.S. firms [31]. Few studies have addressed how firms adopt and utilize new IT within cross-cultural environments [31], and even fewer GSS studies have advanced a theoretical perspective on possible cultural effects [19]. The moderating effect of culture and nationality on the behavior and attitudes of employees in foreign operatives has been well established by many researchers in numerous cross-cultural studies on psychological need perceptions and need satisfactions [2, 12, 16, 23, 24, 26, 27, 38, 39]. For example, in an early comparative study of American managers located in the United States and American managers located in Europe, Ivancevich and Baker [12] found that culture had a significant influence even if the respondents were not of that culture.

Prior cross-cultural research has found pronounced differences between Asiatic and western–English-speaking cultures $[8]$ . For example, while U.S. companies extensively use electronic mail, Japanese firms show a significant preference for FAX $[31]$ . According to Straub $[31]$ , the differences between these contrasting uses of IT may be attributed to cultural preferences. Some researchers believe that even preferences for the “type of analysis” may differ among different cultures $[25]$ . For example, Europeans and Japanese seem to prefer more analytical solutions and normative models, while Americans prefer heuristics and descriptive models $[35]$ .

## Prior Cross-Cultural GSS Research

The majority of empirical research in the GSS literature has adopted an input–output perspective and has compared outcomes from GSS groups with traditional face-to-face or manual groups $[5, 8]$ . Traditional manual interactive techniques such as the Delphi method and the Nominal Group technique have been shown to improve group performance with idea-generation and decision-making tasks $[4, 5, 34]$ . The inclusion of a discussion leader or “facilitator” has been shown to improve the performance of some groups with “intellective” (e.g., local or creative) tasks $[5, 14, 15]$ .

While these manual interactive group techniques have been shown to be useful, however, past research has repeatedly pointed out GSS technology's tremendous potential for improving group performance [33]. Since the exchange of information among participants is the principal activity in group meetings [6], researchers have been interested in whether or not electronic support technology (e.g., GSS) can improve group meeting communication and help make face-to-face groups more productive and efficient. Clearly, if differences in preferences for different modes of

IT across cultures exist, we would expect to see clear differences in the performance of an electronic group support system between different cross-cultural environments. Further, the implementation of a U.S.-designed GSS within another culture is worthy of study, particularly in a country like Mexico, where the success of technology has been shown to affect the proliferation of computing technology in smaller neighboring Latin American counties $[18, 19, 21]$ .

## Hofstede's Model of Cultural Differentiation

For the current field study, we used Hofstede's “model of cultural differentiation” [9, 10, 11] as a theoretical framework to illuminate the potential differences in GSS meeting consensus levels and perceptions that could be attributed to cultural effects. In a field study of over fifty-three countries, which included over 116,000 respondents, Hofstede [9, 10] described a model of “cultural differentiation” that included four dimensions of national culture along which people’s value systems may vary: power distance, uncertainty avoidance, individualistic–collectivistic, and masculinity–femininity. The power-distance dimension describes the relative distance and relationship between a supervisor and a subordinate. Power distance is the extent to which a particular national culture accepts and recognizes the unequal distribution of power (e.g., boss and subordinate) in institutions and organizations. Countries that score high on power distance apparently emphasize autocratic or paternalistic behavior, while countries that score low on power distance tend to favor participative management relations, equal rights, and the use of legitimate rather than coercive power [9, 10].

Uncertainty avoidance is the extent to which members of a particular culture feel uncomfortable or threatened by unknown outcomes. Countries that score high on the uncertainty-avoidance dimension tend to have little tolerance for uncertainty and a greater need for formal rules. Countries with high uncertainty-avoidance scores have less tolerance for people or groups with deviant ideas or behavior and are more likely to resist innovation. Countries with low uncertainty-avoidance scores were inclined to take more risks and were more tolerant of deviant behavior and innovative ideas [9].

The individualistic–collectivistic dimension refers to the relationship between the individual and his or her larger environment. In individualistic cultures, people are more self-reliant and are expected to look after mostly themselves and their immediate families; relationships or links to outside individuals are usually not very strong. Collectivistic cultures tend to have a preference for cohesive and tightly knit social networks. Collectivistic cultures strive to maintain harmony and avoid confrontation or disagreement among group members [35].

The masculinity–femininity dimension describes the relative tradeoff between an assertive environment versus a supportive or nurturing environment. High masculinity (low femininity) scores emphasize power, assertiveness, and individual achievement. Low masculinity (high femininity) scores indicate a greater emphasis on people, quality of life, nurturing, and collective cooperation [35].

## Consensus, Satisfaction, and Participation Equity

Consensus, as defined for this study, refers to general agreement among group members. Specifically, “level of consensus” refers to the degree of agreement generated among group members in the ranking of the group’s main ideas from “most important” to “least important.” “Concordance” is the statistic generated to measure the degree of agreement or consensus within a given group with regard to the ranking of the group’s main ideas or idea categories. Consensus was based on the concept that, although each individual may not have been completely satisfied with his or her group’s overall ranking of ideas, the acceptance of the group’s ranking results, rather than unanimity, was satisfactory.

Satisfaction with decision reflects group members' satisfaction with the group's rankings of the idea categories generated during the session. Numerous U.S. studies found that groups using GSS technology experience more process gains (e.g., satisfaction, synergy, more information, more learning, stimulation) and fewer process losses (e.g., production blocking, domination, apprehension, conformance pressure, coordination problems) than groups using non-GSS technology or manual technology [5, 6, 21, 22, 28, 37]. GSS technology is expected to contribute to the achievement of group and organizational goals by reducing communication barriers (i.e., process losses) associated with power-distance structures and face-to-face discussion [3]. However, there is little theory concerning decision satisfaction within computer-mediated communication environments [7]. Although empirical findings for “satisfaction with decision” have been mixed, the evidence seems to favor increased satisfaction for GSS-supported groups [5, 6, 7].

Participation equity is the perceived opportunity for equal participation among group members in a meeting $[7]$ . The potential effectiveness of GSS has been justified by the argument that electronically supported communication in group meetings promotes equal participation and influence among group members (since all members can type at the same time and thus participate simultaneously). Though several studies found more equal participation in GSS groups than in non-GSS groups $[5, 6, 7, 22, 37]$ , other studies found no differences in participation rates $[6, 13, 36]$ .

## Research Framework and Hypotheses

THE PREVIOUS REVIEW OF THE GSS LITERATURE AND HOFSTEDE'S MODEL of cultural differentiation [9, 10] formed the basis for the following set of hypotheses regarding the behaviors of U.S. and Mexican groups. The field study was divided into two major areas of analysis: analysis of GSS versus non-GSS performance and perceptions within each national culture (hypothesis group A), and comparison of performance and perceptions between U.S. and Mexican groups (hypothesis group B).

## Hypothesis Group A: Analysis within Each Culture

Hypotheses on Consensus Levels (within the United States and Mexico)

On the whole, empirical studies seem to be consistent with findings of lower consensus levels for GSS-assisted groups than for non-GSS or manual groups [8]. The concept of consensus, however, may be interpreted differently within dissimilar cultural settings. In highly “collectivistic” countries such as Singapore, consensus is considered a high priority and essential to national goals $[35]$ . According to Hofstede $[9, 10]$ , Mexicans, like the Singaporans, score high on the collectivistic dimension and may wish to minimize dissent and nonconformity. Economically, Watson et al. $[35]$ state that highly collectivistic host countries may want to be seen as politically and socially stable and therefore attractive to foreign investment. On the other hand, in highly individualistic societies such as the United States, conflict is often expected and consensus is acceptable insofar as it represents a genuine reconciliation of differences $[35]$ .

Clearly, the role of consensus in socially interactive environments like group meetings can be expected to be significantly different for collectivistic (e.g., Mexico) and individualistic (e.g., U.S.) cultures. According to Jarvenpaa et al. [13], the anonymity offered by GSS reduces the level of commitment. The removal of identification and the consequent removal of commitment appear to be important for groups solving certain convergent tasks (e.g., voting, ranking, choosing alternatives) because members are obliged to defend their ideas for consensus formulation [13]. It is predicted, therefore, that GSS groups across both U.S. and Mexican cultures will generate lower consensus levels than non-GSS (manual) groups as the level of identity decreases. Specifically, as the identity level of group participants decreases from Manual-Identified to GSS-Identified to GSS-Anonymous treatments, the level of group consensus will correspondingly decrease. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis A1. The consensus levels generated with regard to the ranking of idea categories will be higher in non-GSS (Manual) groups than in GSS groups within both the U.S. and Mexican national cultures.

In general, the research findings regarding group consensus levels and relative changes in consensus levels appear to be mixed. Several studies assert that GSS technology may actually reduce consensus levels $[6, 8]$ , while others report no change in consensus levels $[36]$ . Only a few $[29, 32]$ report that GSS technology increased group consensus levels. With regard to changes in consensus levels, the role of consensus may be different between highly individualistic countries, such as the United States, and highly collectivistic (or low individualistic) countries such as Mexico $[9, 10, 11]$ . Encouraging a group to reach consensus may be interpreted differently within each society $[8, 35]$ . Clearly, GSS and its supporting technology reduce the traditional social cues found in conventional meetings and may thereby increase social distance among members. This may lead to lower levels of group consensus $[13]$ . We therefore hypothesize:

Hypothesis A2. The change in consensus levels with regard to the ranking of idea categories will be greater in non-GSS groups than in GSS groups within both U.S. and Mexican national cultures.

Hypotheses on Perception Levels (within the United States and Mexico)

U.S.-based studies by George et al. [7], Nunamaker, Applegate, and Konsynski [22], and Steeb and Johnson [28] found that GSS groups were more satisfied with the decision-making process than were non-GSS groups. Additional research studies have shown that GSS enhances group meeting quality by permitting equal opportunity for contributing instantaneous and anonymous communication [6, 7, 13, 28, 32, 33]. The particular communication features of GSS, such as parallel processing, anonymous input, and task structuring, are predicted to have similar positive perceptual effects among Mexican GSS participants as previously experienced by U.S. GSS participants. We predict further that the high uncertainty-avoidance index ascribed to the Mexican national culture (i.e., a need for more information and certainty) [9, 10, 11] could be satisfied by the data-generation and data-retrieval features of GSS technology (i.e., equal, instantaneous, and anonymous input from all group members, group memory) such that Mexican groups supported by GSS technology may also express more satisfaction with their decision than non-GSS or manual groups. Therefore, we hypothesize:

Hypothesis A3. Participants from GSS groups will express more satisfaction with their group's decisions than participants from non-GSS groups within both U.S. and Mexican national cultures.

Hypothesis A4. Participants from GSS groups will perceive more participation equity with their meeting process than participants from non-GSS groups within both U.S. and Mexican national cultures.

## Hypothesis Group B: Analysis between Cultures

## Hypotheses on Consensus Levels: United States versus Mexico

Hofstede [9, 10, 11] characterizes many Latin American countries (e.g., Mexico) as ideological opposites to U.S. culture. U.S. culture is characterized by high individualism and low power distance [10, 11]. Americans tend to see themselves as “I” (versus “we”) and to strive for self-actualization [35]. Mexican culture, on the other hand, is characterized by low individualism and high power distance. Mexicans tend to perceive themselves with a “we” identity and are more concerned with belonging and with the general welfare of the group [9, 10]. Mexican groups, therefore could be predicted to have different views and perceptions of group decision making than American groups. This almost bipolar difference in cultural propensities would lead us to predict that U.S. groups would experience less cultural pressure to reach agreement or consensus, since American society supposedly places a higher value on individualism (see figure 1). Conversely, we would predict that, because of their highly collectivistic propensities, Mexican groups would feel more impelled to seek and generate higher levels of group agreement. This leads to our next hypothesis:

Hypothesis B1. Mexican groups, across all experimental treatments, will generate higher levels of consensus than U.S. groups.

With regard to the changes in consensus levels between the United States and Mexico, Hofstede's model [9, 10] would also lead us to expect Mexican groups to generate a higher level of consensus than U.S. groups in the presence of verbal discussion of their group's rankings and a second opportunity for generating consensus or agreement. Since high-power-distance and highly collectivistic cultures (e.g., Mexico) may be predicted to experience more evaluation apprehension, conformance pressure, and domination during face-to-face exchanges than low-power-distance countries (e.g., the United States), Mexican groups would be expected to generate greater improvement in their collectivistic level of agreement or consensus than U.S. groups. Specifically, Mexican groups would be expected to generate more pressure than U.S. groups to reach a higher level of agreement during the experiment's face-to-face verbal discussion period (immediately preceding the second ranking of idea categories) with potentially less patience or tolerance for dissenting opinions and individualistic thought. In contrast, U.S. groups would be expected to demonstrate more individualistic tendencies during the experiment's face-to-face discussion, with less pressure to reach a higher “collectivistic” level of agreement than Mexican groups. We therefore propose:

Hypothesis B2. The change in consensus levels with regard to the ranking of idea categories will be higher for Mexican groups than for U.S. groups.

Hypotheses on Perception Levels: United States versus Mexico

Since Mexico generates a higher “collectivistic” score than the United States $[9, 10]$ , Mexican group participants would be expected to generate decisions that support the general welfare of the group. Morales et al. $[21]$ found that GSS technology supports this “collectivistic” behavior and evokes a feeling of comfort and satisfaction among Mexican participants regarding the quality of the GSS process and their group’s final decision. A particular information technology such as GSS that supports traditional and collectivistic norms would be expected to generate higher satisfaction scores for Mexican GSS groups than for “individualistic” U.S. groups, who may be less concerned about “collectivistic” group decisions. Consequently, we expect these cultural traits to make Mexican GSS participants more receptive to the group-supporting features of GSS technology, and hypothesize:

Hypothesis B3. Mexican GSS group participants will express more satisfaction with their group's decisions than will U.S. GSS group participants.

According to Steiner [30], communication barriers to equal participation and effective group interaction are often driven by cultural norms. This may allow individuals with higher status to dominate meetings and to intimidate (and therefore inhibit contribution by) members of the group with lower status [13]. In high-power-distance cultures such as Mexico, traditional face-to-face discussion may inhibit frank discussion and generate inequalities with regard to member influence and participation. In contrast, the U.S. culture often enjoys an “individualistic” and egalitarian environment where equal participation is expected and often assumed. A GSS creates a more effective meeting environment by restricting the communication modality between group members and, thus, reduces the power-distance effects when they are dysfunctional [35].

Only a handful of studies, however, have addressed the effect of culture on GSS technology and how it may affect group processes. Morales et al. [21] claim that GSS tend to “level the playing field,” thereby encouraging more equal Mexican participation without the pressing concern for what patrónes (i.e., superiors) are thinking. Moreover, the Morales et al. [31] study supported the Hofstede model to the extent that Mexican participants agreed that individual participation (i.e., “participation equity”) was greater in an anonymous GSS environment than in face-to-face interaction. Since GSS technology is expected to reduce many of the aforementioned power-distance effects found in traditional nonegalitarian cultures such as Mexico [9, 10, 11], Mexicans groups would be expected to perceive more participation equity than U.S. groups, for whom equal participation is the norm. We therefore hypothesize:

Hypothesis B4. Mexican group participants will express more participation equity within their group meeting process than will U.S. group participants.

## Research Methodology

THE RESEARCH STUDY WAS DIVIDED INTO TWO AREAS OF ANALYSIS. The first area investigated the effects of GSS upon group performance and individual perceptions within the Mexican culture and within the U.S. culture. Each national field study, therefore, was a $2 \times 2$ within-subjects factorial design with supporting technology (GSS vs. non-GSS) and identification features (anonymous vs. identified) as the two independent variables.

The second area of analysis was a cross-cultural comparison of the effects of GSS technology upon group consensus levels and perceptions of satisfaction between U.S. and Mexican cultures. This analysis constituted a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ factorial design where supporting technology (GSS vs. non-GSS), identification features (anonymous vs. identified), and national culture (U.S. vs. Mexican) represented the three independent variables. The particular emphasis throughout this study, however, was on national culture and its relative effect on group performance and group member perceptions. Ideally, this $2 \times 2 \times 2$ design would create eight cells. However, because of the perceived artificiality of an “anonymous communication” treatment within the Manual group treatments, two of the cells were not considered appropriate or realistic for experimentation. Specifically, most organizations would not employ an “anonymous” generation of ideas or an “anonymous” discussion of their viewpoints within a traditional face-to-face meeting environment. The removal of these two cells, therefore, left a total of six treatment cells in the final study design.

## Research Sites

Several Mexican research sites were considered for the study. After visits to several potential sites, the Instituto Tecnológico y de Estudios Superióres de Monterrey (ITESM) in Monterrey, Mexico, proved to be the best match for the U.S. site (University of Arizona, Tucson). Criteria for matching sites were similarity of the institutions' academic programs, similarity of the participant sample base, and similarity of hardware and group software used by each site. The use of identical group software was especially critical so that differences recorded within each culture and between cultures could not be attributed to differences in software. The ITESM campus at Monterrey used the identical DOS version of "Ventana GroupSystems" group software used at the University of Arizona.

## Subjects

The U.S. sample consisted of 22 work groups made up of 230 upper-division (juniors and seniors) production management and business administration students. The Mexican sample consisted of 20 work groups from 239 upper-division industrial engineering and business administration students. Both national samples were divided into three experimental study treatments: GSS-Aonymous, GSS-Identified, and Manual-Identified. There were six to seven groups for each of the three treatments from each culture, for a total of 42 groups.

## Task and Field Study Procedures

The field study conducted across both cultures was composed of nine phases for the group participants from each national culture:

1. Explanation of the purpose of the field study and the experimental task to participants (with examples and illustrations);

2. Idea brainstorming by participants for exactly fifteen minutes;

3. Preliminary categorization of brainstormed ideas into logical groupings or "unique idea categories";

4. Review and modification of preliminary idea category list by participants;

5. First ranking of idea category list from most important to least important;

6. Graphic display of group agreement level (concordance statistic) and verbal discussion (ten minutes) of group's first ranking results;

7. Second ranking of idea categories (resubmitted in random order);

8. Discussion of second ranking (five minutes);

9. Completion of post-survey "Perceptions Questionnaire".

The principal researcher, a fifth-generation U.S. citizen of Mexican and Spanish heritage, explained the nature and purpose of the study to the group participants in the native language (i.e., English and Spanish) of each research site. The experimental task was displayed either on an overhead transparency or written on a white/blackboard. Three examples of appropriate ideas or answers to the task question were also presented to the group, who were told that there were really no “right” or “wrong” ideas. Participants were asked to use either their real name (for the GSS-Identified and Manual-Identified treatments) or a pseudonym (for the GSS-Anonymous) in submitting their brainstorming ideas.

Participants were then asked to submit (either manually or electronically) as many ideas or comments as possible for exactly fifteen minutes. During the brainstorming session, facilitators simultaneously generated a preliminary list or logical grouping of ideas into “idea categories.” After the fifteen-minute brainstorming session, group participants from each experimental treatment reviewed the preliminary list of “idea categories” generated by the group facilitators and were asked if the list of categories accurately reflected their ideas and comments. Participants were allowed to add categories to the list (in case the facilitator had omitted some ideas), to combine similar categories, and to correct the wording of the idea categories list.

All participants (GSS and non-GSS) were then sent an electronic ballot with the corrected and “agreed-upon” final list of idea categories generated during the session. Participants were asked to rank each of the idea categories from “most important” to “least important.” All ranking for all experimental treatment groups was done electronically, even for the manual groups. Prior GSS research has demonstrated that the effect of automating a manual voting or ranking process is minimal $[6]$ . After all participants submitted their individual rankings, GSS software generated the group’s first consensus-level measure, or “concordance” statistic. This statistic, along with the relative rankings for each idea category, was displayed on a large screen.

Those particular idea categories with high standard deviations were earmarked for discussion to encourage group members to arrive at a greater level of agreement in their rankings. Group participants were encouraged to argue about why some category should or should not have been ranked as high or low as shown on the screen. After ten minutes of verbal discussion, group members were sent a second ballot with an identical list of the idea categories (now in random order) and asked a second time to rank, in order of importance, the list of idea categories based on the preceding verbal group discussion. After the second ranking, the group looked at a new electronically generated concordance statistic. After a five-minute discussion of the results, each group member was asked to fill out a sixty-three-item Likert scale post-test questionnaire (GSS Group Perceptions Questionnaire) that asked each participant to rate his or her satisfaction with the group process and his or her perceptions of various other group process gains and losses.

## Spanish Translation of Questionnaires

A double translation process was used to translate the post-test questionnaire from English to Spanish and then back to English. The purpose of this double translation and verification procedure was to insure that the Spanish version of the post-test questionnaires represented the intent and spirit of each item in the questionnaire and was not a mere literal translation. The first English-to-Spanish translation of the post-test questionnaire was done at the University of Arizona by a native-born Mexican citizen with a master's degree in bilingual education. A second English-to-Spanish translation was also done at the Mexican research site in Monterrey, Mexico, by two native-speaking GSS group facilitators with master's degrees in engineering. The two English-to-Spanish versions were compared by the principal researcher; the two translations were nearly identical, except for stylistic differences. Both translations questionnaire were then back-translated from Spanish to English for final comparison and verification.

## Dependent Variables and Measures

This research focused on four dependent variables:

\- Consensus level, or the degree of agreement among group members regarding the ranking of the idea categories generated by the group.

\- Change in consensus level, or the relative degree of change in the level of agreement from the group's first ranking to the second ranking of categories.

\- Satisfaction with the group's decision, or the perceived satisfaction of the group members with the group's final rankings of the idea categories.

\- Participation equity, or the perceived opportunity for equal participation among group members in the group exercise.

## Levels of Significance

A significance level of 0.05 was used for testing our hypotheses. However, Javenpaa et al. [13] report that, because there are so few studies examining the effect of computer support on group meetings (particularly, cross-cultural GSS field studies [19]), many research studies of this nature may be considered “exploratory.” They therefore recommend a more liberal significance level of 0.10 in testing the null hypotheses so that any effect would be likely to be noticed (or equally, unlikely to go unnoticed). We adhere, however, to the 0.05 standard, though we also identify and briefly discuss those research findings that fall between 0.10 and 0.05 in the interest of encouraging further exploratory studies into cross-cultural applications of GSS.

## Field Study Results

THE RESULTS FROM THE FIELD STUDY WERE GROUPED INTO TWO SECTIONS: the analysis within each national culture (hypothesis group A) and the analysis between national cultures (hypothesis group B). Both analyses used a significance level of 0.05 for hypothesis testing. These results are summarized in Tables 1 through 4.

For the analysis within each national culture, we used single-factor ANOVAs and t-tests for planned comparisons. A single-factor ANOVA helped to determine if, within each culture, observed differences between the means of the dependent variables under each of the three experimental treatments levels (GSS-Anonymous, GSS-Identified, Manual-Identified) were chance occurrences or true systematic effects (i.e., experimental treatment). If significant F-ratios were found, we ran Tukey and Sheffé post-hoc tests. The t-test was used to determine the significance of the change in consensus level from the group's first ranking of categories to its second ranking.

For the analysis between national cultures, we used two-factor ANOVAs and t-tests for planned contrasts. The two-factor ANOVA was used to test for main effects (i.e., culture and experimental treatment) and any possible interaction effects. The t-tests were used for a priori comparisons between cultures under each of the three experimental treatments for each of the dependent variables. Comparisons within each culture and between cultures were well-considered planned comparisons and planned contrasts based upon previous cross-cultural research, Hofstede's theoretical model of cultural differentiation, and previous GSS laboratory and field literature.

## Analysis between Cultures

## Consensus Levels

While t-test planned contrasts revealed no significant differences in consensus levels between the United States and Mexico under each of the three experimental treatments, a two-factor ANOVA revealed main effects for both group rankings. For the first ranking, the independent variable experimental treatment, generated a main effect $F=4.12, p<0.03$ . Specifically, higher consensus levels were recorded in the groups' first ranking of categories by manual groups, regardless of the cultural factor effect. For the second ranking, the independent factor national culture generated a main effect, but this was only significant at the p<0.09 level $F=3.02$ . This suggests that higher consensus levels for the groups' second ranking were generated by Mexican culture, regardless of the experimental treatment factor effect. There were no differences between U.S. and Mexican groups with regard to changes in consensus levels across all experimental treatments (see Table 3).

## Satisfaction and Participation Equity

The field study reported highly significant differences between U.S. and Mexican groups with regard to satisfaction and participation equity perception levels. Mexican groups for all experimental treatments recorded higher levels of satisfaction than U.S. groups (p < 0.001 for all three experimental treatments). A two-factor ANOVA revealed that these differences were due to the main effects of culture (F = 132.77, p < 0.001) and not to differences in experimental treatments (see Table 4). However, of consideration was the presence of an interactive effect between the culture factor and experimental treatment factor at the p < 0.06 level (F = 2.80). Although not significant at the p < 0.05 level, these levels may be an indication that satisfaction scores were influenced by both culture and the identity level of the experimental treatments. For both cultures, the “GSS-Identified” experimental treatment improved the Mexican scores while decreasing the U.S. scores on satisfaction by approximately the same percentage (5–8 percent).

Mexican groups perceived higher levels of participation equity across all experimental treatments; these differences were significant for the GSS-supported groups (GSS-Anonymous, t-4.27, p < 0.001; GSS-Identified t = 3.95, p < 0.002). Of interest was that Mexican manual groups also generated higher levels of perceived participation than did U.S. manual groups, but differences were not significant at the p < 0.05 level. A two-factor ANOVA revealed that differences in perceived participation equity were due to the main effects of culture (F = 26.64, p < 0.001) and not to differences in experimental treatments.

## Discussion

Hypothesis Group A: Analysis within Cultures

## Consensus Levels

CONTRARY TO OUR PREDICTIONS, MANUAL GROUPS DID NOT GENERATE higher consensus levels than GSS-supported groups in either the U.S. or Mexican national culture samples (Table 5). Although it appeared within the U.S. sample that identified treatment groups (i.e., GSS-Identified and Manual-Identified) generated higher consensus levels than the anonymous treatment group (i.e., GSS-Anonymous), these results were not significant at the p < 0.05 level. A similar pattern was observed within the Mexican sample. Although the Mexican sample results were only significant at the p < 0.06 level (F = 3.25), this suggest that as the identity level of group members within each experimental treatment was increased (i.e., from Electronic-Anonymous to Electronic-Identified to Manual-Identified), that consensus levels also increased, though not proportionately. This finding partially supports Jarvenpaa et al.'s earlier findings [13] that the anonymity feature of GSS may actually reduce the commitment level of group members toward their input, thereby reducing the motivation for consensus.

Within the Mexican sample, differences in consensus levels between GSS and manual groups seem to be more pronounced than in U.S. groups, though this difference was significant only at p < 0.06 (F = 3.25). This difference could be partially explained by the high-power-distance and highly collectivistic nature of the Mexican culture [9, 10]. Specifically, high-power-distance cultures may experience more group process losses (evaluation apprehension, conformance pressure, domination) within traditional face-to-face meetings than within GSS environments. Conditioned by collectivistic values, Mexican manual group environments may generate more face-to-face pressure to reach a higher level of agreement with less tolerance for dissenting opinions and individualistic thought than GSS group environments. In contrast, face-to-face U.S. groups may demonstrate more individualistic tendencies with more emphasis on individual expression and less pressure to reach agreement [9, 10, 11, 19].

Contrary to our predictions, GSS-supported groups within both the U.S. and Mexican cultures generated greater changes in consensus levels than manual groups across all experimental treatments (see Table 1 t-scores). Ironically, the smallest changes in consensus levels across both cultures were generated by manual groups. Since GSS environments minimize the social and interactive cues often used in face-to-face meetings, we expected changes in consensus levels within GSS environments to be lower. That is, dominant individuals within GSS environments would have less opportunity and influence to sway group members to reconsider their ranking preferences (e.g., during the second ranking) and generate higher subsequent consensus levels. However, these field study results clearly indicate that greater changes in consensus levels within both cultures were generated by GSS-supported groups and not by manual groups as predicted by the literature. It was of interest that although consensus-level changes for Mexican Manual-Identified groups approached only the p < 0.06 level, the larger relative change when compared with U.S. group consensus levels may reflect a collectivistic Mexican trait of assigning greater importance to reaching higher levels of agreement than U.S. groups.

## Satisfaction and Participation Equity

Because GSS technology was expected to encourage a more candid and less evaluative meeting environment, via the reduction of traditional communication barriers, it was predicted that GSS participants across both U.S. and Mexican cultures would perceive more satisfaction and participation equity. Within the U.S. sample, however, no differences in “satisfaction with the group’s decision” and “perceived participation equity” between GSS and manual groups were reported. On the other hand, Mexican GSS-Identified groups reported more satisfaction (t = 3.38, p < 0.04) and perceived more participation equity than manual groups, although these differences were only significant at the p < 0.10 level (see Table 2).

These results only partially support our predictions with regard to the Mexican sample and the cultural propensities proposed by Hofstede's model [10]. Participants from individualistic and low-power-distance cultures such as the United States may take equal participation in groups settings for granted and, subsequently, may not perceive higher satisfaction levels within GSS environments. Conversely, the egalitarian features of GSS may have generated a greater relative effect on participants from collectivistic and high-power-distance cultures such as Mexico who may not normally operate in equal and participative environments. GSS technology may have encouraged Mexican GSS participants to participate and exert themselves more often than previously possibly, potentially reducing the high-power-distance effects of evaluation apprehension, dominance, and superior-subordinate intimidation traditionally manifested in face-to-face meeting environments. Subsequently, Mexican GSS participants may have expressed more relative satisfaction with their group's decision and genuinely perceived higher levels of "participation equity." This observation is partially supported by Sheffé and Tukey post-hoc tests, which revealed that the greatest differences in satisfaction and perceived participation equity scores were between Mexican GSS-Identified and Mexican Manual-Identified treatments (see Table 2).

An additional reason why groups within the United States may not have generated higher satisfaction and perceived participation scores with GSS environments may be due to the nature of the study task itself. Although the sparse U.S.-based research on satisfaction and perception levels reports increased satisfaction for GSS-supported groups $[7]$ , the nature of the tasks in the aforementioned studies differed from the task used in the present cross-cultural field study. Specifically, U.S. subjects in many of the studies cited by George et al. $[7]$ were involved in planning tasks, where groups did not have to resolve conflict to reach consensus. This may partially explain why non-conflict-resolving groups in prior GSS research recorded more satisfaction with GSS technology than did the groups in the current study, who used GSS for conflict or priority resolution (i.e., ranking of various ideas in order of importance).

## Satisfaction and Participation Equity

As predicted, Mexican group participants expressed both significantly higher levels of satisfaction with their group's decision $p < 0.001$ and greater perceptions of participation equity $p < 0.001$ than did U.S. groups across five of the six planned contrasts of experimental treatments. Though Mexican groups within the Manual-Identified treatment also indicated higher perceptions of participation equity than U.S. groups, these findings were not significant at p < 0.05 $p < 0.28, t = -1.09$ . The communication and group-supporting features of GSS may have been compatible with Mexico's collectivistic and low-power-distance tendencies. Also, the Mexican culture's high uncertainty-avoidance propensity (i.e., need for more information and certainty) [9, 10] may also have been satisfied by the data-generation and -retrieval features of GSS technology such that Mexican GSS groups would express more satisfaction (because of less uncertainty) than their U.S. counterparts.

Since Mexicans come from a traditionally nonegalitarian culture $[9, 10]$ , the Mexican groups also may have perceived more participation equity via GSS technology than they are accustomed to. In addition, these significant differences may also be due to the fact that Americans often experience more relative participation and often take it for granted. The principal investigator observed that Mexican participants tended to be more expressive (both verbally and electronically) than their U.S. counterparts and that they commented more often on the opinions of other group participants than did the U.S. participants. Clearly, traditional face-to-face discussion of ideas transmits social and contextual information through eye contact, facial expressions, inflection, and other verbal and paraverbal cues that cannot be transmitted with computer-mediated communication $[3]$ . In high-power-distance cultures such as Mexico, face-to-face discussion may have traditionally inhibited frank discussion and caused inequalities with regard to member influence and participation. As previously stated, GSS environments tend to “level the playing field” by allowing more equal Mexican participation without the pressing concern for what superiors, or patrones, are thinking $[21]$ . In a survey by Morales et al. $[21]$ , 96 percent of the Mexican GSS respondents were satisfied with the quality of their group’s results and felt that they were able to express themselves fully in a GSS environments.

## Limitations

The first limitation of this field study has to do with the considerable reliance upon Hofstede's model of cultural differentiation for predicting the behavior of groups and participants in each national culture. Hofstede's model has been criticized because it is based on a study of 116,000 respondents taken exclusively from one multinational company (i.e., IBM). Indeed, whether the personnel of a single multinational corporation are representative of a particular culture may be questionable. (As a former IBM engineer, the principal researcher appreciates this critique, since much has been written about the "IBM corporate culture"). Since Hofstede's theory was based on "organizational communication," some may argue that his cultural differentiation theory may not be applicable to interpersonal or group communication research. However, as Hofstede's model has often been used to explain interpersonal phenomena and observed cross-cultural differences in interpersonal communication [8, 18, 19, 21, 35], we felt Hofstede's study was particularly relevant. It also included cultural variability scores from other Latin American countries such as Argentina, Brazil, and Chile, which provided further bases of comparison with U.S. culture. A final criticism is that, since Hofstede's data were collected in the 1970s, there may have been significant changes in either the U.S. or Mexican national cultures in the intervening years. Hofstede [10, 11] argues that culture is quite stable over long periods of time and requires sharp or drastic discontinuity (e.g., a military conquest by another culture) to precipitate significant shifts in norms and values [35].

A second limitation of our study has to do with the general state of the Mexican economy during the time period when the experiments were undertaken. Between May 1994 and June 1995, the Mexican peso suffered a severe devaluation in the world currency market, decreasing in value from N\$ 3.00 “nuevos pesos” per U.S. dollar to approximately N\$ 6.25 per U.S. dollar, a devaluation of approximately 108 percent. As a manifestation of a nationalistic need to trabajar juntos por el bien de la republica Mexicana (“work together for the good of the Mexican republic”), the Mexican participants in our study may have been affected by the state of the economy, possibly further activating their “collectivistic cultural propensity” [9, 10] to work together and come to even higher levels of agreement (relative to the U.S. groups) than they might normally reach. Future cross-cultural research studies must, therefore, consider the current socioeconomic conditions of the particular country under study in interpreting their final cross-cultural comparisons.

Finally, because of the particular cross-cultural design of this field study, the researchers acknowledge that many t-tests were undertaken that compared results within cultures (e.g., the United States and Mexico) and contrasted results between cultures (U.S. versus Mexico), thus increasing the possibility of Type-I errors (rejecting the null hypothesis when in fact it is true.) However, the researchers believed that test-wise Type-I error rates were somewhat controlled as these tests were a priori, or planned, comparisons and contrasts based on previous cross-cultural literature, Hofstede's model of cultural differentiation, and numerous GSS empirical studies prior to undertaking any statistical testing. In addition, the a priori planned comparisons and contrasts tests undertaken within the U.S. and Mexican samples were independent of each other and did not utilize indiscriminate testing or "fishing expeditions."

## Implications and Conclusion

THIS CROSS-CULTURAL FIELD STUDY GENERATED SEVERAL IMPORTANT IMPLICATIONS and conclusions. First, groups, like organizations, use technology to accomplish certain objectives and are strongly fashioned by the surrounding cultural norms $[31, 35]$ . This echoes the earlier findings of Attewell and Rule $[1]$ that the impact of (organizational) culture greatly influences technological effects. The groups in our study possessed certain norms shaped by the history, tradition, and cultural propensities of the national cultures of the United States and Mexico. As shown by our study results, culture may be correlated with certain group consensus outcomes and may affect certain of the group members' perception levels. Clearly, the ability of Mexican groups to generate higher satisfaction and participation equity scores than U.S. groups across nearly all experimental treatments may be strongly correlated with cultural effects. Conversely, even though culture may be somewhat resistant to change, technology can be an overwhelming force under certain sociological and economic conditions $[35]$ .

Second, the design and implementation of GSS technology by cultures other than where it was designed must be sensitive to the particular norms of the host country, especially with regard to social protocols and group meeting traditions. The recent introduction of GSS technology into Mexican business operations represents a new mode of interaction for a traditional culture where collectivistic thought and power distances between superior and subordinate have been perpetuated and respected for centuries. The ability of GSS technology to mitigate age-old power-distance meeting protocols and to increase the constructive exchange of information must be analyzed with cautious enthusiasm. Electronically supported meeting technology and GSS must be viewed by the host country as a facilitating tool to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of group meetings, not as a faddish intrusion that seeks to destroy traditional social and group norms $[17]$ . Therefore, the implementation of U.S.-designed IT in Mexican applications proves interesting because its success in Mexico has been shown to affect the downstream proliferation of computing technology in smaller neighboring Latin American countries $[18, 19, 21]$ .

Finally, as we hope this cross-cultural study has pointed out, although different cultures possess different social settings and meeting protocols, the common feature of problem-solving groups remains the exchange of information $[35]$ . An effective GSS application must be flexible so that its assortment of information-exchanging tools can be rearranged to operate within different cultural environments $[35]$ . For example, the anonymity feature of GSS within Mexican meeting environments may be appropriate for idea brainstorming and ranking of various alternatives, but should not be utilized when the group is discussing the relative merits of various alternatives prior to group voting or ranking. A GSS technology with restrictive communication features may impose a culturally determined model (e.g., North American) that is inappropriate for certain cultures $[35]$ .

Within today's global market environment, cross-cultural studies of GSS technology are particularly relevant as international and multicultural project work teams negotiate with each other for limited resources and the exchange of information to maintain their competitive advantage. While the strategic importance of information technology has steadily increased for both developed and underdeveloped nations, the conspicuous and surprising scarcity of cross-cultural studies in the area of GSS technology has been an unfortunate shortcoming of current IT research. This certainly proved to be a motivating factor for the current study. It is hoped that the results of this cross-cultural study will provide some direction and encouragement for researchers and organizations endeavoring to determine the effects of GSS technology, particularly as it is implemented and utilized by other cultures.
