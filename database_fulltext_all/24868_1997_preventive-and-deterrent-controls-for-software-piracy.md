# Preventive and Deterrent Controls for Software Piracy

RAM D. GOPAL AND G. LAWRENCE SANDERS

RAM D. GOPAL is an Assistant Professor of Operations and Information Management in the School of Business of the University of Connecticut. His current research interests are in database design and security, economics and ethical issues relating to software piracy, organizational impacts of information technology, and multimedia applications. Dr. Gopal's work has been published in journals including Decision Support Systems, ORSA Journal on Computing, IEEE Transactions on Knowledge and Data Engineering, and Annals of Operations Research.

G. LAWRENCE SANDERS is an Associate Professor of Management Science and Systems in the School of Management at the State University of New York at Buffalo. He has also taught and lectured three summers in the People's Republic of China. He received his Ph.D. from Texas Tech University on 1983. Dr. Sanders's current research interests are in the ethics and economics of software piracy, systems success measurement, strategic database design, and systems development. He has published papers in journals including Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, Journal of Management Systems, Database Programming and Design, and Decision Sciences. He is coeditor of a book on strategic information systems and author of a recent book on data modeling.

ABSTRACT: In an attempt to protect their intellectual property and compete effectively in an increasingly dynamic marketplace, software publishers have employed a number of preventive and deterrent controls to counter software piracy. Conventional wisdom suggests that reducing piracy will force consumers to acquire software legitimately, thus increasing firm profits. We develop an analytical model to test the implications of antipiracy measures on publisher profits. Our results suggest that preventive controls decrease profits and deterrent controls can potentially increase profits. Empirical results are also presented that support the proposition on the impact of deterrent controls on the extent of software piracy derived from the analytical model.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: computer ethics, economics of software, information systems controls, software piracy, software pricing.

THE EFFECT OF PIRACY ON FIRM PERFORMANCE is a central economic question facing the software publishing industry. It is estimated that the unauthorized copying of software by American businesses alone deprives software publishers of \$1.6 billion per year [37]. Worldwide estimates on the annual dollar amount of software pirated range from \$7.5 billion [13] to \$17 billion [7]. Software piracy has been identified as the worst problem facing the software industry [15]. Because software has the characteristics of a public good where the consumption utility is not reduced by sharing with others, the typical market dynamics of exclusivity do not come into play. Therefore, those who do not pay directly for a public good can still consume it and receive the accompanying benefits. Software is very much like art displayed in the town square, the national defense system, or a public park, even though it is classified as a private good and is produced for commercial purposes.

In the face of an increasingly competitive marketplace, software publishers have employed antipiracy technologies and legal and educational campaigns as a way to protect their intellectual property. The objective of these strategies is to reduce the size of software piracy clubs. The effect of these strategies on reducing the size of software piracy clubs and the ensuing effect on the economic performance of software publishers are the subject of this paper.

## Strategies to Counter Piracy

THE CRIMINOLOGY LITERATURE IDENTIFIES PREVENTIVES AND DETERRENTS as two measures that can be used to combat crime [4]. Preventives impede criminal activities by forcing the perpetrator to expend and deplete resources in the pursuit of a goal. The idea is to dissuade criminal activity by wearing the perpetrator down. On the other hand, deterrents attempt to hinder individuals from committing crimes through the threat of legal sanctions. The objective of undertaking deterrent controls is to prevent intent from becoming reality. Deterrence theory posits that individuals are deterred from criminal activity only if they perceive legal sanctions as being certain, swift, and/or severe [35]. Ehrlich [11] developed an economic theory of criminal activity where the decision to commit a crime is a rational choice involving the optimal allocation of resources. Thus, the opportunities and rewards can be modeled in terms of the costs and gains of legitimate and illegitimate activity and that incentives (deterrents) can be used to counter criminal offenses.

Straub [32] identifies various preventive controls and deterrent controls for combating computer hardware abuse. Preventive controls include using security software to impede unwarranted access and designing physically secure facilities. Typical deterrent controls include guidelines and policy statements on legitimate use, security briefings, and audits. Similar preventive and deterrent strategies have also been employed by software publishers to counter piracy.

Preventive controls use technology to increase the costs of engaging in acts of piracy. Such controls, often referred to as front-end strategies, are usually undertaken through hardware- or software-based copy protection schemes $[34]$ . Examples of hardware-based protection methods include nonstandard disks, Coder cards, and hardware locks $[20]$ . Software-based protection methods are employed by embedding special codes in the software that make copying more difficult for the users $[20]$ . Additional preventive strategies include providing customer support only to registered users and having documentation that is difficult to duplicate. The objective of preventive controls is to increase the costs associated with using pirated software.

Deterrent controls, in contrast to preventive controls, do not directly increase the cost of pirating software. Deterrence is achieved if an individual avoids criminal behavior out of the perceived threat or fear of the inherent elements of sanctions. Deterrent controls, often referred to as back-end strategies, attempt to dissuade users from copying software by disseminating litigious information about software piracy to the general public. In addition to distributing information concerning the illegality of software pirating, software companies also distribute information on the effect of piracy on new software development. These measures are usually employed through educational, investigative, and legal campaigns $[14]$ . Educational campaigns attempt to teach users about copyright laws and to inspire attitude changes about appropriate software copying behavior $[23]$ .

The Software Publishers Association (SPA), a commercial software watchdog group representing 565 software publishers, has taken to using billboard advertising to get the message across $[16]$ , but it has also taken legal action against individuals and organizations who exhibit flagrant disregard for copyright law. For example, the SPA often conducts unannounced software audits of firms and files lawsuits against overt offenders of the law $[19]$ . The intention of the informational, investigative, and legal campaigns is to draw attention to the fact that everyone has legal, ethical, and social responsibilities related to piracy behavior.

Most studies on criminal behavior focus their attention on the impact of preventives and deterrents on reducing crime. Consequently, the dependent variable of interest is typically either the aggregate crime rate [e.g., 11] or individual crime behavior [18]. In this research, we study the impact of preventives and deterrents on software publisher profits. We show that, even when both preventive and deterrent controls can effectively reduce piracy, only deterrents have a positive impact on profits.

The following develops and analyzes an economic model to determine the influence of preventive and deterrent strategies for reducing piracy on the profitability of software publishers. Buchanan's [6] economic theory of clubs, where economic actors engage in consumption ownership arrangements, provides the theoretical foundation for modeling software piracy behavior.

## Model Development

THIS DISCUSSION DESCRIBES THE MATHEMATICAL FOUNDATIONS of an economic model for determining the effect of software protection strategies on software piracy and firm performance. The model makes the following assumptions:

1. Software publishers attempt to maximize profits.

2. The demand for software is linear and downward-sloping. (The linear specification for the demand is used for analytical tractability. In appendix A, we show that the key results of our analysis also hold true for a general demand function.)

3. The marginal production costs are negligible. (As noted by Conner and Rumelt [9], the marginal production costs are typically a small percentage of the total production costs for software, since they only involve the costs for duplicating a CD-ROM disk or floppy diskette and printing the documentation.)

4. The utility a consumer derives from using the software does not depend on whether the software is an original or a pirated copy.

In reference to the last assumption, we assume that an individual's consumption utility depends on the features offered by the software and is unaffected by whether it is an original or a pirated copy. However, individuals who pirate software face additional costs owing to the lack of adequate documentation and technical support from the vendors, the amount of effort expended to defeat the hardware and software protection schemes, and the likelihood of sanctions if caught.

Let $P_{market}$ denote the market price of the software. The software publisher employs a variety of preventive and deterrent measures to combat piracy. The extent of preventive and deterrent controls are indexed through the variables $\rho$ and $\delta$ , respectively. The publisher incurs a fixed cost F of developing and marketing the software and undertaking preventive and deterrent controls. The degree to which preventive and deterrent controls are applied increases the fixed cost, and thus:

$$
\frac {\partial F}{\partial \rho} > 0 \text { and } \frac {\partial F}{\partial \delta} > 0.\tag{1}
$$

Consumers can, of course, exploit the unique characteristic of software and reproduce the software in an attempt to acquire the software at a price lower than $P_{market}$ . This mechanism involves a group of individuals who purchase a copy of the software at the market price and make copies for all group members. The resulting economic group has the characteristics of what has been described as a “private goods club” [6, 21, 31]. The incentive for club formation lies in the decreasing cost function affiliated with acquiring the software. One can visualize a club consisting of a group of individuals who purchase a software package and make copies of the software and relevant documentation for the club members. Or a club need not be so formal. An individual may give a copy of the software to a friend or a business associate with the implicit agreement that the friend or business associate reciprocate the transaction in the future with some other software.

Consider a club of n individuals. In addition to sharing the price of software, each individual in the club faces additional costs, in part because of the preventive and deterrent controls imposed by the publisher. The first element of the cost is incurred in making a copy of the software and the relevant documentation and foregoing the benefits offered to legitimate buyers of the software. Let $C_{p}$ denote this cost to each individual. $^{1}$ We assume that the publisher can increase this cost through preventive controls. Therefore, $\partial C_{p} / \partial \rho > 0$ . Besides the cost of copying the software, individuals also face additional costs because of the likelihood of sanctions as a result of engaging in illegal activity. We assume that the level of deterrent controls employed by the publisher, and the size of the club influence the cost to an individual. The assumption related to the size of the club stems from the observation that larger economic clubs are more easily detected [2]. In fact, much of the Software Publishers

Association's success in combating piracy has been with cases involving widespread, almost flagrant, abuses of the software copyright law [17]. In this paper we model the cost to an individual due to the threat of legal sanctions as $C_{\delta}(n - 1)$ , where $C_{\delta}$ is the effectiveness of deterrent controls imposed by the publisher and it satisfies the condition $\partial C_{\delta} / \partial \delta > 0$ .

Let $P_{paid}$ be the total cost to an individual in the club. From the above discussion, we have: $^{2}$

$$
P _ {p a i d} = \frac {P _ {\text { market }}}{n} + C _ {\rho} + C _ {\delta} (n - 1).\tag{2}
$$

Individuals are strict value maximizers. Accordingly, individuals form clubs that minimize the total cost to each individual. Minimizing equation (2) with respect to n yields:

$$
\eta = \sqrt {\frac {P _ {m a r k e t}}{C _ {\delta}}}\tag{3}
$$

where $\eta$ is the optimal club size, given the market price and the levels of preventive and deterrent controls. Note that $\eta$ represents the number of copies of software in use for each copy of the software legitimately bought from the publisher. Thus, it can be construed as the “extent of piracy.” Note that in certain instances factors such as fewer number of software users in a community and smaller organizations may limit the maximum size of the clubs that can be achieved. An assumption in equation (3) is that the optimal club size is attainable and is unrestricted by such size limiting factors. Proposition 1 establishes the impact of deterrent controls on the extent of piracy; it follows from equation (3) and the condition $\partial C_{\delta} / \partial \delta > 0$ :

Proposition 1: The publisher can reduce the extent of piracy (club size) through deterrent controls.

The proposition is empirically validated later in the paper. Interestingly, preventive controls do not influence the extent of piracy. However, preventive controls, in addition to deterrent controls, can be employed in an effort to eliminate piracy. This follows from the fact that software club will cease to exist if $P_{paid} \geq P_{market}$ .

Proposition 2: The publisher can eliminate piracy through preventive or deterrent controls.

Proof: From equations (2) and (3), it follows that

$$
P _ {p a i d} = C _ {\delta} \left(2 \sqrt {\frac {P _ {m a r k e t}}{C _ {\delta}}} - 1\right) + C _ {\rho}.\tag{4}
$$

The software club, and hence software piracy will not exist if $P_{paid} \geq P_{marker}$ . For the proposition to hold true, we need only show that $P_{paid}$ increases monotonically with $\rho$ and $\delta$ . From equation (4) and the conditions $\partial C_{p} / \partial \rho > 0$ and $\partial C_{\delta} / \partial \delta > 0$ , we can show that $\partial P_{paid} / \partial \rho > 0$ and $\partial P_{paid} / \partial \delta > 0$ . QED.

The above properties suggest differing behavior mechanisms of clubs in response to preventive and deterrent controls. In the face of increased preventive controls, individuals in the club pay a higher price for acquiring the software but do not alter the club size. On the other hand, deterrent controls force individuals to form smaller clubs, which increases the cost of software acquisition for each individual.

While either preventive or deterrent controls can effectively combat piracy, from the software publisher's perspective, their effectiveness is ultimately determined by their impact on profits. Let the demand $Q$ for software be

$$
Q = z - \alpha P _ {p a i d},\tag{5}
$$

where $\alpha$ is the reduction in the demand per unit increase in price paid by the users and z is the zero-price demand. The total number of originals sold by the software publisher $Q_{s}$ is $Q/\eta$ . The publisher's revenue, R, is $Q_{s}P_{market}$ and the profit, $\Pi$ , is R - F. The publisher's problem is to set the market price and the levels of preventive and deterrent controls that maximize the profit. Our analysis yields boundary conditions for the optimal values of preventive and deterrent controls. Consequently, our initial analysis focuses on the optimization only with respect to market price. This is followed by a discussion of the optimal levels of preventive and deterrent controls. Profit maximization yields:

(6)

$$
P _ {\text { market }} ^ {*} = \frac {[ z + \alpha (C _ {\delta} - C _ {\rho}) ] ^ {2}}{1 6 \alpha^ {2} C _ {\delta}}\tag{7}
$$

$$
\eta^ {*} = \frac {[ z + \alpha (C _ {\delta} - C _ {\rho}) ]}{4 \alpha^ {2} C _ {\delta}}\tag{8}
$$

$$
\Pi^ {*} = \frac {\left[ z + \alpha \left(C _ {\delta} - C _ {\rho}\right) \right] ^ {2}}{8 \alpha} - F
$$

where $P^{*}$ is the profit maximizing price, and $\eta^{*}$ and $\Pi^{*}$ are the corresponding club size and profit. The following propositions outline the comparative static analysis on the effects of preventive and deterrent controls:

Proposition 3: Increases in preventive and deterrent controls reduce the market price of the software.

Proof: We have

$$
\frac {\partial P _ {m a r k e t} ^ {*}}{\partial \rho} = \frac {\partial P _ {m a r k e t} ^ {*}}{\partial C _ {\rho}} \times \frac {\partial C _ {\rho}}{\partial \rho}.
$$

Since $\partial P_{\text{market}} / \partial C_p < 0$ (from equations 6 and 7) and $\partial C_p / \partial \rho >$ , it follows that $\partial P^{*}_{\text{market}} / \partial \rho < 0$ . Similar analysis with respect to deterrent controls yields the stated result. QED.

Proposition 3 highlights the price effects of preventive and deterrent controls. Some industry analysts argue that stricter enforcement of antipiracy programs increases the costs incurred by the publisher, which ultimately leads to a higher price for the software. Our result suggests that preventive and deterrent controls do not translate to a higher market price for the software. The publishers set higher prices in the face of widespread software piracy in order to compensate for revenues lost to pirates. As antipiracy preventive and deterrent controls effectively curb piracy, the publishers can then lower the market price. Thus, price and preventive/deterrent controls constitute substitute strategies.

Proposition 4: Increases in preventive controls reduce publisher revenues and increases in deterrent controls increase publisher revenues.

Proof: Using $R^{*} = \Pi^{*} - F$ and equations (7) and (8), it follows that $\partial R^{*} / \partial \rho < 0$ and $\partial R^{*} / \partial \delta > 0$ . QED.

As $\partial F / \partial \rho > 0$ , the publisher's profit decreases monotonically with preventive controls. Thus, a profit-maximizing publisher should not undertake any preventive controls. In determining the optimal level of deterrent controls, the first-order condition is

$$
\frac {\partial R ^ {*}}{\partial \delta} - \frac {\partial F}{\partial \delta} = 0.
$$

Thus, the publisher should set deterrent controls at levels where “the marginal revenue from deterrent controls equals the marginal cost of implementing the deterrent controls.”

The above proposition can be explained as follows: Note that the total cost to an individual to acquire the software consists of (a) the cost to share the price of software and (b) the cost associated with the antipiracy controls implemented by the publisher. Of the two, only the cost to share the price of software results in revenues to the publisher. Thus, the price paid by each consumer to the publisher is $P^{*}_{market} / \eta$ , and this can be equivalently expressed as $[z + \alpha (C_{\delta} - C\rho)] / 4\alpha$ . Clearly the publisher cannot appropriate a higher price from each individual through increases in preventive controls, and thus the negative effect on profit. However, through deterrent controls, the software publisher can appropriate a higher price from each user. Intuitively, in the face of increasing preventive controls, individuals do not alter the club size (proposition 1). If the preventive controls employed by the publishers are too high, the individuals simply drop out of the club. Such reaction from individuals does not translate to increased revenues for the publisher. On the other hand, individuals react to deterrent controls by forming smaller clubs. This translates into a larger number of clubs and thus higher revenues to the publisher. The net effect of deterrent controls is positive if (a) they effectively reduce club size and (b) the fixed costs of implementing the deterrent controls are not prohibitively high.

## Discussion

THE MODEL ARTICULATED IN THE PREVIOUS SECTION PRESENTS two interesting results. The first is that price and preventive/deterrent controls are substitute strategies.

The second result is that, while preventive and deterrent controls can both effectively eliminate piracy, preventive controls have a negative effect on profits while deterrent controls can potentially increase profits if they are effective in curtailing club formation and the associated fixed costs are low.

There is some anecdotal evidence supporting these results. Around 1985, a number of software companies started removing copy-protection devices from their software [1]. At the same time, the software publishers began active educational and legislative campaigns [19]. Further anecdotal evidence for the applicability of the model is the software companies' practice of selling both a copy-protected and an unprotected version of the same software [8]. The unprotected version sells at a higher price than the protected version.

We now consider the social-welfare implications of antipiracy controls, where social welfare is defined as the sum of producer and consumer surplus. The consumer profits are given by:

$$
\int_ {P _ {p a i d} ^ {*}} ^ {z / \alpha} \alpha \left(P - P _ {p a i d} ^ {*}\right) \partial P.
$$

Upon simplification, the social-welfare function (producer surplus + consumer surplus) can be expressed as:

$$
\frac {\left[ z + \alpha \left(C _ {\delta} - C _ {\rho}\right) \right] ^ {2}}{4 \alpha} - F.
$$

It is easy to verify that social welfare is negatively correlated with preventive controls and positively correlated with deterrent controls (when they are effective and the accompanying fixed costs are low). This result is consistent with the active role of the U.S. Congress in enacting legislation against software piracy. For example, the 1992 Software Copyright Protection Bill upgraded software piracy from an exclusively misdemeanor offense to a possible felony [24].

An interesting phenomenon is that most firms have opted to implement deterrent controls through trade associations. The Software Publishers Association, a major trade association representing over 500 firms, enforces copyright laws on behalf of its member companies, mainly through educational and legal campaigns. A plausible reason may be the significant economies of scale in undertaking deterrent controls.

Within the framework of the model, we can evaluate the extent to which software piracy represents a drain on publisher revenues. Consider a hypothetical scenario in which piracy of software is not possible or where individuals decide not to pirate software. In other words, individuals either legitimately purchase software or do without. In this situation, the demand for software is given by $Q = z - \alpha P_{market}$ , and it is a simple exercise to show that the publisher's revenue is $z^{2}/4\alpha$ . Comparing this with equation (8), we observe that no more than 50 percent of the revenues are lost due to software piracy and that these losses can be curbed and, in the limiting case, completely eliminated by undertaking deterrent controls.

The following sections present a limited empirical validation of the proposed model. We empirically evaluate proposition 1, which illustrates the relationship between deterrent controls and the size of the software club.

## Empirical Research on Software Piracy

AS YET, THERE ARE NOT VERY MANY EMPIRICAL STUDIES related to software piracy. Many of the studies on software piracy have been primarily descriptive, reflecting the relative newness of the topic. For example, Soloman and O'Brien [30] report that software piracy is widespread among business students and that these students view piracy as socially and ethically acceptable. Shim and Taylor [29] found that software copying is perceived by IS faculty members as more prevalent among IS faculty and that business managers did not perceive the same degree of software piracy with their employees. Swinyard et al. [33] found that Asians have a more casual attitude toward piracy than Americans and that Asian attitudes are rooted in cultural mores that emphasize sharing creative work.

Particularly interesting at this stage of inquiry are studies that focus on the demographics and personality characteristics of software pirates and the social and organizational context of software piracy. Eining and Christensen [12] found negative attitudes toward computers, individual perceptions concerning the net benefits of piracy, and personal norms related to the amount of pirated software possessed by business students. Solomon and O'Brien [30] found that females engage in less piracy and younger students pirate more software. In a recent article, Givon et al. [15] used a diffusion modeling approach to illustrate that more than 80 percent of the word processing and spreadsheet software purchased in the United Kingdom was the result of the influence of software pirates.

## The Deterrence Proposition

ACCORDING TO OUR EARLIER DETERRENCE PROPOSITION, A PUBLISHER can reduce the extent of piracy (club size) through deterrent controls. Our proposition on the effect of deterrent controls on the club size is grounded in general deterrence theory. The hypothesis underlying deterrence theory is derived from the general proposition that human behavior can be influenced by incentives [4]. The theory suggests that perceived certainty or severity of punishment inhibits individuals from engaging in antisocial acts. A significant body of empirical research lends support to the efficacy of deterrence theory [26].

With respect to software piracy, a typical deterrence approach employed by software publishers is the unfolding of educational campaigns to inform users about copyright law and the consequences of breaking the law. Deterrent controls attempt to dissuade users from copying software by informing them about the illegality of software pirating. The purpose of educational campaigns is to inform users about copyright laws and inspire attitude changes about appropriate software-copying behavior. In most studies, software piracy is measured as a single item score, usually by asking the respondents whether they copy software and how much they copy. Another important aspect of software piracy is the number of people willing to participate in a piracy transaction. We noted earlier that a club is formed when (1) a group of individuals purchase a software package and make copies of the software and relevant documentation, and (2) when there is an implicit agreement among individuals to engage in reciprocal pirating transactions. The number of individuals participating in the club defines the club size and we hypothesize that increasing deterrent controls reduces the size of the club in which an individual participates.

We also investigated the role of ethics in the propensity of an individual to participate in the formation of a software club because several of the subjects in the pilot study commented on the obvious link between software piracy and ethics. The literature lends support to the importance of the ethics construct. A number of previous studies found morality or ethics to influence criminal behavior, especially white-collar crime. Sacco and Zureik [27] found that beliefs about ethics had a significant impact on computer misuse. Schwartz and Orleans [28] found that appeals to conscience had a stronger effect on tax compliance than legal sanctions. Paternoster [22], in a study of juvenile delinquents, found moral considerations had a significant impact on criminal behavior. Bommer et al. [5] argue that, besides the legal consequences of breaking the law, managers are motivated by a desire to comply with the moral force behind the law.

In addition, several studies refer to the ethical overtones of software piracy [1, 8, 33]. As such, a general measure of ethical attitudes (an ethical index $^{3}$ ) was included as a control variable in the empirical analysis in order to better assess the effect of the deterrent controls on the propensity of individuals to participate in software piracy clubs.

The thrust of this empirical research is to investigate the validity of the deterrence proposition; however, we believe that an individual's propensity to form a club is also affected by his or her ethical index (see figure 1):

H1: Individuals who receive deterrence information will participate in smaller clubs.

H2: Individuals with a lower ethical index will participate in larger clubs. $^{4}$

As noted earlier, Solomon and O'Brien [30] found that age and gender were related to software piracy and that females engaged in less piracy and younger students engaged in more. Two additional hypotheses were thus included to substantiate these earlier findings and to provide corroborating evidence for the validity of the club-size measure:

H3: Females will participate in smaller clubs.

H4: Younger students will participate in larger clubs.

The formal model to be tested is:

Club size = f (deterrence information, ethical index, gender, age).

## Club Size

FOUR ITEMS WERE USED TO OPERATIONALIZE THE CLUB SIZE (Table 1). The items consist of hypothetical behavioral scenarios describing an individual making illegal copies for him or herself at home, for a family member, for a friend, and for other colleagues. The sum of the responses for the four items results in a measure of the club size in which an individual is willing to participate. The club size is a measure of intended behavior related to the propensity of an individual to engage in piracy activity. The scale was constructed so that higher values for the club-size measure are associated with smaller club sizes.

The club-size measure is reasonably reliable. Table 2 shows the correlations between the four items used to measure the club size. All four items are significantly correlated, indicating the existence of a single construct. We also performed an exploratory factor analysis, which also indicated the existence of a single factor. Finally, Cronbach's coefficient alpha for the four club items is 0.80, indicating that the scale is fairly stable and consistent.

## Deterrence Information

This is a one-page information sheet on software piracy (Table 3). Deterrence information provided on the sheet included current copyright laws, the consequences of being caught for violating copyright laws, actions taken by the SPA to curtail piracy, and the negative effects of software piracy on software firms and users. ## Copyright law and computer software

Computer software is copy protected under U.S. Code Section 17. The software producer has the exclusive right to make and distribute copies of the software. When the user purchases the software, he or she is only buying the right to use the software. The program itself remains the property of the software publisher. Most software packages come with a license agreement that restricts users from making copies for purposes other than backups. Usually the license agreement prohibits the use of the software package on more than one hardware device.

## Penalties

Under federal law, unauthorized duplication of computer software carries both civil and criminal penalties. Any person infringing a copyright may be fined up to \$10,000 and/or imprisoned for up to one year.

Software publishers have taken a number of steps to deter piracy. The recently formed Software Publishers Association (SPA) performs corporate audits. Specialized software is available to detect pirated copies of software in computer hard disks. While the audits are voluntary, if a company refuses, the SPA will take it to court. The number of court cases has increased dramatically in the past few years. SPA has stepped up its educational campaign to inform users about the copyright laws.

## Impacts of piracy

Software industry analysts argue that piracy is a huge drain on the software publishers' profits. They estimate that \$170 million to \$4 billion worth of software is pirated and it directly affects over 11,000 companies engaged in software development. Industry analysts predict that continued piracy will inhibit the development of new software as companies will not be able to recoup their software development costs. Thus, piracy will not only affect the software companies but it will also affect users as new software will not be developed.

## Conclusions

WE HAVE INVESTIGATED THE EFFECTS OF PREVENTIVE and deterrent controls on software publishers' profits. The results suggest that preventive controls do not increase publisher profits, while deterrent controls can have a positive effect on profitability. Future areas for research could involve:

\- Further validation and refinement of the research model and the research variables. One limitation of this study is that the club-size measure is a behavioral intention to pirate software and not an actual measure of pirating behavior. A useful extension of this study would be to examine the relationship between the club-size construct and actual club formation behavior and to reexamine the role of deterrence information, ethics, gender, and age on actual club-formation behavior.

\- Determining the type and durability of the deterrence treatment. Is the effect the same if the deterrent is altruistic or legal? This should provide insight into the timing and intensity required to wage effective educational campaigns.

\- Investigating the case where there are substitute products available. Consider the case of two firms producing near substitutes. There may be incentives for one publisher to allow formation of larger software clubs to attract consumers away from the other firm's product.

\- Investigate the role of piracy as an advertising mechanism. The marketing literature has suggested that shadow diffusion, where a product is pirated rather than bought, is an important element in generating revenues [15].

\- Additional investigation into the role of ethics in economic models. What effect do different ethical foundations have on economic behavior?

In response to the final observation, traditional economists assume that ethics has no role in economic behavior. In our case, this would mean that ethics should have no effect on the formation of software clubs. However, our findings that ethical levels have a significant effect on club size parallel the assertion by Reilly and Kyj [25] that “classical economic thought advocates a non-ethical decision-making context and is not functional for a modern complex, interdependent environment” (p. 691). We believe that differential ethical mores have a profound impact on the propensity of individuals to engage in piracy transactions and that this area deserves further investigation.

## NOTES

1. Strictly speaking, the cost is incurred only by $(n-1)$ individuals in the club since one legitimate copy of the software is acquired. For analytical tractability, however, we assume that all individuals in the club use copies of the software. Previous studies have employed a similar approach in analyzing reproducible products such as printed matter, audio and video tapes [3].

2. The model assumes that the cost to every individual from the threat of legal sanctions is identical. Conventional wisdom and our later empirical analysis suggest that individuals differ in terms of risk attitudes, ethical values, and a variety of individual and contextual situations. The net result is that there are differences in how individuals perceive deterrence costs. However, the model and the derived results remain valid if the market is perceived as a collection of segments, where all individuals in a segment are identical in their perception of deterrence. The model can then be applied to each market segment and the corresponding results derived. Because the results derived are similar for all segments, it follows that the results hold true for the entire market.

3. The ethical index is the ethical profile of an individual as represented by a single summative score obtained from a general ethics questionnaire.

4. A natural extension of this hypothesis would consider whether individuals with less ethical values would participate in a greater number of software piracy clubs.

5. Recall that larger values for the club-size measure are related to smaller club sizes.

\frac {\partial R ^ {*}}{\partial \rho} <   0

and

\frac {\partial R ^ {*}}{\partial \delta} > 0.
$$

Proof: As $\partial C\rho / \partial \rho > 0$ and $\partial C_{\delta} / \partial \delta > 0$ , we will prove that $\partial R^{*} / \partial C\rho < 0$ and $\partial R^{*} / \partial C_{\delta} > 0$ to support the proposition. From equations (A2) and (A3), we have,

$$
