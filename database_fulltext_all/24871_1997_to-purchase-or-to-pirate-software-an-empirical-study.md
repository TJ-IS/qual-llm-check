# To Purchase or to Pirate Software: An Empirical Study

HSING K. CHENG, RONALD R. SIMS, AND HILDY TEEGEN

HSING K. CHENG is Assistant Professor of Information Technology at the Graduate School of Business Administration, College of William and Mary. He received a B.S. in electronic engineering, an M.B.A. from National Chiao-Tung University (Taiwan), and a Ph.D. from the William E. Simon Graduate School of Business Administration of the University of Rochester. His research interests include information systems economics, managing the risks of computer system breakdowns, and analyzing the impacts and policy implications of information technology on society. His research has appeared or will appear in Computers and Operations Research, Decision Support Systems, European Journal of Operational Research, IEICE Transactions on Communications, Journal of Business Ethics, Journal of Management Information Systems, and leading conference proceedings. He contributed a chapter on computer fraud to Corporate Misconduct: The Legal, Societal, and Management Issues.

RONALD R. SIMS is Floyd Dewey Gottwald Professor of Business Administration at the College of William and Mary where he teaches organizational behavior and human resources management. His research focuses on experiential learning theory and its application to school and work settings, ethics, and a number of human resource management related topics. His work has been published in a variety of scholarly and practitioner-oriented journals. Two of his most recent books are Corporate Misconduct: The Legal, Societal, and Management Issues and Organizational Decision Making: A Call for Renewal.

HILDY TEEGEN is an Assistant Professor in International Business at George Washington University. Her research interests include managing cross-border interfirm collaboration, the NAFTA region, and the protection of firm assets in collaboration. Her work has appeared in American Journal of Management Development, Columbia Journal of World Business, Journal of Business Ethics, and Journal of Management Information Systems.

ABSTRACT: Illegal copying of computer software, usually called software piracy, is a prevalent and serious problem. Some researchers attribute the widespread incidence of software piracy to people's attitudes toward piracy behavior and peer norms. However, current literature leaves unanswered a fundamental question of why individuals pirate software. The objective of this paper is to identify the underlying reasons why individuals pirate software. We also identify what motivates individuals to purchase software as opposed to pirating it. Understanding why individuals purchase and pirate software has clear value for policy makers to develop effective measures to curb the software piracy problem.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: income effect in software acquisition, software piracy, software purchase.

OBTAINING UNAUTHORIZED COPIES OF COMPUTER SOFTWARE, usually referred to as software piracy, is a prevalent problem in the workplace, the academic community, and among individuals. An independent study commissioned by the Business Software Alliance (BSA), an international organization of software firms, considered software piracy the industry's worst problem [1]. From a survey of seventy-seven countries, the BSA estimates that worldwide piracy cost \$15.2 billion in losses to the software publishing and distribution industries in 1994, an increase of \$2.4 billion from \$12.8 billion in 1993 [2]. Piracy losses in twenty-five European countries totaled more than \$6 billion, accounting for the largest share of the global total. The United States had the lowest piracy rate, 35 percent, and incurred \$2.8 billion in losses in 1994 [2]. Software pirating is so prevalent that some even argue that this behavior has become socially acceptable [20].

As a response to pressure from the software industry, U.S. Senate Bill 893 (effective October 28, 1992) increased penalties for copyright infringement, which was classified as a misdemeanor under the old law. The maximum penalties of the new law include fines of as much as \$250,000 and jail terms of up to five years. The new law does not, however, eliminate the software piracy problem. While the U.S. software industry grew 269 percent in real terms from 1982 to 1992, the industry still suffers enormous losses due to software piracy [19].

Inherent features of personal computers make software piracy a unique and difficult problem. Duplication of most software packages requires only simple commands and mouse manipulations. Unlike photocopying an article or a book, the quality of duplicated software is not appreciably diminished. Furthermore, the proliferation of personal computers makes it difficult to enforce the copyright law at the level of individual computer users. Since the existence of antipiracy law does not seem to prevent software piracy, effective measures to address the software piracy problem cannot be developed without knowing the underlying motivations and justifications for individuals to pirate software.

Despite the scope and prevalence of software piracy, there is a lack of research on this serious problem. Pioneering research on software piracy confirms common perceptions of the software piracy problem. For example, in a study by Shim and Taylor [16], about 90 percent of business school faculty members believe their colleagues have duplicated copyrighted software. Young professionals have been found to have no scruples about copying software illegally [12]. Some researchers such as Christensen and Eining [4] and Reid, Thompson, and Logsdon [15] attribute the widespread incidence of software piracy to individuals' attitudes toward piracy behavior and peer norms. The extant literature on software piracy, however, does not empirically examine the fundamental question of why individuals pirate software.

The primary objective of this paper is empirically to identify the underlying reasons/motivations that individuals pirate software and to rank the importance of these identified reasons. Understanding these reasons has clear value to government agencies, software vendors, and the academic community in developing effective measures to curb this problem. In addition, we study what motivates individuals to purchase software in order to gain a complete understanding of the software piracy problem. We also present a further inquiry into whether an individual's household income is correlated with his or her piracy and purchasing decision concerning computer software.

## Research Methodology

TO IDENTIFY THE UNDERLYING REASONS FOR INDIVIDUALS to pirate as well as to purchase software, business students were chosen as the study subjects. Research has suggested that software piracy is more prevalent in academia than in business; colleges and universities are believed to be breeding grounds for software piracy. Furthermore, the problem of software piracy begins before young business students become practicing managers and business students are more likely to use software in their professional careers upon graduation [12].

## Questionnaire Development

Interviews with both undergraduate and graduate business students were conducted to obtain a preliminary list of reasons/motivations to pirate and to purchase software. An open-ended questionnaire was also used in parallel to identify these motives. Once a list of common reasons for individuals to pirate as well as to purchase software was derived, this list was included in the final questionnaire (administered to new respondents) asking respondents to rank, in order of importance, the reasons to pirate and the reasons to purchase software. Spaces were provided for respondents to add other reasons that were not in the original list. Respondents were also asked to specify reasons that are irrelevant to their piracy and purchase decisions of computer software by assigning a zero next to the irrelevant reason. Entries with zeroes were deleted from the importance rankings. Respondents were asked about their personal behavior rather than the abstract notion of buying or pirating software.

The final research instrument was pretested on twenty-five M.B.A. students and twenty-eight undergraduate business students before being administered in its final form. The questionnaire was then refined according to the pretest results and peer review comments on the research instrument from academic colleagues. Respondents participating in the pretest were excluded from the sample group. The final instrument used to analyze why individuals pirate software and why individuals purchase software is shown in Table 1.

Table 1 contains ten common reasons for individuals to purchase software and nine reasons to pirate software. These reasons are deemed to be exhaustive since none of the respondents added additional reasons in the final sample.

## Sample Group

The research instrument was tested on the sample group consisting of 73 resident M.B.A. students, 27 executive M.B.A. students, and 240 undergraduate business students. The sample group covers a wide range of majors including accounting, finance, general management, information systems, marketing, and operations management. The questionnaires were tested during the first day of classes, maximizing the number of respondents and avoiding nonresponse problems.

## Research Results

We used correspondence analysis, a nonparametric technique, to classify groupings of reasons since some of the reasons in Table 1 may share the same construct. We ran correspondence analysis several times using a different number of dimensions to find the best-fit groupings. However, the result of groupings (clusterings) was painfully nonsensical in some clusters. Moreover, the Kuder-Richardson test scores of some groupings were not high enough to meet standard thresholds. Since the conventional statistical approaches did not yield meaningful directions, we report the research findings sorted by means of rankings in the following section.

## Research Findings and Discussions

NORMATIVE ECONOMIC MODELS OF CONSUMER PURCHASE DECISIONS require that the price of a product under consideration be less than the consumer's reservation price, where the reservation price equals the expected value resulting from the purchase less the expected cost associated with the purchase (see, e.g., [11, 18]). In the case of reproducible consumer products such as computer software, consumers have the alternative of reproducing—that is, pirating—the software instead of purchasing it. Alternatively, the potential software consumer may choose neither to purchase nor to pirate the software. A consumer's final decision depends on three variables: the cost of pirating the software, the price of the software, and his or her reservation price. For example, the consumer will pirate the software if the cost of pirating it is less than both the price of the software and his or her individual reservation price. On the other hand, a purchase decision will be made if the price of the software is the smallest among the three decision variables.

To illustrate, let C be the cost of pirating software, P be the price of the software under consideration, and V be the reservation price of a potential consumer of the computer software. Then, as formulated in Conner and Rumelt [5], the consumer decision is characterized by the following model:

$$
\begin{array}{l l} \text {BUY} & \text {if P\leq\min (V,C),} \\ \text {PIRATE} & \text {if C <   \min (P,V), and} \\ \text {DO WITHOUT} & \text {if V<  \min (P,C).} \end{array}
$$

The present research attempts to enrich the normative economic models of consumers' software purchase/pirate decision by discovering the factors that affect consumers' valuation of computer software and their cost components of pirating software.

This research shows that the most important reason for respondents to purchase software is “required for school work or workplace,” that is, the necessity to use software. Potential software buyers have to be convinced of the necessity of the software in order for them to purchase it. As evidence supporting this finding, the sales of antivirus software peaked in the second quarter of 1992 because of very high users’ awareness of the Michelangelo virus [3].

The second most important reason to purchase software is “use the software all the time.” Besides the necessity to use the software, software that will be used more often is more likely to be purchased. This reason should refer to both the frequency and length of using the software. One may initially use software very frequently, but the intense usage lasts only a short while. A typical example is the computer game. The utility of playing a computer game tends to diminish sharply after the user gets bored with playing it. In fact, the short duration of using software appears as one of the major reasons to pirate software.

The third most important reason to purchase software is the “availability of manual.” When a manual is necessary for a user to access certain features of the software, this need may instigate a purchase. Another possible explanation for the importance of this reason is that it is more troublesome to copy a manual than it is to copy the software. From the perspective of the normative economic model, the software manual adds value \ software product and thus increases the V, the customer’s valuation of a product under decision. Meanwhile, the difficulty of copying a software manual increases the cost of pirating software, the $C$ part of the model. Both effects make a purchase decision more desirable.

Today's software is characterized by a built-in help facility. The built-in help facility is essentially an on-line manual intended to resolve most of a user's questions. The inclusion of built-in help facilities reduces the need for a software manual, presenting a dilemma for the software vendor. On one hand, the software vendor wants to make the software more user-friendly by including the built-in help. On the other hand, the built-in help will decrease potential customers' incentive to purchase the software. As the built-in help facility becomes a good substitute for the software manual in the future, we will expect "availability of manual" to be a less important reason for individuals to purchase software.

“It’s the law” and “It’s school/company policy” are the fourth and the sixth most important reasons for purchasing software in Table 2. These imply that conformance to law or company policy has little impact on individuals’ software purchase decision. “Can use technical support in case of problems” ranked fifth as a reason to purchase software, while “availability of manual” has a more important ranking. This probably implies that respondents resort to manuals to resolve their software usage problems and rely upon technical support less frequently.

This research finds that “required for school work or workplace,” “use the software all the time,” and “availability of manual” are key factors affecting consumers’ reservation price (valuation) of computer software. On the other hand, “availability of manual,” “can’t find someone who has it (i.e., software unavailable to pirate),” and “it’s the law (school/company policy)” contribute to the overall cost of pirating software. An interesting observation is that potential consumers in our sample seem to assume away the price factor and concentrate on weighting different factors of their reservation price and cost of software piracy when asked to specify their reasons of purchase.

## Reasons to Pirate Software

The rankings of reasons to pirate software are summarized in Table 3, with the most important reason at the top. The mean and standard deviation of each reason are shown in the third and fourth columns. All reasons in the table are tested for whether they are “more” important than the next-ranked reason. The corresponding significance test results are reported in the last column of Table 3.

Table 3 addresses an important question that the literature has not yet answered: Why do individuals pirate software? We found that the most important reason for software piracy is “software too expensive.” The third most important reason, “can’t afford the software,” complements “software too expensive.” Both reasons ranked high in respondents’ piracy decisions. Software piracy is often used by software vendors as grounds for pricing the software higher in order to recoup the potential losses due to copying without purchase. Our findings suggest that a higher software price actually makes piracy more desirable, creating a vicious circle.

Whether or not software is truly overpriced cannot be determined without knowing the software developer's cost and revenue information. Although software prices have fallen in nominal terms, in real terms, and by the measure of software functions per dollar, software pirates may perceive that software products are overpriced, particularly when the price of the software, $P$ , is far greater than the minimum of their reservation price, $V$ , and the cost of pirating, $C$ . The perception that software is overpriced is further enhanced by the fact that the cost of software piracy is typically very low for individual consumers. This perception explains a moral inconsistency related to software piracy: Stealing a candy bar would not be tolerated while pirating software worth (priced at) hundreds of dollars is generally condoned.

A recent study by Logsdon, Thompson, and Reid [9] found a high level of tolerance toward unauthorized copying; software piracy is perceived as an issue of low moral intensity. Their study calls into question the software industry's strategy of concentrating exclusively on institutional compliance with copyright laws, rather than working to raise the perceived moral intensity about software piracy at the individual level. Besides lowering the software price, another way to combat the software piracy problem is to raise both consumers' reservation price and perhaps their "moral" cost of piracy.

One may question the finding that “software too expensive” is the most important reason for individuals to pirate software by noting an interesting phenomenon in the software industry. If the affordability of software is the primary reason for pirating software, why is piracy still prevalent in the entertainment software market where the price of entertainment software is so much lower, relative to other types of software $[8]$ ? The second and the fourth most important reasons for respondents to pirate software in Table 3 answers this dilemma directly. When respondents want to try out the software or the software is only used temporarily, respondents tend to copy the software without purchasing. In general, the utility (pleasure) of playing entertainment software diminishes dramatically after a short time. This short life span of entertainment software makes it vulnerable to piracy even though its price is low. This finding is consistent with the reasons for individuals to purchase software reported in the previous section. Respondents tend to purchase the software when they use the software all the time, the second most important reason to purchase software in Table 2.

As a reason for individuals to pirate software, “want to try out the software” makes estimating the costs of software piracy difficult. For instance, the revenue loss estimate by the Software Publishers Association is calculated by multiplying the shortfall between the actual and the expected software units purchased per personal computer, by the number of PCs sold, by the average price of a business software package. Different interpretations of the expected sales of software will certainly lead to notably varying estimates $[3]$ . That every unauthorized copy translates to revenue loss may not be an accurate estimate. In reality, end users may simply pirate the software to try it out as suggested by our research findings.

The ease of pirating software is commonly believed to be at the root of the software piracy problem, or at least to aggravate the problem. Our findings indicate that “it’s easy to copy software” only ranked fifth as a reason for respondents to pirate software. This is further confirmed by the low importance of “little chance of being caught.” The sixth most important reason to pirate software includes the case where a new version or upgrade of software is due out shortly. Potential buyers may tend to delay their purchase decision until the new version is available.

Both “little chance of being caught” and “most people I know copy software”—the opportunism dimension of piracy in our study—are among the least important reasons for respondents to pirate software. It appears that respondents base their piracy decisions more on the economic parameters of the problem and behave as “rational” individuals. Future research is needed to understand individuals’ software piracy decision-making process, especially when this complex process involves variables such as the pricing of software, the enforcement level of copyright law to individuals (the probability of getting caught), and individuals’ moral tolerance toward software piracy.

## Income Effect

To corroborate the findings of this research, we examine the correlation between respondents' household incomes and their reasons to purchase as well as to pirate software. In particular, if cost/affordability of software is the most important reason for respondents to pirate software, respondents' household incomes should be correlated with this reason. Respondents' household incomes, instead of their personal incomes, are analyzed for possible correlation since the undergraduate students part of the sample typically have minimal personal incomes and rely on financial support from their family.

Our research found that household income significantly correlated with “can’t afford the software” as a reason to pirate software. The (Pearson correlation coefficient, p-value) pair between household income and “can’t afford the software” (reversed scored) reason is $(0.188, 0.008)$ . This implies that the smaller the household income, the more important “can’t afford the software” is as a reason to pirate software. None of the other reasons to pirate software is significantly correlated with household income. This conforms with our prediction of the income effect on software piracy.

Our finding suggests that pricing software according to a potential buyer's household income is beneficial to both software vendors and consumers. It not only reduces the likelihood for individuals who cannot afford the software to pirate it, but also gives them an opportunity to access the software without resorting to piracy. Some software vendors implement such a pricing policy by custom packaging their products, selling them at a lower price to individuals with lower incomes such as educational professionals and students. In some cases, these software packages come either with fewer features than the full working version or with other limitations.

On the software purchase decision side, we found a very interesting correlation between the “availability of manual” (reversed scored) reason to purchase software and respondents’ household income. The (Pearson correlation coefficient, p-value) pair between household income and the “availability of manual” reason is $(-0.131, 0.053)$ . This means that the higher the respondent’s household income, the more important the “availability of manual” is as a reason to purchase software. Respondents with a higher household income place a higher value on the software manual in their purchase decision.

## Concluding Remarks

## Implications

THIS PAPER AIMED AT UNCOVERING AND RANKING THE UNDERLYING reasons/motivations for individuals to pirate as well as to purchase software. A primary objective was to enhance our understanding of the normative economic model of consumers' software purchase/pirate decisions by discovering the factors that affect consumers' valuation of computer software and their cost factors of pirating software.

Our research found that the three most important reasons for respondents to purchase software were “required for school work or workplace,” “use the software all the time,” and “availability of manual.” The importance of “availability of manual” is worth noting. From the perspective of the normative economic model, the software manual adds value to a software product. Meanwhile, the difficulty of copying a software manual increases the cost of pirating the software. Both effects make a purchase decision more likely. Hence, the software vendor should issue manuals even if the software has a built-in help facility.

The three most important reasons for pirating software included “software too expensive,” “want to try out software,” and “can’t afford the software.” These reasons, with the affordability of software as the common thread, are used by software pirates to justify their piracy. Software piracy is often used by software vendors as grounds for pricing the software higher in order to recoup the potential piracy losses. Our findings suggest that a higher software price makes piracy more desirable and a lower software price may in fact be called for. Besides lowering the software price, another way for software vendors to combat the software-piracy problem is to raise both consumers' reservation price and perhaps their "moral" cost of piracy.

Our research discovers a significant correlation between household income and “can’t afford the software” as a reason to pirate software. Our research also finds that household income has no correlation with other reasons to pirate software. This finding suggests that pricing software according to a potential buyer’s household income will reduce consumers’ piracy intention.

## Limitations and Future Research

There are some limitations to this research. Although this study involved a reasonably large sample, a larger sample to include respondents from other universities and possibly more representation from nonstudent professionals will be useful for substantiating the generalizability of the research results. Other factors such as respondent fatigue and reticence about self-reporting unethical behavior may also be limitations of the current approach.

This paper represents a first attempt to identify the underlying reasons/motivations for individuals to pirate as well as to purchase software. However, our approach is by no means the only way to examine the software piracy problem. Studies that relate the software piracy propensity to demographic and other variables will also be very useful (see $[15, 17]$ ). With “software too expensive” being the most important reason to pirate software, whether the pricing of software will affect respondents’ software piracy behavior is an important area for more extensive research. Our future research will study how respondents’ software piracy behavior will react to a number of important variables including software prices. Finally, the framework and methodology developed in this paper will be applied to other countries for a cross-cultural examination of the software piracy problem.
