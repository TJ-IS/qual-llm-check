# Information Technology in Three Small Developed Countries

PHILLIP EIN-DOR, MICHAEL D. MYERS, AND K.S. RAMAN

PHILLIP EIN-DOR is Professor of Information Systems, Faculty of Management, Tel Aviv University, and former director of the Marcel and Annie Adams Institute for Business Management Information Systems. Professor Ein-Dor has published widely in the information systems field and is an Associate Editor of MIS Quarterly, and member of the Editorial Boards of Information & Management and the Information Resources Management Journal.

MICHAEL D. MYERS is Senior Lecturer in the Department of Management Science and Information Systems at the University of Auckland, New Zealand. Dr. Myers is an Associate Editor of Information Systems Journal, an Associate Editor of MIS Quarterly, the Editor of the ISWorld Section on Qualitative Research, and member of the Editorial Boards of Information Technology & People and the Journal of Systems and Information Technology.

K.S. RAMAN is Adjunct Associate Professor, Department of Information Systems and Computer Science, National University of Singapore. Prior to this appointment, he was Senior Fellow and Coordinator of the Information Systems Area of the department. Professor Raman has published widely in the information systems field and served as an Associate Editor of MIS Quarterly.

ABSTRACT: The perception that small size may no longer be an economic disadvantage to either organizations or countries has become fairly widespread. We share the perception that not only are small countries not at a disadvantage, but they may actually have an advantage over larger competitors with regard to information technology industries. Indeed, small developed countries are achieving considerable success in the development of their information technology industries. The three countries studied here—Israel, New Zealand, and Singapore—are among those that have seen the rapid development of their IT industries in recent years, despite the fact that they are among the world's smaller countries, are considerably different in many geographic, cultural, and political respects, and are geographically dispersed around the globe.

The objective of this study is to describe and compare the information technology industries of these three small developed countries. All three countries, with no inherent advantages in raw materials and only small domestic markets, have the IT infrastructure and the human skills needed. They are nimble and flexible and can find niche markets in which to specialize. Not all three, however, have developed IT industries to the same degree. There could be factors specific to small developed countries that facilitate the development of indigenous IT production. The dominant factor that seems to provide some explanation for different levels and directions of development of IT production is government policy in promoting IT production directly, in supporting IT industry R&D, and in education policies designed to provide appropriately trained labor pools.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: factors of national computing, information systems in developed countries, international computing, national computing policies.

SOME SMALL DEVELOPED COUNTRIES ARE ACHIEVING CONSIDERABLE SUCCESS in the development of their information technology industries [10]. This parallels a perception quite widely accepted of late that small size may no longer be an economic disadvantage to either organizations or countries. The three countries studied here—Israel, New Zealand, and Singapore—are among those that have seen the rapid development of their IT industries in recent years. This is despite the fact that they are among the world's smaller countries, are considerably different geographically, culturally, and politically, and are widely dispersed around the globe. Our objective here is to describe and compare the information technology industries of the three countries studied. They have not all developed IT industries to the same degree; it is suggested that this is mainly the effect of endogenous factors rather than of natural causes. More specific propositions relating to groups of variables are presented in the conclusions, where we also offer some thoughts on where the advantage of small countries may lie.

A number of studies provide support for the proposition that small countries do not suffer from relative disadvantages in their IT industries [43]. Naisbitt [40] argues that small organizations are not at a disadvantage in the emerging global economy but may even have relative advantages; this is summed up in the subtitle of his book, The Larger the World Economy the More Powerful Its Smallest Players. We draw on two additional studies to help explain how Naisbitt's proposition applies to IT industries in particular. These are Reich's [47] analysis of symbolic analysis/problem solving as the new paradigm for economic activity and Grossman and Helpman's [16, 17] macroeconomic analysis of trade and growth in the small open economy.

Reich's [47] two major propositions are (1) that the principal accretion of value has shifted from industrial production to symbolic analysis/problem solving—solving problems in order to put things together in new ways, identifying customers' problems in order to help them understand their needs and how they can best be met by customized products, and linking problem solvers and problem identifiers, and (2) that symbolic analysis/problem solving is independent of any particular geographical location and is performed wherever it is most advantageous to do so. Thus, a manufacturer may order R&D on a new product in one country, design in another, component manufacture in whichever locations offer the best price/quality tradeoff, and assemble in yet another country. The liberation offered by telecommunications from the constraints of geographical location obviates many of the advantages previously afforded by proximity to manufacturing locations and the concentration of such locations in large countries. The size factor may never have been as important as has been supposed. Even in large countries, manufacturing companies in a particular industry have tended to locate in specific regions—computers in Silicon Valley (California) and Route 128 (Massachusetts), automobiles in Detroit, and shoes in Yorkshire.

A more rigorous, macroeconomic theoretical background for this study is provided by the work of Grossman and Helpman $[16, 17]$ on the relationship between technology development, trade, and growth as applied to small open economies. They define a small economy as “one that does not affect the larger economic environment in which it operates” $[17]$ .¹ Although theirs is a theoretical study, it is of interest to compare the results of their analysis with the empirical data to be presented here.

Their model is used to analyze trade and growth in the small, open economy: "Growth stems from endogenous technological progress, as far-sighted entrepreneurs introduce innovative (intermediate) products whenever the present value of the stream of operating profits covers the cost of product development" [16] (emphasis added). The intermediate products are then used in the production of consumer goods, but not traded directly. Our study deviates from this model in that we discuss intermediate goods that are largely traded directly rather than being used domestically in further production. Grossman and Helpman find that the first-best growth path "can be attained with subsidies to both R&D and the production of intermediates" (emphasis added). The second-best growth path can be achieved with subsidies to R&D alone. In our conclusions we shall examine the applicability of these points to the industry and countries studied here.

Given that the three countries that serve as the basis for this study exhibit considerable, albeit varying, success in developing IT industries, it is the goal of this study to explain this phenomenon. In order to do so, we have considered four groups of variables. These variables are all frequently quoted in the context of industrial success and are exhibited in Table 1 with reference to their appearance in the theoretical groundings cited above.

The control variables are not, of course, controlled by the researchers in the usual sense. They are controlled, however, in that they determine which countries are eligible for inclusion in the study. The dependent variables, which represent the degree of success of a country's IT industry, comprise various measures of such success. The last four variables are those most readily controlled or influenced by firms and governments and we refer to them as “endogenous mediating factors.” The exogenous mediating factors are those over which little or no control is possible at any level and that must be accepted as given. Graphically, this model is represented in figure 1. The operationalizations we have employed for the variables in the model are given in Table 2.

It is not possible to describe all the variables with the same degree of rigor; they are described for each country on the basis of data available. However, we did try to operationalize the variables as consistently as possible. After we present the data, we place them in the context of the theoretical models mentioned earlier in order to make sense of the findings.

## Methodology

THIS STUDY IS THE FIRST IN A SERIES PLANNED to bring about understanding of the dynamics of development of IT production in small, developed countries; reaching such understanding will, at some point, also require comparisons with developments in large countries and in other small countries facing different circumstances. As the first in the series, however, this study has the modest goal of describing the current situations in the countries studied, identifying commonalities and differences, and tracing their effects in the countries described.

Thus, the methodology is essentially to collect data suggested by the theoretical model, with an attempt to provide as much comparability as possible. This is in itself a major problem; given the economic importance of IT and IT industries, the level of officially available data is surprisingly poor. The Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD) tells us that “IT statistics remain a new and challenging area of work . . . [as] available data is very scattered and generally inadequate” (quoted in [37]). This certainly applies to all three countries studied here.

## IT Product Sales and Contribution to GDP

IT firms in Israel account for over 32 percent of manufacturing sales and 4.6 percent of service sales (Dun & Bradstreet, Israel; see note 2); thus, IT plays a substantial role in the Israeli economy. The statistics do not differentiate IT sales from those of electronics in general. Electronics production was valued at \$4.6 billion in 1993; we estimate that about 77 percent of this—that is, \$3.5 billion—was IT proper. The breakdown by categories was about 24 percent defense systems, 40 percent telecommunications equipment, 20 percent industrial and medical systems, and 16 percent components. The annual growth rate of hardware sales has been about 10 percent in recent years, with growth in 1993 reaching 15 percent [1]. Sales of the local software industry were about \$650 million in 1993. While still quite small, the software industry is the fastest growing industry in Israel, with average annual growth of 20–25 percent over the last eight years [28].

Although IT plays a relatively small role in the New Zealand economy in terms of domestic production, hardware exports increased fourfold in the period 1989–91, while software exports increased tenfold in the period 1985–90 [37].

The total revenue of the IT industry in Singapore in 1991 was 13.5 percent of manufacturing GDP. The breakdown of total industry sales is: hardware \$1,129 million (72.5 percent); software \$188 million (12.0 percent); and IT services \$242 million (15.5 percent) [42]. During the period 1982–91, IT production in Singapore grew at an average annual rate of about 27 percent.

## IT Industry Success Measures

IT industry success is indicated here by the range of products developed locally and by IT export sales.

## Locally Developed IT Products

A force motivating the development of the IT industry in Israel was the need to develop advanced defense systems capable of meeting exacting conditions. As a result, Israel attained world leadership in some electronic sectors. These developments also encouraged the development of equally sophisticated software capabilities. Finally, the transfer of technology from the defense to the civilian sector contributed to the development of state-of-the-art civilian applications $[1, 4]$ .

As indicated earlier, much of Israeli IT production is concentrated in communications and defense products. An additional well-developed capability in Israel is the integration of electronics with other disciplines. Thus, Israel is a pioneer in incorporating electronics into the textile and printing industries, agriculture, paper manufacturing, and medicine. Finally, a number of Israeli firms have specialized in semiconductor design, manufacture, and manufacturing control. Israeli IT production is based to a great extent on local innovation.

Of the 150 software houses in Israel, 60 percent are very small; the remaining 40 percent have between 20 and 400 employees. About 35 firms export software products. The major areas of specialization of Israeli software houses are telecommunications, databases and applications, CAD/CAM, computer system management and protection, tailor-made software and systems, and educational systems.

Most hardware suppliers in New Zealand are subsidiaries of large multinational corporations. The few indigenous hardware manufacturers that do exist specialize in specific niche markets such as electronic printers for use in the defense industry.

The New Zealand software industry has been more successful; it includes approximately 300 firms. According to Crocombe, Enright, and Porter [9], local entrepreneurship has played a critical role in developing and sustaining the position of New Zealand software companies. “New Zealand,” they tell us, “lacks many of the benefits that are present in the United States and some other nations. New Zealand does not have a plethora of world-class university software programs or research efforts, leading-edge computer hardware companies, a large pool of trained software engineers or large and sophisticated software markets. In spite of these disadvantages, several New Zealand software companies are successful in particular segments. ‘Kiwi ingenuity’ has been a major factor” [9].

In Singapore, several top manufacturing companies are in IT—these are local operating units of multinationals. Several Japanese electronic companies have substantial manufacturing facilities in Singapore. In the initial stages of development of the IT industry in Singapore, multinationals viewed Singapore as a low-cost assembly and manufacturing center and used designs developed overseas. This has changed in recent years and multinationals have established significant R&D activities in Singapore. Some local IT companies have a strong R&D orientation. For example, Singapore companies have nearly an 80 percent share of the world market for sound cards and chips (the Soundblaster multimedia card and chip developed for IBM-compatible PCs was developed in Singapore). Many local software companies focus on system integration and the development of bridging software.

## IT Industry Exports—General Trade Conditions

All three countries share a heavy dependence on foreign trade, each for different reasons. The extent of their IT exports will be placed in the context of their general trade conditions.

Despite the similarity in economic development noted earlier and the heavy dependence of all three countries on foreign trade, the degree of dependence on foreign trade varies considerably between them, as shown in Table 6. The considerable difference in the pattern of foreign trade between the three countries requires some explanation.

The metal and electronics industries in Israel began developing rapidly in the late 1960s, both qualitatively and quantitatively, thanks to the demand of the defense forces. Since the mid-1970s, defense demand has steadily declined with consequent pressure to export; as a result, exports of metal and electronic products grew by a factor of 15 in the period under discussion and reached \$3.2 billion by 1989 [28]. Because of the political situation, in particular the Arab boycott, Israel was isolated until recently from its nearest potential markets in the Middle East and so turned westward to Europe and North America. With the easing of political tensions, significant new markets are beginning to open up in Asia, particularly China, India, and Japan.

Four markets account for a large proportion of New Zealand's trade: Australia, the European Economic Community, Japan, and the United States. Of the three countries studied here, Singapore is by far the most dependent on foreign trade. This is at least partly the result of a conscious policy to exploit Singapore's strategic location in the Malacca Straits in order to become a major trade center. Because Singapore's export data include reexports of imported goods [13], they are not directly comparable with those of other countries.

## IT Exports

Total electronics exports in Israel amounted to 3.2 billion in 1993 [1], of which we estimate 2.5 billion to be IT proper. This represents about 30 percent of total exports, excluding diamonds [48]. $^{3}$ Software exports are still relatively small but have been expanding rapidly; Software exports were 110 million in 1991, grew by 27 percent in 1992 to 140 million and then by 20 percent to a projected 170 million in 1993 [19].

While New Zealand's IT hardware exports have been negligible in the past, telecommunications hardware exports have increased rapidly, to \$58 million in 1993 [12]. Accurate figures on other hardware exports are difficult to obtain, since many companies are privately owned. Software exports from New Zealand increased tenfold in the period 1985–90, from \$4 million to \$59 million [37]. The profitability of New Zealand's software exports was ten to twenty times that of New Zealand's resource-based industries. "In terms of the profits generated for New Zealand, software was a significant contributor" [9].

As noted earlier, of the total 1991 IT sales in Singapore of \$1,559 million, the domestic market accounted for \$1065 million (68.3 percent) and exports for \$494 million (31.7 percent) [42].

However, some of the differences between the countries may be the result of different capital-raising practices, ownership preferences, or of differing size and maturity of IT firms in the various countries. ## Summary

Table 4 gives a summary of IT industry development and success measures. It becomes evident that all three countries have developed substantial IT industries. However, the IT industry in Israel appears to have developed to a greater extent and exhibits higher success measures than those in the other two countries, with Singapore in second place and New Zealand third. It is our objective in the remainder of this paper to suggest explanations for this phenomenon.

It may be significant that, while IT production in Israel is about three times that of New Zealand and Singapore, the number of IT firms is not much larger than that of New Zealand and only half that of Singapore. Thus, on average, Israeli IT firms are considerably larger than those of the other two countries.

## Exogenous Mediating Factors

The exogenous factors we examine are geographical location, materials, and national culture (Table 7).

## Geographical Location

Israel has, until now, been isolated from its immediate neighborhood because of hostile relations with most Arab countries. While this may now be changing, recent developments have not yet had an impact on the market situation. Given the stage of development of most Middle Eastern countries, it is likely to be some time before they become major markets for Israeli high-tech products, even assuming a full political settlement in the region. As a result, Israel's major markets are in relatively remote countries in western Europe or very remote ones, such as the United States, and are likely to remain so. More recent developments follow the same pattern, with the most rapidly developing new markets being those in eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union (relatively close) and in the Far East (distant). The effect of remoteness from feasible markets for Israeli IT firms has been to make marketing and product service more expensive and to drive firm strategies, which are discussed later.

New Zealand is, of course, geographically isolated in a relatively remote part of the earth. The nearest developed country is Australia, which is approximately 1,000 miles to the west. Historically, most of New Zealand's international trade was with the antipodean United Kingdom. In the last two decades, however, the pattern of international trade has changed dramatically. As for Israel, the effect of geographical isolation on New Zealand IT has been to impede the development of foreign markets.

Singapore is a member of the Association of South East Asian Nations (ASEAN) and has cordial relations with the other member countries, Brunei, Indonesia, Malaysia, Philippines, Singapore, and Thailand. It also has cordial relations with China and other countries in the Pacific Rim, and with the United States and Europe. Hardware exports are worldwide, but software and services exports are mostly within the region and China. Several Singaporean IT companies have operations in China.

Some small countries in the region seek the professional services of organizations such as the National Computer Board of Singapore in national IT strategies and government computerization, and Singapore Telecom in setting up a telecommunications infrastructure. Thus, Singapore's geographical proximity to and cultural affinity with major markets has supported efforts to expand IT production.

It becomes fairly clear that the trade of the countries we have studied tends to be greatest with neighboring regions but can also be quite substantial with remote regions. We may conclude that remote and/or isolated locations impede but do not preclude IT industry success in small developed countries.

## Raw Materials

Israel has almost no raw materials or local energy sources, with the exception of chemical deposits that are the basis for a substantial chemical industry. Otherwise, the primary natural resource is human capital; development of this resource has permitted the current level of development of IT and other high-tech industries. “In the main, industry has concentrated on manufactured products with high added values, in view of the country’s lack of most basic raw materials” [27].

New Zealand has a wide range of energy resources ranging from coal and hydrocarbons to hydroelectricity and geothermal energy. The New Zealand electricity system is mostly self-sufficient and is dominated by hydroelectric power. New Zealand's mineral resources are diverse, but mining is generally on a small scale. The total value of metallic and nonmetallic minerals mined in 1991 was \$215 million. New Zealand has large potential resources of iron ore [44].

Singapore has no natural resources or raw materials. Its primary resource is human capital. Singapore policies aim to develop this resource as fully as possible. This includes a tremendous emphasis on and investment in education at all levels and a liberal immigration policy regarding highly qualified/skilled professionals (see the section on education policies).

When we consider the role of raw materials in all three countries, it seems that their absence does not impede the development of IT production. On the contrary, it seems plausible that the paucity of raw materials drives countries to invest in high-technology industries that require few natural resources.

A brief summary of national culture for the three countries is presented in Table 7. Education is an additional aspect of national culture, closely related to literacy, but it is so important that it is discussed in much greater detail below.

The obvious conclusion from Table 7 is that, while the three countries are very different in terms of underlying cultural factors—ethnicity, language, and religion—they are remarkably similar in terms of indicators of general level of development—life expectancy, infant mortality, literacy, and newspaper circulation. One excellent example is the adoption of English as an almost universal second language in both Israel and Singapore.

Clearly, there is great variety among the three countries in terms of exogenous variables. Table 7 suggests that paucity of raw materials does not prevent development of IT production. Geographical location relative to major markets does not seem to affect the development of IT production either, but it does affect the direction of trade. National culture, likewise, seems to have little impact on IT development levels. Overall, exogenous variables do not affect IT industry success in small developed countries.

## Endogenous Mediating Factors

Endogenous mediating factors are those factors over which firms and countries can exercise control and so are the most significant in devising strategies for IT industry development.

## Domestic IT Use

Domestic IT use is an important endogenous mediating factor: It is an indication of the local sales base for IT industries; intensive local use of IT is clearly an important element in promoting the development of indigenous IT industries. This fact is recognized in the national IT plan for Singapore, discussed in greater detail below, which addresses both IT industry development and domestic IT use. No composite measures are available for the relative importance of IT, which makes this factor difficult to estimate. However, national spending on IT, the number of computers installed, and the volume of telecommunications services give some indication of IT's importance. Domestic IT use data for the three countries are summarized in Table 8.

IT spending relative to GDP, and number of computers installed: The value of the installed base of computers in Israel in June 1992 was estimated at \$2.2 billion. IT imports in 1991 totaled \$593 million, of which \$498 million were for hardware and \$95 million were for software [15].

It is estimated that New Zealand ranks seventh of thirty-two countries in terms of IT spending as a proportion of GDP, ahead of the United States and Japan $[24]$ . The size of the New Zealand market, excluding telecom services, was 3.5 percent of GDP $[37]$ . It is predicted that the New Zealand IT market will continue to grow at 7.5 percent per annum $[37]$ . In 1992 it was estimated that there were 425,000 PCs, 5,500 mid-range systems, and 80 mainframes in New Zealand; this equals one computer for every 7.7 people.

In Singapore, the domestic market accounted for \$1,065 million of IT sales (68.3 percent) in 1991. One of the strategic goals of the National IT Plan of Singapore is to facilitate all economic sectors to exploit IT fully. Government has led the way in IT use through the Civil Service Computerization Program and use of state-of-the-art technologies such as EDI and imaging. There was a significant increase in the number of firms using computers since 1985, with the highest growth rate in the smallest firms from 26 percent to 56 percent compared with an increase from 74 percent to 90 percent for the largest firms. The 1992 user survey conducted by the National Computer Board [42] showed that eight out of ten companies with more than ten employees use computers.

Firm strategies are represented here in terms of product/market selection strategies, global presence strategy, and labor cost strategy. Table 9 summarizes the strategies of firms involved in IT production.

Product/market selection strategy: The most successful Israeli IT firms have concentrated on unique products in which, at least initially, there is no competition and in which, by constant innovation, they can obtain and maintain a lead that is difficult for competitors to overtake. This strategy was epitomized by one entrepreneur who recently declared, “During the twenty years I managed Scitex, we never copied products. Our policy was always to be first in the world in every field we entered” (Efi Arazi, quoted in [20]). Other leading companies have followed similar strategies in communications compression, CAT scanner, and computer system management software.

This strategy has led to concentration of successful Israeli IT hardware firms in relatively small niche markets such as specialized rather than general-purpose hardware (e.g., CAT scanners, computerized printing, communications equipment). This is somewhat less categorical with respect to software; some firms have concentrated on special-purpose software (e.g., computer system management, data compression, data encryption, virus detection and correction), while a small number have succeeded with more general-purpose software such as application generators.

All of the New Zealand hardware firms have specialized in relatively small niche markets such as data encryption units, communications equipment, and so on. New Zealand software firms, on the other hand, produce a wide variety of software. Firms have succeeded in various fields; some examples are general-purpose software such as application generators, application software packages for specific industries, software for niche markets such as a geothermal data management system, and horizontal market software such as a voice-mail system. Most of the software produced in New Zealand was developed initially for the small New Zealand market; then most New Zealand-based software firms entered Australia, as a stepping stone toward internationalization; thus, Australia may be considered a natural extension of the local market.

Successful Singaporean IT firms have focused on quality products and services at internationally competitive prices. This was recently articulated by Johnny Moo, of CSA Holdings, one of the very early and successful IT companies: "My strategy has always been to provide world class products and services at about 80 percent of international prices; it has never been my strategy to offer low quality at say 50 percent of international price." Because of the very small domestic market, IT firms in Singapore start to "think regional" very early. Cultural affinity, English as the language of business, and an open economy are the main considerations in going regional. Successful IT companies in Singapore have had operations in the ASEAN countries. More recently, they have started to take an interest in China, India, and Vietnam.

In the first stage of development of the PC industry in Singapore, firms used cards and components from Taiwan to produce low-cost products. During the last two years, these firms have found it difficult to compete with the reduced prices of Compaq, Digital, and IBM, and have begun to offer high-quality products at competitive prices.

The software firms usually focus on systems integration and developing application packages for minicomputers (the so-called value-added retailer) and the PC platform for small businesses. In general, the Singapore IT industry has tended to focus on classes of general-purpose hardware and more special-purpose software without specializing in specific niches.

In summary, IT firms in small developed countries tend to adopt specialized and innovative products with high added value based on local expertise. They also tend to adopt either niche or more general market strategies for hardware products, but not both. Software seems to provide opportunities for both niche and general-purpose products in each country.

Global presence strategy: Because of the heavy dependence of all three countries on foreign trade, access to foreign markets and representation in them is of prime importance.

One effect in Israel of the relative remoteness from potential markets is to encourage export of high-value, low-volume products on which transportation costs have little impact; IT products clearly meet this constraint. Another effect is that only the largest firms can operate successfully overseas through branches or local subsidiaries; the vast majority of firms must develop strategic alliances with established firms in the export markets in order to be able to provide acceptable levels of service and support. Even the largest firms, with subsidiaries in the largest markets, must rely heavily on local representatives if they aspire to global coverage.

Another solution that seems to be increasingly popular is the concentration of R&D efforts of local firms in Israel while manufacturing, marketing, and service are performed by target-country subsidiaries $[22]$ . This, together with the prestigious list of overseas firms with local R&D, seems to indicate that the IT industry in Israel will increasingly concentrate on R&D while successive stages of the value chain are located in the principle markets $[2]$ .

Recognizing that on their own they are too small to compete in international markets, most New Zealand software firms have formed strategic alliances with larger multinational corporations, especially for worldwide marketing. The two major strategies are to work with worldwide marketing agents and local strategic alliances. For example, Platinum Software manages its own extensive dealer network in Australia and New Zealand, while in Singapore the products are marketed and distributed by local firms [8].

Like IT companies in New Zealand, IT firms in Singapore have tended to form strategic alliances with larger foreign firms, since they are too small on their own to compete internationally. A recent study $[23]$ showed that nearly 71 percent of IT companies have some kind of strategic alliance. Increasingly, the R&D efforts of Singapore IT companies are being located in countries such as China, India, and the Philippines, where high-quality technical manpower is available at lower costs.

Thus, Singapore's strategy seems to be the opposite of Israel's; while Singaporean firms tend to do R&D abroad and manufacture domestically, in Israel the tendency is to do R&D domestically and manufacture abroad. Regardless of R&D strategies, it is fairly clear that IT firms in small developed countries generally tend to form strategic alliances with, or buy into, symbiotic firms in major markets.

Labor cost strategy: IT industry firms are geared to high productivity rather than low wages. In fact, workers in the electronic equipment industry enjoy the highest average wages in Israel—about \$36,000—70 percent above the average of \$27,000 for all employees [6].

In New Zealand, likewise, IT firms are geared to high productivity rather than low wages. In May 1993 the average annual salary for all occupations was \$17,550 [32], whereas the average annual salary for programmers in the business services industry (which includes data processing, telecommunications, and legal) was \$22,684 and for senior systems analysts \$26,360 [45].

Multinational corporations active in Singapore initially utilized low labor costs to manufacture or assemble products designed and developed elsewhere. Over the years, the cost of labor in Singapore has steadily increased, and Singapore now has a larger pool of trained and qualified IT people. Multinational corporations and local IT companies are now able to focus on design, development, and manufacture of new products in Singapore. The second phase of Singapore's economic development, which started in 1980, was based on higher wages and increased productivity. Wages have risen steadily since 1980, and Singapore is now close to becoming a high-salary/high-productivity country. The average annual gross starting salary for fresh IT graduates is about \$22,000 and for systems analysts (about four years' work experience) about \$30,000.

The conclusion here is that IT firms in small developed countries do not build on low-cost labor but rather pay high salaries for skilled labor.

## Government IT Policies

The desirability of government industrial policies is a highly charged and hotly debated issue. The three countries studied here have adopted three very different stances in this respect, which makes a comparison of the policies and their presumed effects enlightening. The three aspects of IT policy studied here are summarized in Table 10.

Degree of active encouragement and R&D support: The government of Israel has for many years pursued a policy supporting industrial development, especially in industries with export potential. The main instruments of this policy are preferential tax laws for industry, export subsidies, and R&D funding. The tax law instrument applies primarily to depreciation and expense allowances specific to industrial development. This formulation applies equally to all industrial firms and clearly applies to IT hardware also. In the past, however, the law was interpreted to exclude software production as a nonindustrial activity; thus, the law did not encourage software production. The situation began to change in about 1986, when the government recognized that industrial production does not consist of only tangible goods, and culminated in 1993 with a reevaluation of the Research and Development Act. Following the reevaluation, all discriminations against the software industry were removed and it was put on an equal footing with all other industry. The export subsidies, largely in the form of insurance against foreign-trade risks and exchange-rate fluctuations, apply to all exports, regardless of category, and so apply equally to all forms of IT exports.

In Israel, direct government funding of R&D was about \$99.2 million in 1990—about 20 percent of all R&D in industry. “The rate of government funding varies by economic sector according to the Government’s R&D encouragement policy. The rate varies between 28 percent in ‘chemical and petroleum products,’ 21 percent in ‘electrical and electronic equipment’ … down to 4 percent in ‘food, drink, and tobacco’” [5]. Thus, IT is not the government’s top R&D priority but is favored more than most industries.

In line with a policy of deregulation and free-market economics that has been carried out since 1984, there is little government support for the IT industry in New Zealand [23, 33, 34]. The New Zealand government has a noninterventionist policy regarding industry development; thus, there are no special tax incentives, loans, or grants available for IT companies. The government believes that any intervention in the market would herald a return to the pre-1984 days of subsidy and tariff protection, something which it wants to avoid.

In response to a review of the IT industry in New Zealand in 1993 [37] and representations from industry groups, the government established an IT ministerial portfolio, an IT advisory group, and an information technology unit as part of the Ministry of Commerce. All of these initiatives, however, were limited to advising the government on IT policy, although a very small fund was established (\$14.5 million over three years) to supply venture capital to established companies.

The New Zealand government has rejected the idea of a fully developed plan for the development of the IT industry, as exists in Singapore and Taiwan. Instead, the government has adopted broader strategies that are not industry-specific. One such strategy is aimed at putting New Zealand exporters in the best possible position to take advantage of business opportunities in Asia. Another strategy proposes the maintenance of a consistent government macroeconomic policy, with the objectives of holding inflation at internationally competitive levels, maintaining a favorable and stable exchange rate, and reducing government expenditure [37].

Two main goals of the New Zealand Trade Development Board are substantially to increase the value of New Zealand exports per capita and to diversify the range of exports by adding more value to existing products and raw materials [37]. The board's support to New Zealand firms exporting IT generally comes in the form of advice and guidance.

Singapore tends to be unique in the degree to which government sets the direction and policies in almost every sphere of life, including family, education, health, savings, and investment. This is evident with respect to the area of IT policy, where the Singapore government has been highly proactive $[18]$ . Policies initiated in 1980 went through two phases: In the first phase, started in March 1980, a high-level ministerial Committee on National Computerization was formed to develop a strategic IT plan for Singapore. This committee identified two major goals; the first was to make Singapore into a software center, and the second was to improve productivity in government through the application of IT. To achieve these goals, the committee made four recommendations. The first two concerned the supply side: Increase the number of IT professionals and promote the software industry. The third concerned the demand side: Accelerate computerization in the government ministries and departments (civil service). The fourth concerned implementation of the IT policy: Create a National Computer Board (NCB) as the key coordinating agency for the IT plan.

The NCB was established in 1981 with the mission of strengthening both demand and supply of IT. To accomplish this mission, NCB adopted four functions: (1) administration of government computerization; (2) creation of a system of national accreditation for IT professionals; (3) incentives to development of software industry; and (4) establishment of training programs for IT professionals and users.

Government incentives to encourage IT production include preferential tax rates. Both IT producers and users are permitted rapid depreciation of IT equipment for tax purposes and employment of foreign professionals. Training is encouraged through a 70 percent subsidy from the Skills Development Fund. Furthermore, the government awards grants to firms to develop new products, both hardware and software. Institutions such as the Information Technology Institute, an arm of the National Computer Board, actively work with firms in developing prototypes of new software systems.

In the second phase of these IT policies, the government decided to broaden the scope of the first phase and develop a strategic IT framework at the national level. The general concept behind the National IT Plan (which was released in December 1986) was to raise the level of service and productivity of service companies and to reduce the costs of manufacturing companies through the use of IT.

Priority attached to IT development: Israeli government policy seems to have been somewhat ambivalent or inconsistent with respect to the IT industry; policies have been rationalized in recent years as described above. The percentage of R&D support for IT is in second place, not the highest; thus, it is important but not a top priority. The software industry, previously not considered a manufacturing sector and treated less generously than hardware production, is emerging as a top priority for government industrial policy. Some of the reasons for this change in position are the extremely high value added of software, the low investment required per worker, and the availability of a large pool of computer-literate scientists and engineers among recent immigrants from the former Soviet Union. Over 50 percent of immigrant engineers absorbed in high-tech industries are employed in software rather than hardware production.

As mentioned previously, there is no special priority attached to IT development in New Zealand. However, there is no special priority attached to any other development. Export subsidies for agriculture and industry were phased out from 1985 on, and import licensing and quotas were phased out in most industries in 1988. In a period of six years, the country was converted from one of the most regulated, protected, welfare-state economies in the western world into one of the most deregulated.

In Singapore, development of the IT industry and use of IT in all economic activities and to improve the quality of life are top priorities for government policy. This is reflected in the several initiatives undertaken by the government. A recent example is IT2000, a strategic initiative by the National Computer Board with five thrusts: (1) developing Singapore into a global hub; (2) boosting the economic engine; (3) linking communities locally and globally; (4) improving quality of life; and (5) enhancing the potential of individuals [41].

## Government Education Policies

As is true for all high-tech industries, IT depends on the existence of a highly educated pool of scientists, engineers, and production workers. Thus, in comparing the performance of IT industries in different countries, the availability of such a labor pool is essential.

General education levels: In Israel, kindergarten, elementary, and high school are free and compulsory until age sixteen. University education is not free but, thanks to heavy government subsidies, is relatively cheap at about \$2000 per annum. Currently there are about 100,000 students in six universities and other academic institutions, which equates to about one-third of all young adults. English is a required second language and this clearly supports development in the IT industry. However, there also seem to be some advantages to the use of a very different language—Hebrew—as the main language of discourse. The prime advantage is that this provides a relatively sheltered market for software products geared to the native language. Second, solution of problems peculiar to the local language—for example, right-to left script—provides products that can be adapted to markets with similar peculiarities—say, in Arabic and Urdu.

In New Zealand, kindergarten, primary (elementary), and secondary (high) school are free and compulsory from ages five through sixteen. Two-thirds of all students continue through high school until age seventeen. University is not free but here, too, it is heavily subsidized by the government. In 1993, the average fee for full-time tuition was \$870 per annum. There are seven universities, twenty-five polytechnics, and five colleges of education (teacher training). In 1991, there were approximately 88,000 students enrolled at university, 69,000 in polytechnics, and 8,000 in colleges of education [44]. Having English as a native language is another advantage in working with information technology.

Computer-science education: All six major universities in Israel have computer-science departments with undergraduate and graduate programs. In the 1992–93 academic year, 6,144 university students were enrolled in mathematics, statistics, computer science, and combinations thereof—about 85 percent in computer science.

Of these, 4,952 were undergraduates, 827 master's degree candidates, 348 doctoral candidates, and 17 diploma candidates [7]. Five of the six universities also have graduate programs in information systems. Nevertheless, one may often hear the complaint that there is a shortage of high-level software engineers. Apparently the number of university graduates in relevant fields has not kept pace with the burgeoning demand.

There have been complaints that New Zealand is educating too many lawyers and accountants and not enough scientists and engineers. There have also been complaints that educational institutions do not train technical specialists in the latest technologies or provide the skills demanded by industry [9]. There has been some improvement, however, in this area in recent years. Six of the seven universities now have computer-science departments, while five have separate departments of information systems. Almost all of these departments have been established within the last ten years. University enrollments in computer science and information systems have increased from between 10 percent and 70 percent per annum since the late 1980s [31]. There has also been a dramatic increase in the number of students studying computer and information science at polytechnics. Most polytechnics now offer diplomas and/or degrees in business information systems. There has also been much improvement regarding IT education in New Zealand schools. In 1994, the Ministry of Education embarked on an ambitious program to train 4,000 primary and secondary teachers per year in information technology. The IT program had trained 12,000 of New Zealand's 22,000 teachers by the end of 1996 [42].

Two universities and four polytechnics in Singapore offer programs in IT with options to specialize in: (1) computer science, (2) computer engineering, (3) CAD/CAM, (4) CIM, (5) information systems, and (6) software engineering. All schools in Singapore have computer centers and students become computer-literate quite early. Students in junior colleges can opt to study computer science as a subject. Most community centers have computers and offer adult computer-literacy courses. Other opportunities are available for people who do not qualify for admission to the computer-science programs in tertiary institutions in Singapore. Several private institutes offer courses and train students for British Computer Society examinations. About 50 percent of all students who take these exams are from Singapore. About 350 students were enrolled in the first offering (1994) of the degree program in computer science of the Open University, which offers people in the work force an opportunity to upgrade their education levels and skills.

The conclusion here—perhaps the most important of this study—is that government policies differ considerably among the countries studied and may explain differences in IT industry success.

In their study of nine countries in the Asia-Pacific region, Kraemer, Gurbaxani, and King [35] found that the overall pattern of growth in total computing expenditures was exponential. They did not find a difference between countries with national computer plans and those without such plans. Indeed, New Zealand, which had the least government support for the IT industry, had the highest annual rate of growth in computing expenditures as a percentage of GDP compared with the other eight

Asia-Pacific countries. Kraemer et al. concluded that there was no correlation between national computing plans and computing diffusion.

The evidence we have gathered, however, suggests that there is a link between government policies and domestic IT production. The national computerization plan adopted by Singapore and supported by appropriate investments in education seems to have had a dramatic impact on the spread of IT use and on IT production. Israel's IT policy is not as all-embracing as Singapore's but has nevertheless led to the establishment of an impressive industry, despite the fact that investments in education have not kept pace [3]. As shown in Table 10, the number of computer-science students is roughly the same as that in Singapore, which has half the population, and the general student body is relatively smaller. Thus, the future growth of IT production in Israel is threatened by underinvestment in education, and the industry is already experiencing a shortage of trained personnel. Just how critical the education gap could become is highlighted by the fact that scientists, engineers, or technicians comprise 57 percent of employees in the electronics industry [1].

In the case of New Zealand, a number of software companies have been relatively successful in international markets, but many of the most successful firms have been sold to foreign interests. Mostly this is because of the difficulties in expanding into international markets with insufficient capital. As Crocombe et al. [9] point out, limits in New Zealand's equity and debt markets force New Zealand entrepreneurs to finance operations with personal savings or contract programming. Kraemer and Dedrick [34] suggest that the low levels of private-sector R&D and the lack of government policies to stimulate such efforts do not bode well for the future of New Zealand industry in world markets. This is especially true of an R&D-intensive industry such as IT.

It does seem, therefore, that the local New Zealand IT industry is disadvantaged by the lack of government support. Although the software industry has been reasonably successful, Table 6 shows that IT exports are much lower in New Zealand than in Israel and Singapore. The continued growth of the Israeli industry, however, is threatened by insufficient investment in education. The effect of government intervention and its modalities is a potentially fruitful area for further study.

## Conclusions

WE HAVE DESCRIBED AND COMPARED THE STATE OF DEVELOPMENT of IT industries in three small, developed countries—Israel, New Zealand, and Singapore—all of which have seen the rapid development of their IT industries in recent years. We have identified factors that have facilitated IT industry development and have offered explanations for the differences among the three countries. While we do not exclude the possibility that other factors such as cultural, historical, or economic factors may have some influence on the differences among these three countries, some of the major findings suggested on the basis of the data that have been collected are listed below:

1. The three countries are very similar in terms of their general level of economic development.

2. All three countries have developed indigenous IT industries.

3. The IT industry seems to be most developed in Israel, followed by Singapore and then New Zealand.

4. Exogenous variables—geographical location, availability of raw materials, and national culture—do not seem to explain the differential levels of IT industry development. This view is bolstered by a recent review of Asian management that concluded that any cultural and environmental differences are probably ephemeral [14].

5. Of the endogenous variables, local IT use and firm strategies do not seem to explain differential developments in IT industries. There does seem to be a relationship, however, between the size of firms and the level of development.

6. The dominant factor that does seem to provide some explanation is government policy in promoting IT industries directly, supporting IT industry R&D, and in education policies to provide appropriately trained labor pools. The experience of Singapore suggests that massive government intervention can indeed promote the domestic use of IT and that this, in turn, encourages domestic IT production. At the same time, it appears that significant support concentrated on R&D, as in Israel, can promote even higher levels of IT industry growth.

The last conclusion supports the theoretical analyses of Grossman and Helpman $[16, 17]$ , namely, that there is an optimal level of government support for industries in small, open economies. Our findings suggest that government support for the IT industry (as in Singapore) is substantially better than no support at all (as in New Zealand), but that the optimal level of government support is somewhere in between (Israel). The government support that Grossman and Helpman find optimal are subsidies to R&D and to production of intermediates; these are two of the areas in which the countries studied here are most clearly differentiated. Thus, the empirical data here and the analysis of Grossman and Helpman are mutually supportive.

The importance attached by Reich [47] to appropriate educational programs is also supported by the findings presented here. Differences in the policies of governments seem to be major explanatory factors for differences in IT industry development. The backbone of development of IT industries in the countries studied here comprises innovative products developed by “symbolic analysts.” A shortage of the skills represented by this class of workers is a major impediment to the more rapid evolution of the countries studied. This underscores the crucial importance of government education policies to develop the requisite skills.

The development of Israel as an R&D center for foreign corporations and Singapore's assignment of R&D activities to China and India also support Reich's contention that symbolic analysis/problem solving is now performed in whichever location is most advantageous, irrespective of geographical and cultural differences.

Given the dominant role of government policies in explaining the differential development of IT industries in the countries studied, one may ask arises whether this is any different from the effect of government policies in large countries. The difference does not seem to lie in the degree of ethnic or cultural homogeneity of the countries studied, since Israel and Singapore are definitely not homogeneous in these dimensions while some much larger countries are. We suggest that the major advantage of small countries is the ability of governments to apply policies universally and consistently and that the larger the country, the more difficult this becomes. The three countries studied, all with populations well below 10 million, already fit Naisbitt's [40] millennial prediction of a world of small countries.

To summarize the current study, although the three countries studied have no inherent advantages in raw materials and only small domestic markets, they have the necessary IT infrastructure and the human skills. They are nimble and flexible and can find niche markets in which to specialize. Consequently, they have all developed substantial IT industries.

This study is only the first in what is clearly an area requiring much additional work if a better understanding of the dynamics of IT industry development in small countries is to be established. Some areas in which further research could yield valuable insights for more complete understanding and could assist policy makers and business executives in determining optimal courses of action are:

1. A follow-up study of the same countries a few years after the current study might indicate the extent to which the findings presented here persist and the extent to which they represent basic underlying phenomena.

2. A similar study should be conducted in a much larger number of small countries. This could show whether the findings reported here are indeed generalizable and might increase the robustness of the findings. Given a larger number of observations, it would also be possible to reach more confident conclusions concerning the effects of national culture and natural and geopolitical environments.

3. To address whether small countries do indeed have some advantages compared with large countries requires a comparative study of IT industries in large and small countries.

4. Work is required to adapt and to integrate existing economic models, or to devise new ones, to describe and explain the phenomena studied. A good model of this type would be invaluable to business decision makers and policy makers alike.
