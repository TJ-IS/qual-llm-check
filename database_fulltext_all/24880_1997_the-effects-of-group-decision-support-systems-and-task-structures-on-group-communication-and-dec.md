# The Effects of Group Decision Support Systems and Task Structures on Group Communication and Decision Quality

SIMON S.K. LAM

SIMON S.K. LAM is an Associate Professor in the School of Business at the University of Hong Kong. He received his Ph.D. from the Australian National University. His current research focuses on the managerial decision processes and quality management in service industries. He teaches courses in management science, operations management, and quality management.

ABSTRACT: This research examines whether structures of decision tasks moderate the effects of group decision support systems (GDSS) on patterns of group communication and decision quality of decision-making groups. It seeks to show that the effects of GDSS on decision-making processes and outcomes are task-structure-dependent, and the effects of GDSS cannot be evaluated on the basis of outcomes alone; decision processes must also be evaluated in order to understand how decisions are made and why GDSS can improve group outcomes in some situations but provide negative effects in others.

A controlled laboratory experiment was conducted with a $2 \times 3$ factorial between-subjects design, manipulating two independent variables: levels of support (GDSS support and no support) and task structures (additive, disjunctive, and conjunctive). Practicing managers were chosen as subjects. The discussion records of the decision-making process were coded using a coding scheme. These communication patterns formed the first dependent variables. Another dependent variable was decision quality. The results support the hypothesis that the structures of a decision task moderate the effects of GDSS on both the patterns of group communication and the decision quality of a decision-making group. GDSS significantly improve decision quality in disjunctive and conjunctive tasks. GDSS also significantly alter patterns of group communication in disjunctive and conjunctive tasks. However, no significant differences in decision quality and patterns of group communication exist between groups using GDSS and face-to-face groups in additive tasks.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES:, decision tasks and processes, group decision quality, group decision support systems.

A SIGNIFICANT AMOUNT OF DECISION MAKING IN ORGANIZATIONS is performed in group meetings, and nonproductive group meetings can have major consequences for the success of organizations. Group decision support systems (GDSS) are a development in computer technology designed as a tool to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of group meetings involving problem solving and decision making [26]. GDSS can be defined as interactive computer-based systems that combine communication, computer, and decision technologies to support problem formulation and solution in group meetings [11]. GDSS are distinguishable from other computer systems from which they are developed by their focus on group rather than individual activities. The goal of GDSS is to improve the process of group decision making by removing common communication barriers, providing techniques for structuring decision analysis, and systematically directing the pattern, timing, or content of discussion [11, 26, 45]. By reducing this “process loss” [43] associated with group meetings, GDSS aim to increase the efficiency, reliability, and quality of group decision making.

Whether GDSS technology can really improve the decision outcomes of group decision making is an important research question. Although there has been substantial research in this area, the effectiveness of GDSS remains an open issue $[10]$ . The literature on GDSS has progressed beyond the early thinking that GDSS have unequivocal effects, regardless of other moderating variables. Instead, what is considered more important and of greater interest is how GDSS affect group decision quality and under what conditions $[2]$ .

In this study the structure of the decision task is isolated as a potentially important variable that may moderate the effects of GDSS use on decision-making groups. This research also recognizes the importance of understanding the group communication process as a key to explaining the relationships between the effects of GDSS technology and group decision making. Hence, this research seeks to provide empirical evidence of how the effects of GDSS vary across different task structures and how these effects can be explained through the study of the group communication process in decision-making groups.

## GDSS Research

THERE HAS BEEN A SUBSTANTIAL AMOUNT OF RESEARCH ACTIVITY on the effects of GDSS on group decision making $[10, 20, 37]$ . These studies attempted to establish the effectiveness of groups supported by GDSS in comparison with groups not supported by GDSS. However, generalizations about the effects of GDSS on group decision making have been plagued by inconsistencies among study findings. Some studies found strongly positive results $[7, 15]$ , while others found mildly positive results $[1, 16]$ , and still others found mixed, neutral, or negative results $[2, 17, 45, 46]$ .

Qualitative and quantitative reviews of empirical work in this field have attempted to integrate the seemingly inconsistent results by attributing differences to one or more situational factors. Based on an input-process-output scheme, Nunamaker et al. [36] proposed a research framework for the analysis of the effects of GDSS. This framework suggested that the effects of GDSS are contingent on a myriad of group, task, context, and GDSS features that differ from situation to situation. Using meta-analysis procedures, Benbasat and Lim [3] quantitatively integrated the results of thirty-one experimental studies on the effects of GDSS use. Their results showed that GDSS effects were moderated by group, context, and technology variables.

## Task Structures

Of all of the factors that have been suggested as moderating the effects of GDSS use, the variable “group task” is emerging as an important variable. Poole, Seibold, and McPhee [40, p. 88] point out that “the general variable group task is emerging as an especially important variable, often accounting for as much as 50 percent of the variance in group performance.” DeSanctis and Gallupe [11] note the importance of tasks and task differences in GDSS research. Kraemer and Pinsonneault [30] provide a conceptual framework for analyzing the impacts of GDSS on group process and outcomes, and task differences are depicted as an important class of contextual variables for GDSS use.

In spite of the importance of the task dimension, little effort has been devoted to a systematic analysis of the differences between tasks and how these differences affect group performance. GDSS research has generally been based on empirical evidence gathered in relation to a single task, and the growing volume of research on GDSS has not focused on a specific class of task. Studies have been lumped together in efforts to understand process and quality variable effects of small-group decisions. Little systematic consideration has been given to the differences in tasks from one study to another.

Group tasks can be classified according to the degree of complexity [30], for example, simple versus complex tasks. Gallupe [15] compared the effects of GDSS support between a simple and a complex task and suggested that GDSS were more suitable for a complex task. Group tasks can also be classified according to the function of the task [31], for example, decision-making task versus idea-generation task. Hollingshead, McGrath, and O'Conner [24] studied the effects of GDSS among different functional tasks and found no difference in performance between computer groups and face-to-face groups for idea-generation and decision-making tasks, but face-to-face groups performed better than their computed-mediated counterparts on negotiation and intellectual tasks. Moreover, group tasks can also be classified according to the task structure associated with a particular task [43]. Task structure can be defined as the overall configuration of the problem space [35] that underlies the task. In every task performed by a group, there is a set of collective or shared purposes that evolve into a set of strategies for accomplishing these tasks [33]. Task structure, therefore, provides a procedural orientation or the “rules of the game” for how members in the group make decisions. Task structure affects the group’s need for problem analysis and procedural orientation and planning. That is, with different task structures, the group faces different procedural requirements for accomplishing its objective(s). Steiner [43] identified three types of task structures typically imposed on decision groups: additive, disjunctive, and conjunctive.

In an additive task, each group member contributes a part to the group decision, and group performance is determined by the aggregation of individual effort. Each group member has similar responsibilities and information. According to Zaccaro and Lowe [47], each group member must maximize his or her own individual performance in order to maximize the overall group effort in an additive task.

In a disjunctive task, a group selects one optimal solution from an array of solutions proposed by individual group members $[32, 43]$ . The success of the decision group depends on whether there is a member who has the ability to solve the problem and whether group members recognize and accept the superior contribution of an individual's solution to the exclusion of all others. The success of a disjunctive task is therefore heavily influenced by the performance of the members who make the greatest contribution.

In a conjunctive task, however, the successful decision can only be achieved when all the group members maximize their efforts because all group members have unique information. Conjunctive tasks differ from additive tasks because each group member has different information. Conjunctive tasks also differ from disjunctive tasks because no single group member has enough information to suggest the correct answer or optimal solution. A group achieves a successful outcome only when all of the information held by individual group members is accurately communicated to other group members. The whole group will fail if only one member fails to contribute to the decision task. The success of the decision task is heavily influenced by the performance of the member who makes the least contribution.

Although GDSS researchers have studied some aspects of task differences and their effects on GDSS use, these studies focused primarily on the complexity and the type of task that groups perform. The structure of the task has received no attention. Within the social-psychology literature, Steiner [43] and others [32, 47, 48] have suggested that the performance and success of a decision group depend basically on the structure of the group's task. Some researchers also argue that task structure is a more fundamental concept than the functional type of a task [19, 23, 43]. According to Steiner [43], even within a decision-making task, different task structures can exist, and these subordinate task structures will alter how a group approaches the main task and eventually affect the behavior of the group members and the performance of the decision groups. Although the main objective of GDSS technology is to provide support for the group decision process, we still lack empirical studies of how GDSS technology may affect group decision performance under different task structures. This study attempts to fill this gap in the current GDSS literature.

## Research Questions and Hypotheses

THE PRIMARY OBJECTIVE OF THIS STUDY IS TO EXAMINE whether the structure of a decision task moderates the effects of GDSS on the patterns of group communication and decision quality in a decision-making group. The secondary objective is to examine the relationship between the patterns of group communication and decision quality in face-to-face and GDSS-supported groups working on a decision task. Within the framework of the objectives, this study seeks to answer the following questions:

1. Are there any systematic differences in the decision quality between GDSS-supported and face-to-face decision-making groups across different task structures?

2. Are there any systematic differences in the patterns of group communication between GDSS-supported and face-to-face decision-making groups across different task structures?

Gallupe [15], Jarvenpaa, Rao, and Huber [28], and Cass, Heintz, and Kaiser [7] reported significant positive effects of GDSS on the decision quality of decision-making groups. Although these results suggest that the decision quality of decision-making groups is significantly affected by the use of GDSS, the effects of GDSS on decision quality may differ among decision groups working with different task structures. This study attempts to show that there are significant differences in decision quality between GDSS-supported and face-to-face decision-making groups and that the effects of GDSS are moderated by the structure of the task on which the groups are working. Gallupe, DeSanctis, and Dickson [16] have suggested that GDSS are particularly helpful in the groups working on more difficult tasks. GDSS can be helpful not only in difficult task types but in difficult task structures. When the task structure is straightforward (i.e., in an additive task where all members have the same information and no information sharing or task coordination is required), GDSS support produces no gains in decision quality. But GDSS can improve the decision quality of the groups working with disjunctive and conjunctive tasks when task coordination and information sharing play a major role in the quality of the group decision outcome.

H1: There is a significant interaction effect between the decision quality of GDSS groups and non-GDSS groups across different task structures.

H1a: There is no significant difference between the decision quality of GDSS groups and non-GDSS groups working on an additive task.

H1b: There is a significant difference between the decision quality of GDSS groups and non-GDSS groups working on a disjunctive task.

H1c: There is a significant difference between the decision quality of GDSS groups and non-GDSS groups working on a conjunctive task.

This study hypothesizes that the effects of the use or nonuse of GDSS on the patterns of group communication are moderated by the structure of the decision task in which the groups are working. When the task structure is additive, with all members having the same information and minimal need for task coordination, GDSS support produces no direct effects on how groups communicate to reach a decision. However, GDSS alters the group communication patterns when the groups are working on a disjunctive or a conjunctive task, when task coordination and information sharing play a significant role in the quality of the group decision outcome.

H2: There is a significant interaction effect between the patterns of communication of GDSS groups and non-GDSS groups across different task structures.

H2a: There is no significant difference between the patterns of communication of GDSS groups and non-GDSS groups working on an additive task.

H2b: There is a significant difference between the patterns of communication of

GDSS groups and non-GDSS groups working on a disjunctive task.

H2c: There is a significant difference between the patterns of communication of GDSS groups and non-GDSS groups working on a conjunctive task.

## Method

THE RESEARCH METHOD USED IN THIS STUDY WAS AN EXPERIMENT with a 2×3 factorial between-subjects design, manipulating two independent variables: group decision support (computer support and no computer support) and task structure (additive, disjunctive, and conjunctive). Subjects were practicing managers. With a personnel recruitment exercise the experimental task, the experiment was administered when the subjects were participating in a management training course. The dependent variables examined were decision quality, decision time, and patterns of group communication.

## Subjects

Most of the experimental research on GDSS involved students as participants. Researchers have widely discussed the validity of using student subjects for experimental research $[5, 21]$ . The criticisms of using student subjects include the low generalizability of the experimental results for practicing managers and the students' lack of knowledge of how to perform real organizational tasks in the experiment. In this study, practicing managers participating in a five-day management training program were chosen as subjects in order to enhance the generalizability of the findings for organizations. Also, a training environment represents a stable research environment within which the researcher can carefully manipulate the characteristics of the experimental task, while controlling the other variables, and a large number of research participants were needed for proper statistical analysis; this made this group an attractive subject pool.

The subjects were 216 midlevel managers from thirty-five diverse organizations participating in a five-day management training program. The thirty-five organizations included five conglomerates, eleven financial institutions, five trading companies, eight manufacturers, and six retail companies. Typical job titles of the subjects included sales manager, administrative officer, finance manager, and accounting officer. Before the start of the experiment, each subject was given a questionnaire that asked for their personal demographics, educational background, work experience, typing skill, computer knowledge, and experience with group decision making.

The subjects ranged in age from 24 to 41 with a mean age of 28.4 years; 73 percent were male; 91 percent had at least a high school education; 67 percent had an undergraduate degree. Subjects had been employed in a full-time job from four to ten years and had been in their present position from two to nine years with a mean tenure of 6.2 years. Subjects' average typing skills were very good; all were frequent computer users in their job. The subjects were also familiar, on average, with 3.4 computer applications.

Subjects were randomly assigned to one of the six experimental conditions in three-person groups. The group size of three was chosen because the average number of people attending a decision-making meeting is less than five [14], and since most of the experimental research in GDSS involved a group size of three [16, 31, 46], the study results could be compared with the previous findings. The study used a total of seventy-two groups (twelve groups per treatment).

Statistical tests were conducted to confirm that the subjects' backgrounds were not significantly different across the six experimental treatments. Thus, the effort to have an even spread of participants across the treatment was fruitful.

In order to prepare subjects for the experiment, all were exposed to the concepts of group decision-making processes and GDSS technology during the training course. This helped to control individual differences in their understanding and ability to engage in group decision making and their exposure to GDSS. At the beginning of each session, the members of the GDSS-supported group were given a brief training session on the use of GDSS.

## Experimental Task

The experimental task was adapted from The Management Game developed by Burst and Schlesinger [6]. The management game involves a fictitious commodities company, Consolidated Commodities, Inc. (ConCom), which has recently created a new specialty foods division. The specialty foods division's main task is to promote a new line of imported Italian food products. Participants were asked to assist ConCom, Inc. in selecting a product manager for the new division and were given information about the fictitious company, the foods division, and a job description for the new product manager.

The exercise was selected because Burst and Schlesinger [6] provided a realistic and relevant format for the group decision-making process. Most hiring decisions are made in a group, and most middle managers have experience in the decision process of hiring for an important position. In addition, since the task was not dependent on the functional specialty of the participant, all group members could participate actively in the decision process [44]. Furthermore, the task was very flexible, which allowed the structure of the task to be manipulated to create a research environment for this study. This task has also been successfully used in other experimental studies [34]. Another advantage of using this task is that Burst and Schlesinger [6] provided an expert solution with which the group's decision could be compared directly.

## Computer Aided Helper

In the case of the GDSS group, subjects were provided with a GDSS called “Computer Aided Helper” (CAH). The author was involved in the design, development, and testing of CAH. It ran in the AIX operating system environment, IBM’s version of the Unix operating system. AIX is a multiuser operating system and provided facilities for exchanging messages and data between various programs within the CAH system. The software ran on an IBM RISC System/6000 model 320. The configuration comprised X-stations and IBM 3151 terminals linked with the server by an ethernet connection.

The CAH system was organized into three modules. The first module, Group Talk, enabled users to enter, send, view, modify, and delete messages generated by the group members. CAH had two windows in each station: a private window and a public window. Each workstation had a full-screen window running the private window in which users could enter individual messages and then send them to the public window. A second window allowed users to toggle back and forth between the private and the public windows so that they could view the messages sent by other group members. This module also allowed group members to modify and delete their messages on the public window.

The second module, Decision Aid, provided a multicriterion decision model support for users. This module enabled users to define the problem, select decision criteria, weigh decision criteria, and define and evaluate alternatives. These functions were useful for complex decisions with multiple alternatives and criteria. This module would therefore help in the experimental task in this study. Personnel selection required consideration of different criteria (e.g., academic qualification, working experience, and personal character) to evaluate candidates (the alternatives) who applied for the job. This part of the program allowed decision makers to express their subjective preferences by weighing the relative importance of different criteria and then systematically evaluating how well the alternatives met the criteria.

The third module, Voter, provided users with a range of voting features. In a typical vote, a ballot was initiated by one of the users, voted upon by the participants in the meeting, and finally the results of the vote were displayed. The module also included a set of yes/no and rank-order voting methods. Under this module, any member could initiate a vote and propose a voting method.

The design of the system was similar to GDSS systems used in the experimental design in the major GDSS research centers (like SAMM—Software Aided Meeting Management—at the University of Minnesota, and GroupSystems at the University of Arizona). These features had been identified as appropriate for supporting the communication needs of the group $[11, 26]$ , and similar GDSS features were being used successfully in other studies of GDSS $[12, 28]$ .

## Experimental Manipulations

The experiment involved two GDSS environments. In the GDSS treatment, each group member worked in a decision room. The room had tables arranged in a U-shaped pattern, surrounded by swivel chairs with a private terminal in front of each chair. Microphones were placed on the tables and an audio recorder was mounted at the front of the U-shaped table. In addition, a chair was separately positioned for the administrator to watch the group session. The administrator monitored the process of the experiment but did not participate in the group decision process. The administrator only answered questions related to the use of the GDSS and ensured that the system was operating properly.

Face-to-face groups without computer support made decisions in a room identical to the one used by the GDSS groups. No GDSS was provided for these groups but a flipchart was provided for recording and displaying public information. In both GDSS and non-GDSS treatments, each group member had the use of a pencil, paper, and a small hand calculator. Figures 1 and 2 demonstrate the physical layout of the decision room.

Three types of task structures were manipulated by defining group member roles and responsibilities concerning the division and sharing of information. These structures were taken directly from the typology developed by Steiner [43]. Three pieces of information were provided to the group about each candidate: a resume, a detailed work history, and a confidential character evaluation report. In the additive task structure, each group member received all three pieces of information and was asked to discuss the decision and to help determine the group ranking. Thus, each group member had the same information and the group ranking depended on the aggregation of combined inputs from the individual group members.

In the disjunctive task, each group member received all three pieces of information, ranked the candidates individually, but was told that the group was then to decide which individual member's ranking was optimal. Thus, each group member had the same information and the group ranking or solution depended on the group's ability to recognize the superior contributions of a solution provided by an individual group member.

In the conjunctive task structure, each group member received only one type of information about each of the three candidates. For example, one group member received all of the resumes for the three candidates while another received all of the confidential performance evaluation reports for the three candidates. Thus, each group member had different information and the group ranking depended on the individual contributions of each group member, as the holder of specific information.

## Experimental Procedures

The selected members of each group reported to a seminar room at an appointed time. The researcher introduced the experiment as a session for assessing the decision-making skills of the participants. Then they were told that they would be participating in a simulation exercise and that they would be role-playing the part of a member of a strategic decision-making team involved in selecting a new product manager from a set of available candidates.

After the introduction, subjects were asked to fill out a questionnaire requesting their personal demographics, educational background, work experience, computer knowledge, and attitudes to and experience with group decision making.

Each subject was given an information packet with case material, an instruction sheet, and individual information sheets according to the experimental group to which they were assigned. All groups were asked to follow the same general decision-making steps:

1. Read and analyze the material individually,

2. Discuss the material,

No verbal exchanges were allowed in the GDSS-supported groups. Decision forms were provided to record the final decision. The time they began was noted, but groups were not given a time limit for completing the task. They were asked to hand in their decision on the decision forms immediately after the decision was noted, and the time they had reached a decision was marked on the decision sheet.

After the group handed in their decision sheet, individual group members were asked to fill out a set of questionnaires that asked about how they reached the decisions on the task they had just finished and about the group's decision-making environment. These questions served as a check on the success of the experimental manipulation.

Finally, after each group had filled in the questionnaires, a debriefing session was held. This session served to collect qualitative information regarding any questions, comments, or feedback on the decision-making session. The group was also asked to comment on the experiment as a whole.

## Dependent Variables

The two dependent variables examined were decision quality and patterns of group communication. To measure decision quality, the overall rank ordering for the solution provided by the group was evaluated. Each of the six possible rank orderings of the three candidates was graded in terms of most to least preferred based on the goals and objectives described by Burst and Schlesinger [6]. The most preferred rank ordering was assigned a score of six points and the least preferred rank ordering was assigned a score of one point. Intermediate rankings were assigned points ranging from two through five. Higher scores indicated higher decision quality.

The codings of the group discussion records were used as the measure for patterns of group communication. The discussion records, cassette tapes for the face-to-face meetings, and copies of the electronic logs for the GDSS groups were analyzed by two coders. The coding for the discussion content was carried out according to the coding scheme developed by Gettys et al. [18], according to which the discussion records could be divided into separate units that consisted of a single comment or argument. These units could then be classified into eight categories: decision proposals, support arguments, clarifications, critical arguments, queries, procedure-related comments, other unrelated comments, and total number of comments. For the GDSS session, the scheme also included system-related comments to capture comments related to the system and its operations.

## Results

Manipulation Checks

CHECKS ON BOTH EXPERIMENTAL MANIPULATIONS WERE EMBODIED in the post-experimental questionnaire. To assess the task structure manipulation, participants were asked, “Which of the following best describes how you made a decision on the task you have just finished? (1) Each group member has the same information and the group works together as a team to reach the best solution; (2) each group member has the same information and the group selects one best solution from an array of solutions championed by individual group members; or (3) each group member has different information and each group member contributes to reach the best solution.” Of the 216 responses, only 7 were misclassified. The single sample chi-square demonstrated a good fit between the experimental conditions (additive task, disjunctive task, and conjunctive task) and the subjects’ perception of the task structure in the experiment ( $\chi^{2}=391.86$ ; df=4, p<0.00001).

Next, decision support manipulation was assessed using responses to a postexperimental question, “Which of the following best describes your group’s decision-making environment? (1) a face-to-face meeting; or (2) a computer-supported environment.” Of the 216 responses, only 6 were misclassified. The single sample chi-square showed a good fit between the experimental conditions (non-GDSS and GDSS-supported) and the subjects’ perception of the decision support environment in the experiment ( $\chi^{2}=192.67; df=1, p<0.00001$ ). Both manipulations, then, appear to have been successful.

## Decision Quality

Table 1 summarizes means and standard deviations for the measure of decision quality across different experimental treatments. Table 2 presents the results of the ANOVA on the variables.

A two-way ANOVA on decision quality revealed significantly higher mean scores in GDSS-supported groups when compared with non-GDSS supported groups ( $F_{1,66} = 12.689$ , p < 0.05). The decision quality of GDSS-supported groups was significantly different from non-GDSS-supported groups. On average, GDSS-supported groups outperformed non-GDSS-supported groups in terms of decision quality.

A two-way ANOVA on decision quality also revealed a significant interaction effect $F_{2,,66}=3.17, p<0.05$ between GDSS support and task structure. H1 was therefore supported. The interaction effect indicated that the effect of GDSS on decision quality was dependent on the decision task structure supported by the systems. In the additive task structure, the simple effect revealed no significant differences between non-GDSS and GDSS-supported groups $F_{1,66}=0.0564, n.s.$ , at the significance level of 0.05. GDSS support was found to have no significant impact on the decision quality of additive tasks. H1a was supported. In the disjunctive task structure, however, the simple effect analysis demonstrated a significantly higher decision quality in GDSS-supported groups than in non-GDSS-supported groups $F_{1,66}=14.415, p<0.01$ . In the conjunctive task structure, simple effect analysis again showed a significantly higher decision quality in GDSS-supported groups than in non-GDSS-supported groups $F_{1,66}=4.561, p<0.05$ . GDSS support was found to have a significant impact on improving the decision quality in disjunctive and conjunctive tasks. H1b and H1c were therefore supported.

## Summary and Conclusions

THE PRIMARY OBJECTIVE OF THIS RESEARCH HAS BEEN to examine whether structures of decision tasks mediate the effects of group decision support systems (GDSS) on patterns of communication and decision quality of decision-making groups. This research also examines the relationship between patterns of group communication and decision quality to see if a change in group communication can have a direct impact on group decision quality.

## Decision Quality

The results of this experiment show that in general a GDSS increases the decision quality of the decision-making groups. There exists a main effect between GDSS support and group decision quality. This result is consistent with Gallupe [15], Jarvenpaa, Rao, and Huber [28], and Cass, Heintz, and Kaiser [7] who reported significant positive effects of GDSS on decision quality of decision-making groups. However, a conclusion regarding the main effect between GDSS support and decision quality can only be stated with caution due to the presence of the interaction effect on task structures. It is found in this experiment that GDSS significantly improves group decision quality when the decision groups are working in a disjunctive or conjunctive task situation. GDSS results in no significant quality gain when the groups are working with an additive task. The results indicate that the effectiveness of GDSS for improving group decision quality tends to increase as the “complexity” of the task structure increases. When the task structure is straightforward, GDSS support results in no decision quality gains, but GDSS can improve the decision quality of the groups working with the more complex disjunctive and conjunctive tasks.

## Patterns of Group Communication

The results of this experiment suggest that the effects of GDSS on group communication vary as a function of task structures. There is a strong interaction effect in the patterns of group communication between GDSS-supported and face-to-face decision-making groups across different task structures. In additive tasks, GDSS support made no significant difference in terms of how groups communicated in reaching a decision. GDSS groups working on a disjunctive or conjunctive task engaged in more discussion, were more critical in their discussion, and spent more time discussing how to approach the decision task than the face-to-face groups. These results indicate that the effects of GDSS on the patterns of group communication between GDSS-supported and face-to-face decision-making groups are moderated by the structure of the decision task at which the groups are working.

The results provide strong support for the existence of a systematic relationship between the patterns of communication and the decision quality among the GDSS-supported and face-to-face decision-making groups. The results also provide empirical evidence for the understanding of how GDSS shapes the patterns of group communication and can explain why GDSS can improve group quality in some task structures but is not helpful in others.

## GDSS, Patterns of Group Communication, and Decision Quality

How does the use of GDSS affect the patterns of group communication in this experiment? Several investigations $[29, 41, 42]$ have suggested that computer-mediated communication results in deindividuated interaction. According to this explanation, computer-mediated communication causes persons to become “depersonalized”

because it reduces social-context information (i.e., nodding approval or frowning with displeasure and social artifacts in the physical environment), resulting in persons acting in a more impulsive and assertive manner, with less regard for the feelings of those who are on the receiving end of a communication. This opinion is used to explain why people are typically less inhibited and more critical when communicating in a computer-mediated environment. This argument is consistent with the empirical evidence in this experiment. In this study, members of GDSS groups were more critical during group discussion and they also perceived a higher level of conflict behavior than did face-to-face groups. Data gathered in the debriefing sessions also supported this explanation. Members in GDSS groups reported more open discussion and saw computer-mediated communication as a lesser threat and a more impersonal medium than face-to-face communication.

Although prior research has shown that GDSS groups are generally more critical $[41, 42]$ , in this experiment GDSS support changed the patterns of group communication only in the disjunctive and conjunctive groups, and not in the additive groups. These results can be explained by adaptive structuration theory $[38]$ . Decision-making groups utilize different GDSS features in different situations so as to meet the requirement of the task and GDSS cannot be expected to have the same effects in all task situations.

In a conjunctive task, the information requirement of the task increases $[43]$ . Individual members (even highly competent ones) find it difficult to work through the task alone, because they do not possess all the information needed to arrive at a viable decision. Consequently, they find it necessary to interact with other group members in order to obtain or properly interpret task-relevant information. Thus, the increased information requirement of the task structure decreases the likelihood that individual members will be able to work successfully through the task without interacting with other group members. The role of group interaction and communication in determining group performance therefore increases. As the GDSS groups seem to recognize the specific requirements of a conjunctive task, they then adopt the specific features that a GDSS provides (the more open and impersonal computer-mediated communication channel) to facilitate the decision-making process. As a result, GDSS groups working on a conjunctive task generate significantly more critical arguments and more group interaction (in terms of the total numbers of comments) when compared with non-GDSS groups working on the same task.

A disjunctive task requires group members to evaluate critically and to select the most preferred decision among group members $[43]$ . During these high-conflict processes, the group tends toward “groupthink” $[27]$ or to pursue conformity within a group rather than to find the optimal decision. Conformity pressures and conflict minimization result in less information sharing, poorer communication, fewer challenged assumptions, and suboptimal decisions $[27]$ . A face-to-face process becomes less effective because the merits of each individual proposal are not fully explored. Thus, in a disjunctive task, a face-to-face process may exacerbate tendencies toward conformity and “groupthink.” The procedural requirement of a disjunctive task can be higher than that of both the additive and the conjunctive task $[43]$ . As the group needs to select an optimal solution for the decision proposed by its members, the need for organizing how the group decides on the optimal solution becomes critical. The results on this experiment show that GDSS groups seem to recognize these task requirements and adapt GDSS effectively to meet these requirements. GDSS support seems to provide an environment so that group members can better combine their efforts to proceed effectively with the task and the critical evaluation of the information.

In an additive task, each member has the same information and the group has to reach a decision collectively [43]. In this experiment, the results revealed no significant difference in the patterns of group communication between GDSS and face-to-face groups. One explanation is that, unlike the conjunctive task, each group member has similar information and the group is not required to evaluate each member's proposed solutions. Cooperation and coordination among group members are less critical to the success of the additive task. Although GDSS can provide a critical process for decision making and provide a communication means to discuss decision procedures, group members may not see the need to adopt these features. As a result, GDSS support does not make a significant impact on the patterns of group communication.

The results of this experiment also show a strong relationship between how groups communicate and decision quality. While computer-mediated discussion is widely experienced as impersonal and critical $[22]$ , previous studies have shown that critical discussion processes do not have a consistently positive effect on decision quality $[9, 42]$ . The results of this study provide some support for the view that critical deliberations during the decision process can improve the decision quality of the decision-making group. One explanation for this inconsistency is that, in GDSS research, different scholars have used different definitions for measuring critical arguments. For example, in Sproull and Keisler $[42]$ , critical arguments were measured in terms of uninhibited speech such as swearing or insulting among group members. In Connolly, Jessup, and Valacich $[9]$ , critical arguments were measured by the critical remarks that group members expressed to oppose a decision. In the field of conflict management, scholars have divided group conflict into task and social types $[4, 25]$ . Falk $[13]$ notes that task conflict is constructive and represents a difference of opinion regarding the issues involving the analysis of the problem, interpretation of information, criteria for achieving a solution, and selection of solutions, whereas social conflict is disruptive and reflects clashes stemming from personality and personal differences.

In this study, GDSS groups were more impulsive and assertive and generated significantly more critical discussion and arguments than non-GDSS groups. However, upon careful examination of the content of these critical arguments, uninhibited speech such as swearing, insulting, or hostile comments only made up a very small portion (less than 2 percent) of these critical comments. Almost all of these critical comments represented differences of opinion regarding the task when group members tried to resolve conflicting ideas, rather than the personality or personal differences among group members. GDSS groups outperformed non-GDSS groups when groups were participating in a more critical task evaluation. According to the discussion above, the cause of the increase in decision quality can be attributed to the fact that GDSS tended to increase the task focus and critical decision evaluation in disjunctive and conjunctive tasks, which in turn affected the decision quality of the decision-making groups.

While there have been attempts to integrate findings about the effectiveness of GDSS and to draw general conclusions about decision groups' behavior and quality, so far no general theory of GDSS effectiveness has appeared. This research suggests the possibility that no single general theory can encompass and deal simultaneously with the complexity of factors that have an effect on how groups use GDSS in different situations. It is very difficult to have a single theory explain or predict the relationship of all the relevant factors and how they are related to group decision processes and quality. Instead, it may be necessary to settle for a number of smaller theories, each of which is able to offer an explanation for a specific aspect or phase of the GDSS decision process and quality under certain specific circumstances. While this is not as elegant an approach as some might desire, the development of subtheories of process-performance relationships would nonetheless represent a notable improvement over the current understanding of group processes with GDSS use and its impact on group effectiveness.

## Future Research

A number of future research directions emerge out of the current study. First, it would be useful to carry the analysis of patterns of communication further by investigating how GDSS can enhance the ability of a decision-making group in developing a group decision. Groups supported by GDSS may not only communicate differently, as the current research demonstrates, they may also follow different sequences of decision paths compared with nonsupported groups $[38]$ . This study focused on the types of communication activities in a decision-making group, not on the order or sequence in which these activities are enacted in a group decision. This research clearly demonstrates the way in which patterns of group communication can be examined in a decision-making meeting. Using a modified coding system, the different types of communication activities can be coded and mapped over different decision phases. The decision paths between the GDSS-supported and nonsupported groups and across different tasks can be compared, and the relationship between group decision paths and decision quality can be analyzed. Studying how GDSS can shape the decision path should enable us to examine directly how GDSS influences the way a decision is formed in a decision-making group.

Second, the objectives of this research were to study whether the structures of a decision task mediate the effects of GDSS. Using a decision task as the experimental task, the structures of task were manipulated by varying the roles of group members and information distribution among group members. A logical extension of this study would be to replicate the study using a different type of task. For example, an idea-generating task $[31]$ could be used and the structure of the task manipulated in a manner similar to the procedures in this study. The results could then be compared to see whether the same results hold true for different task types.

Finally, the groups in this study used the GDSS for only a single session in reaching their decision. Chidambaram [8] shows that the effects of GDSS can vary over time. While the findings of this study may hold for a one-time GDSS use, they may not hold when using GDSS across different task structures over a period of time. Long-term use can reveal the effects of decision processes more clearly and may alter the effects of GDSS due to the decreased novelty of the technology [59]. Rather than gather data after a single meeting, future research could observe and gather data over a series of meetings, enabling the longitudinal impacts of GDSS and task structures on group decision making to be studied over time.
