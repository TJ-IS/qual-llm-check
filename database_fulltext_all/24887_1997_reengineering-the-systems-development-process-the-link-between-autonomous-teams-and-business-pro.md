# Reengineering the Systems Development Process: The Link between Autonomous Teams and Business Process Outcomes

BRIAN D. JANZ, JAMES C. WETHERBE, GORDON B. DAVIS, AND RAYMOND A. NOE

ABSTRACT: Increases in employee autonomy and the formation of teams often result from reengineering and process innovation efforts, as do moves to “downsize” or “flatten” organizations. Information systems departments have not been insulated from these trends. In spite of the rising interest in these initiatives, little is known about their impact on the systems development process. Past research in blue-collar contexts suggests that teams produce improvements in performance, while anecdotal evidence in the IS industry suggests that such improvements may never materialize. This paper reports on research conducted with 231 IS professionals from 27 systems development teams across 13 organizations. The results indicate that, while autonomy may lead to increased levels of satisfaction and motivation, the level of team development and an organization’s learning capacity may be more important in achieving improved work outcomes.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: business process change, business reengineering, cooperative learning, employee autonomy, process improvement, self-direction, systems development, team development, teams, teamwork.

With increased levels of competition in the marketplace, the push for “downsizing,” and the contraction of time and distance brought about through advancing information technology, the level of interest in “business process reengineering” (BPR) [27], “business process improvement” [28], and “process innovation” [13] has risen steadily as organizations seek dramatic improvements in business as usual. This increased interest in BPR has not gone unnoticed within the field of information systems (IS). Chief information officers (CIOs) rank business reengineering as a top priority for the balance of the decade [4].

Within IS organizations, the information systems development (ISD) process represents an area for potentially fruitful BPR efforts. From a time perspective alone, IS organizations are trying to be more responsive to their end users as they attempt to improve upon application development times measured in years, to those measured in months, weeks, and even days $[4, 49]$ . Such advances indeed constitute dramatic improvements distinguishing them from less dramatic process improvement, such as TQM $[11]$ or process redesign $[28]$ .

Since BPR initiatives often require the formation of autonomous team-based organizations $[27]$ , it is not surprising that the interest in autonomous teams in IS has risen dramatically. However, in spite of the popularity of these teams, little is known about them in the IS domain or about their efficacy as a vehicle for BPR success in the ISD process. In fact, anecdotal evidence suggests that autonomous teams in IS are typically less than successful $[50]$ .

Recent literature suggests that a majority of BPR projects fail $[8]$ . Furthermore, the inability to overcome human resource issues (such as implementing teams) has been identified as the most negative correlate of BPR success $[14]$ . Since team-oriented work structures have been identified as an intervention to initiate change $[23]$ , as well as an organizational and human resource enabler for BPR efforts $[13]$ , they have the potential to achieve dramatic results for ISD organizations. Therefore, it is imperative for IS practitioners concerned with the overall success of their ISD reengineering efforts to understand team implementation issues.

The lack of knowledge, high levels of uncertainty, and criticality of understanding team-based BPR initiatives poses a problem that this research addresses: the effect that autonomous teams as a BPR initiative have on the quality of work life and ultimately the performance of workers in the ISD domain.

## Background

AUTONOMOUS TEAMS ARE ALSO REFERRED TO IN THE LITERATURE as self-managed teams, empowered work groups, or self-directed work teams (SDWTs) [41]. For consistency, we refer to these groups as SDWTs.

SDWTs are groups of colocated workers (as opposed to dyads or organizations) who self-regulate work on interdependent tasks. Workers on SDWTs typically (a) have a variety of skills, (b) apply those skills to a meaningful, whole task (i.e., a task that has an identifiable beginning and end), and (c) have control over the management of work methods, task scheduling, and assignment of group members to tasks. Compensation and feedback are based in part on the performance of the group as a whole rather than on that of the individual. In general, it is believed that the increases in autonomy from SDWTs will result in increased motivation, job satisfaction, and enhanced job performance $[25, 31]$ .

SDWTs are relevant in the BPR domain since their empowered, team-based nature is consistent with guidelines suggested in the BPR literature [27]:

\- Flattening organizational hierarchies;

\- Moving from functional departments to cross-functional process teams;

• Combining several jobs into one;

\- Empowering workers in order to increase bottom-up decision making;

\- Reducing checks and controls; and

\- Transforming managers into "coaches."

In addition, implementing teams to improve the quality of work life as well as customer satisfaction—often the motivating factors for moving to teams—is consistent with the principles of BPR [35].

The research presented here studied SDWTs responsible for information systems development. The ISD domain was chosen for several reasons:

1. The ISD process is central to the IS discipline and embodies sufficient unique characteristics to warrant further investigation [2]. For example, teams involved in ISD must translate business requirements into technical specifications, develop a system that addresses end-user requirements, and then retranslate the solution back into the language of business [16].

2. The project orientation of ISD brings with it conflicting expectations of success. ISD projects represent meaningful whole tasks. They typically have an identifiable beginning (e.g., the project “kick-off” and end (e.g., cut-over to production) and are important to the organization. Task whole-ness and meaningfulness enrich work and should promote team success [42]. On the other hand, it has been hypothesized that the short-term nature of ISD projects (compared with providing ongoing support) causes SDWTs to be unsuccessful since there may be insufficient time for the team to learn how to work together [9].

3. Researchers have suggested that the outcomes stemming from SDWTs are sensitive to the domain in which they are implemented. Consequently, research needs to be done within the context of interest [39].

4. SDWTs in IS might actually represent a decrease in autonomy. The essence of IS work hinges upon the ability to stay current on ever-changing technology. It is common for IS professionals to perform tasks with technology about which their managers or supervisors know little, if anything. This expertise gives IS workers a certain amount of autonomy in how they approach their job. The self-directed nature of SDWTs provides new forms of autonomy. However, SDWTs often bring a certain amount of structure to the job in terms of developing and adhering to group norms. If these norms outweighed the increased autonomy of SDWTs and the autonomy inherent in possessing technological expertise, SDWTs would represent a net decrease in overall job freedom. This “myth of self-leadership” has been discussed in the organizational behavior literature [38].

5. By examining a similar context (in this case ISD) for all SDWTs, the potential for task-related confounding effects is reduced.

## Theoretical Foundations

JOB CHARACTERISTICS THEORY [25], THE COOPERATIVE LEARNING THEORY [33], and the literature on organizational climate [36] will be enlisted within a theoretical BPR framework [35] to help us better understand SDWTs in ISD. Briefly, process change initiatives can be placed along a continuum running from incremental change (i.e., TQM) on one end to radical change (i.e., BPR) on the other [13, 35]. Since the SDWTs presented here are challenged with achieving radical process improvements, a framework stemming from the current theoretical work on business process change (BPC) is presented. Job characteristics theory suggests a positive relationship between the characteristics of work and the affective and performance outcomes of work. Cooperative learning theory suggests that if members of a group have interdependent goals, teach each other the needed skills for work, and periodically evaluate the group's performance, the achievement and productivity of the group will be enhanced. The prevailing climate of an organization serves as a measure of the organization's readiness for BPR initiatives and will also be discussed.

## A Framework for BPR Research

Kettinger and Grover propose a theoretical framework for BPC management that embodies many of the dimensions relevant to the introduction of SDWTs in an organization $[35]$ . From figure 1, we see that at the heart of BPC lie strategic initiatives—the vision and commitment to innovation required by top management to initiate radical change in the organization. As a strategic BPR initiative, top management often consciously decides to “de-layer” traditionally hierarchical organizations by empowering workers and creating team-oriented work environments in hopes of making them more responsive $[15, 32]$ . Since the defining characteristics of SDWTs that are both consistent with the principles of BPR and different from other forms of traditional work are the level of autonomy afforded to workers and the notion of team-oriented work, these dimensions are measured to determine the degree to which SDWTs, as a strategic initiative, have “taken hold” in the organization.

In addition to strategic initiatives, the BPC framework suggests that an organization's learning capacity, knowledge capability, cultural readiness, IT leveragability, and relationship balancing are all correlated antecedent conditions necessary for improved BPC outcomes. In terms of the learning capacity of an organization and its workers, how people interrelate, how their work is structured, and how the organization is designed will have some effect [24, 35]. In addition, variations in learning, such as single-loop and double-loop learning, have been posited as important to organizational learning [1, 27], as has process management [14, 35]. Since cooperative learning theory encompasses these dimensions and serves as an indicator of the presence of and capacity for learning, it is used as a measure of learning capacity.

Organizational culture embodies the deeply held, shared beliefs of an organization and is slow to develop or change over time $[44]$ . Kettinger and Grover and others have posited that an organization’s culture influences its ability to learn, share information, and make decisions $[35, 44]$ . Within the context of BPR, an organization can be limited to the extent that it is “culturally ready” for fundamental rethinking and radical change. Another dimension that is often used as a measure of the shared beliefs of an organization is organizational climate $[37, 43]$ . Culture and climate often measure the same phenomena—organizational reward systems, organizational support, and the like—but climate is considered the more visible and adaptive of the two $[30]$ . Since adaptability and measurability are critical within the context of BPR, organizational climate is presented here as a gauge of an organization’s “readiness” for change.

By definition, IT must be leveraged in BPR initiatives [26]. However, IT leveragability is conceptually distinct from SDWTs (which are posited to provide organizational and human resource leverage). Similarly, relationship balancing, the ability to reengineer an organization's value chain through interorganizational alliance, and knowledge capability, effectively combining learning and information, are outside the scope of the SDWTs studied here. As such, we do not measure these dimensions of the Kettinger and Grover framework here.

The outcomes of changed processes include improved processes as well as improved quality of work life (QWL). As discussed earlier, process improvements can be placed along a continuum from incremental improvement to dramatic improvement. Although improvements to ISD can also fall along this continuum, it is the desire for dramatic changes in the responsiveness of the systems development life cycle to the customer (e.g., effectiveness, efficiency, and cycle times) that most often serve as the basis for BPR initiatives in ISD [4]. Consequently, this research solicited stakeholder perceptions (end users and managers) as an unbiased source of evidence of improved ISD processes. Since the tradition of SDWTs traces back to the origins of sociotechnical design [5, 12], it is no surprise that the implementation of SDWTs is intended to have an impact on QWL issues such as job satisfaction, satisfaction with personal growth, and worker motivation. These issues serve as measures for QWL and are discussed later.

As such, the research here does not speculate on this linkage. ## SDWTs in Organizations

The most widely known research on SDWTs derives from sociotechnical systems research conducted in blue-collar industries $[5]$ . The findings indicate that the intervention of SDWTs correlates with modest improvements in productivity but slight decreases in job satisfaction $[22]$ . Acknowledging the trend toward SDWT implementation in knowledge work settings, recent studies are beginning to consider the impact of SDWTs in these contexts $[31]$ . Overall, the results from SDWT interventions are somewhat mixed, and there is still a dearth of SDWT research in knowledge work settings.

## Autonomy

Autonomy (also referred to as “empowerment,” “self-direction,” or “self-management”) is the extent to which an individual or group of individuals has the freedom, independence, and discretion to determine what actions are required and how best to execute them $[38, 41]$ . In terms of our definition of SDWTs, autonomy includes responsibility for such things as the management and scheduling of work methods, hiring and firing team members, and the assignment of group members to tasks. Theoretically, the more autonomy an individual or team possesses, the more responsible the individual or team will feel for the outcomes of their work [25]. This heightened responsibility is a “critical psychological state” that translates to increased motivation and satisfaction (i.e., QWL), and work effectiveness [24, 25].

As a strategic BPC initiative, SDWTs vary in how they are implemented. For example, SDWTs can be implemented with varying levels of autonomy. Given that we know little about the impact autonomy has on BPC initiatives, the following question is proposed:

Question 1: In organizations engaged in reengineering efforts, what is the relationship between the level of autonomy, BPC outcomes, and other BPC dimensions?

Cooperative Learning Theory

Cooperative learning exists when team members work together to maximize their own as well as other team members' learning [33]. Certain characteristics are preconditions for cooperative learning: positive interdependence, face-to-face promotive interaction, and group process.

With positive interdependence, members of a group are linked in such a way that each member feels that he or she cannot be successful unless all other members of the group are successful. A shared group goal that each group member identifies with and accepts is one way to achieve positive interdependence. In terms of an organization's learning capacity, positive interdependence reflects the importance of learning as both individual cognition and group cognition.

Face-to-face promotive interaction is the degree to which members of the group identify the individual strengths and weaknesses in the group and try to help others develop the skills necessary for the group to achieve its goal through explaining or teaching. An organization's learning capacity is in proportion to the extent that promotive interaction occurs within the team.

Group process refers to groups periodically assessing: (a) those things it has done effectively, (b) those things it has done ineffectively, and (c) measures that the group might take to address the identified weaknesses. Insofar as group process represents a concerted effort to evaluate the performance implications of given behaviors, it is very much like the double-loop learning discussed in the literature on organizational learning $[1]$ and process management $[14]$ .

In the BPR literature, organizational learning, learning capacity, and the capacity for double-loop learning are closely related. Unfortunately, little research has investigated the impact of these phenomena on BPC outcomes or related BPC dimensions. This paucity of research leads to our second question:

Question 2: In organizations engaged in reengineering efforts, what is the relationship between its learning capacity, BPC outcomes, and other BPC dimensions?

## Team Development

A characteristic common to all teams is how they mature over time. In general, a more mature team should have a better sense of itself as a team than a less mature team. One of the most widely known development models suggests that groups pass through a series of stages over time: (1) forming, in which the group is first assembled with fairly high levels of anticipation; (2) storming, in which interpersonal conflicts dominate as team members strive to find their place within the team; (3) norming, in which the teams begin to “gel” and develop their own set of team rules and norms; and (4) performing, in which the team works effectively as a coordinated unit [47]. A more mature team would have a better-developed sense of team unity or norms, of the team’s mission, and would perform better than less mature teams (perhaps by acting as more of a coordinated unit). This research investigates the general phenomenon of team development and investigates how the development of the SDWT relates to BPC outcomes and the other dimensions of BPC. Thus, the following research question is formulated:

Question 3: In organizations engaged in reengineering efforts, what is the relationship between the level of team development, BPC outcomes, and other BPC dimensions?

## Cultural Readiness

Organizational climate, like organizational culture, often refers to an organization's value system in terms of risk taking, reward systems, and providing a warm and supportive environment [37]. Empirical research suggests that organizational climate has a significant effect on organizational performance and employee satisfaction [37, 43]. Given that this influence is conceptually similar to cultural readiness and represents a potentially important force within the BPC framework, it makes sense to account for it. Therefore, following from the BPC framework in figure 1, this perspective is called cultural readiness and brings us to our final research question:

Question 4: In organizations engaged in reengineering efforts, what is the relationship between the level of cultural readiness, BPC outcomes, and other BPC dimensions?

## Research Development

## The Dependent Variables: BPC Outcomes

BPC OUTCOMES INCLUDE IMPROVEMENTS IN THE PROCESS of producing goods or services as well as the effective results of the work—the attitudes and feelings people have about their QWL. Hackman and Oldham's [25] job characteristics theory represents these QWL outcomes with the following constructs: job satisfaction, growth satisfaction, and internal motivation.

Growth satisfaction is a measure of the opportunity for personal learning and growth offered by the work itself. Given that an individual has a certain need for growth, increasing growth opportunities translates to more growth satisfaction. Job satisfaction is a measure of how satisfied a worker feels with the work he or she does. Internal motivation refers to the individual's feelings about his or her level of performance [25]. Higher levels of performance result in positive feelings that serve as a "self-reward." This reward motivates the individual to seek continuing levels of high performance. Conversely, lower levels of performance would not offer these self-rewards and the individual should then seek to increase his or her level of performance in order to attain the self-reward. To measure growth satisfaction, job satisfaction, and internal motivation, we used sixteen survey items from Hackman and Oldham's Job Diagnostic Survey (JDS) [25]. The JDS has been widely used in the organizational design and organizational behavior literatures (e.g., [7, 20]; see the appendix for a breakdown of each survey scale). In the BPC framework presented in figure 1, growth satisfaction, job satisfaction, and internal motivation comprise the construct improved quality of work life (QWL).

The term “effectiveness” is often used in descriptions of how teams perform. Hackman and Oldham [25] suggest using quantity and quality of the work output, while Hackman [24] more explicitly suggests that one element of effectiveness is that work outputs meet the expectations of those who review it—such as a supervisor or customer. Henderson and Lee [29] have developed a nine-item stakeholder instrument that solicits end-user perceptions of ISD team performance along the dimensions of effectiveness, efficiency, and timeliness. This customer perspective is consistent with the BPR literature [27], and, in terms of the BPC framework, these dimensions are enlisted under the construct of improved process.

Effectiveness is the degree to which the system meets users' requirements. Efficiency is the volume of work produced for a given amount of resource inputs. Timeliness is the teams' ability to meet scheduled deadlines. These variables are measured by asking key stakeholders—such as managers and end users who review the teams' work products—to evaluate SDWT performance. Stakeholder evaluations have the added benefit of reducing the potential for common method variance [7].

## SDWT Autonomy and BPC Outcomes

Autonomy takes many forms in SDWTs. BPR initiatives often include bestowing authority on employees for nontraditional tasks such as team member selection, goal setting, discipline, compensation, and work planning. Several forms of autonomy typically given to SDWTs listed in the appendix were borrowed and adapted from a previously validated instrument $[3]$ . Since little was known about the past use of this instrument, two four-person panels of experts (business faculty and doctoral candidates) categorized ten survey items along two dimensions: people-related autonomy and planning-related autonomy. Complete consensus was reached by each panel.

Previous relevant research studies generally show a modest “more is better” effect on work outcomes [8]. This leads to the following proposition:

Proposition 1: Reengineered ISD organizations with highly autonomous systems development processes will have greater improvements in the ISD process and employee QWL than reengineered ISD organizations with lesser degrees of autonomy.

## Team Development and BPC Outcomes

Team development is a measure of how well the transition to teams, as a strategic initiative, has taken hold in the reengineered organization. Team development is operationalized into three variables: mission clarity, team coordination, and team unity. Mission clarity is the degree of team members' understanding of the team's mission and/or overall purpose. Team coordination is how organized and efficient the team members feel their team is, and team unity is the degree to which team members feel their team works together in harmony. More mature or highly developed teams will have greater mission clarity, team coordination, and team unity. These variables were measured with thirteen items borrowed from a previously validated instrument [6].

We predict that, if SDWTs possess higher levels of team development, there will be higher levels of change outcomes. In other words:

Proposition 2: Reengineered ISD organizations with well-developed teams will have greater improvements in the ISD process and employee QWL than reengineered ISD organizations with low levels of team development.

## Learning Capacity and BPC Outcomes

The previously discussed elements of cooperative learning (positive interdependence, promotive interaction, and group process) serve as evidence of an organization's learning capacity. The level of cooperative learning is determined by measuring twenty survey items borrowed from instruments previously developed, validated, and used widely in practice [6, 20]. The BPC framework suggests that a positive relationship exists between learning capacity and BPC outcomes. In other words:

Proposition 3: Reengineered ISD organizations with a high capacity for learning will have greater improvements in the ISD process and employee QWL than reengineered ISD organizations with lower capacities for learning.

## Cultural Readiness and BPC Outcomes

Within the literature on organizational climate, several measures have been offered $[36, 37]$ . Although the choice of which measures to use may ultimately be an educated guess, it makes sense within the BPC context to focus on those that have empirically shown a positive relationship with organizational performance or worker satisfaction $[37, 43]$ . We chose the variables risk, reward, warmth, and support since each was found to be most common in organizational climate studies. Risk measures the orientation of the organization toward undertaking potentially innovative initiatives with uncertain outcomes. Higher levels indicate that an organization is comfortable with risks and does not penalize workers for taking them. Reward is a measure of how the organization recognizes employee performance. Warmth is a measure of how friendly the atmosphere is in the organization. Finally, support is a measure of the organization's interest in the welfare of the employee. These variables were measured with twenty-one survey items borrowed from a previously validated and widely used instrument [37].

We predict that, if an organization possesses high levels of cultural readiness as measured through organizational climate, there will be associated high levels of process change outcomes. In other words:

Proposition 4: Reengineered ISD organizations with high levels of cultural readiness will have greater improvements in the ISD process and employee QWL than reengineered ISD organizations with low levels of cultural readiness.

## Methodology

THE RESEARCH SETTING CONSISTED OF ISD ORGANIZATIONS that had implemented SDWTs as part of their reengineering initiatives. SDWTs involved in ISD projects were chosen since ISD projects: (1) are a core responsibility of most IS organizations; (2) are, in general, readily identifiable and similar across organizations; and (3) have a finite duration (as opposed to open-ended tasks such as systems support).

Invitations to participate were distributed at two systems development seminars. One seminar was regional in scope, while the other was attended by organizations from across North America. Included in the invitation was a commitment to share research findings with participating organizations. This invitation generated responses from twenty Fortune 500 companies. Followup phone interviews were conducted to describe the research and to determine if the teams met the requirements of SDWTs defined for this research: (1) the teams were involved in ISD activities, (2) the teams were formed to achieve dramatic (as opposed to incremental) improvements, and (3) the teams were viewed by themselves and their organization as self-directed in nature. This resulted in a final sample of 270 team members from 27 SDWTs belonging to 13 organizations across the United States and Canada.

As suggested earlier, the research methodology consisted of survey administration (see the appendix for a breakdown of individual scales). Surveys with eighty-three items were administered to each member of the SDWTs, while a separate nine-item stakeholder survey was administered to SDWT management and end users. For each team, we sought input from five stakeholders. Responsibility for choosing the stakeholders was left to the contact individual for each organization, who in all but one case was not a member of the team. This was done to minimize common method variance as well as possible bias from stakeholders who might have been team confederates.

Prior to survey administration, pilot surveys were administered to check reliability, readability, and time requirements. We chose five one-year-old SDWTs working on field-based systems development M.B.A. projects and one four-year-old SDWT from industry for the pilot. Since every effort was made to keep the items and scale scores identical to that of the formats found in the original surveys, items had 5-, 6-, or 7-point Likert scales. As a result of pilot subject comments, the readability of all items was found to be satisfactory; however, warning notes were inserted in the surveys at points where the scale numbering changed. The revised instruments were tested on a new M.B.A. team and the results were favorable. The pilot survey responses indicated that, in all but a few instances, the survey items had reliability scores above 0.70 (measured by Cronbach's alpha), indicating an acceptable level of internal consistency [10]. Since reliability was to be tested again after the actual survey administration, we decided to retain all of the items for the final versions of the surveys.

## Analysis of Results

IN ORDER FOR A TEAM'S RETURNED SURVEYS TO BE CONSIDERED usable, responses were required from at least three team members and at least three stakeholders. This helped ensure anonymity among respondents. Usable surveys were received from 231 team member respondents (85.6 percent). Of the possible 135 stakeholders who could have provided performance feedback on the SDWTs (5 stakeholders × 27 teams), surveys were received from 114 (84.4 percent). Table 1 provides a breakdown of research participants by industry.

On average, the teams surveyed were 19.2 months old and consisted of 9.9 systems development professionals. The team members were highly educated (over 75 percent with college degrees) and highly experienced (over 75 percent with more than five years of ISD experience) and had significant tenure in their organizations (over 75 percent had over three years of experience in their respective companies). These are characteristics we might expect for knowledge workers and differ significantly from the characteristics of workers measured in previous SDWT research. Of the stakeholders who responded, 36.8 percent were in management, 51.9 percent were internal end users of the teams, 1.9 percent were external customers, and the remainder were listed as “other”—nonteam peers, suppliers, vendors, and so on.

As a generally accepted guideline, prevalidated instruments should have Cronbach's alpha values of at least 0.7, while nonvalidated items should have alpha values of at least 0.6 [40]. This type of data reduction is common and accepted within the literature on groups [7].

Since the sample size of teams was not large $n = 27$ , it is important to point out that individual-level responses were sought in order to analyze team-level phenomena. In “cross-level” analyses like this it is important to understand the level of agreement by respondents within teams before the validity of an individually supplied team measure can be accepted.

Much has been offered when considering the aggregation of survey measures from one unit of analysis to another $[21, 30, 48]$ . Since the majority of the measures used here were sufficiently explicit in informing the respondents that they were to supply team-level information, aggregation was considered appropriate $[48]$ . In those cases where individual-level phenomena are aggregated to arrive at a team-level measure (e.g., individual satisfaction aggregated to arrive at a measure of “team satisfaction”), the aggregation issue is more pertinent. Georgopoulos $[19]$ suggests that data should possess an $\eta^{2}$ (the square of the intraclass correlation) of at least 0.16 and an F-ratio greater than 1.0 (with p < 0.05), suggesting that individuals within teams share a level of agreement that is statistically greater than individuals across teams, thus signifying that a team-level effect is operative.

Intraclass statistics were calculated for each scale (Table 3). Every scale surpasses Georgopoulos's criteria for aggregation. This indicates support for an individual-level analysis of team-level phenomena. Therefore, the proposition testing that follows is based on individual-level measures that have been aggregated to the team level.

## Discussion

## Implications for Theory

THE RESEARCH PRESENTED HERE WAS DESIGNED TO INVESTIGATE a BPC initiative (SDWTs of highly skilled knowledge workers in the information systems development profession) and its relationship to BPC outcomes (improved systems development processes and improved quality of work life), and to test the BPC framework empirically. The demographic profiles of the responses indicate that a representative target sample was obtained; the workers were indeed highly experienced in the field of information systems and were highly educated. In addition, the results suggest that a statistically powerful test of the research propositions was achieved.

There was strong empirical support for the BPC framework proposed by Kettinger and Grover $[35]$ . In terms of theory building, the research presented here was unable to falsify any of the proposed relationships—an indication that the theoretical BPC framework has some validity. As hypothesized, BPC strategic initiatives (in this case measured by increased levels of autonomy and team development), the learning capacity of the organization (measured by cooperative learning), and cultural readiness (measured by organizational climate) were positively correlated with each other as well as with BPC change outcomes (improved QWL and improved work processes). This is completely consistent with past theoretical work on autonomy, team development, cooperative learning, and organizational climate, and suggests that these robust theory bases can offer insights in future reengineering and ISD research.

Of particular interest is the high level of correlation between cooperative learning and team development and between these two dimensions and improved processes and QWL. These interrelationships suggest that, when using either learning or team development theories, researchers may want additionally to consider the complementing theory when looking for explanatory power.

The support found for the importance of cooperative learning is important not only because it is consistent with the already large body of classroom research on cooperative learning, but also because it extends cooperative learning theory to the workplace—a very different context than the classroom. Because of the dearth of literature investigating cooperative learning on teams in business settings, these findings should be considered a contribution to both theory and practice since they illuminate some of the underlying group mechanisms that contribute to team performance. Furthermore, the findings presented here reinforce the importance of double-loop learning $[1, 14]$ and of creating a “learning organization” $[45]$ . The support found for the importance of team development adds further support for the potential positive impact of team-structured work $[24]$ .

The positive relationship between autonomy and quality of work life outcomes suggests that increased autonomy in SDWTs may improve the satisfaction and motivation levels of IS workers. These findings are consistent with the fundamental tenets of sociotechnical systems theory $[12]$ . The relatively modest correlation between IS autonomy and BPC performance outcomes suggests that the autonomy inherent in SDWTs may represent only a marginal increase in the autonomy IS workers have come to expect, or, worse, the structure and role expectations of the SDWT represent a decrease in autonomy that IS workers value (i.e., the myth of self-leadership as discussed by Manz $[38]$ ). Alternatively, people-related autonomy such as hiring, firing, and disciplining fellow team members may not be universally desired by all team members. While correlating with improved QWL, these responsibilities may serve as a distraction—or, worse, a disincentive—to improved ISD performance.

Although cultural readiness measured in risk, reward, warmth, and support appears related to improved quality of work life, it seems to matter little to BPC performance outcomes. This is somewhat inconsistent with the organizational literature $[37, 43]$ . This inconsistency may indicate that, within the context of BPC, less attention needs to be directed to macro-level issues (like fostering a warm and supportive organization-wide environment), while more attention should be directed toward more immediate micro-level issues (like helping teams work and learn together). Alternatively, the low degree of correlation between cultural readiness and performance (in fact, we found a slight negative correlation between warmth and performance) may result from the fact that BPR initiatives often accompany drastic organizational changes, such as downsizing with SDWTs. While performance may improve as a result of such initiatives, the employees' sense of organizational support as well as their personal welfare may be negatively affected.

## Implications for Managerial Practice

The support for the BPC framework suggests that managers should consider using it to learn more about their own reengineering efforts. In essence, the research and the framework discussed here suggest that strategic initiatives cannot be implemented in a vacuum. Ancillary dimensions such as learning capacity, as well as the organization's readiness, must be considered in addition to the strategic initiatives themselves. These findings are probably not unique to the reengineering context. Since the BPC framework was originally intended to be applicable to all process change initiatives [35], managers may find that the prescriptions offered here are well suited to their efforts, whether they are dramatic or more incremental in nature.

This research does not posit causal relationships. However, it appears that providing more autonomy in BPR initiatives is coincident with improved QWL and performance, although the performance relationship may be somewhat weaker. This suggests that managers involved with reengineering efforts should seek to increase their employees' autonomy, but should do so judiciously. Empowering workers with planning-related autonomy (e.g., setting team goals and objectives, developing project budgets and having spending authority, and developing work schedules such as flex-time) promotes the success of reengineering efforts $[26]$ while increasing the level of employee "buy-in" in the new work processes $[32]$ . Within an ISD environment, these types of autonomy are relatively easy to provide $[32]$ . The typically more sensitive people-related autonomy issues (e.g., hiring, firing, and disciplining) may improve QWL, but may also cause performance-hampering anxiety within the team. Therefore, people-related autonomy should be given to teams when they have had time to develop and adjust to previous levels of empowerment $[31]$ .

The importance of an organization's learning capacity on QWL and performance suggests that it would be prudent for management to emphasize and provide ample opportunity for cooperative learning while teams address their work objectives. In this case, focusing on the organizational learning required to achieve work objectives as well as providing opportunities for double-loop learning should prove beneficial [14]. Providing team “time-outs” for conscious analysis of the team’s work results is one way to achieve this. Managers should bear in mind that cooperative learning can be strengthened by instilling a sense of interdependence in everything the team does. Placing priority on the ability to “learn how to learn” [27], developing a large percentage of team-level objectives, and ensuring that team members are responsible for backing each other up all enhance cooperative learning and the level of interdependence. The fact that this learning should be cooperative in nature—that is, performed in real time within the team through cross-training—addresses the increasing pressures to reduce training budgets for off-site or out-of-team training and has been shown to enable process innovation [13]. Furthermore, the learning dimension is well suited to ISD workers as well as to the technology-intensive nature of today’s ISD efforts.

The weak relationship between motivation and stakeholder perceptions of performance suggests that, although teams are performing well, they may not be experiencing the sense of reward necessary to motivate them. This may reflect the introspective nature of the stereotypical ISD worker $[2]$ and could be addressed in a cooperative learning environment by incorporating a larger portion of team-based rewards. For example, one of the highest performing (and highly motivated) teams involved with this research expressed that nonmonetary rewards such as team recognition, flex-time, and paid time off from work were extremely important to them $[31]$ . This is an important point associated with BPC initiatives: If business processes and organizational structures are radically changed as part of the BPC effort, compensation and reward systems need to be realigned to support the new processes and structures.

Finally, the importance of team development should be considered by managers facing the challenge of BPR. Past reengineering research suggests that the formation of teams can be used to manage resistance to change when BPR initiatives are being implemented [23]. However, while this research suggests that team-based organizational structures may have a positive impact on BPR outcomes, it is important to provide assistance and adequate time for teams to develop into mature, high-functioning teams. Of the teams studied here, teams formed by management decree were not successful overnight. The most successful teams were allowed time to evolve into high-performing groups [31]. The high correlation with learning capacity suggests that fostering a cooperative learning environment may allow groups to develop more quickly by helping them understand the team's mission and to work in a united, coordinated manner.

In summary, when undertaking BPR initiatives, major process changes such as team formation, increased autonomy, and the creation of a “learning organization” may provide improvements in both QWL and work processes. These initiatives should not be viewed as distinct efforts or as temporary “quick fixes.” By considering the multidimensional nature of BPR efforts and how the dimensions work together to facilitate change, improve QWL, and ultimately improve worker performance, organizational leaders can better manage strategic BPR initiatives.

## Limitations and Future Directions

As with most research in the early stages of exploration, some limitations exist in the area of theoretical conceptualization. Future research in this area should seek to further clarify and refine the operationalization of constructs such as autonomy and cultural readiness. For example, the notion of individual empowerment versus team autonomy might yield useful insights. The importance of team development demonstrated here suggests that this dimension should be further investigated to elucidate how teams develop as well as how the effectiveness and pace of development might be enhanced (e.g., through training, cooperative learning, etc.).

Second, although attempts were made to secure objective, quantitative end-user performance data, the data on which these findings were based focused on the team and were perceptual in nature—based on the perceptions of team members and external stakeholders. This limits the ability to understand the relationship between team performance and ultimate customer success. Although difficult, future research should attempt to capture tangible performance measures for teams—for example, project duration, error rates, budget compliance—and for customers—such as increased revenue, decreased costs, and improved cycle times.

Third, future research should seek larger numbers of teams. The research here is based on a sufficient sample size of individuals, but a marginally sized sample of teams $n = 27$ . It would be worthwhile to conduct a team-level analysis similar to the individual-level analysis presented here to see if the findings are corroborated.

Fourth, the research presented here is cross-sectional and correlational in nature. As such, it offers us some understanding of the relationships between variables, but a deeper understanding of causality cannot and should not be inferred from the relationships discussed here. Future work to validate the BPC framework should examine the cause and effect linkages proposed in the model as well as the time-dependent effects between change initiatives and BPC outcomes. Research employing path analysis, or a time-series/pre-post treatment design should address this. Furthermore, only a portion of the BPC framework has been successfully validated here. Team members and stakeholders were surveyed to learn specifically about the ISD process. Future research should seek to gather customer success and performance data in order to test the relationship between improved BPC processes and ultimate end-user success.

The research presented here is concerned with SDWTs within IS organizations involved in systems development activities across a wide variety of industries. As such, the generalizability of the findings is limited to other autonomous systems development teams in IS organizations. Attempts to generalize these findings to other domains should be considered informed speculation only.

Finally, more research needs to address the phenomena of learning in the workplace. As our findings suggest, cooperative learning may not only provide a significant amount of employee satisfaction and performance, but facilitate and accelerate the change process—a true win-win proposition in these times of radical business change.
