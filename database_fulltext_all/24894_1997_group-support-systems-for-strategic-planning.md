ABSTRACT: Strategic planning is typically performed by groups of managers. Group support systems (GSS), an information technology designed to improve group work, may therefore have useful application to strategic planning. In this paper, the application of GSS is examined with respect to four dimensions: process support, process structure, task support, and task structure. A GSS may assist the communication aspects of group meetings by providing process support to improve interaction among participants and process structure to direct the pattern or content of the discussion. GSS may also provide task structure, such as structured analysis and modeling techniques, and task support, such as access to important task information. This paper reports on the use of a GSS in the strategic planning processes of thirty organizations. The ability of the GSS to provide process support was found to be the most important contributor to strategic planning success, with task structure and process structure secondary. Task support contributed little. Those organizations that made greater use of electronic communication, structured problem analysis techniques, and structured meeting processes reported greater success.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: group support systems, groupware, strategic planning, strategic management.

STRATEGIC PLANNING IS FREQUENTLY CITED AS A TOP CONCERN for managers (e.g., [14, 50]). Strategic planning is a complex and iterative task typically conducted by groups of managers [1, 13, 47]. Key activities of strategic planning include goal formulation, environmental analysis, strategy formulation, and strategy evaluation [46]. Understanding and improving strategic planning have been an active research area for many decades. Prior research has investigated ways to improve planning through structured analytical techniques (e.g., [41, 42]), the application of information technology for sophisticated quantitative analyses (e.g., [14, 33]), and structured group process techniques (e.g., [31, 47]).

This paper examines strategic planning from a group process perspective and evaluates the ways that the fundamental communication infrastructure and information exchange processes inherent to strategic planning (e.g., face-to-face verbal meetings) may be affected by a group support system (GSS). A GSS is an information technology-based group meeting environment that enables groups to meet face to face with computer-mediated electronic communication to supplement or replace verbal communication [38]. GSS is an emerging technology that is being adopted by a rapidly growing number of organizations; the number of GSS facilities in public and private organizations has grown from fewer than ten in 1988 to several thousand today. In addition to enabling the use of software for quantitative analyses and information retrieval, and the use of more structured group processes [8, 20, 39], the electronic communication infrastructure of these meeting environments can fundamentally change the nature of group interaction [15, 52].

One of the greatest challenges in using GSS is identifying what GSS capabilities to use in what circumstances, in this case strategic planning. The objective of this study was to examine what GSS capabilities were most useful for supporting the strategic planning process.

## Previous GSS Research on Strategic Planning

GIVEN THE IMPORTANCE OF STRATEGIC PLANNING and the concerns over current practice, it is not surprising that some of the earliest applications of GSS were for strategic planning (e.g., [27]). As yet, however, there is little direct empirical evidence of how GSS affects strategic planning success beyond individual case studies. Prior research has focused on two general forms of GSS: single workstation and multiple workstation.

The single workstation form of GSS attempts to extend the strategic planning group's problem structuring and analysis capabilities by using special-purpose software and facilitator-based process techniques [10, 11, 12, 32, 39, 43]. With this approach, the key six-to-ten decision makers come together and discuss the issues verbally while the facilitator builds a model of the decision situation using a single computer workstation with a linear programming, cognitive mapping, or other analytic modeling technique. Research suggests that this form of GSS can be useful to strategic planning groups, although not in all cases [32].

This study focuses on the multiple workstation form of GSS. With this form of GSS, all participants use networked computer workstations and special-purpose software to communicate, propose ideas, organize options, and evaluate alternatives by typing as well as talking, with a facilitator also available to assist. Participants in studies of this form of GSS have perceived GSS use to increase strategic planning efficiency and effectiveness and to enable broader participation in the strategic planning process $[7, 36, 51]$ . There are many types of multiple workstation GSS, such as GroupSystems and VisionQuest. In this paper, we focus solely on the use of one of these systems: GroupSystems.

## Measuring Strategic Planning Success

ONE CHALLENGE IN STRATEGIC PLANNING RESEARCH has been finding appropriate ways to measure the “success” of strategic planning processes. While financial measures have been used [26], this measuring approach can be problematic since there are significant difficulties in linking financial performance to strategic planning processes [23], qualitative benefits do not always emerge quickly in financial measures (e.g., organizational learning [1, 29], enhanced communication and participation [24, 49]), and it is difficult to apply financially based performance measures to nonprofit organizations. Wood and Laforge [57] have thus concluded that financial measures are not appropriate for the overall success of strategic planning.

Instead, Venkatraman and Ramanujam [54] proposed measuring success by examining the “capabilities” of strategic planning processes. They developed a set of twelve capabilities that may be linked to improved strategic planning success. The capabilities can be organized into three broad sets: (1) production and identification of information (enhancing the generation of new ideas, identifying new business opportunities, identifying key problem areas, and enhancing innovation); (2) communication and integration of information (communicating top management's expectations down the line, communicating line managers' concerns to top management, fostering organizational learning, and integrating diverse functions and operations), and (3) flexibility and leadership (anticipating surprises and crises, providing flexibility to adapt to unanticipated changes, fostering managerial motivation, and fostering management control).

Venkatraman and Ramanujam developed a model hypothesizing that strategic planning processes providing these twelve capabilities led to the attainment of a six-item set of strategic planning objectives (short-term performance, long-term performance, management development, prediction of future trends, problem avoidance, and better evaluation of strategic alternatives). Confirmatory factor analysis (Lisrel) using data from a questionnaire administered to participants in 202 strategic planning units found this model to be appropriate, as the twelve capability measures were predictors of the six planning success measures. These six subjective success measures were then validated against “objective” performance measures (e.g., financial performance) for these same organizations and found to be reliable. For this study, we used Venkatraman and Ramanujam’s capability measures to assess GSS-supported strategic planning.

## GSS Capabilities

NUNAMAKER ET AL. [38] ARGUE THAT GSS CAPABILITIES can be analyzed along four dimensions: process support, process structure, task support, and task structure.

## Process Support

Process support has to do with the communication infrastructure within which strategic planning participants interact. It is this GSS dimension that differentiates GSS-based strategic planning approaches from more traditional approaches. Because of the scope and interrelatedness of the activities and issues associated with strategic planning, this process requires wide organizational participation as no one person or organizational unit has all the information and insight needed to address all issues $[1, 49]$ . Therefore, the production, communication, and integration of information among the various participants becomes important.

Traditionally, strategic planning participants have come together in a meeting room to discuss the issues verbally, with some support provided by paper and pencil, flip charts, or white boards. With a GSS, participants can communicate electronically by typing comments as well as—or instead of—talking verbally. The electronic communication channel used in GSS-supported meetings provides three principal features: parallelism, anonymity, and a group memory [38]. Parallelism is the ability for all participants to work simultaneously. In traditional, non-GSS meetings, participants must wait for others to finish speaking before they can contribute. This problem, known as production blocking, is one of the most significant causes of productivity loss in group work [9]. Parallelism can mitigate production blocking and result in improved performance [17, 52].

Anonymity enables participants to make contributions without attaching their names. Anonymity may reduce evaluation apprehension—the fear of negative evaluation that can cause individuals to withhold ideas and opinions $[9, 28]$ . It may also reduce the pressure to conform to the opinions of others, whether the pressure is intentional or not. Anonymity has the greatest impact when there are power and status differences among members or there is a critical tone to discussions $[37]$ .

A group memory is the ability of the GSS to record all electronic communication for later use either during or after the meeting. A group memory may reduce difficulties associated with the need to listen constantly to and remember the comments of others by enabling group members to pause, think, and reflect on the issues $[28]$ .

## Process Structure

Process structure incorporates process rules to direct the pattern, timing, or content of communication among strategic planning participants. The objective of process structure is to increase managers' understanding of the information, to promote opportunities for new information to be discovered (i.e., synergy), and to ensure that important information is not overlooked. Process structures may be global to the entire process (e.g., a meeting agenda) or local to one step (e.g., rules governing idea generation via brainstorming). Many group process structuring techniques have been used in strategic planning processes, such as devil's advocacy and dialectical inquiry $[31, 47]$ .

## Task Support

Task support is the ability to access and integrate available information. This has been a traditional role of information technology in the strategic planning process—providing organizational and industry information through internal and external databases, executive information systems, and the like [4].

## Task Structure

Task structure involves the use of analytical techniques and structures to improve managerial analysis and decision making. Information technology has long been used in this way to improve quantitative analyses and to build forecasting models for use in strategic planning $[4]$ . However, there are many nonquantitative task structuring techniques that have traditionally not included information technology, such as value chain analysis $[42]$ and strategic assumption surfacing and testing $[31]$ . While the use of software for quantitative analyses is common, software can also be used to support qualitative structuring techniques, such as stakeholder analysis $[45]$ .

The key difference between task support and task structure is that task support makes additional information available to participants, while task structure adds value by taking the available data and making implicit relationships more explicit. For example, one way in which a GSS could provide task support would be the integration of databases into a GSS so that participants can easily access key information during their discussions. For example, a GSS could tap into the PIMS (Profit Impact of Market Strategy) database, which contains information on strategy and market data on thousands of firms $[14]$ . In contrast, consider an example of GSS task structure involving the SODA system $[10]$ , in which the GSS facilitator solicits information from participants and enters it into a cognitive map so that participants can better see what factors influence other factors. Here, the primary contribution is the task structure (provided by the cognitive map), which enables a more complete understanding of the complex causes and effects of various options available for the strategic plan.

The key difference between process structure and task structure is that process structure speaks to the general process by which some task is performed or the order in which its component subtasks are performed, whereas task structure delineates the specific analyses applied to task information. Process structure changes the rules of discussion; task structure changes the organization of task information. For example, process structure such as dialectical inquiry directs the participants to form two or more groups that each produce a separate analysis of alternatives. One group argues for one alternative, another for a different alternative, and so on, thereby producing a more complete problem analysis. No rules for how the available information should be analyzed (i.e., task structure) are specified.

Process structure is by nature more general than task structure; it is often appropriate for many tasks other than strategic planning, whereas task structure is less generalizable and typically tied to specific tasks. For instance, in gathering the requirements for a new information system, it is more difficult to visualize the use of a task structuring technique such as Porter's [42] value chain analysis than the use of a process structuring technique such as dialectical inquiry. Many formal planning techniques combine both process structure and task structure. For example, Mason and Mitroff's [31] assumption surfacing technique specifies the nature of the analyses through stakeholder identification, assumption surfacing, and two importance ratings (i.e., task structure), as well as the order in which they are to be performed (i.e., process structure).

## Examples of GSS Use

THERE ARE MANY WAYS IN WHICH GSS CAN BE USED to support the strategic planning process $[7, 51]$ . In this section, we examine three. The GSS used for this study was GroupSystems, developed by Ventana Corporation. GroupSystems provides a set of tools, each designed to support a different type of work. There are four basic types of tools: (1) exploration and idea generation tools, which involve the development and exploration of issues relevant to the task; (2) idea organization tools, which involve the synthesizing, structuring, and organizing of ideas into specific alternatives, which may follow the generation of ideas; (3) prioritizing tools, which support the individual members in evaluating alternatives (e.g., voting); and (4) policy development and evaluation tools, which support formal methodologies (e.g., stakeholder analysis). The tools may be used in whatever order the group chooses: While there is no mandatory order, many tasks follow a natural order of idea generation, idea synthesis, prioritizing, and exploration of important issues. Table 1 summarizes the specific GSS tools in the GroupSystems toolkit. For more information on GroupSystems, see [38].

## Example 1: Multinational Manufacturer

A publicly held multinational electronics manufacturer used GSS to support a three-day strategic planning meeting. This organization chose to use planning processes that were high in process support (i.e., the GSS electronic communication channel was used extensively) and only occasionally to use process structure (i.e., few facilitator-chaired discussions, no formal process structures used). Task structure to analyze the plans was extensively used, while no task support was used.

On the first morning, the group used Electronic Brainstorming (EBS) to generate ideas about expected corporate performance, which were then organized and sorted using Issue Analyzer (IA). Through the rest of the first day, the five-year plans for each of the organization's divisions were examined and critiqued in turn by the entire group via an internally developed structured analysis methodology using Topic Commenter (TC).

On the second day the organization examined each divisions' one-year action plan using an approach similar to that of the first day (i.e., the TC tool and the structured analysis methodology). Late in the second day, EBS and IA were used to generate and organize specific plan elements to accomplish the next year's overall corporate objectives.

The third day began with a continuation of the process of organizing these plan elements. Participants used the Vote module to rank order the generated plan elements three times—first by overall benefit to the firm, second by time frame (short term versus long term), and third by feasibility. The top five issues from the benefit ranking were then entered into TC for further group discussion. After lunch on the third day, the participants were divided into four work groups to develop specific plan elements for the next three to twelve months. GSS support was not provided to these work groups as they did not think that it would be useful with such small groups.

## Example 3: For-Profit Hospital

Thirteen members of a hospital corporation operating several hospitals spent two half-day sessions addressing the corporation's strategic plan. This organization used a level of process support and process structure between that of the previous two examples. The use of the Stakeholder Identification and Assumption Surfacing (SIAS) tool provided some process and task structure, while the use of Briefcase provided task support.

The first session began with the group using EBS, IA, and Vote to discuss, organize, and rate evaluations of the current strategic direction of the firm. During the idea organization and consolidation phase, the group had electronic access via Briefcase to seven files ranging from the goals and mission of the organization to financial reports.

The second session was held one week later to enable the group to review the results of the first session. This session began with the group generating ideas with EBS on how best to allocate the fixed amount of resources across the projects identified in the first session—or new projects introduced during this session. These ideas were then organized with IA and ranked in order of importance using Vote. The group ended the session discussing the implications of one key project with SIAS.

## GSS Effects on Strategic Planning Capabilities

THE EFFECTS OF GSS USE DEPEND TO A GREAT EXTENT on what capabilities the GSS provides and how a group chooses to use them. Not all GSS provide the same support (e.g., single-workstation GSS do not provide process support), and not all groups choose to use all the available GSS capabilities. Identifying what GSS capabilities to use in what circumstances is often a major challenge. The objective of this research was to determine what GSS capabilities affected organizations' capabilities to conduct strategic planning and, if effects were found, to identify which capabilities seemed to have the greatest effects. In this section, we discuss how the four dimensions of GSS capabilities—task structure, task support, process structure, process support—may influence the three sets of capabilities linked to strategic planning success—information production and identification, information communication and integration, and flexibility and leadership.

## Information Production and Identification

RQ1: To what extent does use of the four major GSS capabilities enhance or impair the production and identification of information?

The first set of capabilities identified by Venkatraman and Ramanujam $[54]$ is the production and identification of information, which includes idea generation, innovation, and the identification of problems and new opportunities. Strategic planning typically requires the involvement of many participants because different participants have different information, ideas, viewpoints, and goals, each of which can be important $[30, 34]$ .

The GSS features inherent in the process support provided by the GSS—parallelism and anonymity—should significantly improve this set of capabilities. Parallelism has been cited as an important factor in the improved idea generation produced by GSS use $[18, 51, 52]$ . Anonymity has also been found to be important, particularly when there is a critical tone or there are differences in power and status among participants $[38]$ , as is common in strategic planning. The group memory provided by process support may also reduce difficulties when interruptions occur in the decision-making process $[35]$ .

In addition, both process structure and task structure have played important roles in idea and information generation in previous research. Process techniques such as nominal group technique have been found to improve idea generation $[53]$ , while the use of task structure to analyze problem situations has improved the elicitation of more problem elements $[2, 40]$ . Thus, it seems likely that greater use of process and task structure should improve the capability to identify and produce information.

Finally, identifying problems or new opportunities requires knowledge of the current situation. Task support enables every participant to quickly access internal and external databases and information sources from their individual workstations. We therefore reasoned that the use of task support to provide information during strategic planning meetings should also help.

In summary, we believed that all four GSS capabilities—task support, task structure, process structure, and process support—would have an impact on information production and identification (see figure 1).

## Information Communication and Integration

## RQ2: To what extent does use of the four major GSS capabilities enhance or impair the communication and integration of information?

The second set of capabilities identified by Venkatraman and Ramanujam $[54]$ is the communication and integration of information, which includes top-down and bottom-up communication, organizational learning, and the integration of diverse functions. Given the equivocal nature of many strategic planning activities, it can be important to bring all participants together at one time to communicate information accurately $[5]$ . However, the number of potential participants is often quite large, and large group meetings are usually ineffective owing to difficulties in ensuring that all members have opportunities to participate $[48]$ . The GSS process support feature of parallel communication should greatly improve the opportunity to participate, thereby improving communication $[51]$ .

Communication among managers at different levels in a hierarchy can also be problematic owing to differences in power, perspective, information, and goals [21]. Because of mobility and security concerns [3, 16], subordinates have a tendency to distort information provided to superiors. Communication among bargaining coalitions may likewise pose problems, as participants may be reluctant to share information or may discount information contributed by members of “opposing” coalitions due to polarization around different viewpoints [22, 44]. The anonymity feature of GSS process support may help reduce these types of problems [51].

The integration of information from many different sources requires individuals and groups to fit together related pieces of information to better understand how all interact. Information integration is often aided by both process structures and task structures that enable individuals to better understand the separate pieces of information and the relationships among them [19].

In summary, we believed that three GSS capabilities—process support, process structure, and task structure—would affect information communication and integration (see figure 1).

## Flexibility and Leadership

## RQ3: To what extent does use of the four major GSS capabilities enhance or impair organizational flexibility and leadership?

The third set of capabilities, providing flexibility and leadership, includes motivating and controlling middle management, anticipating surprises, and promoting flexibility. Many of these activities occur outside the regular strategic planning cycle or are carried out on a one-to-one basis with key managers. A key issue related to this set of capabilities for group-based strategic planning processes is the tracking and monitoring of information. Here, task support to provide information and task structure to organize the available information seem to be especially relevant. Thus, we believed that two GSS capabilities—task support and task structure—would have an impact on flexibility and leadership.

## Method

GSS TECHNOLOGY IS AN EMERGING TECHNOLOGY. Because the population of organizations that have used GSS to support strategic planning is small and self-selected, it is impossible to select a set of organizations at random from a large universe for study. Therefore, we chose to study the entire population of organizations that used one widely adopted GSS for their strategic planning activities during a three-year period. This GSS was GroupSystems.

To provide greater research control, we studied GroupSystems use at two facilities operated by the University of Arizona. The facilities were available on a fee-for-service basis, drawing organizations from across the United States. One facility provided sixteen networked microcomputers built into a U-shaped table. An additional microcomputer, connected to a large-screen video projector at the front of the room, served as the facilitator's station. There are several adjacent breakout rooms and a printer room. The second facility provided twenty-four networked microcomputers built into two concentric tiers of desks. Two additional microcomputers, both connected to two large-screen video projectors, served as the facilitators' stations. For additional information and photographs, see $[38, 51]$ .

During the three-year period of study, more than a hundred organizations used these facilities. Groups from thirty-three of these organizations performed some aspect of strategic planning. All thirty-three organizations were asked to participate in this study, but three declined, leaving a total sample of thirty organizations. While several organizations used the GSS more than once over the three-year period of the study, we only included an organization's first-time use of the GSS to enable comparison across organizations.

The sample of organizations was diverse and included both for-profit organizations and not-for-profit organizations. Groups using the GSS ranged in size from eleven to thirty-eight participants (mean = 21) and the length of the meetings ranged from a half-day to four days (mean = 1½ days). The most common planning activity performed by the organizations was strategy formulation (73 percent of organizations), followed by goal formulation (37 percent), environmental analysis (30 percent), and strategy evaluation (20 percent). ## Data Collection

Two distinct styles of data collection were undertaken to triangulate across research methods, participants, and time. The first source of data was case reports. Data accumulation began with an initial contact report completed by the GSS support staff member who was contacted by the organization (typically the GSS facility managers). Reports from planning interviews between the organization's leader and the GSS staff (e.g., key issues, agendas, etc.) and final reports completed by the facilitator, assistants, observers, and researchers were also included. Transcripts of all electronic communication that occurred in the GSS processes were recorded. Other sources of information for several organizations included organization planning documents (those prepared both before and after the GSS-supported strategic planning processes), corporate annual reports, and newspaper and magazine articles; these were primarily used as background material, but any descriptions of the GSS meetings were used to corroborate the interview data.

The second source of data was an interview using the measures validated by Venkatraman and Ramanujam $[54]$ . In prior GSS research (ours and others), participants have typically been interviewed immediately after using the GSS. Our concern about this approach was that participants might be overly excited about their experiences and would tend to rate them more highly than they might if given more time. We believed that interviewing participants after they had used the results of the GSS-supported planning processes would better enable them to assess the success of these processes. Therefore, we scheduled the interview for after the organization had had the opportunity actually to use the results of the strategic planning process and to reflect on its usefulness. Depending upon the organization and its strategic planning cycle, this was anywhere from one to four months after the GSS processes.

The first part of this interview was devoted to completing an instrument using the Venkatraman and Ramanujam measures, while the second part was spent on a list of open-ended questions (see the appendix). Following the procedures of Venkatraman and Ramanujam [54], the instrument was administered to one participant—specifically, the person managing the organization's strategic planning activities. In sixteen cases, this was the most senior executive present (CEO, COO); in nine cases this was the second most senior executive (Group VP, VP-Finance); in five cases this was a staff expert (director of strategic planning). These individuals were considered the appropriate evaluators because they would ultimately be held accountable for the quality of the strategic planning processes.

## Measures

There were two primary sets of measures: (1) the extent to which each of the four GSS capabilities (e.g., task support) was used by the organization, and (2) the three measures of strategic planning success (e.g., information production and identification).

## Use of GSS capabilities

The extent to which each organization used each of the four GSS capabilities was assessed by two raters on a 1–9 scale (1 = very little, 5 = moderate, 9 = very much) as described below. The raters used the meeting agendas (actual agendas rather than planned agendas) to determine the extent of process support, process structure, task support, and task structure used by each organization. One rater rated all cases. A second rater independently rated ten cases. The correlation between the two ratings was 0.95, so the first rater's ratings were used.

Process support can be provided in three general ways, each of which may be used at different stages of the same meeting $[38]$ . The smallest amount of GSS process support is delivered through the “chauffeured” meeting style. With this approach, all communication is verbal, with one person (typically a facilitator) recording information at a computer workstation that displays information on a video projection system so that all participants can see it; in this case, the GSS is an electronic version of a traditional flip chart, but with sophisticated modeling capabilities. GroupSystems tools that involved the chauffeured style were Issue Analyzer, Vote, Alternative Evaluator, and Policy Formation. An intermediate level of process support is provided via a “supported” meeting style, in which participants combine electronic communication with traditional verbal communication; participants use both the computer workstations and verbal conversation to discuss substantive issues. GroupSystems tools that incorporated the supported style were Idea Organizer and Stakeholder. The highest degree of process support is associated with an “interactive” meeting style, in which all participants communicate electronically via the computer workstations; participants send typed comments to other participants and no one talks. Interactive GroupSystems tools were Electronic Brainstorming and Topic Commenter.

The extent to which each organization in this study used process support over the course of the GSS-supported planning session was quantified using a process developed by Dennis [6]. Four different categories of process support (i.e., no GSS, chauffeured, supported, and interactive) were assigned weighting factors of 0, 0.25, 0.5, and 1, respectively. The proportion of time a group spent using each type of process support was multiplied by its weight and summed, giving a score from 0 (no use of GSS at any point) to 1 (entire meeting run using interactive processes). This value was then multiplied by a factor of 10 and the resulting value was converted to a 1–9 scale by rounding to the nearest integer (e.g., 4.3 would convert to 4).

Because all groups employed a similar amount of global process structure (i.e., all meetings had prepared an agenda), this measure focused only on process structure used during the planning meetings. Meeting time classified as involving process structure included time spent using a formal process methodology that specified the order in which activities were to be performed (e.g., use of the Stakeholder tool) and time when the facilitator formally chaired verbal discussions. The proportion of meeting time involving process structure was calculated. This value was then converted to a 1–9 scale in a manner similar to that described for the process support measure.

The use of task support was determined by using the proportion of time in which the tool providing electronic access to premeeting information (i.e., Briefcase) was available, and converted to a 1–9 scale in the manner described earlier.

The use of task structure was determined by the proportion of time spent using a formal methodology that specified how the task should be analyzed (e.g., use of the Topic Commenter tool for a value chain analysis), and converted to a 1–9 scale in the manner described earlier.

## Strategic Planning Success

The twelve strategic planning capabilities were measured using the items validated in the Venkatraman and Ramanujam study $[54]$ (see the appendix). The original phrasing for these items asked respondents to compare their “current” strategic planning approach with their “previous” approach. The phrasing used in this study asked respondents to compare the “current computer-supported” approach with their “previous manual” approach to ensure comparison between the GSS approach and the non-GSS approach. All items were otherwise anchored as in the Venkatraman and Ramanujam study $[54]$ : A score of 1 indicated the current computer supported process was “much worse,” a 2 indicated “worse,” a 3 indicated “no difference,” a 4 indicated “better,” and a 5 indicated “much better.”

In order to assess GSS effects on the three sets of capabilities, we built three scales using Venkatraman and Ramanujam's [54] capabilities, one for information production and identification, one for information communication and integration, and one for flexibility and leadership. The first two scales were reasonably homogeneous (both with Cronbach alphas of 0.71), but the third scale was less so (alpha = 0.62). Question 10 (fostering management control) was the least related of the four items in this third scale. When it was removed from the scale, the alpha increased to 0.72. Question 10 was analyzed separately but no statistically significant effects were found, so it was dropped from the study.

Two additional sets of data were collected to assess whether the responses of the leaders/organizers were representative. First, one additional participant from four organizations was interviewed using the Venkatraman and Ramanujam measures at the same point in time as the leader/organizer. These respondents had similar views to those of their leaders/organizers (alpha = 0.81). Second, participants from eleven of the organizations completed anonymous questionnaires at the very end of the GSS processes. The agreement between the mean response on these questionnaires and that of their leader/organizer was adequate (alpha = 0.78). Thus, we concluded that the ratings provided by the leader/organizers were reliable measures.

## Discussion

THE RESULTS OF THIS STUDY SUGGEST THREE MAJOR FINDINGS. First, organizations that made more extensive use of process support (i.e., greater use of purely electronic processes rather than chauffeured processes) experienced improved information production/identification and information communication/integration. Second, organizations that made more extensive use of process structure experienced improved information production/identification and possibly improved information communication/integration. Third, organizations that made more extensive use of task structure experienced improved information communication/integration and improved flexibility/leadership. In this section we use qualitative analyses to examine these results in more detail, examine the less-successful cases, and also report on a set of other observations.

## GSS Capabilities

## Process Support

All but one organization used at least one software tool with high process support from an interactive meeting style (e.g., electronic brainstorming). Regression analyses suggest that process support was a key factor in improving production and identification of information, and communication and integration of information.

The purely electronic processes provided parallel communication, group memory, and anonymity. During interviews, the leaders/organizers from twelve organizations cited parallel communication and group memory as important in providing the opportunity for greater participation and information exchange.

Anonymity was seen as an important—or the most important—contribution by fourteen leaders/organizers. They and other participants reported that anonymity encouraged more open and honest discussions than had occurred in the past. Anonymity was perceived to reduce politically based decision making (e.g., said a hospital vice president, “Most organizations bring politics. With anonymity, politics is reduced. It becomes more of a team process”). Anonymity was also perceived to encourage more participation, particularly of lower-status participants (e.g., said a university department head, “Faculty—especially senior faculty—tend to minimize the benefit of anonymity, but junior faculty appreciated it”).

Thus, while participants felt that parallel communication and group memory encouraged wide participation and information exchange, they perceived anonymity as improving the quality of that exchange. The effects of high process support were evident when it was not present: When groups used tools providing lower levels of process support (i.e., a chauffeured meeting), we observed that a few group members typically dominated the discussions, with most lower-status members not participating.

## Process Structure

Regression analyses suggest that process structure was a factor in improving the production and identification of information, and possibly the communication and integration of information. One of the key ways to provide process structure was through the facilitator. The role of the facilitator in providing process structure (e.g., agenda development and chairing the meeting) was seen as important by seventeen leaders/organizers. Five facilitators using similar but not identical methods supported the processes of the thirty organizations in the study. We conducted an additional analysis to determine if there were facilitator effects. We performed three additional ANOVAs (one for each of the three dependent variables) using facilitator as a (random effects) factor. In none of the analyses was the facilitator factor statistically significant. Thus, there were no facilitator effects, all of these facilitators performed similarly, or there were not enough data to permit sufficiently powerful tests for differences.

Process structure was also provided via the GSS software. For example, consider the planning processes of a committee representing an industry association. In a two-day non-GSS process held the previous year, this committee had developed a mission statement that the association's board rejected for being too narrow. As part of the GSS strategic planning cycle, the committee developed a mission statement in two hours that was subsequently accepted by the board. The process structure imposed by the GSS software tool required each member of the committee to contribute his or her own wording of the mission statement, thus ensuring that all participated actively. All potential objections by various stakeholders were voiced and addressed in committee before the statement was submitted to the board.

## Task Structure

Regression analyses suggest that task structure was a key factor in improving the communication and integration of information, and flexibility and leadership. Twenty-five organizations used tools providing high task structure. In most cases, task structure was provided by Topic Commenter, a GSS tool that provided a user-defined framework to analyze the task. For example, an electronics manufacturer used a seven-part framework to evaluate divisional strategic plans, a federal prison used a four-part structure (building, buying, developing alliances, status quo) to discuss the acquisition of its base technology, and a commercial lending organization used a variation of Porter's value chain [41] to develop strategies for competitive advantage.

The ability to structure the discussions was seen as important by eight organizations. However, it is interesting that the tools supporting more complex analytical techniques were used little, as the organizations in this study preferred simpler techniques. Preferences for simple over complex models have been observed elsewhere and may be due to satisficing, parsimony, or the desire to avoid decisional conflict raised by explicit consideration of tradeoffs promoted by more complex strategies [25].

Nonetheless, our study found that simple structures were considered useful. For example, the leader of the commercial lending organization noted that this task structure helped “to reshape our mental models of the organization,” while the leader of a bank observed that it “allowed us to take two large and very diverse operations—a parent company and a wholly owned subsidiary—and integrate the agendas of both sets of executives in setting the framework for one strategic plan.”

## Task Support

GSS task support enabled groups electronically to access and use information developed in earlier stages of the strategic planning processes. While all groups actively used information developed during the GSS-supported meetings, only four opted for electronic access to information developed before the GSS meetings (although participants from all organizations brought paper reports with them). One example of task support involved an electronic manufacturing organization that used the Briefcase tool to provide electronic access to divisional plans, product forecasts, and competitive analyses of rival firms. It could be that GSS task support was not used because task support could be more important for the ongoing use of GSS rather than for the one-time use studied here, since the organizational memory it provides can link decision-making events to a greater extent than traditional paper reports. Conversely, leaders/organizers might simply have thought that the Briefcase tool was not as well implemented or as easy to use as the other tools and may have shied away from it for that reason. Regression analyses found little impact from the provision of task support, although this may be attributable to the small number of organizations that used it.

## Relative Importance

Which GSS capabilities were most important to strategic planning success? To answer this question, we consider the results of the regression analyses and perceptions of participants. Each of the regression analyses in Table 3 provides parameter estimates that indicate the relative importance of each of the factors. Analyses of the variance inflation factors found no evidence of multicolinearity, the presence of which might make these parameter estimates unreliable. In the analyses of information production/identification and communication/integration, process support had the highest parameter estimate, indicating that process support had the strongest effect on those planning capabilities. Task structure was the second most important factor for information communication/integration and was the only significant factor for the strategic planning capabilities relating to flexibility and leadership. Process structure was the second most important factor for information production/identification.

Interviews with the organization leaders/organizers led to similar conclusions. Eighteen out of thirty cited one or more of the process support features (i.e., anonymity, parallelism, group memory) as important, seventeen out of twenty-six who used process structure mentioned facilitation as important, eight out of twenty-five who used task structure mentioned it as important, one out of four who used task support (i.e., Briefcase) mentioned it.

We conclude that, for the organizations and planning activities covered in this study, process support appeared to be the key factor, with task structure and process structure also playing important roles. Overall, task support appeared to have little additional value for these organizations (however, this may be due to the fact that only four organizations utilized a high degree of task support). The implication here is that GSS approaches can add substantial leverage to traditional strategic planning techniques that rely on process structure and task structure as their primary contribution.

## Understanding Lower Ratings

While GSS use was seen as successful by most organizations, there was substantial variation in the mean capability ratings across organizations, as the mean rating (averaged over Venkatraman and Ramanujam's twelve capability items) ranged from 2.75 to 4.88 (where $1 = \text{low}$ , $5 = \text{high}$ ). No statistically significant relationships were found between the mean ratings and group size, strategic planning activities, or type of organization (i.e., for-profit versus not-for-profit).

Three organizations (designated as organizations 4, 11, and 20 in Table 2) gave a mean rating across the twelve capability measures below 3.0, suggesting that they perceived their GSS experiences as less successful than their previous non-GSS planning processes. Each of these cases was examined to understand the low ratings.

## Organization 4

The objectives of this organization's planning meeting were to develop short-term strategic action plans and to identify key long-term issues. The development of the meeting agenda and the selection of specific GSS tools were done in a preplanning meeting between the facilitation staff and an internal consultant. Three issues that reduced meeting success were identified by the CEO: (1) the planning group was not adequately prepared for a discussion of long-term issues; (2) the group did not bring important electronic data (i.e., they were unaware that the GSS could provide task support); and (3) a tool providing low task structure (EBS) was used when one providing high task structure (TC) would have been more appropriate.

## Organization 11

The objectives of this organization were to define key aspects of one subarea of the consulting firm's practice and to determine how the firm would acquire the base technology required to support the practice. The meeting was organized by the partner responsible for the subarea and was attended by partners and office managers responsible for that subarea from the major offices in the United States. The goal of this meeting was to formulate a national strategy to standardize the practice. This was not accomplished. Each member of the group had significant decision-making power for his or her geographic area but little accountability in developing the overall U.S. strategy. The group members did not want to address the issues presented by the leader since standardizing the practice could have diminished their power. The outcome was not what the leader wanted, but he felt that using the GSS helped communicate the partners' concerns and identify key problems.

## Organization 20

This organization spent four days over a three-month period formulating goals (a mission statement), analyzing the environment (i.e., identifying the needs of the association's current and prospective future members), and drafting a specific plan to guide the association over the next three years. While the group accomplished these objectives (producing a strategic plan that was accepted by the association's board of directors), the planning process was problematic. The group leader was a nonvoting member of the planning committee and was weak politically in the face of several strong coalitions within the task force. The agenda was developed by the leader and was strongly challenged on two occasions during the course of the meetings. There was also confusion between the leader, several committee members, and the facilitation staff on the responsibility for between-meeting activities, such as conducting a survey of the association's members, performing the statistical analysis of the survey data, and mailing the minutes of the meeting and reminder notices to committee force members. The group leader perceived no differences between the GSS and non-GSS processes, except in identifying key problem areas and fostering organizational learning, for which the GSS process was less effective.

One common thread linking these three cases was a lack of communication between the group leader/meeting organizer and meeting participants. With organization 4, the CEO was not involved in the session planning, and therefore the wrong tool was used, the group lacked complete preparations, and appropriate system capabilities were not used. With organization 11, the leader had a different set of objectives from the group. With organization 20, there was a lack of agreement on the agenda and policy and poor between-meeting management. We believe that these three organizations spent too little time in sense-making activities, the activities that occur prior to the decision-making stage in which organizational players first realize that a decision has to be made and then define the cause and effect linkages that exist [55, 56]. Thus, when the group actually met and tried to perform the tasks defined by the leader/organizer, there was no agreement on what the task was and how to begin solving it. A rival hypothesis, for which there is less support, is that the lack of success was due to a politically weak leader facing strong political coalition(s), as was the case with organizations 11 and 20. However, meetings of three other organizations with politically weak leaders facing strong coalitions were successful.

A general conclusion is that, while GSS may help, it is not a panacea. It cannot magically resolve underlying conflict or replace accurate communication and planning of planning processes.

## Other Observations

Analysis of the qualitative data suggested several other interesting observations.

## Group Size

One unexpected observation has to do with the size of the strategic planning groups participating in the GSS-based strategic planning processes. Based on the interviews, we found that nineteen of the thirty organizations chose to increase the number of participants from that in previous strategic planning cycles. In previous strategic planning processes without GSS support, the number of participants had been constrained to prevent ineffective meetings; the decision to use GSS lessened this constraint. Interviews with leaders found four motivations for increasing the number and diversity of participants: (1) increasing the information, knowledge, and skills available to the strategic planning group; (2) including those charged with implementing the strategy in its development to facilitate implementation; (3) promoting management development and organizational learning; and (4) gaining political support and co-opting coalitions.

For-profit organizations were more likely to have increased the size of the strategic planning groups than were not-for-profit organizations (14 out of 15 versus 5 out of 15: $F(1,28)=17.72, p=0.001$ ). The nonprofit groups were mostly standing strategic planning committees carefully formed to balance (not always equally) different political interests in the organizations. Increasing (or decreasing) participation might have upset this balance, and thus the leader chose not to change the size.

## Decision Making

Only three of the thirty organizations finalized strategic plans during the meeting. Most decisions were officially announced one week to three months after the GSS-supported meetings. Of the three organizations making formal decisions, two were nonprofit organizations whose decisions were made by democratic votes of all committee members. In the third case, decisions were made individually by each senior manager based on the input received during the meeting; each VP made decisions pertaining to his or her division based on the discussion of the group as a whole.

Thus, in general, few strategic decisions were officially “made” during the GSS sessions. For twenty-seven organizations, the strategic plan was not finalized during the GSS meetings. The GSS meeting was seen as a better way to capture essential information and to begin discussions, but not to finalize them. Decisions were made after subsequent consultation, review, and bargaining among a smaller set of key stakeholders. Therefore, we conclude that, while the use of GSS enabled broader input into the strategic planning process, it did not result in management by democracy.

## Perceived Efficiency

GSS use was perceived as resulting in faster resolution of the strategic planning activities in person-hours and/or calendar time (the time from start to finish of the strategic planning process) by the leaders/organizers of eighteen organizations. There were no negative comments about efficiency. For example, the CEO of a gas utility and bank noted that “we did in two 4-hour sessions what would have taken several months to accomplish.”

## Implications

This study has several implications for managers, system developers, and researchers. One important limitation, however, is that the organizations in this study used GSS for strategy formulation more than any other planning activity. Thus our conclusions apply primarily to the use of GSS for strategic planning processes involving formulation and perhaps less so to processes that do not involve it. Another important limitation is that we studied the use of only one GSS, GroupSystems. While we believe that the results can apply to other GSS that provide similar capabilities, this is an empirical question for future research.

## Managerial Implications

First, from a managerial standpoint, the GSS capability that was most strongly linked to greater planning success was increased use of the purely electronic interactive style of process support. Organizations that used a greater proportion of this form of support were more likely to report better information production/identification and better information communication and integration. The implication here is that greater use of interactive meeting styles leads to greater strategic planning success, although we doubt that relying completely on the electronic communication of interactive processes would prove successful. Some issues simply require verbal discussion to reach closure $[5]$ .

Task structure was arguably the second most important factor, playing a role in improving the communication/integration of information and in promoting flexibility and leadership. Process structure was also important, particularly in improving information production. The implication here is that the greater use of appropriate process and task structures should improve strategic planning success.

We studied one form of GSS where all participants came together in the same room at the same time. There are other forms of GSS that permit participants to work together from different locations and at different times. One application for this technology is for strategic planning in multinational or multidivisional organizations that have executives working in different states or countries. By using a GSS from remote sites, organizations could reduce some of the time and travel costs associated with strategic planning. Initial discussions, idea generation, and analyses could be conducted by participants working in separate locations. When coupled with video conferencing or even audio conferencing, GSS could become an even more powerful tool. While it would be possible to conduct an entire strategic planning cycle without participants coming together for a face-to-face meeting, we believe that most organizations would choose to couple remote GSS use with some face-to-face meetings, in much the same way organizations in this study coupled GSS use with non-GSS face-to-face meetings.

We conclude that GSS can be another useful tool in a manager's strategic planning toolbox. The process support capability is perhaps the most distinctive contribution to the strategic planning process, as process structure, task structure, and task support are often found in non-GSS strategic planning processes (provided either by facilitators or other forms of information technology). We see considerable opportunities for synergy by combining traditional techniques with GSS technology.

## GSS Development Implications

This study suggests that process support features of GSS were especially influential in leading to higher ratings for GSS-supported planning capabilities. The process and task structuring techniques that were used were derived directly from previous non-GSS research (e.g., Porter's value chain), or by adapting techniques used by these organizations in their previous planning processes. While it is important that GSS used for strategic planning incorporate these types of structuring capabilities, we believe developers should focus primarily on developing new and better forms of process support.

One problem with the task support provided by the GSS used in this study was the difficulty in obtaining a global view of the organization's information. While participants could easily navigate through all detailed information produced by the group, and the GSS provided tools to help summarize, cluster, and categorize the information, it was still difficult for the participants to view all of the desired information concurrently, even in summarized form. There simply was not enough room on the computer monitor to display all the information. Non-GSS planning suffers from this limitation to a lesser extent, as one can always tape another flip chart page to the wall (although this manual process can create difficulties in organizing, copying, and managing information). The global view problem will be reduced somewhat as monitors and public display screens get bigger, but we believe this will remain a fundamental problem in GSS-based strategic planning.

## Research Implications

From a research perspective, this study suggests a new area for information technology and strategic planning research that moves beyond the development and application of structured analytical techniques. While these continue to be important, it is also important to consider the opportunities for new or modified techniques for strategic planning made possible by the new communication modalities available in advanced information technologies such as GSS. These technologies may fundamentally change many organizational processes such as strategic planning $[30]$ . For example, bad news has difficulty traveling upward in an organization $[3, 16]$ . Does the use of GSS promote more communication and discussion of negative events during strategic planning, and thus improve organizational performance? As another example, an organization could install a GSS throughout the organization with all managers having desktop access to an ongoing strategic planning “meeting.” With lower-level managers able to discuss strategy with senior managers at will, would this enable an organization to recognize and act more quickly on emerging market opportunities (and threats) that often are first seen by lower-level managers?

Another issue for future research is the nature of managerial participation in the strategic planning process. In the organizations we studied, lower-level managers participated in strategic planning processes much more than they had in the past. They perceived of themselves as having played an important role in shaping the organization's strategy, as did the senior managers present. The question, of course, is whether their input did in fact have an impact on the resulting strategic decisions. In our case, since the GSS was totally anonymous, we could not track contributions to their source. However, it would be possible to alter the GSS to enable researchers to track message sources while leaving the GSS anonymous to participants (provided, of course, that organizations and participants agreed to such a modification). In this way, future research could examine strategic decisions and attempt to identify the source of issues raised in group discussion to determine whether the information and opinions of lower-level managers had any real impacts.

An additional question requiring more investigation is, how does GSS use change over time? In this study, we examined only the first-time use of GSS, although sixteen of these organizations used GSS again for strategic planning and/or other decision making—ten returned to the university and six built their own GSS facility. Based on subsequent interviews with six of these sixteen organizations and observations of their follow-on use of GSS, we observed two general trends: First, the overall assessment of the usefulness of GSS remained quite high, but decreased slightly with increased use. The leaders we interviewed and the participants who completed questionnaires still reported favorable opinions of the GSS, but they were not as overwhelmingly positive as their first use. These decreases could be due to the different strategic planning activities that were performed in the different meetings or to the acceptance of GSS as a common technology, not worthy of special attention. On the other hand, the first-time users may have been influenced by an overly positive “novelty effect,” which wore off in subsequent uses.

A second observation has to do with the manner in which the organizations subsequently used GSS. All six organizations adopted different meeting processes for their second use of GSS. Those that used extremely high or extremely low levels of process support used more balanced levels in subsequent meetings. For example, one organization that initially used almost entirely interactive styles (i.e., only electronic communication) chose a more balanced approach that included more supported styles (i.e., both electronic and verbal communication). Likewise, another organization that initially used very little electronic communication used it much more in its next meeting. These differences could simply be due to the different activities performed in the different meetings, but we believe they are better attributed to a learning effect. Based on their experiences with their first use of GSS, leaders changed their opinions of the “best” way to use GSS for their purposes and adjusted their next use of the GSS accordingly.

One critical area of study is therefore the routine use of GSS in the strategic planning process. How do organizations adopt GSS for strategic planning and what GSS capabilities prove useful over the long haul? What long-term impact might these improved capabilities have on organizational performance and the development of management personnel? As yet, few organizations have adopted GSS for routine use. This study provides some insight into how the technology can be used, but a critical need for additional research remains, although it is likely to be several years before such research of routine use can be undertaken.
