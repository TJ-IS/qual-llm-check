# Integration between Business Planning and Information Systems Planning: An Evolutionary-Contingency Perspective

THOMPSON S.H. TEO AND WILLIAM R. KING

THOMPSON S.H. TEO is a Lecturer in the Department of Decision Sciences at the National University of Singapore. He received his Ph.D. in management information systems from the University of Pittsburgh. His current research interests include IS planning, teleworking, IT personnel, IT adoption and diffusion, and electronic commerce. His academic work has been published in journals such as Communications of the ACM, Decision Sciences, Information and Management, and Journal of Management Information Systems.

WILLIAM R. KING is University Professor at the University of Pittsburgh and Founding President of the Association for Information Systems. He has served as Senior Editor of MIS Quarterly and as president of the Institute of Management Sciences (now INFORMS).

ABSTRACT: One of the key elements of strategic planning for information systems (IS) is the integration of information systems planning (ISP) with business planning (BP). This integration enables IS to support business strategies more effectively. Although this issue has received significant attention in recent years, empirical research focusing specifically on BP-ISP integration is still relatively sparse. This research extends existing results by examining the evolution of BP-ISP integration and the contingency variables that may influence BP-ISP integration.

The results confirmed the existence of an evolutionary pattern that can be defined in terms of movement through four types of BP-ISP integration: administrative integration to sequential integration to reciprocal integration to full integration. Only a few firms indicated that they had reached full integration. Bypassed phases and reverse evolution, though observed, were uncommon. Among the contingency variables, the business competence of the IS executive appeared to be a key factor in influencing the extent of integration.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: alignment of MIS plans with business plans, information systems planning, integration of MIS and business plans.

INFORMATION SYSTEMS PLANNING (ISP) CAN BE DEFINED AS THE PROCESS of establishing objectives for organizational computing and identifying potential applications that the organization should implement [36, 37]. ISP has become increasingly important as organizations attempt to leverage information systems (IS) applications to improve efficiency, reengineer business processes, gain competitive advantage, and compete more effectively.

For ISP to be effective, it is crucial that IS plans be aligned with business plans so that IS can more effectively support business strategies. Chan and Huff [6] found that IS strategic alignment was consistently related to various dimensions of IS effectiveness. Similarly, Das, Zahra, and Warkentin [9] cited an A.T. Kearney study showing that organizations that integrated business plans with IS plans generally outperform those that do not. Hence, it is not surprising that alignment (or integration) between strategic business planning (BP) and ISP has been given significant attention in recent years [42, 54, 63] and has been ranked among the top issues facing IS executives [3].

Despite this importance, past research focusing on BP-ISP integration is mainly at the conceptual level. Most empirical research has focused on general ISP issues rather than on BP-ISP integration. Issues that have been examined include ISP practices $[10]$ , methodologies $[35]$ , problems $[36, 37]$ , steering committees $[14]$ , value $[17]$ and impact $[49, 51]$ , top management support $[52]$ , organizational characteristics $[50]$ , and directions and guidelines for research $[1, 61]$ .

This study extends existing research on BP-ISP integration by combining two different schools of thought—the evolutionary perspective and the contingency perspective—in a single research model.

Specifically, this study addresses the following questions:

1. Does BP-ISP integration within a firm follow an evolutionary pattern over time that can be characterized in terms of an evolutionary typology?

2. Do organizations evolve through all types of BP-ISP integration—that is, can any type of BP-ISP integration be bypassed?

3. What are the relative times spent at each stage and the reasons for movement to the next type of integration?

4. Can contingency variables that influence the extent of BP-ISP integration be identified?

## Background

THE RESEARCH QUESTIONS ADDRESSED IN THIS STUDY ARE BASED on prior research on the evolution of BP-ISP integration and contingency perspectives concerning organizational development.

## Evolution of BP-ISP Integration

King [26] was among the first IS researchers to recognize and emphasize the importance of BP-ISP integration. He proposed an ISP methodology that can be used to integrate IS strategies with business strategies. This methodology emphasizes that the IS strategy set (system objectives, system constraints, and system design strategies) should be derived from the organizational strategy set (business mission, objectives, strategy and other strategic organizational attributes). King's Strategy Set Transformation was later adopted by IBM as part of its Business Systems Planning [18], thereby providing evidence of the practical significance of the concept of BP-ISP integration. Furthermore, empirical evidence of such alignment in practice has been established [54, 63].

King's conceptualization of one-way sequential integration (from BP to ISP) was later expanded by King and Zmud [32] to a two-way reciprocal integration between BP and ISP. The change was brought about by the realization that ISP can be used not only to support but also to influence business strategies. This concept of two-way reciprocal integration was later refined by King [27] and has formed the basis for most subsequent work in this area.

Synnott [59] conceptualized ISP in terms of a number of varieties of BP-ISP integration:

1. No planning: No formal BP or ISP.

2. Stand-alone planning: Presence of either business plan or IS plan, but not both.

3. Reactive planning: IS function reacts to business plans and has no input in the BP process.

4. Linked planning: BP is “interfaced” with ISP. Systems resources are matched against business needs.

5. Integrated planning: BP is indistinguishable from ISP. They occur simultaneously and interactively.

Clearly, in Synnott's first and second types of planning, BP-ISP integration is virtually nonexistent. However, several features of Synnott's conceptualization bear some similarities to earlier conceptualization, such as reactive planning is similar to sequential integration by King [26], and linked planning is similar to reciprocal integration by King and Zmud [32].

Another conceptualization of integrated planning was offered by Goldsmith [13], who illustrated how BP-ISP integration is carried out at Simon Engineering (in England). Both Porter's value chain and competitive forces framework [47] are incorporated into the information engineering methodology for ISP. Goldsmith emphasized that, rather than separating ISP from BP (and aligning them through linkages), ISP should be integrated within BP. He further argued that, in order for competitive advantage to be secured from IT applications, information strategies need to be developed in the same process and at the same time as business strategies (similar to integrated planning by Synnott [59]).

The literature review thereby provides support for four distinct forms of ISP [31, 60] that are identified and used in this study:

\- Type 1: separate planning with administrative integration. In this type of integration, there is a weak relationship between BP and ISP. Generally, there is little significant effort to use information technology (e.g., computers, telecommunications) to support business plans.

\- Type 2: one-way linked planning with sequential integration. In this type of integration, BP provides directions for ISP. ISP primarily focuses on providing support for business plans.

\- Type 3: two-way linked planning with reciprocal integration. In this type of integration, there is a reciprocal and interdependent relationship between BP and ISP. ISP plays both a role in supporting and influencing business plans.

\- Type 4: integrated planning with full integration. In this type of integration, there is little distinction between the BP process and the ISP process. Business and information systems strategies are developed concurrently in the same integrated planning process.

The evolutionary perspective suggests not only that there are different kinds of BP-ISP integration in various firms but that firms may evolve from one level of integration to another (generally from lesser levels of integration to greater levels), as planning matures and becomes more established and more important to the organization.

This evolutionary view suggests that each successive integration level raises the strategic potential of IT and enables more effective alignment between IS and business strategies. The roles of the IS function for types 1 and 2 are primarily reactive in nature since ISP has negligible influence on business strategies. Conversely, the roles of the IS function for types 3 and 4 are primarily proactive in nature since the IS function plays a role in both supporting and influencing business strategies.

## Contingency Perspectives

The contingency perspective has been widely applied in both organizational and IS research $[50, 62]$ . Contingency theory, introduced by Lawrence and Lorsch $[34]$ and refined by Kast and Rosenzweig $[23]$ , is based on the idea that there is no single best way to achieve the necessary “fit” between organizational factors and (in this case) planning process types. This approach attempts to understand the interrelationships within, and among, organizational factors, thus emphasizing the multivariate nature of organizations.

Typically, in IS research, both organizational characteristics (e.g., information intensity) and environmental characteristics (e.g., environmental uncertainty) have been studied. Both categories of contingency variables have been found to influence ISP [20] and the strategic use of IT [56]. Both organizational and environmental characteristics can also therefore be expected to influence the extent of BP-ISP integration.

## Combining the Evolutionary and the Contingency Perspectives

THE EVOLUTIONARY PERSPECTIVE AND THE CONTINGENCY PERSPECTIVE differ in that the contingency perspective emphasizes that there are no predictable patterns (“no best way”) whereas the evolutionary perspective emphasizes the presence of predictable patterns. While both perspectives have been widely used in both organizational and IS research, they are quite different ways of viewing organizational situations.

Although evolutionary models have been used in IS research for many years $[8, 45]$ , the simplistic deterministic nature of such models suggests that they have limited potential for explaining complex organizational phenomena. Venkatraman $[61]$ emphasized that the development of any research framework on ISP should reflect a contingency perspective as it is too simplistic to expect that ISP can be described in universalistic terms (as in the evolutionary perspective).

In this study, we use the evolutionary and contingency approaches in combination in order to take advantage of the benefits of both. Table 1 shows how a “pure” evolutionary or “pure” contingency view of the types of BP-ISP integration would differ. In the right column, it shows how a combined approach can achieve some of the values of both of these approaches.

This combined approach permits alternative possible evolutionary paths rather than allowing a single inevitable path. This does not mean that firms evolve in a random manner; rather, it means that some paths are more likely than others. In addition, firms need not necessarily start at the least sophisticated level of integration and end at the most sophisticated level of integration. Evidence of the presence of multiple paths and bypassed phases can be found in both the organizational [41] and IS [2] literatures.

In terms of reverse evolution, the evolutionary perspective emphasizes that it is unlikely [33], though under the contingency perspective, reverse evolution is possible. By combining both perspectives, a reversal of direction from a level of greater integration to one of lesser integration is not ruled out and will be empirically assessed.

The evolutionary perspective emphasizes that contingency variables are correlated with the level of growth (e.g., [25]). Similarly, a contingency perspective emphasizes that contingency variables are correlated with the extent of integration (e.g., [9]). The two perspectives can be combined by using the type of integration (types 1–4) as a measure of the extent of integration.

Thus, the combined evolutionary-contingency model appears to be superior to either of its constituent perspectives in that:

1. The evolutionary perspective provides some “structure” to the contingency perspective by suggesting the existence of nonrandom predictable patterns of growth applicable to most firms. The development of contingency theory in terms of organizational gestalts [41] is perhaps an indication of the need for greater “structure.”

2. The contingency perspective reinforces the “realism” of the evolutionary perspective by suggesting that it is too simplistic to expect firms to evolve in the same manner. Multiple paths of evolution, bypassed phases, and reverse evolution are made possible by the combined approach, which adds richness to the analysis.

## Operationalizing the Research Constructs

IN ORDER TO IMPLEMENT THE RESEARCH MODEL, the contingency variables and the phases of integration must be operationalized.

## Contingency Variables

This section describes the organizational and environmental characteristics that are likely to influence the evolution of the firm through the various stages of BP-ISP integration. The variables were chosen on the basis on their likely impact on IS planning and the strategic use of IT and, consequently, the evolution of BP-ISP integration.

## Organizational Characteristics

1. Information Intensity of Products/Services: Linder and Ives [38] defined information intensity as “the amount of intellectual work done by people as they conduct their affairs . . . products are information intensive if their selection, purchase, use and maintenance require careful research and thoughtful consideration by the customer” (pp. 4–5). IT is more likely to play a major role in organizations with high information intensity of products/services.

Porter and Miller [48] suggested several indicators of information intensity: products that also provide information, products whose operations require substantial information processing, and so on. Although these indicators were originally intended for the information intensity of products, they can be equally applied to the information intensity of services.

## Figure 1. Research Model

2. Information Intensity of the Value Chain: IT can also be used to support the various stages of a firm's value chain. A value chain is a system of interdependent activities performed to transform raw materials into final products that are delivered to customers.

Busch et al. [5] suggested the following indicators of value chain information intensity: the extent of information use, the frequency of information updating, and accuracy of information. Porter and Millar [48] suggested that the number of steps in the production or service process is also indicative of the information intensity of the value chain. These operationalizations assume that a greater number of, variety of, and time spent at each activity of the value chain results in the need to process more information.

3. Top Management's Perception of IT Importance: Past research has shown that top management influence and support is a key factor in influencing the strategic use of information resources [28] and IT plan quality [4]. In addition, top management's perception of IT importance has been found to be significantly associated with the progressive use of information resources [5, 21].

Top management's perception of IT importance can be operationalized in terms of: top management's recognition of the strategic potential of IT [44], top management's commitment to IS function [42], top management's knowledge about information assets and opportunities [62], top management's recognition that IT is essential to the success of the firm, and top management's view of IT spending as a strategic investment [5].

4. IS Competence: IS competence refers to the capabilities of the IS function. It can be operationalized in terms of perceived competence within the firm and its relative competence compared to competitors. Hence, it can be operationalized in terms of general technical expertise and technological leadership in the industry [44]. In addition, the presence of extensive computer facilities within the organization [43], the reliability and efficiency of services provided by the IS function [42], the IS executive's knowledge about business [22], and the IS executive's ability to identify and plan for future challenges [12] are also indicators of IS competence.

## Environmental Characteristics

1. Environmental Uncertainty: A major problem facing most organizations is the uncertainty of the environment, which can make even a well-formulated plan obsolete and impractical. IT has been used by many organizations to reduce environmental uncertainty. For example, IT enables organizations to manage interdependence better through improved coordination [55]; IT provides decision support to better evaluate alternatives [15]; and IT increases the ability to cope with information overload by increasing the organization's information-processing capacity [11]. In fact, the use of IT is increasingly recognized as a necessity for many companies to survive in the uncertain competitive environment [29].

Environmental uncertainty can be defined in terms of dynamism, heterogeneity and hostility of the environment. The operationalizations proposed by Miller and Friesen [40] and Sabherwal and King [56] are used in this study whenever they are appropriate.

Dynamism refers to the rate and unpredictability of environmental change and can be operationalized as the rate of product obsolescence, the rate of product change, the unpredictability of demand changes, and the unpredictability of competitors' moves.

Heterogeneity is the “complexity that encompasses variations among the firm’s markets that require diversity in production and marketing orientation” [40, p. 222]. It can be operationalized as diversity in customers’ buying habits, diversity in the nature of competition, and diversity in product lines.

Hostility refers to both the availability of resources and the degree of competition. It can be operationalized as the threat posed by the availability of resources, price competition, and competition in product quality or product differentiation.

## Operationalizing BP-ISP Integration

In this study, a description and a conceptual portrayal of each type of integration were deemed an adequate operationalization (see survey instrument in the appendix). This self-typing approach has been commonly used in organizational research $[19, 58]$ .

## Research Propositions and Hypotheses

PROPOSITIONS THAT DEAL WITH THE PATHS OF EVOLUTION, reverse evolution, and contingency variables are developed. Subsequently, research hypotheses relating contingency variables to the extent of integration are also developed.

## Paths of Evolution

The importance of IT has increased over the years. As a result, BP-ISP integration becomes increasingly important as firms strive to better deploy IT to support business strategies. Over time, therefore, we can expect increasing levels of integration between BP and ISP as the IS function begins to have a greater impact on the organization. It follows that generally the evolution of BP-ISP integration should be in the direction of increasing extent of BP-ISP integration, namely: administrative integration → sequential integration → reciprocal integration → full integration.

However, the question remains as to whether all firms necessarily pass through all four distinct stages. Nolan [45] indicated that stages of evolution in the (different) context that he studied cannot be bypassed. However, the findings of King and Rateb [29] pertaining to the evolution of information centers, and by Brancheau and Amoroso [2] pertaining to the evolution of end-user computing management strategies, suggest that some stages of evolution might indeed be bypassed.

Miller and Friesen [41] found that organizations need not necessarily go through the phases in the same sequence; for example, growth may be followed by maturity or decline. It follows that there are likely to be multiple paths of evolution rather than only one path. Furthermore, evolution need not necessarily start with the simplest type of integration or end with the most sophisticated, and it is possible for the firm to bypass certain phases. It follows that:

Proposition 1: Various firms will demonstrate different paths of evolution.

## Reverse Evolution

Empirical evidence for reverse evolution has not been reported in the IS literature. However, it is possible that a firm might experiment with a more “advanced” level of BP-ISP integration, find that level inappropriate, and revert to its former level. However, in order for the stage model to be valid, reverse evolution should be the exception rather than the norm $[33]$ . Hence,

Proposition 2: Among firms that experience evolution, reverse evolution occurs infrequently.

## Contingency Variables

Contingency variables have been previously defined in terms of organizational and environmental characteristics. These variables are likely to have a contingent effect on the evolutionary process of BP-ISP integration as discussed in the research model in figure 1. It follows that:

Proposition 3: Environmental and organizational variables will have a contingent effect on the evolutionary process.

Subsequently, we can derive research hypotheses pertaining to specific contingent relationships between each variable and the extent of BP-ISP integration.

## Information Intensity of Products/Services

Porter and Millar [48] emphasized that the greater the information intensity of products offered by the firm, the greater the likelihood that the IS function will play an important or strategic role in the firm. Furthermore, past research has shown that the information intensity of products is positively related to the progressive use of IT [5] in the firm. Consequently, firms with high information intensity of products are more likely to have greater sophistication in ISP and greater extent of BP-ISP integration than other firms. (Since these arguments can equally be applied to the information intensity of services, the terms “products/services” are used.)

It follows that:

Hypothesis 1: The information intensity of products/services is positively related to the extent of BP-ISP integration.

## Information Intensity of the Value Chain

Firms characterized by high information intensity of the value chain should have greater opportunities to exploit IT (e.g., launch new IT-related products, form strategic alliances through interorganizational systems) than other firms. It follows that the higher the information intensity of the firm's value chain, the greater the potential role of the IS function [48] and the greater the need for BP-ISP integration. Furthermore, firms with high information intensity of the value chain are more likely to have greater sophistication in ISP than other firms. Consequently, it is likely that firms with high information intensity of the value chain will operate at higher levels of BP-ISP integration compared to other firms. Thus,

Hypothesis 2: The information intensity of the value chain is positively related to the extent of BP-ISP integration.

## Top Management's Perception of IT Importance

Top management's perceptions and decisions are likely to influence the importance allotted to ISP and therefore to the extent of BP-ISP integration in the firm. If top management perceives that IT can be leveraged to support strategic initiatives and improve organizational performance, then it is likely that more emphasis will be given to BP-ISP integration. Hence,

Hypothesis 3: Top management's perception of IT's importance is positively related to the extent of BP-ISP integration.

## IS Competence

IS competence should be a major factor in determining the appropriate role of the IS function in the firm and the feasibility of proposed business strategies that attempt to leverage IT for strategic purposes. Firms with greater distinctive IS competence should be more likely to leverage IS applications for competitive advantage [28, 43, 44] than are firms with lower distinctive IS competence. Furthermore, IS competence (especially in terms of knowledge about business processes) should play an important role in the successful interaction between the IS function and user/business management during strategy formulation and implementation. Consequently, IS competence is likely to influence the firm's evolution through the various levels of BP-ISP integration. Thus,

Hypothesis 4: IS competence is positively related to the extent of BP-ISP integration.

## Environmental Uncertainty

Companies should be more likely to be more dependent on the use of IT under conditions of high environmental uncertainty than under low environmental uncertainty. High environmental uncertainty also should require greater sophistication of planning systems. However, for planning to be effective, functional plans (e.g., IS plans) should be formally aligned with organizational goals and objectives, thereby resulting in a need for greater BP-ISP integration. Furthermore, greater BP-ISP integration should help to ensure that strategies are feasible and do not become obsolete due to changing environmental conditions.

Since environmental uncertainty has been operationalized in terms of three dimensions—dynamism, heterogeneity, and hostility [40]—it follows that:

Hypothesis 5a: Environmental dynamism is positively related to the extent of BP-ISP integration.

Hypothesis 5b: Environmental heterogeneity is positively related to the extent of BP-ISP integration.

Hypothesis 5c: Environmental hostility is positively related to the extent of BP-ISP integration.

## Research Methodology

## The Sample

A RANDOM SAMPLE OF 600 FIRMS WAS OBTAINED from the Corporate 1000 book. This listing includes the top 1,000 corporations in the United States, as well as the names of their senior executives. The sample was taken from this source because firms that are listed are generally medium-sized to large and consequently likely to have experience in ISP as well as to have more formal planning processes than smaller firms.

Questionnaires were prepared and sent directly to the CEO with instructions requesting him or her to forward the questionnaires to the senior business planner and to the senior information systems executive. The directions for distribution said that if ISP was carried out centrally at the corporate level for most business segments (i.e., divisions or business units), the questionnaires should be forwarded to the senior business planner and the senior information systems executive responsible for BP and ISP at the corporate level. Conversely, if ISP was carried out independently in each business segment (i.e., division or business unit), the CEO should select a core business segment and forward the questionnaires to the senior business planner and the senior information systems executive responsible for that segment.

## The Instrument

The four different types of BP-ISP integration (administrative, sequential, reciprocal, and full) were described and respondents were asked to indicate which type best described their firm's current BP-ISP integration (see the survey instrument in the appendix).

The IS executives were asked whether they had experienced each type of integration, how much time they had spent at each type, and the primary reasons for moving to another type. In addition, they also indicated their current type of integration.

The business planners were asked questions relating to organizational and environmental characteristics. Since the current types of integration and contingency variables were measured using different respondents within each firm, the problem of common source variance is reduced.

A series of pretests were carried out with doctoral and M.B.A. students, IS practitioners, and graduate business school faculty members over a period of about three months. Changes to the instrument were made following comments and feedback from respondents at each stage of pretesting. The pretests resulted in changes in the wordings of certain items and the rearrangement of the order of the items to improve clarity and to minimize ambiguity. These pretests also provided part of the basis for the distribution procedure, since they indicated that, in some instances, the organizational subunit needed to be clearly identified.

## Response Analysis

Two followup mailings were made; the followups were made about three and seven weeks from the date of initial mailings. Forty-five firms declined to participate, citing one or more of the following reasons: it was company policy not to respond to questionnaires, the firm lacked manpower, or the company was currently undergoing restructuring. Usable “matched pairs” were returned by 157 firms (26.2 percent). This response rate is adequate but slightly lower than that sometimes achieved in other surveys. This may be explained by the high executive level of the respondents and the fact that we required two responses per firm. Previous research using the “matched-pair” design has usually involved an IS manager and an IS user, neither of whom might be a senior executive (e.g., [53]). This study involved two senior executives.

## Assessing Construct Validity and Reliability

Since a single item might not fully tap a construct or might be subject to misinterpretation by the respondents, multiple items were used for each construct. Construct validity was determined using factor analysis of the multiple items comprising each construct. Specifically, principal component analysis with varimax rotation was used to determine if all items measuring a construct cluster together, that is, all items measuring a construct should load onto a single factor. Items with loadings of less than 0.5 on any factor or with loadings greater than 0.5 on more than one factor were dropped from subsequent analyses [44]. Furthermore, each item should add to the reliability of the construct. Two separate joint factor analyses for organizational and environmental characteristics were carried out to get a more stable solution by increasing the ratio of the sample size to the number of items.

The results of the factor analyses for organizational and environmental characteristics, as shown in Tables 3 and 4, confirm that each construct is distinct from other constructs. However, the constructs measuring IS competence, environmental dynamism, and environmental hostility loaded onto two factors. A closer examination of the items comprising the constructs revealed that there were actually two dimensions of IS competence: namely, technical competence and business competence. This distinction between technical competence and business competence is potentially important since some research [22] has emphasized that strategic applications of IT are greatly facilitated when the IS function is both technically competent and knowledgeable about the business.

Similarly, environmental dynamism is composed of both the changeability and predictability of the environment, while environmental hostility is composed of both hostility due to both the scarcity of resources and the degree of competition. These dimensions of environmental dynamism and environmental hostility are consistent with the findings of Sharfman and Dean [57].

For statistical analyses, the two dimensions of IS competence, environmental dynamism and environmental hostility, were treated as separate constructs. The multiple items comprising each construct were summed together into a single score. This assumes that each item contributes equally to the total score. This approach has been used successfully in previous research examining ISP [49] and the strategic use of IT [44].

The reliability of the scales was assessed using Cronbach's alpha. The value of Cronbach's alpha gives an indication of the internal consistency of the items measuring the same construct. High values of Cronbach's alpha indicate high internal consistency of the multiple items measuring each construct, hence indicating high reliability of the individual constructs. In general, the reliability coefficients are above the recommended value of 0.60 (see Table 5) for exploratory research [46].

## Results and Discussion

## THE RESULTS ARE DISCUSSED HERE IN TERMS OF THE research questions posed earlier.

## 1. Does BP-ISP integration within a firm follow an evolutionary pattern over time that can be characterized in terms of an evolutionary typology?

The results show that sequential integration (41.4 percent) and reciprocal integration (41.4 percent) occur most often, followed by administrative integration (10.8 percent) and full integration (6.4 percent). This is not unexpected since, in today's technological era, most firms are likely to move forward rapidly from administrative integration. However, somewhat surprisingly, very few firms indicated that they had reached full integration.

The distribution of the stages of integration is generally intuitively appealing in that most firms were midway between administrative and full integration. That so few firms had reached full integration implies that ISP had yet to become an integral part of business planning.

As expected, the paths generally run from administrative integration to sequential integration to reciprocal integration to full integration. ## 2. Do organizations go through all of the types of BP-ISP integration, i.e., can any type of BP-ISP integration be bypassed?

Table 6 shows that thirty-seven firms (23.6 percent) did not indicate any evolution. Evolution without bypassed phases of integration constitutes about 58 percent of the sample. In contrast, evolution with bypassed phases constitutes about 16.6 percent of the sample. Statistical comparison between evolution with and without bypassed phases showed significant results (chi-square = 36.11, df = 1, p < 0.001). This supports the notion that the bypassing of phases occurs relatively infrequently. The occurrence of different paths of evolution provides support for proposition 1: “Various firms will demonstrate different paths of evolution.”

Only three firms (1.9 percent) indicated the occurrence of reverse evolution, that is, evolution from greater integration to lesser integration. Of these three firms, one attributed this reverse evolution to a change in the reporting relationships due to organizational restructuring; another firm explained that there was a change in planning policy by top management; the third firm explained that the attempt to engage in reciprocal integration was unsuccessful due to poor acceptance by other functional units.

Table 7 shows the number of years spent in each type of integration for firms currently in a later phase. It seems probable that, once a firm recognized the potential role of information technology, movement to higher levels of integration took place more rapidly to allow firms more effectively to leverage information technology in support of business strategy formulation and implementation. Another reason for this acceleration is that the movement to greater levels of integration is facilitated by the learning and experience acquired at previous lower levels of integration. In addition, rapid advances in information technology have made the use of IT more necessary for organizational survival. This implies that the use of IT to support or reengineer business processes may be rapidly changing from an option to a necessity. Consequently, the time spent at each stage decreases as the use of information technology becomes more widespread, and greater levels of BP-ISP integration become necessary to the business planning process.

## Reasons for Movement to the Next Level of Integration

The respondents gave a wide variety of reasons for movement from one level of integration to the next. These reasons were sorted by one of the authors and a doctoral student. Each reason was written on a $3'' \times 5''$ index card and sorted independently. Disagreements were discussed, and the final decision was made jointly. Generally, an agreement rate of the two raters of above 90 percent was obtained.

The reasons for movement were ultimately sorted into six categories: (1) increasing need for better integration; (2) increasing importance of IT; (3) organizational change; (4) increasing competence; (5) increasing need for better information; and (6) miscellaneous. Table 8 shows that the first three reasons encompass the majority of the responses. They may therefore be considered the primary reasons for movement to the next stage of integration. Note that the reasons for movement seem to focus more on internal factors than external factors. This is in agreement with previous research on the progressive or strategic use of IT [5, 30].

We examined this research question at two levels. First, we examined the intercorrelation matrix of the contingency variables and the dependent variable. This provided a more stringent test of the relationship between contingency variables and the dependent variable compared with correlation coefficients because it took into account the presence of other variables.

## Hypotheses Testing

Table 10 shows hierarchical multiple regression analyses of the variables that make up organizational and environmental characteristics used to predict the level (type) of integration. We initially entered all of the environmental variables since they were more remote from the dependent variable and were likely to have a weaker effect than the organizational variables (as evident from the intercorrelation matrix in Table 9). Next, we entered all of the organizational variables. This provided a test of the association of these variables with the dependent variable after the environmental variables were taken into account.

In step 1 of the hierarchical regression analysis, when all the environmental variables were entered, the F-value was not significant and the adjusted $R^{2}$ value was only 0.01. In step 2, we entered all the organizational variables and obtained a significant F-value and an adjusted $R^{2}$ of 0.17. Surprisingly, only one of the organizational variables—namely, business competence of the IS executive (BUSCOMP)—was significant. This suggested that a single variable was largely responsible for the dramatic increase in adjusted $R^{2}$ . To confirm this, we reperformed step 2, but omitted BUSCOMP (step 1a in Table 10). This gave us an adjusted $R^{2}$ value of 0.08, thus suggesting that a single variable, BUSCOMP, accounted for about 9 percent of the variation in the dependent variable.

Hence, only one hypothesis was supported. These results failed to confirm that the information intensity of the products/services (INFOPDT), the information intensity of the value chain (INFOVC), top management's perceptions of IT importance (MGMTIT), technical competence (TECHCOMP) of the IS executive were significantly positively related to the level (type) of BP-ISP integration. In addition, none of the hypotheses pertaining to environmental characteristics (H5a-c) was supported.

These results show that the business competence of the IS executive may be more important than technical competence. This suggests that IS executives should be well versed in the business if they wish to play a more active role in business planning. Note that technical competence without adequate business competence is apparently insufficient to facilitate greater BP-ISP integration.

## Limitations

THE MAIN LIMITATION OF THIS STUDY IS THAT THE QUESTIONS pertaining to the evolutionary paths may be somewhat “leading” in their nature. However, we have mitigated this through extensive pretesting as well as by asking respondents about the time spent in each type of integration and the reasons for moving to the next type of integration. This serves as a validation check to ensure that respondents do think about the evolutionary path their firms have experienced. Also, the fact that some firms indicated that they bypassed one or more types of integration or experienced reverse evolution suggests that this limitation is not serious.

## Implications

THIS STUDY CONTRIBUTES TO EXISTING RESEARCH ON IS planning by conceptualizing a research framework that combines the evolutionary perspective and the contingency perspective. The proposed evolutionary-contingency model enables us to examine whether the contingency variables influence the evolution of the firm through the various types of BP-ISP integration.

Among the contingency variables, a single variable—business competence of IS executive—proved the best predictor of extent of integration. Future research can develop further measures to distinguish between technical and business competence of IS executives.

The development of two sets of measuring instrument to tap various constructs is a methodological contribution of this study. Generally, the instrument exhibits high levels of validity and reliability. Future research can use, adapt, and improve this instrument.

This study also illustrates how multiple respondents can be incorporated in a research survey to enhance both the reliability and the validity of the study by minimizing the effects of common source variance. Common source variance is reduced since the independent and dependent variables are measured separately using different respondents in a firm.

Other avenues for future research include incorporation of other contingency variables in the research model. Future research might also examine the specific factors that influence: (1) movement from one type of integration to another; (2) omission of any particular stage; and (3) time spent at each stage. Attempts may also be made to link the various types of integration with performance measures for the IS function.

The study can also be replicated with a larger sample or with a sample from small companies. In the latter case, comparisons might be made between small and large companies in terms of the frequencies of various types of integration as well as their evolutionary paths. Longitudinal studies might also contribute to our understanding of these phenomena.

The practical implications of the study primarily lie in developing a better understanding of the variables influencing the evolution of BP-ISP integration. Business and IS practitioners should therefore be able to manage and plan the process of integrating BP and ISP better. This in turn should ultimately lead to more successful planning systems in terms of reduced planning problems and increased organizational impact.

Business management might also make use of the conceptualization of the four types of BP-ISP integration as a communication tool to better understand and promote consensus regarding the appropriate role of IT in their firms. IS executives can use the types of integration to communicate and convince top management of the importance of BP-ISP integration in facilitating greater IS contributions to business strategy formulation and implementation. Anecdotal evidence from this study suggests that both of these uses have already been put into practice in some firms.

The empirical finding that business competence of the IS executive plays a key role in facilitating greater BP-ISP integration is important because it indicates that business competence may be even more important than technical competence. Most business firms seem intuitively to understand this in the choice of a chief information officer (CIO), since most individuals selected for this position have strong business competence. However, these results suggest that business management seeking to improve their BP-ISP integration might focus even greater attention on ensuring that IS management is knowledgeable about the business.
