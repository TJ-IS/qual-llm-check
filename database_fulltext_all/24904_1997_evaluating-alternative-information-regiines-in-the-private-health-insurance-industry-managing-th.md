# Evaluating Alternative Information Regimes in the Private Health Insurance Industry: Managing the Social Cost of Private Information

ERIC K. CLEMONS AND MATT E. THATCHER

ERIC K. CLEMONS. For biographical information, see Special Section, Guest Editor's Introduction.

MATT E. THATCHER is an Assistant Professor of Management Information Systems at the Karl Eller School of Business of the University of Arizona. He also works with the Reginald H. Jones Center's Sponsored Research Project on Information: Industry Structure and Competitive Strategy of The Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania. His education includes a B.S. in economics, an A.M. in information technology, and a forthcoming Ph.D. in information technology, all from The Wharton School. His research interests include the economic impacts of information systems, the strategic uses of information, organizational resistance to strategic change, and the productivity of information technology.

ABSTRACT: This paper addresses the cost imposed on an insurance market when individual applicants possess an information advantage over the insurers; in short, we examine the social cost of private information. When consumers differ significantly in terms of their riskiness, and their insurance companies cannot, or are not permitted to assess these differences, insurance companies will attempt to charge all consumers the same premiums for equivalent coverage; unless mechanisms can be designed to allow consumers to signal accurately and credibly their private assessments of their own riskiness, low-risk consumers will consider that they are being overcharged and many will drop out of the market. These consumers, who wish to buy insurance—albeit at a lower price—but who are not now insured, represent a loss of social welfare. As information asymmetries increase, more consumers determine that they are being overcharged, increasing the loss of social welfare. This paper addresses minimizing the loss of social welfare, or maximizing the extent to which the population is insured, in the presence of extremely different consumer risk profiles by examining plausible pricing and policy designs, in order to assess which design minimizes decreases in consumer participation in the insurance market. While unable to make selections among information regimes, which entail a wide range of complex and subjective social choices, the paper does make clear comparisons of alternative designs within each information regime.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: adverse selection, information asymmetry, information economics, insurance regulation.

GROWING CAPABILITIES IN COMPUTING, INFORMATION, AND TELECOMMUNICATIONS technologies, combined with a growing understanding of how to utilize these technologies, have rapidly increased the availability and importance of information in today's business environment. These advances will significantly affect the decisions made by consumers, the products offered by firms, and the structure of many industries. One industry marked by such rapid changes in information availability and in information access is the private health insurance industry. Recent environmental shifts, including advances in technology and changes in regulation, have dramatically affected not only the information endowment of individuals applying for private health insurance, but also that of health insurance companies. The impacts of these shifts on the efficiency of the market, the welfare of individuals, and the performance of firms within the market are poorly understood by insurance executives, industry regulators, and insurance applicants alike. This paper helps consumers, insurance managers, and government regulators understand the vitally important issues presently facing the industry as a result of changes in the availability of information.

In particular, we shall examine how changes in the availability of information in the private health insurance industry affect the efficiency of the market. In more traditional economic terms, we examine the social cost (or allocative inefficiency) imposed upon such a market by the presence of private information. $^{1}$ When consumers differ significantly in their riskiness and their insurance companies cannot or are not permitted to assess these differences, insurance companies will charge all applicants the same premiums for equivalent coverage; unless mechanisms can be designed to allow consumers to signal their private assessments of their own riskiness accurately and credibly, those consumers who believe they are at low risk will determine that they are being overcharged and many of them will drop out of the market. $^{2}$ These consumers, who would want to buy insurance—albeit at a lower (or more fair) price—remain uninsured; this inability to insure lower-risk consumers represents a loss in social welfare attributed to the existence of private information.

This work addresses the problem of reducing the loss of social welfare or, alternatively, maximizing the extent to which the population is insured in the presence of widely differing consumer risk profiles. This is done through examinations of the alternative information endowments of consumers and private health insurance companies that result from the interaction of technology and regulation on the parties' information levels; these alternatives are called information regimes. We then examine plausible pricing and policy designs in order to assess which design allows the smallest decrease in consumer participation in the insurance market. While we are unable to make selections among information regimes, since these depend not only on market participation but also on other complex social choices, our model does facilitate clear comparisons among alternative designs within each information regime.

## Context

A definition of insurability generally includes the following characteristics:

\- Risk pooling: There must be a suitable level of information and a suitable level of ambiguity or uncertainty about certain events for insurance markets to exist. For example, both insurers and policy holders can determine rough estimates of risk based on construction and location of a house. This enables insurance companies to estimate their expected losses from a large pool of similar houses over a long enough period of time and enables consumers to estimate the expected value of the policy, but neither party can determine exactly what the risk is to any individual home or any individual homeowner.

\- Indemnification: There must be credible off-loading of risk, through an enforceable contract, from the insured to a financially secure insurer, such that the insuring firm and not the insured individual is at risk once the contract is written.

\- Rating: Using information on similar policy holders and recent historical data, it must be possible to assess expected losses with sufficient accuracy to support a profitable (or at least break-even) insurance market and to price policies appropriately.

Advances in technology and changes in regulatory policy have generated much debate in the private health insurance industry on each of these criteria. The rapidly growing availability of information and the statistical ability to correlate, mine, and find patterns in it fundamentally threaten risk pooling. The shrinking of risk pools alters the fundamental structure of the private health insurance industry and potentially destroys insurability, as we will see. While too much information destroys risk pooling, too little information, or one party with far more information than the other, likewise can destroy rating and alter the structure of the industry.

## Information Technology

## Genetic Testing

Advances in biological and genetic research have enhanced our understanding of the link between human genes and disease. $^{3}$ Genes responsible for over one-half of the 6,500 diseases known to be inherited through single-gene defects have been identified [26]. Researchers have identified genetic components of Alzheimer's, breast cancer, cystic fibrosis, Huntington's, and Tay Sach's, as well as those associated with nontraditional diseases such as mental illness, obesity, and alcoholism [25, 32]. $^{4}$ More specifically, studies now show that mutations of the P53 gene are closely associated with the development of most cancers, including ovarian, colonic, pulmonary, and testicular cancers [2], and that women who inherit mutations of the BRCA–1, BRCA–2, or P53 genes are more likely to develop breast cancer than those who do not [3, 17].

This growing knowledge of human genetics has enabled researchers to develop tests, such as DNA and chromosome analysis, that can determine if individuals possess genetic mutations that may increase their chances of developing certain diseases. $^{5}$ While the predictive power of these genetic tests is increasing with advances in research and technology, the associated costs (i.e., the prices of these tests) are decreasing [13, 32]. These tests may reveal individuals' predispositions to a wide range of diseases, affording them more accurate assessments of their propensity to acquire such diseases. If individuals test as genetically at risk, they may be able to engage in behaviors (e.g., preventive care and life-style changes) that can help mitigate their genetic propensity to develop such diseases. However, this may often not be the case because the ability to prevent, treat, or cure many diseases (e.g., breast cancer) is still quite limited [3, 8].

## Data Mining

On the other hand, rapid advances in data warehousing and data mining enable insurance companies to draw more accurate inferences about the expected claims profiles of insurance applicants. These are technology-based statistical and logic tools that search through large databases for hidden patterns, finding predictive information that experts may miss because it lies outside their expertise or because its detection was previously too time-consuming. High-speed systems have the power to perform data mining and other complex processes at speeds not possible before $[11]$ . Insurance companies are beginning to use these technologies to develop better assessments of insurance applicants' personal riskiness based on factors such as family history, life-style choices, and consumption patterns. To facilitate the effective use of data-mining techniques, a growing number of insurance companies are extracting data from existing legacy systems, which tend to be product-focused, and transforming these data into customer-focused information databases.

Data-mining techniques provide insurance companies with the tools to manage effectively the growing size and complexity of business data, enabling the development of customized products and services that combine what customers want with what the company can profitably offer. Data mining is not merely about using IT to improve efficiency; it is about using information in new ways to compete effectively in a rapidly changing environment $[29]$ .

## Regulatory Policy

## Information Restriction

Regulators have been wary of the implications of this growing availability of information in the private health insurance industry for consumers' ability to purchase coverage. In fact, as of this writing, twelve states had passed legislation that in some form or other restricts insurance companies' use of genetic information to make underwriting decisions on individual applicants. The most recent, and most stringent, of these laws is the New Jersey Genetic Privacy Act (NJGPA), passed in the summer of 1996 and signed into law the following December. This legislation bans health insurance companies from denying policies to—or setting higher rates for—people who are genetically predisposed to certain diseases; that is, insurance companies cannot use any genetic information—whether the result of a genetic test or of observations of family history—in their underwriting decisions. Eleven states, including Arizona, Colorado, Georgia, Minnesota, and New Hampshire, had previously passed weaker versions of this legislation, generally banning health insurance companies from using information that turns up in genetic tests to set rates or deny coverage. However, these states still allow insurers to use genetic information such as family history to segment its insureds [17, 21].

## Policy Design

Insurance companies have designed a new type of insurance policy which, for convenience, is called a disease exclusion policy. This type of policy covers individuals for all conditions except for a specified (set of) disease(s). However, legislators such as Senator James M. Jeffords have argued that limited coverage provisions are unfair. The Kassebaum-Kennedy health-care reform bill attempts to place limitations on insurance companies' ability to offer such exclusionary policies while also providing for the guaranteed renewal of insurance $[7]$ . Meanwhile, recent court rulings suggest a similar trend toward restricting use of policies that exclude coverage for specified conditions $[34]$ .

## Information Regimes

Rapid advances in technology are increasing the information available both to the applicants for private health insurance and to the providers of such insurance. At the same time, however, regulatory initiatives are restricting insurance companies' access to, and use of, such information and limiting their use of alternative product designs. These initiatives are meant to protect the interests of the insurance applicants and policy holders. The remainder of this paper examines how these changing information conditions affect consumers' decisions and market efficiency and explores the economic impact of these regulatory initiatives; this is done through a modeling of a set of information regimes defined by the interactions among information availability and alternative regulatory regimes concerning the use of information by insurance companies. These information regimes are described below:

## No Information

\- Base case: In this regime neither the applicant nor the insurance company has any information regarding individuals' health status, nor is anyone able to acquire such information. This regime looks much like the environment of the 1950s when researchers had not yet identified genetic links to disease and insurers, unable to segment insureds effectively, typically charged uniform rates for health insurance. It will serve as a base case for welfare analysis, against which we compare the other information regimes.

## Private Information

\- Private family history: In this regime, biological research has advanced and people now understand that certain characteristics, such as height, weight, cholesterol level, and smoking habits may increase the risk of developing certain conditions. In addition, people now understand that some diseases have a genetic component, meaning that family history may provide individuals with a noisy signal of their health status. However, this information is private to potential insureds and is not available to insurance companies, either because they lack the technological capabilities to acquire it or because they are restricted by regulation from using it.

\- Private family history and private genetic test: In this regime, like the previous one, people have access to private information in the form of private observations of their family history and life-style choices. However, further advances in research and technology have made a genetic test available to individuals. In this regime, the test is optional and, if taken, supplies the consumer with additional private information (beyond that supplied by family history) regarding their health status. This regime looks much like that implied by the NJGPA, which essentially makes all genetic information private to potential insureds.

## Partially Private Information $^{6}$

\- Data mining and private genetic test: In this regime, insurance companies use data-mining technologies to make better assessments of consumer risk, making family history and life-style choices public information. However, individuals still have access to a private genetic test. This regime looks much like that implied by the legislation passed in eleven states (among them, Arizona, Georgia, and Montana) restricting insurance companies' use of genetic test results to price policies or deny coverage, but still allowing them to offer policies based on other genetic information such as family background.

## Public Information

\- Data mining and public genetic test: In this regime, no informational asymmetry exists; everyone has access to, and use of, the same information. Information endowment is essentially symmetrical between insurers and potential insureds.

Simulation models have been developed to examine each of these information regimes. Within each regime, consumer behavior is observed, some measure of social welfare is assessed under policy design alternatives, and the best design is identified for each environment. This analysis cannot choose which information regime is “optimal” since that will be determined by society’s preferences among universal coverage, universal affordability, universal testing, and consumer privacy (and other social goals), as well as by preferences for marketplace solutions or governmental programs. However, such analyses of regimes can illustrate that some designs are demonstrably inferior to others; moreover, it can highlight the economic and social implications of specific regulatory policies so that the debate can be driven by an understanding of outcomes.

## Literature Review

TWO MAIN LITERATURE STREAMS ARE BROUGHT TOGETHER in this paper. The first, and better known, examines the conditions under which the presence of information asymmetries leads to the collapse of a market and the mechanisms by which this collapse may be averted. The second stream acknowledges that, even when markets remain operational, information asymmetries may affect the efficiency of those markets. As we will see, there is much disagreement within this stream of literature about the effect of information on market efficiency (i.e., the social value or cost of private information).

## Information, Adverse Selection, and Market Collapse

A number of theoretical studies examine the nature of market equilibrium in the presence of adverse selection and its effect on the sustainability of markets. Akerlof looked at a stylized market for used cars where potential buyers have no information with which to assess car quality $[1]$ . He demonstrated that, in the presence of extreme information asymmetry between buyer and seller, the market for used cars collapses (i.e., no trades can occur) despite the potential for significant gains from trade.

Spence [31] examined the job market and demonstrated that when a credible signaling mechanism can be developed, differences in quality among items in a marketplace can be distinguished and appropriately priced, thus averting market collapse. In Akerlof's example, the sellers of high-quality cars might offer the buyer a partial warranty to signal the quality of the car even when such quality could not be physically confirmed by inspection.

Rothschild and Stiglitz [28] also examined mechanisms for averting such market collapse in the presence of private information. In their seminal paper on insurance, they considered two classes of applicant, high risk and low risk. They assumed consumers know their risk type with certainty while insurance companies know only the population proportions of risk types but not the risk status of individuals. They also assumed that insurance companies offer individuals a set of policies characterized by two dimensions, the premium charged and the copayment portion of the liability for which the consumers are responsible if they incur losses. Assuming Nash behavior, they demonstrated that, if an equilibrium exists in this market, it is a separating equilibrium in which low-risk consumers buy a policy with a high copayment, but at a price that largely reflects their low-risk status, while high-risk consumers buy a policy with no copayments, but at a substantially higher price that reflects their high-risk status. This demonstrates the insurance companies' use of a screening mechanism to offset the problem of adverse selection. $^{7}$

Wilson [33] used an alternative equilibrium concept that assumes that firms have more foresight $^{8}$ to demonstrate that such an insurance market can result in either a separating equilibrium, as in the Rothschild and Stiglitz model, or a pooling equilibrium, where all consumers purchase the same policy [33]. Pooling, of course, involves an implicit subsidy between the high- and low-risk consumer subpopulations. The type of equilibrium that emerges depends upon the level of risk aversion exhibited by the low-risk individuals. That is, if low-risk individuals exhibit high levels of risk aversion, a pooling equilibrium is more likely. Others, such as Miyazaki [23], Grossman [16], and Riley [27] also examine these issues using alternative concepts of equilibrium that require more foresight on the part of insurers.

## The Social Value (or Cost) or Information

The work presented here is most closely linked to the literature exploring the social value (or cost) of private and public information. In particular, in markets that do not collapse completely, the existence of private information may still affect the allocative efficiency of that market. Milgrom and Stokey [22] suggest that private information gathering has no social value and that agents have no incentives to become informed. However, Crocker and Snow [10] show that, if information status is public but the actual information itself is private, the value of information may be positive or negative, depending upon the tradeoff between better screening and higher signaling costs.

Crocker and Snow [9] examine categorical discrimination based on sex, age, and race in insurance markets and conclude that costless, imperfect categorization always enhances efficiency. That is, if attributes are correlated with risk and are observable at little cost, then a ban on categorization would be unambiguously inefficient. When information acquisition is costly, the results are mixed. Marquis [20] also finds that adverse selection is significantly reduced if premiums are varied according to demographic variables. Bond and Crocker [6] further demonstrate that classifying risks based on the insureds' voluntary consumption of products that are correlated with underlying loss propensities (e.g., smoking or skydiving) may also encourage increased participation in the insurance market and enhance efficiency, thus mitigating the social costs of adverse selection. Similarly, Landsburg, in "Truth or Consequences" [19], explores the importance of signaling mechanisms in insurance. He introduces the essay with the paradoxical quote, "Thank goodness for smoking; it can help keep insurance rates down!"—suggesting that nonsmokers can be profitably offered lower rates, more consistent with their risk status, than can smokers.

Finally, Tabarrok [32] examines genetic testing in the context of insurance markets, focusing on distributional objectives for insurance. He claims that, if information is symmetrical, then testing has negative value because consumers face classification risk—that is, the risk that they fall into an undesirable risk category through an accident of birth rather than engaging in risky behavior. In response, he introduces the concept of a “genetic insurance” where people purchase the genetic insurance and then undergo genetic screening; their genetic insurance policies will pay them the expected increase in health insurance premiums that would result from the conditions detected during their genetic screening, and the fully public results of their testing would then determine the actual cost of their health insurance in an efficient market.

## Contributions of this Paper

Despite this rich literature, researchers have yet to develop models that address some of the fundamental and pressing information-based problems facing the market for individual health insurance today.

Much of the previous work has continued to focus on the use of copayments and deductibles, commonly referred to as price-quantity contracts, to induce separating equilibriums in insurance markets and to restore market efficiency. However, D'Arcy and Doherty $[12]$ find little evidence from insurance markets that such price-quantity contracts are actually presented to policy holders. In addition, when considering dread diseases such as cancer or AIDS the copayment or deductible necessary to create a separating equilibrium (where individuals who believe themselves to be at lower risk take a high copayment policy and those who believe themselves to be at higher risk take a low copayment policy) will in general far exceed what most people can afford and what regulators will allow insurance companies to implement. In fact, Pashigian, Schkade, and Menetee $[24]$ find that most people prefer a low deductible despite its clear expense. These observations suggest the need to examine alternative policy designs, which are both practical and implementable, for mitigating the social cost of private information in this environment. This paper investigates the impact of one such policy design—one that excludes coverage for specified diseases.

In addition, most of the previous work that examines the social value of information assumes that information acquisition is determined exogenously. However, little research has explored what happens when consumers in an insurance market have the option to acquire new information regarding their personal riskiness if they wish; that is, little research explores how the results change when consumers have the option to gain, or refuse, access to additional information regarding their quality (or personal riskiness). Examining the impact of acquiring optional information will be a critical area of interest as advances in genetic testing continue. This paper attempts to identify under what conditions individuals have incentives to acquire new private or public information and under which conditions they choose not to do so. As we will see later, such results will have important implications for the desirability of various regulatory policies.

The model allows for varying degrees of information endowment attributed to insurers and potential insureds and can be readily extended to consider new policy types being considered in the marketplace. The analyses that follow examine the information regimes presented and described in this section. Other extensions and relationships to previous work are discussed in subsequent sections.

## Assumptions

INDIVIDUALS ARE POTENTIAL CONSUMERS OF HEALTH-CARE products and services. There are two types of “simulated” diseases in this “simulated” world: cancer (C) and anything but cancer (AB), the risks of which are independent. C has a genetic component (e.g., mutation of the P53 gene) while AB does not, and the treatment costs for these diseases are known and constant.

There are two types of consumers: those who are at high risk (HR) for developing C and those who are at low risk (LR). The consumers' risk type cannot be observed directly, but the population distribution of risk types is common knowledge. Consumers are identical except in their probability of developing C. The probability of a low-risk consumer developing cancer is represented by $P(C \mid LR)$ , the probability of a high-risk consumer developing cancer is represented by $P(C \mid HR)$ , where $0 < P(C \mid LR) < P(C \mid HR) < 1$ .

Applicants for private health insurance all possess the same underlying von Neumann-Morgenstern utility function for wealth, $U(W)$ . At this time, no restrictions are placed on the shape of this utility function except stipulating that consumers are risk-averse (i.e., that U[W] is concave). Therefore, consumers are willing to purchase insurance at prices above actuarially fair rates to avoid the possibility of an uncertain loss later in life. Consumers make insurance decisions to maximize their expected utility.

Finally, the simulation considers a single risk-neutral insurance firm, participating in a competitive market, that engages in actuarially fair, experience-based pricing; that is, the insurance company sets prices this period at levels that would have enabled it to break even in the previous period had those prices been implemented.

## Structure

The structure of the model is:

## Applicants

\- Enter the market for individual health insurance.

\- If possible in the regime being modeled, observe their family history and life-style choices which provide an informative, but imperfect, signal of the individuals' risk for developing $C$ .

\- Observe the set of insurance policies offered by the insurance company that period. This set may include full coverage, exclusionary coverage, or no coverage, depending on the information regime the applicant faces.

\- Based on all available information (population probabilities, family signal, and policy options), decide whether or not to take a genetic test, if available.

\- Based on all available information, choose a policy that maximizes expected utility.

\- Finally, get sick or stay healthy, after which, those with both insurance coverage and sickness file claims.

## Insurers

\- Observe the insurable population.

\- Observe results of data-mining efforts and genetic tests if possible and permitted in the regime being modeled.

\- Based on all available information, offer a set of policies with associated premiums.

• Collect revenues from premiums.

\- Pay claims and adjust premiums as claims are filed.

## Implementation

In the simulation, consumers' decisions concerning insurance coverage depend upon, among other factors, the prices of policies in the marketplace. Policy prices are determined by insurance company claims history, which in turn is determined by the insurance coverage decisions that consumers made in previous periods, which are themselves determined by previous policy decisions. Industrial Dynamics [14] is the most widely used tool for such nonlinear dynamic modeling; the simulations in this paper are developed in IThink®, a proprietary graphical implementation for the Macintosh.

## Results

## Base Case—No Information

IN THIS SCENARIO, THE DIVISION OF THE POPULATION into individuals with high risk and low risk for cancer is not yet suspected, either by insurance companies or by consumers themselves. $^{[11]}$ Applicants do not draw inferences concerning their riskiness, insurance companies do not employ data mining, and genetic testing is not yet available. Under these conditions, a common pooling equilibrium is observed where all consumers participate in the market at the same premium. All policy holders pay a premium of Th 24.50, the actuarially fair rate for the population. This scenario, marked by full market participation, serves as a basis of comparison for the efficiency of the insurance markets analyzed in the remaining information regimes.

## Information Regime 2—Private Family History

In this regime, applicants and insurance companies have become aware that there is a way to distinguish among two separate subpopulations of consumers, those at low risk of developing cancer and those at high risk. Applicants are able to observe a noisy signal from their family (or life-style choices) concerning their fundamental underlying riskiness. $^{12}$ This signal is noisy and imperfect but will lead to inferences of risk for the applicants that are better than chance. Based upon these informed assessments of personal riskiness and upon the prices of insurance policies available, applicants choose a policy. In this information regime, insurance companies are prohibited from performing data mining, either by regulation or (historically) by limitations on the availability of technology, and thus are not privy to the family history information, creating an informational asymmetry that favors consumers.

The model then considers what happens when the company offers two types of policies, one that provides complete coverage and one that provides coverage for AB but excludes coverage for C. In the case when applicants have access to a noisy, imperfect signal of their personal riskiness and when only complete coverage policies are available, those consumers who observe a high-risk signal for cancer will purchase full insurance and those who observe a low-risk signal will opt out of the insurance market and remain uninsured. $^{13}$ As shown in Table 2, those consumers who remain in the market will pay a higher insurance premium than that paid by policy holders in the base case because some of the subsidies provided from lower- to higher-risk consumers have been eliminated.

When applicants can choose between complete coverage and AB coverage, those who observe a high-risk signal again purchase complete coverage, while those who observe a low-risk signal, and who previously chose to be uninsured, now purchase the AB policies. These findings hold even when applicants possess imperfect private information (and for a wide range of parameter values). In terms of Tabarrok's model $[32]$ , one might claim that applicants who already know that they have done well in the “genetic endowment lottery” do not wish to purchase policies for which prices include insurance against both genetic risk (chance of bad genes) and health risk (chance of bad health).

We use the term market participation to denote the proportion of outcome risk (i.e., the proportion of the expected cost of individuals' associated with developing a disease) covered in the market; this serves as a crude measure of social welfare. When considering the economic efficiency of alternative policy options, we should note that in the base case the entire population was fully insured against the risk of developing all diseases. When consumers are endowed with imperfect private information and when only complete coverage policies are available, consumers whose private observations suggest that they are low risk will chose to be uninsured against the risk of both C and AB. This systematic reduction in market participation represents a loss in social welfare from the base case (see Table 2). In the case where AB coverage is also available, these consumers, while not uninsured, are still underinsured, since some are mistaken in their belief that they are low risk, and since even some low-risk consumers will develop cancer, for which they are not insured. In fact, when only complete coverage is available, market participation in this information regime is 61 percent of that in the base case. These results show that, by allowing for the provision of disease exclusions, the market participation can be increased significantly to 82 percent of the base case, representing a 34 percent improvement in market participation. Thus, the availability of AB coverage helps to reduce the loss in market participation created by the presence of private information, but it does not restore it completely.

## Information Regime 3—Private Family History and Private Genetic Test

In this information regime, not only are applicants endowed with an imperfect prediction of their risk based upon private observations, but they also have the option of taking a private genetic test that will provide them with additional information with which to assess their personal risk for developing cancer. $^{14}$ It is assumed that the test is free to all consumers. After observing their family histories, applicants can decide whether or not to take the private genetic test so that they can use the results to update their risk assessment. On the basis of prices of insurance and their assessment of risk, applicants can again decide what coverage they wish to purchase. Insurance companies are still prohibited from using the results of genetic tests and from performing data mining. This information regime resembles the information environment imposed by the NJGPA.

When only complete coverage policies are available, modeling results show that only those applicants who observe a high-risk signal for cancer and a high test result will purchase full insurance and that all other applicants will opt out of the private health insurance market and remain uninsured. That is, not only do applicants receiving a low-risk signal opt out of the market, but applicants who receive a high-risk signal are convinced by a low-risk test result to remain uninsured rather than pay the premium available in the market. This results in a lower fraction of the population being covered—those who most firmly believe themselves to be at risk; since the set of consumers who purchase insurance now principally includes those who are at risk, premiums for those choosing insurance are now higher than those found in the previous two information regimes. When consumers can choose between complete coverage and AB coverage, those applicants who previously decided to opt out of the insurance market now decide to purchase the AB (disease exclusion) policy.

As shown in Table 3, when insurance companies offer only complete coverage, market participation, or the proportion of outcome risk covered in the market, falls to 34 percent of that in the base case, significantly less than that observed in information regime 2. That is, private information imposes an inefficiency upon the market and more private (even imperfect) information exacerbates this result. When AB coverage is made available, market participation more than doubles, to 70 percent of that in the base case. The availability of the AB policy demonstrably helps to mitigate the social cost of private information; however, it does not restore full efficiency, as it still falls 30 percent short of the base case.

These results demonstrate that the availability of a private, imperfect genetic test offered to insurance applicants at a low enough price will impose an additional loss in social welfare; that is, as the amount of private information available to applicants increases, market participation decreases. The results further suggest, contrary to regulators' intuitions, that policies that provide for disease exclusions may play a significant role in mitigating this loss and may help restore some market efficiency.

## Information Regime 4—Public Family History and Private Genetic Test

This information regime looks much like the environment in several states such as Arizona, Minnesota, and New Hampshire. In this regime, applicants still have access to a (free) private genetic test; however, insurance companies are allowed to engage in data mining, essentially making applicants' family histories and life-style choices public information. $^{15}$ It is assumed that data-mining activities essentially give insurance companies access to applicants' preliminary assessments of personal risk based on family history and life-style choices and that insurance companies are able to price policies to reflect these differences in riskiness. That is, insurance companies place applicants into one of two population pools—those that observe a high-risk signal from their family and those that receive a low-risk signal. As before, the insurance companies set their rates on the basis of the previous year's claims history; however, rates are now based upon the losses experienced by each pool. And, as before, the simulation demonstrates that when test results are free, are not accessible by insurance companies, and cannot adversely affect policy prices, all applicants rationally decide to be tested.

## Symmetrical Information Access

In the final information regime, no informational asymmetry exists; consumers and companies both have access to and use of the same information. Information endowment is essentially symmetrical between insurers and potential insureds. Insurance companies can now place each applicant into one of six population pools, using the results of the insurance companies' data-mining efforts (high-risk or low-risk observations) and the results of the genetic test (high-risk or low-risk test results or test not taken).

In this regime, all applicants decide to test. Now that insurance company pricing is based on risk pooling that fully mirrors applicants' own risk assessments, all applicants choose complete coverage and no applicants are uninsured or underinsured. That is, everyone purchases complete coverage because the premiums offered to applicants are perceived as fair; premiums now equal applicants' estimates of the expected costs of their treatment as well as insurance companies' estimates of these costs. Even the smallest degree of consumer risk aversion is sufficient to lead to purchase of complete coverage by all consumers.

Objections to this outcome may lead to considerable moral outrage and thus to pressure from voters for politicians to overrule the marketplace, especially if the high price of insurance precludes some low-income, high-risk families from purchasing insurance. As in regime 4, regulators may be tempted to prohibit insurance companies' use of genetic data for risk assessment, although again, as we have seen in regime 3, this produces other undesirable market distortions.

## Summary

PROBLEMS ARISE IN INSURANCE MARKETS WHEN INFORMATION ASYMMETRIES cause individuals' estimates of their riskiness and of the expected costs of treatment they may require to differ from estimates of insurance companies and to be more accurate than the companies' estimates. Those consumers who believe that they are being overcharged will—in the absence of sufficient risk aversion—forego coverage and opt out of the market. As this adverse selection causes some consumers to exit the market, only those who firmly believe themselves to be at high risk and high cost will remain, and they will be willing to pay the high prices that insurance companies demand; to the extent that applicants actually are at high risk and high cost, policy prices (premiums) will increase and consumers will exit the market until the prices charged for the remaining consumers equal the consumers' own assessment of their expected costs. Since some consumers are uninsured as a result of their rational economic decisions, the presence of private information demonstrably imposes social costs. The greater the information asymmetries and the greater the information advantage of consumers, the greater the social costs caused by this information asymmetry. However, the introduction of an inferior policy—one with disease exclusions—will mitigate some of the problems caused by information asymmetries. Individuals who believe themselves to be highest-risk consumers will accept the best, complete-coverage policies, since it is only for these highest-risk consumers that these policies can be fairly priced in the presence of information asymmetries; individuals who believe themselves to be lower-risk consumers will accept these inferior policies, since they are better than no coverage, and prices will reflect the degree to which policy holders actually represent lower-risk individuals.

The following is a summary of our modeling results for the five information regimes studied:

\- Regime 1 (base case): The absence of any private information means that consumers' estimates of their riskiness and of the expected cost of treatment will not differ from insurance company estimates. The result is full market participation at affordable rates.

\- Regime 2 (private family history, no genetic testing): The introduction of private information causes potential insurance applicants to have more accurate estimates of their riskiness, and of the costs of treatment, than those of the insurance company. Those consumers who perceive themselves to be lower risk opt out of the market since they perceive it as overpriced for their degree of risk; since consumers remaining in the market tend to be those who see themselves as high risk, and since these assessments have some accuracy above the chance level, the costs of treatment for these individuals and the price of their policies increase. However, the availability of exclusion policies helps to mitigate the loss of market efficiency. Even information known to be imperfect gives consumers an advantage and is sufficient to produce these effects.

\- Regime 3 (private family history, private genetic testing): The introduction of additional private information increases the information advantage of potential insurance applicants in estimating their riskiness and their expected costs of treatment; this further reduces the efficiency of insurance markets. Once again, the availability of exclusion policies helps mitigate the loss of market efficiency.

\- Regime 4 (data mining, private genetic testing): Allowing insurance companies to replicate consumers' private use of family history through data mining reduces the information disadvantage of insurance companies and causes insurance company estimates and applicant estimates of their riskiness and their expected costs of treatment to align more closely. This encourages more consumers to purchase insurance, since insurance companies will be able to offer low-price policies to more consumers who see themselves as low risk. Once again, the availability of lower-cost exclusion policies helps restore market coverage and helps reduce some of the social cost of remaining information asymmetries.

\- Regime 5 (data mining, public genetic testing): Allowing insurance companies to perform data mining and to have access to consumers' test results eliminates the information disadvantage of insurance companies and brings together insurance company estimates and applicant estimates of their riskiness and their expected costs of treatment. All consumers will purchase insurance, but at widely divergent prices determined by estimates of their riskiness. The absence of information asymmetries eliminates the need for exclusion policies as screening mechanisms.

## Conclusions

WE HAVE DEVELOPED A TOOL FOR EXPLORING THE CRITICAL impacts of technology on the availability of information, the social costs resulting from this availability, the methods for mitigating these costs, and resulting implications for existing and proposed regulatory policies. We use a simulation model to assess the economic efficiency of a series of information regimes defined by the interaction of technology and regulatory policy in the individual health insurance industry. The results demonstrate that recent regulatory initiatives, implemented to protect current policy holders and future health insurance applicants, will have unintended economic consequences. These initiatives create private information regarding health status for potential insureds, which, through a form of adverse selection, leads to a loss in social welfare. Furthermore, the results demonstrate that certain policy designs that allow for disease exclusions will mitigate the social cost associated with private information. Paradoxically, legislators and regulators typically oppose the use of most policy designs that actually do improve market efficiency and social welfare.

The models developed here and their findings enhance our understanding of the economic implications of a variety of information regimes that may develop in the individual health insurance industry and attempts to provide some guidance for preserving social welfare when facing these changes. The apparent paradox of regulatory policy can readily be explained when comparing regulators' assumptions about consumer behavior to the predictions of the models. Regulators assume that, as insurance companies gain additional information, they will force high-risk individuals to accept policies with disease exclusions. Our models show that, as individuals gain an information advantage, self-selection will lead those who believe themselves to be at low risk to opt out of a market they consider overpriced, leaving a greater number of high-risk individuals paying higher prices; once this happens, insurance companies will not force high-risk individuals to accept exclusions. Rather, only low-risk consumers who have opted out of the market will accept these inferior exclusionary policies. Misunderstanding consumer behavior causes regulators to misjudge who needs their protection; once they have misjudged whom they are protecting from abuses, regulators predictably take the wrong corrective actions.

Unfortunately, the models cannot say much about the desirability of these regimes relative to each other. They cannot make optimal policy decisions based on comparison among these information regimes, as voters' and regulators' preferences among various outcomes will depend upon their subjective tradeoffs among universal coverage, universal affordability, individuals' rights to privacy, individuals' incentives to test for and to mitigate their risks, and the economic efficiency of the market.

The determination depends on numerous concerns and objectives other than maximizing the participation in the market.

\- One concern is for universal affordability; the differential pricing employed by the market may make it impossible for some high-risk, low-income applicants to afford insurance, forcing them to forego coverage unless some governmental subsidies are provided.

\- Another concern is for universal coverage; if applicants possess private information regarding risk status, those at low risk will opt out of the market when insurers are forced to engage in uniform, average-cost pricing.

\- There may be externalities if people in some information regimes do not have insurance, and consequently do not seek or receive treatment for communicable diseases and become carriers.

\- There may be unintended side-effects; for example, to avoid informing a potential insurer of a possible but significant genetic risk, some individuals may forego testing, and therefore may not learn of conditions for which there are behavior modifications that may mitigate their risks.

This is summarized tersely by Blumberg and Nichols [5]: “It is not possible to determine scientifically if the costs of risk segmentation unambiguously outweigh the costs of risk pooling. Much of the valuation is subjective.”

However, within information regimes, it is possible to rank alternatives clearly and unambiguously:

\- It is good to permit price discrimination among consumers, because it encourages participation in the market by allowing lower-risk consumers to purchase insurance at prices that better reflect their risk status.

\- It is good to design policies that offer significant discounts for exclusions of coverage for certain conditions for which there is considerable difference in risk among individuals; accepting exclusions allows low-risk individuals to signal their assessment of their risk status and thus encourages their participation in the market.

These results are often counterintuitive for regulators and voters. They are, consequently, counter to social trends.

## Directions of Future Research

WE HAVE DEVELOPED A FLEXIBLE TOOL THAT CAN EXPLORE a whole range of issues related to the findings in this paper. Numerous extensions to the research are planned, or in some cases are already under way:

\- The degree of risk aversion and the degree of information asymmetry can be varied systematically, to assess whether universal insurance may be more stable than is implied by the conditions reported. This may be the case if low-risk consumers possessed high levels of risk aversion.

\- Consumers can be divided into more complex classifications of riskiness.

\- Consumers can be allowed to differ in their utility functions, or their risk aversion, or their accuracy of inferences.

\- The costs of treatment can be allowed to follow a distribution (incorporating severity risk), rather than held fixed at the expected cost.

\- It is possible to incorporate observed behavioral violations of economic rationality made by consumers when making financial decisions and to examine their impact on market equilibria.

\- Consumers' utility functions can incorporate more considerations, such as the therapeutic value of testing (i.e., the benefits of engaging in preventive care and life-style changes once a propensity for a condition is identified) and prices associated with testing.

\- Conditions under which insurance applicants will choose to acquire additional information (i.e., an optional genetic test) and those under which they will not do so, and the tradeoffs involved, should be explored in greater detail.

The results in this paper have significant implications for insurance companies facing applicants who possess private information regarding their personal riskiness. The results suggest that, to compete effectively in such an environment, an insurer will want to invest in data-mining technologies and engage in consumer segmentation and differential pricing. It will also want to offer policies that encourage profitable market participation. Disease exclusion policies may play such a role. Future research must further explore the implications of this analysis for insurance companies' strategies for various regulatory scenarios.

The concepts identified in this paper are not specific to the private health insurance industry, although this industry does provide a complex, yet manageable, application of these concepts. Technological advances and regulatory changes are dramatically affecting the availability of information in a variety of industries, including credit cards, banking, travel, and telecommunications. This model can be altered to examine the economic and regulatory implications of changing information conditions in a wide range of industries.
