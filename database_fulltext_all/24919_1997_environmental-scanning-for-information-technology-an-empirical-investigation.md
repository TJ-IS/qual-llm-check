# Environmental Scanning for Information Technology: An Empirical Investigation

JERRY L. MAIER, R. KELLY RAINER, JR., AND CHARLES A. SNYDER

JERRY L. MAIER is an Assistant Professor of Management (MIS) at Middle Tennessee State University in Murfreesboro, Tennessee. He received his Ph.D. in management information systems from Auburn University.

R. KELLY RAINER, JR. is an Associate Professor of Management (MIS) at Auburn University, Auburn, Alabama. He has published articles in many leading academic and practitioner journals. His research interests include executive information systems, object-oriented technologies, structural equation modeling, and psychometrics. He is coauthor, with Hugh Watson and George Houdeshel, of Building Executive Information Systems and Other Decision Support Applications.

CHARLES A. SNYDER is Professor of Management (MIS) at Auburn University, Auburn, Alabama. His articles have appeared in leading academic and practitioner journals. His research interests include telecommunications and information resource management. He is coauthor, with Houston Carr, of Management of Telecommunications.

ABSTRACT: An increasing number of organizations are recognizing the strategic significance of their information technology (IT) resources. A process by which emerging information technologies may be effectively identified and evaluated is rapidly becoming a necessity. This process may be accomplished through scanning the external IT environment.

Based on the strategic emphasis given to IT resources, organizations may be classified as exploiter/innovator (EI), competitor/early adopter (CEA), or participant/effective efficient follower (PEEF). This study uses data from 131 top information systems managers to examine differences in the three types of organizations across a number of issues concerning the process of scanning the external IT environment. These issues include: (1) the objectives of scanning the external IT environment; (2) the methods of scanning the external IT environment; (3) the existence of separate units dedicated to IT scanning; (4) the level of IT scanning intensity; (5) the sources of information used for IT scanning; and (6) the level of top management involvement with IT scanning.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: environmental scanning, information technology environment.

the rapidly changing information technology (IT) environment $[28]$ . A major challenge to modern organizations is quickly and effectively identifying and successfully assimilating emerging information technologies that will allow firms simultaneously to enhance their operations and competitive positions $[33, 41]$ . Rapid technological advances and large investments in IT resources make this challenge difficult to meet. Modern managers, therefore, have two tasks: (1) to keep abreast of the advances, changes, and trends in the external IT environment, and (2) to manage and control the assimilation of IT into organizational processes and functions $[40]$ .

Organizations approach planning and using IT resources differently $[4, 9, 39]$ . They differ in the methods they use and the effort they expend to make themselves aware of emerging technologies and trends in the external IT environment. As a result, many organizations miss opportunities in assessing and assimilating emerging information technologies.

Organizations may be classified on the basis of their exploitation of IT for competitive positioning [7, 22]. The classifications are:

1. Exploiters or innovators (EI): These companies first introduce a new competitive use for a particular IT. EI organizations view and use IT as an integral and vital component of their overall competitive strategy. Top management supports aggressive, proactive use of IT to gain or enhance competitive position. These firms are creative and innovative in the application of leading-edge IT and spend significantly more on IT than their competitors.

2. Competitors or early adopters (CEA): These companies quickly follow the lead of innovators but only after the technology has been proven. CEA organizations use IT to provide information for their important strategic business units. These firms avoid the immediate application of leading-edge IT but, after a reasonable period of time, readily adopt a new IT that has been proven effective.

3. Participant or effective/efficient followers (PEEF): These companies eventually move to an information technology out of necessity, not because of innovation. PEEF organizations use IT to provide operational data and information to functional managers. These organizations spend a minimum on IT and introduce a new IT only when forced because of the threat of deteriorating operations.

The literature provides little insight into the process by which an organization becomes aware of advances and changes occurring in the external IT environment [25]. Moreover, existing models do not address the process by which changes or trends occurring in the IT environment are identified [27, 34, 35, 49]. These models appear to assume an organization's awareness of a change or an advance occurring in a particular component of the IT environment. Consequently, most previous research excludes those activities associated with discovering and assessing changes in the external IT environment.

The recognition of IT resources as important strategic forces affecting the organization and limitations of existing research suggest a study that sheds light on the scanning process an organization uses to stay abreast of the dynamic IT environment. The purpose of this exploratory study is to examine the differences among the three types of organizations across a number of scanning variables, which include objectives and methods for scanning the external IT environment, scanning intensity, sources of information for IT scanning, and top management involvement in IT scanning.

Literature Review and Hypothesis Generation

## Environmental Scanning

ENVIRONMENTAL SCANNING, A BASIC PROCESS OF ANY ORGANIZATION, acquires data from the external environment to be used in problem definition and decision making [45]. The environment consists of all those events, happenings, or factors with a present or future influence on the organization that, at the same time, lie outside the organization's immediate control.

The primary purpose of environmental scanning is to provide a comprehensive view or understanding of the current and future condition of the five environmental constituents: social, economic, political, regulatory, and technological [20, 29]. This view becomes the foundation for basing strategic planning, making strategic decisions, or guiding product/service development. To be successful, an organization's environmental scanning process must be able to identify and differentiate among a variety of external issues, if the company's strategic responses are to be focused and effective [31], and to predict the direction in which environmental elements may be moving (i.e., identify trends) [3].

Organizational scanning activities can be placed on a continuum from irregular to continuous scanning $[1, 14, 20]$ . Irregular scanning (called undirected viewing by Aguilar $[1]$ ), is a general exposure to information with no specific purpose in mind. It is characterized as reactive, specific, driven by crisis, and performed by existing staff. Continuous scanning (also called formal search by Aguilar $[1]$ ) is a deliberate effort to obtain specific information that follows preestablished methods. It is characterized as proactive, broad in scope, part of the firm's planning process, and performed by a scanning unit. Fahey and King $[14]$ , in a 1977 study of twelve companies, found only two firms that scanned continuously. Jain $[20]$ , using a larger (186) and more recent (1990) sample of companies, found that less than 10 percent employed continuous scanning, while over 70 percent scanned irregularly.

EI firms, so closely dependent on IT, would be expected to continuously scan the IT environment more than CEA and PEEF firms. Conversely, PEEF companies, less dependent on IT, would be expected to scan the IT environment irregularly. This suggests the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1: The three types of organizations significantly differ in their environmental scanning activities.

All middle- and upper-level managers scan the environment [1, 17, 24]. In addition to individuals scanning informally, some companies use teams $[1, 20, 43]$ . Aguilar $[1]$ proposed that cross-functional, specialized units could be used for environmental scanning (e.g., central research or corporate planning staffs). He noted that these units should not participate in other operational activities. Stubbart $[43]$ stated that environmental scanning units monitor and interpret trends, provide a broad view of a wide variety of issues, and provide predictions of the future. Some organizations give their environmental scanning teams responsibility for specific environmental sectors. Other organizations give their functional areas responsibility for scanning their particular environmental sectors (e.g., the IS department would scan the IT environmental sector) $[20, 43]$ .

EI firms would be expected to employ formally-constituted teams more than CEA and PEEF firms. PEEF firms would be expected to scan the environment informally. This leads to the following hypotheses:

Hypothesis 2: The three types of organizations differ significantly in the methods they use to scan the external IT environment.

Hypothesis 3: The three types of organizations differ significantly in the existence of a formal IT scanning unit.

## Intensity of the Scanning Process

Scanning intensity may be viewed as the amount of effort an organization puts into its environmental scanning activity. The intensity of the scanning process depends on the relationship between the organization and its environment, and the nature of the environment $[17, 24, 42]$ .

The relationship between the organization and its environment is determined by the degree to which the organization depends on its environment for survival. The greater the dependence, the greater will be the intensity of scanning. The nature of the environment refers to the frequency and magnitude of changes occurring in the external environment. The greater the frequency of change and the greater the magnitude of change (i.e., the more dynamic the environment), the greater will be the intensity of scanning.

Environmental scanning tends to focus on those components of the general external environment that are most dynamic and exhibit the greatest degree of uncertainty $[10, 23, 24]$ . Uncertainty or planning complexity can be reduced by data and information obtained by an effective environmental scanning process. In addition, scanning intensity is even greater if the dynamic environmental component is perceived to have strategic implications for the organization $[15, 32, 46]$ .

The literature $[21, 29, 42]$ suggests that surrogates for scanning intensity are: (1) the amount of time dedicated to the scanning process; (2) the resources dedicated to the scanning process (people, budget, etc.); and (3) the frequency with which scanning activities are performed. This study operationalizes scanning intensity with the following measures: the level of difficulty in staying abreast of changes in the IT environment; the number of persons involved specifically in scanning; scanning expenditures; and the extent to which all environmental sectors are scanned.

EI firms, with their close dependence on a dynamic external IT environment, would be expected to exhibit a higher level of scanning intensity than CEA and PEEF firms. EI companies should have more difficulty of staying abreast of changes in the IT environment, should have more people involved in scanning, should spend more money on scanning, and should scan more issues than CEA and PEEF firms. The following hypotheses are proposed:

Hypothesis 4: The three types of organization differ significantly in their level of scanning intensity.

Hypothesis 4a: The three types of organization differ significantly in the level of difficulty in staying abreast of changes in the external IT environment.

Hypothesis 4b: The three types of organization differ significantly in the number of employees specifically devoted to scanning.

Hypothesis 4c: The three types of organization differ significantly in the amount of money spent on scanning.

Hypothesis 4d: The three types of organization differ significantly in the extent to which issues are scanned.

## Sources of Information for IT Scanning

Organizations have many sources of information for identifying and assessing new information technologies in the external environment. These sources may be divided into three categories: documentary, human, and combination $[1, 5, 20, 21, 24, 48]$ . Examples of documentary sources include IT-related trade journals, academic journals, telecommunications journals, newspaper-type publications, practitioner-oriented magazines, and general business magazines. Human sources include vendors, competitors, consultants, friends, customers, and employees. Combination sources provide both human and documentary type input (e.g., IT-related conferences, vendor demonstrations, seminars, and trade shows).

EI organizations would be expected to utilize sources of information with greater frequency than CEA and PEEF companies. Further, EI firms would be expected to place more importance on the sources of information than CEA and PEEF companies. The following hypotheses are proposed:

Hypothesis 5: The three types of organizations differ significantly in the frequency with which they use the major sources of information to scan the external IT environment.

Hypothesis 6: The three types of organizations differ significantly in the importance placed on the major sources of information they use to scan the external IT environment.

## Strategy/Scanning Linkage

As previously noted, all middle and upper-level managers scan the external environment. Aguilar $[1]$ asserted that environmental scanning provided necessary information for executives to formulate strategy. However, Hambrick $[17]$ found only weak support for a strategy-scanning linkage. This was surprising, given that environmental scanning is defined as the foundation on which corporate strategy is built. This study operationalizes the strategy/scanning linkage with the following measures $[17, 18]$ : the amount of top management support for IT scanning, the amount of top management reward for IT scanning, the extent to which IT scanning is linked to corporate strategy, and the existence of a specific policy linking IT scanning to corporate strategy.

EI companies would be expected to have a strong linkage between environmental scanning and corporate strategy, with weaker linkages in CEA and PEEF firms. The following hypotheses are proposed:

Hypothesis 7: The three types of organizations differ significantly in the strength of their strategy/scanning linkage.

Hypothesis 7a: The three types of organizations differ significantly in the amount of top management support of external scanning.

Hypothesis 7b: The three types of organizations differ significantly in the amount of top management reward for external scanning.

Hypothesis 7c: The three types of organizations differ significantly in the extent to which IT scanning is linked to corporate strategy.

Hypothesis 7d: The three types of organizations differ significantly in the existence of a specific policy to link IT scanning to corporate strategy.

## Research Methodology

THIS STUDY EMPLOYED A MAILED SURVEY IN COMBINATION with a telephone interview to collect data. This methodology reduces concern for nonresponse bias, reduces the uncertainty associated with not knowing who actually completes the survey, and increases the useable response return rate [23, 26].

The structure of the questionnaire drew from questionnaires used in previous research $[19, 24, 44]$ . Even though the purpose and scope of those studies differed from this research, the types of questions asked and the information sought provided a suitable foundation for the initial version of the survey instrument used in this study.

The initial version was pilot-tested twice to ensure its validity and suitability for collecting the required data. In the first pilot test, two IS executives, each from a different firm, examined the instrument for deficiencies and made suggestions for modifications. One executive was from a general manufacturing firm with 800 employees and one from a communications company with 30,000 employees. The two organizations were chosen for their differences in industry and size.

In the second pilot test, two other IS executives, each from a different company, were mailed the cover letter and the modified instrument. One executive was from an electronics company with 10,000 employees; the other was from a wholesale goods firm with 2,000 employees. They commented on the form, readability, and meaningfulness of the instrument, and suggested additions, deletions, and modifications. The instrument was modified by following their suggestions, resulting in its final form. These organizations were chosen to differ in industry and size from each other and from the two firms in the first pilot test.

## Data Collection

Companies chosen to participate in this research were randomly selected from the population of companies listed in the Disclosure Database, using the stratified random sampling technique. The Standard Industrial Code (SIC) classification system satisfies McCall's [30] suggested criterion for using stratified random sampling. The Disclosure Database identifies eighty-two industries at the two-digit SIC code level. To ensure an adequate population for sampling within specific industries, only industries with more than 100 companies were considered. This criterion left a pool of twenty-five industries. Twelve industries were selected at random for three reasons: (1) to improve the generalizability of the results; (2) to answer Hambrick's [17, 18] call for a wider sample of industries in research on the external environment; and (3) to keep the research study at a manageable size. From the 4,956 companies in the twelve industries, further random sampling resulted in the selection of 156 companies to participate in this study. The overall goal of the study was to have at least ten companies in each industry. Accordingly, the goal of the random sampling was to obtain thirteen companies in each industry to allow for non-responses to the questionnaire.

The 156 companies were contacted via telephone to accomplish five objectives: (1) establish contact with the top IS executive in the company (e.g., chief information officer, chief technology officer, director of data services, etc.); (2) explain to the participant the purpose of the study; (3) explain to the participant the process to be followed; (4) secure that individual's participation in the study, thus specifically identifying who would complete the questionnaire; and (5) obtain the exact mailing address of the participant. During the interview, no attempt was made to collect any data elicited by the questionnaire. Following the interview, a cover letter and questionnaire were immediately sent to the participating IS executive of each selected company.

Of the 156 executives contacted, one indicated a company policy prohibiting company officials from participating in such studies. All other executives agreed to participate. As an incentive, respondents were mailed an executive summary of the findings. A total of 131 usable responses were returned, representing a return rate of 83.9 percent. This very high return rate is consistent with the response rate findings of Jennings and Lumpkin [23] when they used this data collection method.

Nonrespondents were evenly distributed across all twelve industries. No attempt was made to follow up on nonrespondents.

## Data Analysis and Discussion

## Organizational Types

TWELVE INDUSTRY GROUPS WERE REPRESENTED IN THE SAMPLE, including oil (10 firms), publishing (11), chemicals (11), industrial machinery (10), electronics (12), communications (11), utilities (10), wholesale goods (11), depository institutions (12), investment (12), engineering (10), and general manufacturing (11).

The questionnaire asked respondents to classify their organizations as either an EI, CEA, or PEEF type organization. Participating firms in each category were classified as EI, 19.8 percent; CEA, 39.7 percent; and PEEF, 40.5 percent. Janulaitis [22] suggested, based on empirical evidence, a different percentage of companies in each category: EI (5–8 percent), CEA (70–80 percent), and PEEF (15–20 percent). Three possible explanations help account for this variation in percentages.

First, the top IS executive within each organization categorized his or her own company. As someone inside the organization, this person should know more about the organization and view it differently than an external observer would. Janulaitis was an outsider to the organizations with which he consulted and, as a consequence, may have formulated a different view of the organization than the top IS executive. Second, Janulaitis may have drawn his percentages from a different subset of industries than we did. Third, the number of companies that make up each category may have changed during the intervening years since Janulaitis [22] presented his percentages.

A shift in the percentage of companies that constitute each category may come about for many reasons. The increased pressure on organizations to recognize, manage, and aggressively use IT resources is a powerful motivator that perhaps would cause more companies to become exploiters and innovators (EI category firms) in the application of emerging information technologies throughout the organization. Another reason for a percentage shift in each category may be that more firms are striving to completely exploit the capabilities of an existing IT before exploring and eventually adopting an emerging IT. These firms would tend to be PEEF companies.

The data in this study suggest a polarization in how organizations apply and manage IT resources. The implication of these percentages appears to be that more and more firms are deciding to become creative, innovative, and aggressive in the strategic application of IT (EI firms), and to understand and exploit the capabilities of an existing IT thoroughly before exploring the application of an emerging IT (PEEF firms).

## Individual Demographics

The individuals participating in this study occupied the highest IS position in their respective organizations. The typical title for this person was CIO, Director of Data Processing, Director of Information Services, Vice President of Data Processing, or Vice President of Information Services. Of the 131 respondents, 23 (17.6 percent) were female and 108 (82.4 percent) were male.

The individuals in the three types of organizations exhibited significant differences in personal time for scanning (ANOVA F = 15.49, p < 0.001) and number of personal subscriptions (ANOVA F = 11.38, p < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons revealed that respondents from EI firms spent significantly more time on personal scanning and received significantly more subscriptions than respondents from CEA and PEEF firms. Further, respondents from CEA firms spent significantly more time on personal scanning and received significantly more subscriptions than those from PEEF firms.

## Grouping Questionnaire Items

The questionnaire contained over 200 items related to various facets of scanning the external IT environment. For clarity and brevity in the data analysis, related items were grouped. The sample size relative to the number of variables precluded a factor analysis; therefore, a variation of the procedure explicated by Davis [11, 12] and Moore and Benbasat [36] was used.

Two, two-round judging processes were employed to place related items into groups. Each round contained four judges: an IS Ph.D. student, two IS faculty members, and a CIO. Judges were different for each round. The students and faculty were selected for their research in and knowledge of environmental scanning, and the CIOs for their experience in environmental scanning.

The judges were supplied with the names and definitions of each category of items, and with a randomly ordered stack of $3 \times 5$ cards. Each card had one item printed on it. Judges independently placed the items into the categories. The categories were directly related to hypotheses 4–7: scanning intensity, frequency of use of information sources, importance of information sources, and the strategy/scanning linkage. The first three hypotheses each had a single item that related to it.

The judges assigned eight items to the scanning intensity variable, including the difficulty of staying abreast of changes in the IT environment, the number of persons in the scanning unit, scanning expenditures, and the extent of scanning for social, economic, political, regulatory, and competitor issues. The judges then placed the five items (extent of scanning for social, economic, political, regulatory, and competitor issues) into one category, called issues scanned.

The judges assigned ninety-two items to the frequency of information sources and ninety-two items to the importance of information sources. Finally, they placed four items in the top management involvement category: top management support, top management recognition, a strategy/scanning linkage, and specific policies to track external IT issues.

The reliability of the two sorting procedures was assessed using: (1) the raw agreement between the judges; (2) Cohen's Kappa [8]; (3) the overall frequency with which the judges placed items in the proper category; and (4) Cronbach's alpha for each category (see [36]). The interjudge agreements, Cohen's Kappas, and placement ratios are acceptable (see [36], p. 201). The perfect scores on all three measures for the frequency of use and importance of information sources categories stem directly from the wording of these items on the questionnaire. The Cronbach alphas for the four categories are also acceptable [38].

Subsequent examination of the categories revealed that further grouping was possible. The same two-round procedure was followed as the first grouping process, with the same judges. ## Multivariate Analysis of Covariance

Multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) employs procedures similar to linear regression to remove the variation in the dependent variable associated with continuous covariates, thus increasing measurement precision. A conventional MANOVA is then performed on the adjusted dependent variable. In this study, three surrogates for organizational size, number of employees $r = 0.3077, p < 0.01$ , organizational sales $r = 0.2861, p < 0.01$ , and organizational assets $r = 0.2574, p < 0.01$ were significantly correlated with organizational type. When analyzing the relationship between the scanning variables and organizational type, it was necessary to account for the effect of organizational size on organizational type. The MANCOVA used the number of employees, sales, and assets as the covariates.

## Discussion

THE MAJORITY OF THE HYPOTHESES LED TO EXPECTED RESULTS, which followed from the definitions of EI, CEA, and PEEF organizations. EI firms, more than CEA and PEEF firms, scan the environment continuously, have difficulty staying abreast of environmental changes, scan issues to a greater extent, use information sources more frequently and place more importance on them, have greater top management support and recognition for scanning, and have a strong link between strategy and scanning

that is supported with a specific policy.

There were some surprising results, however. EI firms did not significantly differ from CEA and PEEF firms in scanning methods or in the amount of scanning expenditures. EI firms were expected to utilize cross-functional teams (rather than scan informally) and spend more money on scanning than CEA and PEEF firms.

In addition, the finding that EI firms have more formal scanning units and greater numbers of employees devoted to scanning than CEA and PEEF firms is closely related to the previous two findings when interpreted in relation to organizational size. Because EI firms are significantly larger than CEA and PEEF companies, they would logically tend to have formal scanning units and more employees devoted to scanning. In fact, the ANCOVA for the number of employees in scanning as a percentage of all employees is not significant (F = 1.01, p > 0.05).

Therefore, the three types of organization, relative to their size, spend the same amount of money on scanning, use similar combinations of informal and team scanning methods, and employ similar numbers of people in scanning. Taken together, these findings, contrary to expectations, suggest that the three types of organization actually devote similar resources to IT scanning. This implies that organizations, regardless of their strategic emphasis on IT, recognize the necessity of scanning the IT environment.

## Conclusions

INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY HAS A SIGNIFICANT INFLUENCE on how organizations operate and compete. The array of emerging technologies in the dynamic external IT environment continues to grow at an accelerating rate. As a result, many organizations have shifted from using IT only to provide operational support to using IT resources in the formulation, execution, and control of strategic plans and objectives.

This study describes the characteristics of the scanning process by which organizations obtain information from the external IT environment about emerging technologies and trends. As such, it provides valuable insights about IT scanning for both the researcher and the practitioner.

Our findings point to several major conclusions. First, the amount of strategic emphasis the organization gives to IT resources has an impact on some, but not all, attributes of IT scanning activities. The attributes of the IT scanning activity for an organization that aggressively seeks strategically to exploit leading-edge IT are:

1. The organization will experience difficulty in staying abreast of changes occurring in the external IT environment.

2. The objective of the scanning activity will be to scan the external IT environment continuously.

3. The scanning activity will be accomplished by a separate, distinct department, unit, or individual that reports directly to the top IS executive in the organization.

4. Scanning intensity will be high, as reflected in the extent to which a broad

range of external issues are scanned.

5. Top management will be very supportive of the scanning activity and will visibly demonstrate this support.

6. The scanning activity will have a specific policy to link IT scanning with the organization's strategic plans and objectives.

7. The scanning activity will rely heavily on practitioner-oriented sources of information and will tend to focus on information provided by the vendors of the organization's specific IT implementation.

However, some attributes of IT scanning seem to apply to all organizations, regardless of the strategic emphasis they place on identification and assimilation of IT resources: Organizations will spend the same amount of money on scanning, employ the same number of employees specifically for scanning, and use similar scanning methods.

These conclusions signify that an organization that wishes to become more aggressive and proactive in the strategic uses of IT must do more than increase spending on IT scanning and add employees to that task. The company must scan continuously, must have top management support for IT scanning, must link IT scanning with corporate strategy, and must at least entertain the prospect of dedicating an organizational unit to IT scanning.

Also, there appears to be an evolving polarization, based on the degree to which IT resources are exploited for strategic purposes, in the different types of organizations included in this study (EI, CEA, and PEEF). The findings of this study, compared with those of earlier research, indicate that the percentage of organizations in the “extreme” categories (EI and PEEF) has more than doubled, while the percentage of organizations in the “middle” category (CEA) has been halved. These data suggest that the “wait and see” approach to adopting new IT is losing ground. More and more organizations are deciding either to fully exploit an existing IT before adopting a new technology, or to move quickly and aggressively to adopt leading-edge IT to improve and/or maintain their competitive position.

For the IS executive and other top management personnel, our findings should prove beneficial as they work to create an IT vision and shape the corporate IT culture to take advantage of the strategic benefits offered by application of new and emerging IT. For the IT researcher, this study provides a baseline for future studies to further explore how organizations obtain data and information from the rapidly changing external IT environment. It also provides a baseline of IT scanning characteristics that can be referenced by future researchers to help identify shifts and changes that occur as business application of IT resources evolves.
