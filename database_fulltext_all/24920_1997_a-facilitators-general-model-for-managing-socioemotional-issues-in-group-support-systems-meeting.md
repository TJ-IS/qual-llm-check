# A Facilitator's General Model for Managing Socioemotional Issues in Group Support Systems Meeting Environments

GIGI G. KELLY and ROBERT P. BOSTROM

GIGI G. KELLY is an Assistant Professor at the College of William and Mary. She graduated from James Madison University with a B.B.A. in MIS, earned her M.B.A. from Old Dominion University, and received her doctorate in MIS from the University of Georgia. She teaches in the areas of systems analysis and design, decision support systems, database, and information resource management. Her research interests include group support systems, facilitation, and the integration of information technology and organizational development. She has extensive consulting experience in MIS and has published articles in the Journal of Management Information Systems, Information Systems Management, Journal of Small Group Research, Computerworld, and various conference proceedings.

ROBERT P. BOSTROM is the L. Edmund Rast Professor of Business at the University of Georgia. He teaches in management information systems (MIS) and management areas. Dr. Bostrom holds a B.A. and M.B.A. from Michigan State University, an M.S. in computer science from SUNY at Albany, and a Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Minnesota. Besides numerous publications in leading academic and practitioner journals, he has extensive consulting and training experience in the areas of MIS management, MIS design, organizational development, facilitation, and computer support for teams. He is president of a training and consulting company that focuses on the effective integration of people and technology. His current research interests are focused on facilitation, group support systems, technology-supported learning, and effective design of organizations via the integration of human/social and technological dimensions.

ABSTRACT: This paper addresses the socioemotional dimension in group support systems (GSS) meeting environments from the perspective of the facilitator. A model is presented and discussed. This model represents how facilitators perceive and manage socioemotional issues in a GSS environment. The role of GSS in the model is also explored. The research methodology for gathering and analyzing the data in this paper was based on critical incident technique and semistructured interviews with twenty-six experienced facilitators. The research found that GSS provides a very useful tool for managing socioemotional issues; however, GSS also creates new issues. The implications of this research study's findings for facilitators and future research issues are posited.

An earlier version of this paper was originally published in the Proceedings of the Thirtieth Hawaii International Conference on System Sciences (IEEE Computer Society Press, 1997).

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: electronic meeting facilitation, group support systems, socioemotional aspects of meetings.

SINCE THE LATE 1980S, COMPUTER TECHNOLOGY HAS BEEN COMMERCIALLY available to support group meetings. It has the potential to help solve some of the issues associated with the meeting productivity dilemma. The widespread use of computer technology in meetings (also referred to as group support systems, or GSS) is steadily expanding. One dimension of GSS research has focused on the role of the facilitator. The facilitator's role has been recognized as a critical dimension in GSS meetings [13, 32, 35]. While there is a growing body of research regarding GSS facilitation, there are still many unanswered questions.

One issue that has been recognized as significant but has largely remained unexplored is how facilitators use computer technology to address socioemotional issues (i.e., issues involving emotions and relationships [socio] within groups) in GSS meetings. Emotions and relationships are tightly coupled and result in energy that affects the meeting process $[15]$ . When managing the meeting processes that occur before, during, and after a meeting, facilitators must be aware of the importance of the social dimension in order to help the group achieve its goal $[5, 10]$ .

Socioemotional behaviors, on average, account for one-third of behaviors in meetings, with positive socioemotional behaviors twice as common as negative behaviors [40]. Balance between task and social behaviors is required [8, 9, 16, 27, 16]. There is no prescribed best blend; however, the socioemotional dimension is often disregarded in order to accomplish the task [17]. Disregarding the socioemotional dimension can be detrimental to the meeting outcomes. An overemphasis on task functions may increase a group's short-term performance; however, a continued focus on the task alone can undermine the long-term effectiveness of the group [7].

Individuals demonstrating positive socioemotional behavior, especially positive affect, are more likely to be fully concentrating and to have a high level of energy. This increases the likelihood of participation [33]. Individuals displaying negative affect tend to disengage in the process or attend to detail-oriented process strategies [42]. In a meeting, it is vital that a facilitator attend to both the positive and the negative emotions that participants exhibit. For example, if commitment to the outcome is paramount to the success of the meeting, then any highly negative emotions should be addressed. Ignoring negative emotions will likely jeopardize the outcome of participant's commitment [43]. The facilitator has to manage not only the emotions of the group but his or her own emotions as well. As a facilitator of the meeting process, tremendous demands are placed on the facilitator's ability to address sensitive socioemotional issues.

In the traditional meeting room environment, the facilitator is limited to manual techniques for addressing the socioemotional dimension. The development of GSS for the meeting environment has provided new tools that the facilitator can employ to address not only the accomplishment of tasks but also the management of emotions

and relationships in the meeting.

This paper presents a general facilitator's model for managing socioemotional issues in GSS meeting environments. This paper addresses the following two research questions:

\- What processes do facilitators use to manage socioemotional issues in face-to-face GSS meetings?

• How does GSS currently affect these processes?

## Research Literature Review

THIS RESEARCH BUILDS UPON AN EXISTING STREAM OF FACILITATION RESEARCH in GSS. Prior GSS facilitation research has been both laboratory-based [1, 2] and field-based [5, 10]. Anson, Bostrom, and Wynne [1], in their laboratory research regarding facilitation, concluded that groups with a facilitator in a GSS setting had the highest level of cohesiveness and more positive perceptions of task and process outcomes. Clawson and Bostrom [10], in their field research, identified sixteen critical role dimensions of facilitators in GSS environments. Five of these dimensions can be clearly classified as socioemotional. One recent study developed an affective reward instrument to measure “the positive emotional response sometimes associated with goal attainment” ([38], p. 171). The authors of the affective reward instrument conclude that by using this instrument researchers may better understand the reasoning behind some groups’ excitement with GSS and other groups’ disengagement from GSS. All of these studies note the importance of the socioemotional dimension and call for more research. This study extends the existing stream of GSS research while closing some of the research gaps.

Although awareness of the socioemotional dimension is often acknowledged as critical for a facilitator, there is little empirical literature regarding the process of how a facilitator actually deals with socioemotional issues over the life of a meeting. Phillips and Phillips [37] note that the facilitator's greatest contribution is his or her ability to see and understand the group's life or process. They recommend three main ways that a facilitator can gain this overall understanding: (1) by observing, (2) by attending to overt and symbolic content, and (3) by monitoring the emotions/feelings of the group. This is a first attempt to provide some overall process; however, few details and theoretical justifications are provided. The research reported here outlines a complete process for managing socioemotional issues, highlighting how GSS is used to provide management support.

Although research regarding the socioemotional dimension in GSS meetings is limited, other disciplines such as small group research, social psychology, education, and counseling have moderately addressed this issue $[4, 6, 30]$ . The socioemotional dimension is normally compared and contrasted with the task dimension (i.e., “giving and receiving information, exchanging opinions, and generating ideas, where factual or cognitive skills and outcomes are involved” $[39]$ , p. 89). Most GSS research has focused on task-related issues $[11]$ . However, looking exclusively at task issues in meetings provides a limited and incomplete view of the meeting process; the socioemotional dimension must also be addressed to arrive at a holistic understanding of the meeting process. The need for this research in the context of the GSS meeting is articulated by DeSanctis [14]:

[G]roup-process theories recognize the importance of “socio-emotional” communication in the successful functioning of groups. But few GSSs provide mechanisms for groups to draw on their emotions and value judgments, and even fewer theoretical models give attention to the deep role that interpersonal emotions can play in Group Support System use. [p. 108]

## Research Methodology

SINCE THE GENERAL FACILITATOR'S MODEL WAS A BYPRODUCT of a larger project [28], the research methodology of the larger project is presented. The larger research project was field based and used qualitative research methods. The scope of this research study was limited to same-time, same-place meetings in GSS environments that involved a facilitator. The use of qualitative techniques to explore changes occurring in today's dynamic organizational environment has been advocated as a preferred methodology [12]. After we gain a fundamental understanding of this research area, more controlled and generalizable research studies can be undertaken.

Specifically, critical incident methodology and semistructured interviews were used to structure and analyze the research. A critical incident is a simple description of a behavior or a set of behaviors observed in a focal person $[18]$ . A critical incident for this study was defined as a set of effective and/or ineffective behaviors related to emotions and relationships in a GSS meeting. In addition to collecting behaviors, additional information was gathered using semistructured interview questions. These questions focused on how computer technology was used by the facilitator in the described incident. By using the incident as the focal point for the respondent, these areas of investigation could be treated as a natural extension of the pure critical incident methodology.

In this study, ninety-three critical incidents from twenty-six facilitators were collected. Interviews typically lasted ninety minutes. The study participants included facilitators who had experience in face-to-face GSS meeting environments and were regarded as “among the best” in the profession. A purposeful sample was chosen that included facilitators with a minimum of one year’s experience with facilitating in GSS environments. Facilitators who had been recognized for their outstanding facilitation skills as well as facilitators recommended by GSS vendors and by other facilitators were contacted and asked to participate in this study. Participants were drawn from both the business and academic communities. The purpose of this sample was to gather in-depth and “real-world” facilitator experience in GSS meeting environments. Facilitators had an average of 8.2 years of overall facilitation experience and 4.8 years of GSS facilitation experience. Facilitator demographics are presented in Table 1.

All interviews were tape-recorded and the researcher took field notes $[19]$ . Upon completion of the interview, the researcher and three independent transcribers transcribed the tape-recorded notes. After each transcription was completed, the researcher listened to the tape a second time to verify correctness.

Once the critical incidents were collected, they were carefully read, and explicit behavioral descriptions of the critical dimensions were identified. These behaviors were initially classified and sorted. Two external reviewers independently reviewed the final classification schemes. Initial interrater reliabilities for all classification schemes were greater than 0.90. All items that the external reviewers coded differently from the researcher were reviewed and reconciled with complete agreement among all reviewers and the researcher.

After synthesizing the critical incident data and the replies to the additional questions specifically designed to elicit descriptions of the models used to address socioemotional issues, a general facilitator's model for managing socioemotional issues in GSS meeting environments was developed. This general facilitator's model was originally developed after the pilot studies and literature review [29]. As interviews were conducted, the described general facilitator's models were compared with the preliminary model and modifications were made to align the model with the data. This examination and refinement of the model continued throughout the research study. The final general facilitator's model portrays the integration of the various models that were described by facilitators in this study.

## Facilitator Perspective

Because this model represents how facilitators managed socioemotional issues in a GSS meeting environment, it is important to highlight the facilitator as a model variable. Facilitators bring to the GSS meeting environment experiences, beliefs, and values that influence the processes and techniques they choose to use. This is not a trivial point. When facilitators were asked about their model for managing socioemotional issues, two facilitators replied that they did not have a model; however, as the researcher recalled for them what they had expressed in the critical incidents, they realized that they did have some preconceived ideas.

When eliciting the models from facilitators in this study, there was some variation in their expressed views about addressing socioemotional issues in GSS meetings. The data gathered reflect a continuum of facilitator's beliefs about their ability to address socioemotional issues. At one end of the continuum, facilitators avoid socioemotional issues. At the other end, facilitators understand and have a clear model of the socioemotional component of meetings and group. And in the middle, facilitators are aware of but usually will only address socioemotional issues if accomplishment of the stated task objective is in jeopardy.

In this study the final sample of facilitator's general models represented a normal distribution. At one end, three facilitators expressed their avoidance of socioemotional issues. Below is an excerpt from one of the facilitator's interviews discussing his view as avoiding socioemotional issues:

No, I'm not a real good person with emotions. Friends call me Mr. Spock because I just don't show emotions much and I don't function much with emotions. . . . I don't function very well with emotions when it comes up in these groups. I tend to hold the groups back to be real task-oriented. And that's probably a failing in me as a facilitator, that I'm not as attuned to it as I ought to be and don't react to it as well as I ought to. I have all sorts of task-related mappings and I know that I have some emotion-related mappings. I have some politics-related mappings, looking at the underlying politics. But emotionally I just try to ignore as much as I can.

In the middle, a typical reply from the seventeen facilitators interviewed reflected a tacit awareness and acknowledgment of socioemotional issues. Here is one facilitator's perspective: "Support the positive things you see and any of the negative stuff you tend [to] pass back to the group." Another facilitator noted the following:

My model is to listen. I am a peacemaker. When in doubt, I work to remove tension. I will make jokes. I will use humor. I will do whatever is required to make people not real defensive and work to get people [to] kind of soften, compromising.

Finally, three facilitators clearly articulated their proactive perspective for addressing socioemotional issues. Here is one facilitator's comment:

When I believe there is a need for or potential benefit of reflection and dialogue to gain a more in-depth understanding of the problem at hand, I work into my facilitation an event or events to unfreeze the group (using Lewin's change model—unfreeze/transform/refreeze); that is, I try to slow them down and talk about the problem from multiple perspectives (e.g., their own, those affected, and those who contribute) and to consider possible multiple components and relationships of the problem (e.g., group-dynamics issues).

A majority of facilitators in this study recognized the need to maintain some form of balance between task and socioemotional behaviors. Balance refers to that harmonious equilibrium that exists in order to accomplished the desired outcomes for the meeting. There is not one best mix of task and socioemotional behaviors; what is appropriate depends on the specific circumstances for a particular issue $[27]$ . This balancing of task and socioemotional was noted by one facilitator:

There is a continuum between total task and total social. If you are at the task end then you are only looking at content with no social entity to it, then we have no commitment or ownership to it. If we make a decision where social is the only [focus], then content is being dictated powerfully by group influence, your social influence, groupthink.... What I have tried to do is to think about them along the continuum of task and social.

As stated in the excerpt above, focusing on either extreme can cause problems in accomplishing the desired meeting outcomes. Clearly, the data gathered in this study indicate that the facilitator's perspective will influence his or her method for managing socioemotional issues in GSS meetings.

## Meeting Environment

In this model, the meeting environment is the focal point for the facilitator. Maintaining this focus and understanding the fluid dynamics in a GSS meeting can be a very complex task because of the number of variables present in these environments. The meeting variables consist of people, task, process, and technology, and the relationships between these variables. The main focus in this study was on the people and the corresponding relationships in same-time/same-place GSS meetings.

## Pre-Assessment

The pre-assessment section is directly influenced by the facilitator's perspective. Pre-assessment usually involves the facilitator meeting with the meeting leader/initiator and may involve meeting participants. Facilitators in this study expressed the desire to keep participants in a resourceful and positive emotional state; therefore, potential negative issues that may crop up during the meeting are often sought. This is reflected on the model by the box labeled “Identifying socioemotional problems to watch for.” The identification of preexisting issues or potential problems can assist the facilitator in monitoring the relationships and emotions that occur during the meeting. One facilitator described his view of pre-assessment as:

Well, I'm trying to get a real sense of whom I'm going to be with and if I can get a

sense of is there any conflict. . . . I don't want to walk in and step on any landmines.

And I try to identify sensitive areas or topics that people will react to . . . I want to know what I'm going to be facing.

During pre-assessment, the facilitator may also construct desired socioemotional outcomes, thus, taking a proactive position in addressing socioemotional issues.

To address the socioemotional problems and outcomes that a facilitator identifies during pre-assessment, facilitators will often consider many different meeting scenarios when developing the meeting agenda. Being prepared was cited by facilitators in this study as an important factor for being able to address socioemotional issues effectively. One facilitator noted, “I walk in with a lot of agendas in the back of my head. During the preplanning, I will try different agendas.” Processing several different mental scenarios before the meeting helps the facilitator remain flexible and creative within the meeting. Research has shown the pre-assessment is critical in GSS meetings $[5, 10]$ ; this finding was also articulated by all the facilitators in this study.

## Diagnosis

During diagnosis, the facilitator monitors people and relationships to assess the socioemotional state of the meeting environment. This is a complex task because of the number of variables and relationships that affect the socioemotional state. The facilitator uses both traditional manual techniques and GSS to monitor the situation. For example, in monitoring for negative socioemotional behaviors, a manual technique noted by facilitators was watching for nonverbal behavior such as a frown or a participant slamming his fist on the table. An example of a GSS technique used by a facilitator was inferring from comments input anonymously that some people were really upset about the current topic.

Variance is detected when the facilitator recognizes that what is occurring does not agree with some explicit or tacit standard that he or she has imposed for an observed behavior. For example, ground rules are set at the beginning of the meeting that prohibited participants from personally attacking another individual. If this rule is violated, the facilitator may choose to intervene; however, the facilitator may choose to monitor the situation. Detecting variance is primarily accomplished by the facilitator using his or her own internal criteria and heuristics. The following illustrates one facilitator's view: "You need to use your gut as a barometer, but you need to check with [the] group to check [the] facilitator's barometer." None of the facilitators in this study indicated using GSS for detecting variance, but there are GSS tools that could be used for this purpose. For example, GSS tools such as mood meters and opinion polls allow for instantaneous reactions; these could be used by facilitators to help detect variances. The nonuse of these tools may indicate that the current versions of these tools do not meet the facilitator's need or that the facilitator is not confident about using them. Further research is warranted.

## Intervention

An intervention was defined as “[entering] into an ongoing system of relationship, to come between or among persons, group or objects for the purpose of helping them” ([3], p. 15). The intervention phase of the model is composed of two processes: surfacing socioemotional issues and taking action.

Surfacing socioemotional issues has been identified separately because of the unique and important influence GSS have on this type of intervention. GSS allow socioemotional issues to come to the surface quicker and in a less threatening manner than in non-GSS meetings. Issues that may have been considered taboo verbally in traditional meeting environments may now be addressed through the GSS.

Surfacing socioemotional issues is accomplished via both manual and GSS techniques. An example of a manual technique is brainwriting—having participants share thoughts by writing on cards or paper and then posting them for all to see. The issues are brought to the surface and can then be addressed. An example of a GSS intervention technique to uncover an issue is an anonymous vote or anonymous computer-aided brainwriting, as exemplified in the following facilitator's comments:

What the Option-Finder [vote] emerged on some issues, particularly around areas of people sharing information, was a clear conflict on the Option-Finder [display] that there were a couple of subgroups that disagreed. . . . And they knew as soon as they saw it. It just elicited people's emotions that hadn't had an opportunity to be expressed in the past.

Taking action refers to those intervention techniques used by a facilitator that do not deal directly with surfacing but do deal directly with changing the emotional state of the group. For example, a GSS intervention technique could allow group members to explore various reasons for disagreement among the group. The specific technique to implement this will depend on the meeting software. A workstation GSS could be used for anonymously sharing people's thoughts and getting clarification on needed areas. This would be done to help a group move from the negative to a neutral or positive emotional state.

An intervention is used because the facilitator wants to change the state of the meeting environment. To reflect this change in the model, a dashed line from surfacing socioemotional issue and taking action is connected to the meeting cloud and there are multiple meeting clouds. If the intervention technique is effective, the group progresses to the desired state. As one facilitator simply put it, “If I can’t make them positive, I at least neutralize them. Don’t leave them negative.”

How a facilitator invoked the model depended on the facilitator's perspective, either the socioemotional problem perspective or the socioemotional outcome perspective. The two views are similar; however, there are some subtle but important differences.

## The Problem Perspective

When a facilitator addressed socioemotional issues from the problem perspective, he or she had to deal with problems that occurred during the meeting. In some cases, the facilitator was able to identify problems to watch for during pre-assessment. Often, however, problems would arise during the meeting to which the facilitator would have to be able to react quickly. Many facilitators were faced with very tight meeting time constraints and had to make rapid decision about how, or if, to handle socioemotional problems. A total of 117 socioemotional problems were identified in this study; they were classified into four broad categories: people (66 problems), GSS (19 problems), process (22 problems), and task (10 problems). When addressing socioemotional problems, clearly the people aspect is the focal point. A sample of a socioemotional problem that was classified as “People—Previous Dysfunctional Interactions” is described below:

Probably half of the group had lived through the conflict in the organization a year ago, and half were new to the group. . . . We thought there'd be conflict within the group. And I don't know what their attitude was towards the general. I don't know if there was anger with the general or emotional with the general that brought up those comments at the end or simply was just an opportunity for them to release it at that point.

Effectively addressing people problems is definitely a prime concern for facilitators. It is worth noting that GSS can cause new socioemotional problems as well as help facilitators and groups address existing ones. This topic has received little attention in the GSS literature where the focus has been on GSS benefits.

Based on the findings of this research, facilitators have four pathways to choose from when managing socioemotional problems in GSS meetings. These pathways are summarized in Table 2.

For example, when using a workstation GSS, in response to a question participants generated a tremendous amount of information in a very short time. The facilitator realized that all the information being generated could not be processed in the allotted meeting time (i.e., information overload). The facilitator detected the problem and now had to decide what to do. The facilitator responded to the conditions in the meeting to determine which path to follow; thus, the facilitator was in a reactive mode. The facilitator had a variety of options. Following the P1 pathway, he could do nothing and let the group continue. Following the P2 pathway, he could surface the issue with the group. He could call attention to the group screen and make an explicit statement about the number of comments generated. Following the P3 pathway, he could continue the P2 path and, after surfacing the issue, make an explicit intervention such as turning the technology off. Or, following the P4 pathway, the facilitator could stop or change the generation process. Choosing P2, P3, or P4 would affect the meeting process. In this specific case, the facilitator chose P1 (i.e., did nothing). The incident was classified as ineffective by the facilitator, who noted that it appeared that all the participants moved from a positive to a negative socioemotional state.

## The Outcome Perspective

Facilitators addressing socioemotional issues from the outcome perspective rely on the pre-assessment phase to identify socioemotional outcomes for the meeting. In this study, 31 socioemotional outcomes were generated in the 93 incidents, compared with the 117 problems generated. Facilitators followed the reactive problem pathways of the general facilitator's model more often than the proactive outcome pathways.

Following the same classification process used in the problem categories, the meeting model provided the highest level of categories for sorting the outcomes. There were twenty-two people-classified outcomes, one GSS-classified outcome, two process-classified outcomes, and eight task-classified outcomes. A sample socioemotional outcome classified as “People—team building” is described by a facilitator as follows:

It's about helping people begin to appreciate that there are different perspectives, ways to look . . . at the world. The middle managers were coming from a very diverse set of backgrounds. You've got people looking at all sorts of different aspects of it. What you are trying to do is by trying to help people see the different perspectives begin to appreciate there are more than one angle to an issue. . . . They are able to begin to share the information. And therefore build the sense of a group working together as a team.

Based on this research, when facilitators invoke an outcome perspective, three possible pathways could be followed, as shown in Table 3. For example, during pre-assessment of one meeting, the meeting leader explained that a socioemotional outcome of the upcoming meeting was having group members share constructive comments about a sensitive issue. Following the O1 pathway, the facilitator could choose only to surface the issue. To illustrate, the facilitator could start the meeting with a quick poll, using show of hands, sticky dots, keypads, or voting tools in workstation GSS, or could use other methods such as manual or electronic brainwriting, to bring out the group's opinion on this sensitive issue. Following the O2 pathway, after surfacing the issue, the facilitator could stage an intervention to explore the group's options. Using the O1 or O2 path, the participants actively share data and surface the issue. Following the O3 pathway, the facilitator may choose techniques that take direct action, such as having participants directly explore how to solve the sensitive issue. Facilitators using the O3 pathway have decided not to include the group in surfacing the issue; thus, the facilitator is in greater control. The facilitator is only choosing the intervention technique that he or she hopes will prompt the group to take action. In this case, the facilitator followed path O1 and used Topic Commentor, a tool in GroupSystems™, to surface the group's opinions about the issue that led the group to move from a negative to a neutral emotional state.

## The Role of GSS

For facilitators in this study, the role of the GSS in managing socioemotional issues in meetings varied and was seen as both a benefit and a drawback. Although some facilitators mentioned using GSS during the diagnosis phase, GSS was primarily used to support interventions.

For each of the incidents, the facilitators provided their assessment of the intervention. They identified the emotional state of the group before and after the intervention. The possible states were negative, neutral, and positive. In addition, the facilitator was asked to classify the results of the interventions for the given incident as effective or ineffective. The majority of incidents and corresponding interventions were perceived to be effective (81 percent), and GSS clearly assisted the facilitator in the process for successful intervention.

The beginning state for 58 percent of the incidents was classified as negative. Of those 58 percent, 9 percent of the meeting states remained negative, 25 percent moved to neutral, and 66 percent moved to positive. Eighty-nine percent of these incidents were considered effective interventions and 11 percent ineffective. The movement of the group relates directly to a facilitator's judgment of effectiveness. For incidents where the meeting started in a positive state, ending states of neutral or negative were considered an ineffective intervention. Facilitators wanted to create or maintain a positive meeting environment. The perceived movement in a meeting group is a factor that facilitators are monitoring.

With the introduction of GSS into the meeting environment, GSS can be seen as both an advantage and a disadvantage when addressing socioemotional issues. Certain features can be found in the currently available GSS software. ## Potential Drawbacks of GSS Usage

Drawbacks associated with GSS features were mentioned by facilitators in this study one-fourth as often as benefits. The relatively small number of drawbacks may be partially explained by the fact that the interviewed facilitators were preselected on the basis of their experience as facilitators and previous use of GSS. If the GSS were not seen as an overall asset to the facilitation process, then the facilitator would likely not use it. In addition, because the facilitators were experienced, they often recognized the drawbacks and managed around them.

The most frequently mentioned drawbacks had to do with the GSS features of structuring of process, simultaneous input, and presence of technology (see Table 4). Structuring of process included a variety of drawbacks, including intensifying the process and limiting down time, leading to groupthink, and providing too much structure. For example, one facilitator noted, “The group made the decision quickly but did not have buy-in in such a quick time.”

The information overload drawback associated with simultaneous input was one of the most frequently identified drawbacks. Here, GSS enables information overload to occur and creates a new problem that the facilitator must be able to address. This is one facilitator's rehashing of an incident addressing information overload:

The group cranked out 200 comments in 20 minutes. I summarized key points with the group in another 20 minutes. The meeting initiator directed that we analyze and rephrase each comment in a process that took $2^{1/2}$ hours. The $2^{1/2}$ hours was a nightmare.... I knew the train was going to crash and it did.

In this particular case, more information was generated than could be processed by the facilitator or the group given the time allocated for the meeting. The facilitator had not adequately matched the process and the GSS tool selection. It is quite obvious that GSS can cause negative socioemotional situations that facilitators must be able to manage. These problems and the associated drawbacks of using GSS are rarely mentioned in the GSS literature and need further exploration. For example, Grisé and Gallupe $[21, 22]$ are currently researching the information overload problem.

## Using GSS in the Intervention Phase

GSS was clearly seen by facilitators in this study as a tool that allowed them to intervene in socioemotional situations using new methods and in more successful ways. Facilitators mentioned all the traditional features associated with GSS. However, the group screen, a GSS feature that is often buried in the literature under the category electronic recording and display, was distinctly considered a separate and significant feature for addressing socioemotional issues (see Table 4).

Two other features emerged from the data, presence of technology and real-time feedback. Presence of technology is the existence of technology in the meeting room and how this creates a new and different meeting environment. Many facilitators commented that “we couldn’t have done this without technology.” Another feature identified from the data was the importance of real-time feedback. The GSS’s ability to provide instantaneous feedback was considered important and useful by the facilitators for surfacing and managing socioemotional issues.

Although facilitators in this study used all GSS features, some GSS features appeared more consequential when managing socioemotional issues. Three distinct GSS features emerged from this study's data that were seen by facilitators as key advantages over traditional meetings when using GSS during the intervention phase: the group screen, anonymity, and real-time feedback.

The group screen was clearly identified by facilitators in this study as having a major impact when addressing socioemotional issues and supporting GSS invention techniques. This finding was somewhat surprising and contradicts most GSS research that stresses the benefits associated with anonymity and simultaneous input [26].

The group screen's ability to provide participants with different perspectives for viewing information appears to make a positive impact when addressing socioemotional issues. Facilitators often used the group screen as the focal point for the discussion, which helped participants step into an observer role. This observer role helped participants be more objective about an issue and also tended to neutralize the issue and lessen participants' negative emotions.

Another perceptual benefit of the group screen was the ability to see the “big” picture. Either by mapping issues, displaying votes, or structuring comments, participants were able to see both the forest and the trees, as it were. Furthermore, using the group screen as the focal point helped alleviate some problems that can be caused by simultaneous input. For instance, the data and research confirm that information overload is often caused by facilitators not reining in participants to see what others are inputting. For socioemotional issues that stemmed from misunderstandings (e.g., lack of clarity, incorrect assumptions, inability to deal with large amounts of detailed complexity), the group screen provided a mechanism for creating a holistic view of the issue and its context, and this often led to better understanding of the issue.

For example, one facilitator made the following remarks about the benefits of having people in the observer (third-person) position associated with the group screen coupled with anonymity and the presence of a facilitator when addressing a conflict situation:

One [technique] is the technology; the anonymity really got it out. And then the other was the facilitator and public screen being in the middle. So you got people in observer positions.... You're using the public screen and that keeps the data, gets it out there. Keeps them in observer.

Anonymity is another important GSS feature for surfacing and managing socioemotional issues. Anonymity is one GSS feature that has received a considerable amount of research, although rarely in connection with socioemotional issues. One notable exception is Massey and Clapper's laboratory GSS research that found strong support for the following socioemotional related topics:

With the GSS, subjects

1. Felt more comfortable suggesting ideas,

2. Were less worried about what others thought of them,

3. Felt more relaxed discussing sensitive issues,

4. Felt more comfortable expressing strong feelings toward other group members, and

5. Felt more comfortable expressing negative feelings toward other group members ([31], p. 167).

Our own findings corroborate Massey and Clapper's first four findings. One facilitator had the following to say about a group with a history of conflict:

Technology was vital. Anonymity permitted open expression. Trust issue was raised without explosion of emotions. Members very relieved that issue finally rose to surface so the boss has to recognize and deal with it. Issue would never have come up without the technology.

Drawbacks of anonymity identified included allowing for flaming and surfacing of sensitive issues that people did not want to surface or did not know how to surface without risking embarrassment or threatening others. Most facilitators in this study mentioned that they proactively attempt to avoid the consequences associated with negative expressions toward other participants.

Facilitators in this study often strove to manage around known drawbacks associated with anonymity. For example, to address flaming, facilitators mentioned setting ground rules (a manual technique) or intervening quickly in the event of negative personal comments (a technology technique) as two intervention strategies to dampen the prospect of flaming.

Real-time feedback grew out of traditional survey feedback, which is often used by organization development consultants to deal with both task and socioemotional issues. What are the new possibilities of using GSS tools to increase group reflection and discussion of socioemotional issues by allowing for a rapid capturing of individual inputs and sharing of feedback results? Real-time feedback was a GSS feature that emerged from the analysis of the data. The GSS's ability to provide instantaneous feedback was considered important and useful by the facilitators for surfacing and managing socioemotional issues. Traditional feedback research indicates that spontaneous feedback will lead to superior outcomes $[25]$ . With GSS in the meeting room, feedback can be instantaneous and spontaneous. For the facilitators in this study, immediate feedback often allowed issues to surface and be resolved quickly.

Furthermore, in this study, real-time feedback was often used iteratively to continue to address the same issue until it was resolved. Revisiting previous feedback allowed the facilitator to compare and contrast where individuals and the group were positioned on an issue. Real-time feedback may be strengthened when anonymity is also used. There is very little research addressing the utilization of GSS and real-time feedback analysis to respond to socioemotional issues $[34]$ .

## Implications for Facilitators

THERE ARE SEVERAL KEY LESSONS THAT FACILITATORS SHOULD BE AWARE of when addressing socioemotional issues in GSS meetings. These lessons are highlighted in Table 5 and discussed below.

The first three lessons clearly reveal the need for better training of both new and experienced facilitators. With the growing acceptance and importance of the role of the facilitator in business, it is essential that facilitators be trained effectively and efficiently $[10, 35]$ . One of the most essential skills for leading and contributing to organizations in a complex world is the ability to facilitate diverse human and technological interactions. An opportunity exists to educate facilitators about the process of managing socioemotional issues and how GSS relates. This includes not only understanding the socioemotional issues related to the group but also understanding facilitator's own socioemotional beliefs, attitudes, and values. To improve a group's performance, group members must be allowed to use the full potential of meetings to explore socioemotional issues as well as task issues $[36]$ .

The opportunity to address socioemotional issues proactively needs to be explored. The findings in this study contribute to baseline information for training of facilitators working in GSS meeting environments, as well as the transition training of traditional facilitators seeking to move into the GSS arena. The model presented here provides a framework for structuring training modules to address socioemotional issues and the impact of GSS during these processes.

## Future Research

ALTHOUGH THIS PAPER EXTENDS THE CURRENT LITERATURE BY HIGHLIGHTING the significance of socioemotional issues, the role of GSS, and the ability for facilitators to manage these issues and GSS, many unanswered questions and research opportunities remain. Examining the pre-assessment component of the general facilitator's model may provide insight into why socioemotional outcomes were less prevalent.

Are there some defining characteristics that differentiate those facilitators who are more adept at managing socioemotional issues than those who are not? Do facilitators who have more traditional facilitation experience possess different or better models than facilitators who start with technology? Are less experienced facilitators even less in tune and less adept with their models of managing socioemotional issues?

A more in-depth understanding of the pathways is an area for future research. By establishing socioemotional outcomes, are the instances of socioemotional problems reduced? Can GSS help facilitators help the group achieve the desired socioemotional outcomes? Facilitators primarily used GSS to support interventions. Are facilitators missing an opportunity to take advantage of the GSS tools to enhance their own facilitation and meeting efficacy? Are there features associated with GSS that would assist facilitators in the diagnosis of a situation? What new GSS features are needed?

This study identified the importance that facilitators attach to positive movement by the group in defining the success of an intervention technique. An additional insight revealed that the movement of a group might be more significant when the movement is related to the resolution of a socioemotional issue. Are critical transition points in meetings associated with socioemotional issues?

Research related to GSS features can also be expanded. For instance, this study identified the importance facilitators attribute to the group screen for addressing socioemotional issues. In particular, placing participants in an observer role was found often to neutralize the emotions associated with an issue. Also, the ability to see the context around an issue (the “big picture”) helped to moderate socioemotional issues. The group screen has received very little prior research (exceptions are $[41]$ and $[20]$ ).

Real-time feedback in group settings was identified as a GSS feature that facilitators used to enhance the outcomes of socioemotional issues. This form of feedback has received little attention in the GSS literature. There is a body of survey feedback literature that may be relevant to the study of real-time feedback. For instance, what are the underlying differences and similarities between traditional feedback methods and new GSS feedback methods? What are the implications for group members when real-time feedback is used? There are negative issues associated with real-time feedback that also need to be addressed. Facilitators in this study indicated that group members were not prepared to receive this type of feedback and therefore reacted negatively when it was received. What is necessary on the part of the facilitator to prepare group members to receive this feedback?

In addition to the currently available GSS tools, there is an opportunity to better design GSS tools that can aid the facilitator in managing socioemotional issues. The ability to express emotions electronically is required. Currently, emoticons are used during e-mail discussions. Are there more sophisticated means to help transmit the emotional element of electronic conversations? Suggestions from facilitators in this study ranged from allowing participants to include simple graphics to expressing emotions by sitting in special chairs that monitored body heat. Being able to tap into and capture the emotions of a group electronically in an unobtrusive way could provide new information into the meeting environment. A more out-of-the-box answer that was given by one facilitator when discussing technology follows:

I say the only reason that we have this technology is because we can't see inside each other's head. I can't feel what you feel. I can't share the excitement of the experiences that you've had. I can't celebrate the things that you celebrate. I can't feel the pain that you feel. I don't know what you know the way you know it. So where is this going? Ultimately, we should be able to put on helmets and be able to build a human LAN.

With the rapid growth of the Internet, desktop conferencing, and telecommuting, facilitators and organizations face the question of how to facilitate distributed meetings and teams. The next generation of groupware must be seamless between the individual and the group work $[24]$ . A challenge that developers face is having “sophisticated understandings of prospective users’ workplaces” ( $[23]$ , p. 100). This study focused on a synchronized, face-to-face GSS meeting environment; however, what has been found here is a piece of the puzzle of distributed facilitation. Many of the issues identified in this study may be applicable to distributed meeting environments. For example, the group screen appears to be important when addressing socioemotional issues. As whiteboards and other shared screens growth in the distributed market continues, understanding how to manage group space will increase in importance.

## Conclusion

THIS PAPER HAS PROVIDED INSIGHTS INTO THE SOCIOEMOTIONAL ISSUES facilitators address in GSS meeting environments and the processes facilitators employ to manage these issues. The general facilitator's model presented here expands the general knowledge and understanding of how facilitators manage socioemotional issues in GSS meeting environments and the impacts of GSS on this process. A model is helpful because managing socioemotional issues is a very complex task. The complexity of managing socioemotional issues in GSS meetings may be one reason why so little research has been conducted in this area. This study has provided both conceptual and practical contributions to understand and improve the way facilitators manage socioemotional issues in GSS meetings and the role of GSS during these processes.
