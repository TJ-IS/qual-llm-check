# Flaming in the Electronic Classroom

BRUCE A. REINIG, ROBERT O. BRIGGS, AND JAY F. NUNAMAKER, JR.

BRUCE A. REINIG is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Information and Systems Management at the Hong Kong University of Science & Technology. His research interests include the investigation and applications of group support systems and collaborative work technologies. His work has been published in the Communications of the ACM and the Journal of Management Information Systems. He received his Ph.D. in Management Information Systems from the University of Arizona in 1996 and his B.S. from Truman State University.

ROBERT O. BRIGGS. For biographical sketch, please see the Guest Editors' Introduction.

JAY F. NUNAMAKER, JR. For biographical sketch, please see the Guest Editors' Introduction.

ABSTRACT: Research has shown that group support systems (GSS) can be used to substantially improve learning at all levels of formal education. However, some instructors are reluctant to adopt GSS because they are concerned that learners will engage in flaming, or verbal attacks intended to offend either persons or organizations and often characterized by profanity, obscenity, and insults. This paper posits that flaming is caused by hostility engendered by a perception that vested interests have been or are about to be violated. It further posits that the relationship between hostility and flaming is moderated by personal values and risk of reprisal. A longitudinal experiment is presented that provides empirical support for the model. The paper then reports five field cases highlighting various strategies used by instructors for reducing or eliminating flaming.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: electronic classroom, field study research, flaming.

RESEARCH HAS SHOWN THAT GROUP SUPPORT SYSTEMS (GSS) can improve the classroom experience. In a variety of studies, GSS have been shown to increase observed learning [20], self-reported learning [1, 16], on-task participation [4, 25], and satisfaction with the classroom experience [1]. None of these benefits can be realized, however, if teachers will not use the technology because the students engage in flaming when they work online.

We define flaming as verbal attacks intended to offend either persons or organizations. Flaming is often characterized by profanity, obscenity, and insults. Not all disruptive communication is flaming; off-task jocularity or buffoonery may also occur and may limit the usefulness of online classroom interactions. Flaming and buffoonery are common in e-mail, Internet-based news groups, chat rooms, and discussion forums. Field experience shows that flaming can also occur in GSS-supported, or electronic, classroom interactions and that its presence can lead to avoidance or abandonment of GSS technology in the classroom [3, 21].

## Overview

This paper begins by describing a group support system (GSS) and develops a causal model for flaming. It uses the model to address two important questions regarding disruptive communication in the GSS-supported classroom:

1. Why do students tend to flame more in GSS-supported classroom interaction than in traditional, oral classroom interaction?

2. Why do some students choose not to flame in GSS-supported classroom interaction when other students, subjected to the same conditions, flame?

We then present a comparative study that measured the incidence of flaming and buffoonery in tasks completed in both an electronic and traditional classroom. The results suggest that disruptive communication is more likely to occur in an electronic classroom than in a traditional one. The next section discusses five field cases highlighting various instructor strategies for reducing or eliminating flaming. We conclude with some implications for researchers and practitioners.

## What Is a Group Support System?

A group support system is a suite of special software tools designed to focus and structure the efforts of people making a joint cognitive effort toward a goal. Participants make their contributions by typing into a network of personal computers. The system makes any contribution available to other participants on their screens. Groups often use a public screen to focus group attention during oral discussions of their online contributions.

A GSS allows all participants to contribute simultaneously, so people do not forget what they want to say while waiting for a turn to speak. Because a GSS retains a permanent record of contributions, people can stop to think without missing the intervening conversation. Furthermore, a GSS allows participants to contribute anonymously, so people can contribute unusual or unpopular ideas without having to worry about negative reactions from their peers or their superiors.

Each tool in a GSS focuses group deliberation in some unique way. An electronic brainstorming tool, for example, encourages a group to diverge from customary thinking. A categorizer tool, on the other hand, encourages a group to converge quickly on a salient set of key issues. Comment and outline tools encourage participants to explore a narrow set of ideas in depth. Electronic voting tools reveal patterns of consensus, focusing group attention on areas of disagreement. A group leader selects and juxtaposes the GSS tools so as to move the group efficiently toward its goals.

GSS tools were originally deployed in industry rather than in the classroom [17, 18].

Project teams using GSS have reported labor cost savings as high as 50 percent and reductions in project cycle times as high as 90 percent [9, 19, 24].

## Theory

PREVIOUS RESEARCH ON THE INCIDENCE OF DISRUPTIVE "UNINHIBITED BEHAVIOR" in computer-mediated versus face-to-face communication has yielded mixed results [2]. While some studies have reported substantial increases in the number of flaming remarks in computer-mediated communication versus face-to-face communication [6, 13, 23, 26], others have shown either much smaller increases or no increase at all [8, 10, 15, 22]. Many of these studies used students as subjects, and the George et al. [8] study in particular used a group support system as the electronic communication medium. The results of these studies indicate that students may or may not choose to flame when communicating electronically.

The model of flaming presented in figure 1 posits that flaming is caused by hostility and that the direct causal connection between hostility and flaming is moderated by personal values and the risk of reprisal. Hostility is a negatively valenced affective arousal often derived from the perception that other persons or organizations are behaving in a manner that conflicts with a person's interests. By definition, hostility always has some object—a person, an organization, a process. Flaming is a verbal attack that is intended to offend either persons or organizations and is often characterized by profanity, obscenity, and insults. Flaming need not necessarily be directed at the object of hostility; it may be launched at a convenient target in response to the negative feelings of the flamer. The model posits that hostility derives primarily from a perception that one's vested interests are being or are about to be violated. Examples may include frustration with a classroom process that prevents the learner from reaching a desired end-state, boredom from a perception that a particular task and process represent a wasteful allocation of a person's time, or conflict resulting from disagreement between the individual and other members of the group.

The degree to which hostility causes flaming, however, is mediated by both risk of reprisal and personal values. The influence of risk of reprisal may explain why flaming rarely, if ever, occurs in the traditional classroom environment (despite the presence of frustration, boredom, and conflict). When speaking orally, students can easily be identified by their instructors and peers and subjected to recrimination. In entirely anonymous GSS interaction, however, students can violate social norms without being identified and punished. In addition, some researchers suggest that communicating electronically may reduce the attention individuals give to the existing social context. During online interactions, computers do not transmit important social context cues such as clothing, demeanor, vocal inflection, eye contact, and gestures. These cues normally provide information to people about social norms and serve to enforce social regulation $[7, 12, 13, 23, 27]$ . It is possible that the absence of certain social context cues could lessen the salience of the risk of reprisal.

Why, then, do some students, when they feel hostility and are speaking anonymously, still refrain from flaming? We suggest that such students are engaging in self-regulation and that they choose not to express their hostility in the form of obscene comments because such action would violate their own personal value system. Undoubtedly, the degree to which one's value system affects his or her style of expression varies depending on the group and situation, and many students feel the classroom is an inappropriate environment for swearing or making personal attacks.

In summary, we posit that flaming occurs because people feel a negatively valenced affective response when they perceive that their vested interests have been or are about to be violated, and that they verbalize that hostility toward persons, processes, or organizations. The tendency for hostility to produce flaming, however, is moderated by the risk of reprisal and personal values.

## Hypotheses

OTHER THINGS BEING EQUAL, STUDENTS USING A GSS anonymously should perceive a lower risk of reprisal for flaming than students using only oral communication channels. If something in the classroom setting engenders hostility, the students using GSS should produce more flaming than students in the standard classroom. Hence:

H1: More instances of flaming will occur in the GSS-supported classroom than in the traditional classroom setting.

As mentioned above, not all disruptive classroom communication is flaming. Classroom interactions sometimes include off-task buffoonery that may also increase as the risk of reprisal decreases. Therefore:

H2: More instances of buffoonery will occur in the GSS-supported classroom than in the traditional classroom setting.

## Experimental Design and Analysis

WE PERFORMED A LONGITUDINAL EXPERIMENT TO TEST THE ABOVE HYPOTHESES. This experiment addresses the moderating effect of risk of reprisal on the link between hostility and flaming.

## Subjects

The participants in the study were forty-three students in the honors section of the introductory MIS course offered both during the Fall 1995 and Spring 1996 semesters at the University of Arizona. The Fall 1995 section had twenty-three students (8 women, 15 men), and the Spring 1996 section had twenty students (7 women, 13 men). The age range for each section was similar. The experimenters were not allowed to assign students randomly to course sections; the students themselves chose where and when to enroll.

## Task

Students engaged in eight twenty-minute debates of ethical scenarios addressing MIS issues such as software piracy, database marketing, expert systems and e-mail privacy $[28]$ . The objective of the tasks was to inform learners of ethical issues facing information technology professionals and to teach them to apply ethical frameworks to those situations $[14]$ .

## Treatments

This study included a treatment group using GSS-supported argumentation and a control group using standard oral classroom argumentation. The participants in the GSS treatment used GroupSystems, a group support system developed at the University of Arizona and marketed by Ventana Corporation.

## Procedure

The Fall 1995 class served as the control group, with the Spring 1996 class as the treatment group. All aspects of the course were as similar as possible throughout both semesters, with the exception of the discussion mode for the eight ethical scenarios. The same teacher taught both sections and was able to maintain a high level of consistency by virtue of having taught the course nine times previously. The sections were identical with respect to the course syllabus, assignments, lectures, exams, and students' skill level. Postexperimental review of session transcripts confirmed that the instructor made consistent presentations for both treatments. Both classes were held in similar classrooms in the same building. The treatment group conducted its eight twenty-minute ethical scenario discussions in a nearby electronic classroom. The students were aware that all class sessions were audio-recorded throughout both semesters.

There is a possible history threat inherent in this design because the two treatments were executed in two successive semesters rather than simultaneously. However, pilot tests conducted in the same semester revealed that learners who were not allowed to use the GSS felt slighted and expressed resentful demoralization.

## Dependent Measures

We counted the number of comments per session that constituted flaming and buffoonery. All verbal interactions in all sessions were recorded and transcribed into text. The written portion of the GSS-supported interaction was captured electronically. All contributions were evaluated by two treatment-blind raters who were intimately familiar with the course and the scenarios. They counted instances of flaming and buffoonery in the transcripts. The raters agreed on more than 99 percent of their evaluations, and differences were discussed and reconciled.

## Results

Students in the traditional classroom setting generated a total of 340 comments during the eight twenty-minute scenario discussions, none of which was classified as either flaming or buffoonery. Students in the GSS-supported classroom generated a total of 1,692 comments during the eight twenty-minute scenario discussions, of which five were classified as flaming and thirty-one were classified as buffoonery. The number of flaming and buffoonery comments from each task is presented in Table 1, and the text from each disruptive comment is presented in Table 2.

The data supported both hypotheses. A paired samples t-test compared the instances of flaming for the GSS-supported class with those of the traditional class for the eight tasks. The test was found to be statistically significant, $t(7) = 2.376$ , p < 0.0492, $eta^{2} = 0.446$ .

The difference scores for the buffoonery data were not normally distributed, and therefore the nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used $[5, 11]$ . A Wilcoxon signed rank of the frequency of buffoonery in the GSS-supported class versus the traditional classroom revealed a statistically significant difference, N = 8, T = 0, p < 0.005, $r_{c} = 1.0$ . The strength of this relationship is measured by the matched-pairs rank biserial correlation coefficient ( $r_{c}$ ), which can be interpreted in the same manner as the Pearson correlation coefficient $[11]$ . That is, ( $r_{c}$ ) $^{2}$ represents the proportion of variability of the buffoonery per task that can be explained by the treatment of allocating GSS resources to the discussion. This suggests a perfect relationship between the independent and dependent variables. Clearly, the presence of an anonymous group support system increased the amount of flaming and buffoonery in the classroom.

## Discussion

THE DATA SUPPORT THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN RISK OF REPRISAL and flaming posited in the model in figure 1. Students in anonymous GSS interactions did engage in more flaming and buffoonery than did students engaging in traditional classroom interaction. If vested interests, hostility levels, and the distribution of personal values were similar across groups, then the anonymous GSS environment did reduce risk of reprisals, which in turn did lead to more incidents of flaming.

## Field Cases

INSTRUCTORS CONTEMPLATING THE USE OF GSS TO SUPPORT their classes often express concern about flaming. This section presents five cases that illustrate successful strategies used for reducing or eliminating flaming in the electronic classroom.

## Grammar School, Washington, DC

For four months, a class of twenty-two sixth graders used a GSS for several hours a week to produce a book that expressed the aspirations and challenges they expected to face growing up in Washington, DC. They spent a great deal of effort exploring history to learn how others had achieved their aspirations. Throughout the project, their teamwork was excellent and there were no instances of flaming. However, when the instructor started the next project by asking the students to draft an essay on the value of studying history, he was shocked at the stream of invective comments the students heaped into the system. How could such hard-working and dedicated learners suddenly become so disruptive? The instructor asked the students if they had started a project they did not care about and the students responded with an emphatic yes.

Changing directions, the instructor then asked the students if they knew how the major systems of the human body worked together. They replied that they did not. He asked them if they would like to figure it out, and make a videotape to explain it to other children their age. The children embraced the project enthusiastically and went online again with no further incidents of flaming. This case provides a simple example of an important lesson we have learned from using GSS in dozens of classrooms at all grade levels: If students are convinced they have a vested interest in the outcome of a task, they will actively participate in the process of completing that task, and flaming will not be an issue. By engaging them in a task that more closely aligns with their vested interests, hostility is reduced, which in turn reduces flaming.

Junior High School, Washington, DC

An instructor at a junior high school in Washington, DC, elected to use an anonymous group support system to allow eighteen students to engage in classroom discussions addressing the effects of pollution in and around a local river. Flaming erupted during the first online session, causing the instructor to stop using the technology temporarily. In order to discourage flaming, the instructor required the students to log into the system with the last four digits of their student identification numbers. The system then tagged each contribution with these four numbers, making students anonymous to each other but identifiable to the instructor. The instructor warned that inappropriate behavior online would have negative consequences. No instances of flaming occurred thereafter. Buffoonery occurred only at the end of sessions, which the instructor found acceptable. By tagging the student comments with the students' private identification numbers, the instructor raised the risk of reprisal, which reduced flaming. In this way, he maintained his social influence over the students and was able to reprimand any disruptive communication.

## High School, Tucson, Arizona

A high school in Tucson, Arizona, had been plagued by racial tension that had erupted into a series of violent incidents. An English instructor in an American literature class with thirty-eight eleventh-grade students asked them to use an anonymous electronic brainstorming tool to answer the question, "How do we increase school spirit at our high school?" The students immediately began to enter expletives, racial and sexist epithets, and abusive references to specific students. The teacher discontinued the session, fearing a renewed outbreak of violence.

The teacher was unwilling to expose her students or herself to such indignity but was reluctant to abandon the benefits of the technology completely. She laid out her concerns for the students and used an electronic voting tool to ask whether they would be willing to restrain themselves in order to continue using the computers. Two-thirds agreed, but one-third refused to stop entering antagonistic remarks. In a subsequent anonymous online discussion, some students cited freedom of speech issues as their reason for refusing to refrain from flaming. Interestingly, no incidents of flaming occurred during that particular anonymous discussion.

The instructor did not want to sacrifice anonymity. She believed that the learners were much more willing to think and engage if they could do so without risk of reprisals from their peers. In her traditional, oral classroom setting, if a student responded to one of her questions, other students often jeered, saying “School-boy!” or “teacher’s pet!”

To overcome the flaming problem, the instructor adopted an anonymous social control protocol for her class. She told them that their online work constituted a group utterance, not an individual utterance. She acknowledged their right to say anything they wanted, but also granted them the right to delete anything that offended them. She further offered them private electronic spaces where they could create individual utterances containing any language they wanted, so their freedom of speech would not be abridged. The students expressed concern that people might delete the work of others because of personal malice. The instructor pointed out that the contributions would be anonymous so nobody would know whose work was whose, so personal spite should not be a factor. She also explained that if people were more interested in deleting one another's work than in completing the task, she had probably not chosen an inappropriate task and that they would move on to something more worthwhile. The students agreed that the anonymous social control protocol was fair and continued to work online. There were very few subsequent incidents of flaming, and those few that did occur disappeared almost immediately. The learners did not delete on-task comments.

In this case the instructor may have tapped into two key elements. First, without violating anonymity, she may have raised the perceived risk of reprisal since any effort spent on flaming would be ignored and deleted. Second, by assuring the students they had the power and permission to bring boring or irrelevant online activities to a halt, she may have reduced their perception that their vested interests were about to be violated, thereby reducing hostility and flaming.

In the next class period, the instructor attempted to increase the perception among her students that they had a vested interest in reading American literature. She pointed out to the students that one purpose of literature was to make a statement about something important. She asked the students to address the question, “What is important to you?” The students first brainstormed and then converged to a shortened list of twenty-three concepts that coincided with many of the great themes of literature such as love, friendship, wealth, and power. The learners used the GSS to prioritize the list, and the instructor then told them all subsequent classwork would relate to concepts on the list. No incidents of flaming occurred in any of the subsequent online discussions. At the end of the year the reading and writing scores of the students were 25 percent higher than those of students in nine other eleventh-grade English classes, including two other classes taught by the same instructor using traditional classroom methods [3].

This case is of particular interest because the instructor's approach tapped into a number of aspects of the flaming model simultaneously. She raised risk of reprisal, assured the learners that their interests would be accommodated, and persuaded them that their tasks were closely aligned with their interests.

## College Undergraduates, University of Arizona

The instructor of an undergraduate MIS class at the University of Arizona wanted to increase class participation and expose his twenty-five students to new technology. The instructor knew that, as systems analysts, the learners might someday have to design information systems that could accommodate the needs of handicapped users. He took the class to a special technology-based classroom and showed them a short film on input/output devices for the physically challenged. He asked them to use a group support system to address the following question: "What are the challenges and needs of handicapped individuals?" The instructor had intended to use the results to kick off a discussion of the waterfall model of systems analysis and design. He was appalled by what followed. While some students made excellent on-task comments, others produced many obscenity-laced, sexually insulting references to disabled people. The instructor attempted to narrow the scope of the discussion by asking the students to address several more specific questions. The flaming continued, and the instructor abandoned the technology for the rest of the day.

The instructor had expected the students to find the questions about disabled users interesting, but when he discussed the topic with them afterwards, he realized he had not made it clear to the students why they should be interested in the topic. The next week, he created a new task addressing intellectual property rights and software piracy. Because many of the students were considering careers as programmers, he suspected they might find the topic important. The task placed students in the role of a newly hired IT professional consultant who was being pressured by a large client to install nonlicensed software on a network. The students then went online anonymously and began their discussions. No flaming occurred. In postsession interviews, the students confirmed that they found the discussion interesting and important. They said it was “more realistic.” Said one, “I could see myself having to deal with that in the future.” Another student said that “this was really interesting, I hadn’t thought about it before but I can see these being difficult issues, I mean really, what would you do?” This case serves as another example of eliminating flaming by selecting an appropriate task.

## Junior High School, Tucson, Arizona

A class of twenty-three junior high students in Tucson, Arizona, used an electronic classroom to investigate how they would have to prepare for their intended careers. They fully engaged in their task but interspersed with their on-task contributions were childish personal insults and profanity. The instructor asked the class if they wanted to stop the project, but they insisted it was very interesting and continued their work, punctuating the event with occasional giggles when off-color comments appeared on the screen. The instructor guessed that the learners might have been testing whether the system was truly anonymous, and perhaps playing with their new-found freedom. He decided to simply ignore the problem for a while. The incidents tapered off quickly.

In this case, the instructor suspected that the incidents were not truly flaming, rather they were instances of buffoonery masquerading as flaming, intended to amuse rather than offend. Although the off-color remarks ceased, other forms of buffoonery occurred at the end of every online session. The instructor took this as a sign that the learners had run out of useful ideas and that it was time to move to the next part of the process.

This case represents another lesson learned through repeated field work: That is, students will often engage in buffoonery during the first use of a GSS. This initial buffoonery is sometimes vulgar and can be quite alarming to the instructor. However, students often like to test the anonymity of the system by simply seeing if they can really get away with saying anything they like. It is important in these instances for instructors not to misinterpret vulgar buffoonery as hostile flaming. As is often the case, buffoonery subsides greatly after the initial usage of the GSS.

## Implications for Practitioners

THE EXPERIMENT AND CASES WE HAVE PRESENTED SUGGEST THAT ANONYMOUS electronic classroom discussion may at times be accompanied with flaming and that such behavior may be reduced or eliminated by applying the causal model presented in figure 1.

It may also be useful to keep in mind the distinction between flaming and buffoonery. The presence of flaming typically indicates that something is wrong. Students either do not perceive an interest in the work, or hostility from some other source is spilling over into the classroom. Flaming can often be reduced by switching to a task with a clear vested interest or making greater efforts to convince the students they have a stake in the task at hand. Another approach is to raise the risk of reprisal by reducing or eliminating anonymity.

Unlike flaming, buffoonery may occur without any malicious intention or hostility. Further data analysis supported the supposition that learners may engage in some buffoonery as a reaction to the novelty of anonymous interactions. In the experiment, 58 percent (Table 1) of the instances of buffoonery occurred during the first of eight electronic discussions (figure 2).

There is also some support for the notion that participants may engage in buffoonery when they run out of useful ideas. More than 55 percent (Table 1) of the buffoonery took place during the last five minutes of the discussions (figure 3).

It is important for the practitioner to expect flaming and buffoonery in electronic classroom discussions, and to decide ahead of time how to respond when either arises. The strategies presented in the case studies may be of use.

## Conclusion

THE RESULTS OF THE EXPERIMENT AND CASES PRESENTED HERE SUGGEST that the model presented in figure 1 is a useful representation of the causes of flaming. The model may be useful for practitioners seeking to reduce or eliminate flaming from their electronic classrooms and for researchers wishing to explore the flaming phenomenon. However, the experiment in this paper examines only the moderating role of risk of reprisal. It does not test any of the other relationships in the model. Much more experimentation will be required before the model can be accepted as robust. There may well be other causal and mediating factors that are not included in the model. Further field research will be required to uncover these other phenomena. Finally, this model examines only the causes of flaming. It does not investigate the role that flaming and buffoonery play in group processes, group cohesiveness, or group productivity.
