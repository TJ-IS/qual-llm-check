# Information Exchange and Use in GSS and Verbal Group Decision Making: Effects of Minority Influence

ALAN R. DENNIS, KELLY M. HILMER, AND NOLAN J. TAYLOR

ALAN R. DENNIS is an Associate Professor of Management in the Terry College of Business at the University of Georgia. He received a Bachelor of Computer Science from Acadia University, an M.B.A. from Queen's University, and a Ph.D. in management information systems from the University of Arizona. His current research interests include group brainstorming and decision making, and the design of web-based technologies to support collaborative work. His past research has appeared in journals such as Academy of Management Journal, MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Management Science, Communications of the ACM, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, and Journal of Applied Psychology. His most recent book is Business Data Communications and Networking, with Jerry FitzGerald.

KELLY M. HILMER is a Ph.D. student in MIS at the University of Georgia. For the past two years, she has been involved in various research concerning group support systems, the analytic hierarchy process, and data mining. Her dissertation examines distance learning, specifically, the fit among learning methods, learning tasks, and technology.

NOLAN J. TAYLOR is a Ph.D. student in management information systems in the Terry College of Business at the University of Georgia. He holds a B.S. in electrical engineering from the University of Alabama and an M.B.A. from California State University, San Bernardino. His research interests include decision support systems, cognitive engineering, and human-computer interaction.

ABSTRACT: This study investigated the effects of GSS use on the exchange and use of information with and without a majority/minority split of opinion in the group. When there was a distinct majority/minority, groups exchanged more information, made better decisions, and took no more time when they used a GSS than when they did not use a GSS. In this case, the GSS enabled the minority to overcome the group's inertia toward the majority preference. In the uniform treatment where there was no majority preference, groups exchanged more information but made worse decisions and took more time when they used a GSS than when they did not. The primary cause for the poor performance when using the GSS was that group members did not process and use information received during discussion.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: group support systems, hidden profile.

INDIVIDUAL MANAGERS SELDOM HAVE ACCESS TO ALL RELEVANT INFORMATION, so when truly important decisions have to be made, a group is typically formed to make the decision or to advise the individual who must make the decision [15]. Groups should make better decisions because, collectively, groups have access to a larger pool of information than any of their members acting alone [19, 40, 49], have greater ability to detect errors than any single member [15, 19, 40], and can potentially achieve performance that exceeds the performance of the group's most competent member [40].

However, information exchange in group decision making is often done poorly; a lot of unique information known to some group members is never shared with the group [42]. Individuals, particularly low-status participants, may withhold information out of apprehension about the group's reaction to it or may feel pressured to conform to the views of the group majority [13, 15, 25]. This unique information can be important, leading to poor decisions when it is not considered [14, 43].

Information technologies such as group support systems (GSS) have the potential fundamentally to change the nature of information exchange in group discussion $[12, 34]$ . This study investigated the effects of GSS use on the exchange and use of information in group decision making under two conditions: when there was and when there was not a majority/minority split of opinion in the group.

## Information in Group Decision Making

## Information Distribution

IN DECISION-MAKING SITUATIONS, GROUP MEMBERS TYPICALLY know a host of information about possible alternatives. This information shapes members' prediscussion preferences and, as members communicate information during discussion, shapes the group's decision. The information that members have can be distributed in a variety of ways. It can be known to all members before group discussion ("common"), it can be known to only one before group discussion ("unique"), or it can be known to some but not all participants before discussion ("partially shared").

Unique information can be distributed so that it is unbiased; that is, each group member has a balanced set of information that indicates the optimal alternative. In this case, the group should reach the same decision with or without the unique information. However, information can be distributed so that the optimal alternative is hidden; that is, the common information indicates a nonoptimal alternative, but if the group were to pool the unique information, they would be able to discover the optimal alternative. Stasser [43] calls this situation a “hidden profile task.” In this case, if discussion does not raise all available but unique information, the group likely will make a poor decision. Here, the value of unique information is high, but empirical studies have found that much of it is not exchanged [46, 47]. Groups often fail to discover hidden profiles and make poor decisions despite admonishments to consider all information [51], forewarning about the existence of unshared (unique) information [44], or explicit identification of unique information [41].

We focus here on two conditions of information distribution in hidden profile tasks. One well-studied condition is when a majority/minority exists in the group $[33]$ . In this case, a majority of group members favor one alternative (a suboptimal one) and a minority favor a second alternative (the optimal one). Another, less-studied, condition is when no majority/minority exists in the group; members are evenly divided among the alternatives in their opinions. We call this a “uniform” condition.

## Information Exchange and Use

In order to reach a group decision, members engage in three activities simultaneously: information recall (either from memory or notes), information exchange (either giving or receiving information), and information processing (actually using the information: assessing the cognitive and social implications of the information and storing it in memory) [3, 4]. Humans have a limited amount of cognitive resources to spread across these three activities [1]. Most people can engage in only one activity at one time, so that engaging in any activity limits the ability to engage in the others. For example, actively listening to discussion blocks the processing of newly received information and the recall of related information [25]. We consider each in turn (see figure 1).

## Information Recall

A number of factors affect the accuracy and completeness of information recall. At the individual level, information recall is affected by individual preferences, amount of information, and familiarity with the information $[18, 47]$ . Cognitive limitations make recalling larger information sets in their entirety more difficult, reducing the likelihood that any one item will be recalled $[45]$ . Environmental factors may also affect recall. Task familiarity from past experience with the task or task-related information increases the likelihood of recall $[18]$ .

Information recall of the group as a whole is biased toward common information, owing to the laws of probability. Common information known by all members is more likely to be remembered by at least one individual than is unique information known only to one member $[47]$ . Thus, groups are more likely to focus discussions on common rather than unique information, because common information is more likely to be recalled. Yet, in a hidden profile task, it is the unique information that is critical to success; without adequate consideration of the unique information, group members will miss the optimal alternative.

## Information Exchange

The exchange of information in verbal discussion is not perfect. Members must first decide to contribute the information and then have the opportunity to contribute it. Individuals are often motivated to defend or support their initial preference, so the information they choose to contribute often favors their preferences or challenges an alternative $[8, 46]$ . Information contradicting the preferences of the majority is also less likely to be exchanged $[15]$ , because members may fear a negative evaluation of their comments (“evaluation apprehension” $[13, 25]$ ).

In verbal discussion, only one group member can speak at one time, so all other members are blocked from contributing. This “production blocking” significantly reduces the exchange of information because members who are prevented from contributing information as they choose often forget or suppress it because it seems less relevant or less important later in the discussion $[13, 25]$ .

In a situation where a group majority favors one alternative, the tendency to support one's own choice will result in a majority of members contributing information that supports the majority choice. Likewise, the tendency not to contribute information contradicting the group majority (e.g., information challenging it, or favoring a competing alternative) may also result in incomplete information exchange. The result may be a very biased exchange of information that favors the majority alternative, further reinforcing its apparent superiority.

## Information Processing and Use

Once information is exchanged, members must actually process it before it can affect decisions. Petty and Cacioppo [36] argue that there are two “routes” by which information is processed. The first is the “central route,” in which members actively assess the information and integrate it into their overall understanding of the situation and their preferences (also called persuasive arguments or information influence). Information influence theory argues that unique information not previously known should be more persuasive than information already known and considered [32, 54]. However, empirical evidence has found the opposite: Unique information is more likely to be ignored, while common information known to all members prior to discussion has a greater influence on the group's decision [47]. There are at least two plausible reasons. First, during verbal discussions, members must constantly pay attention to other members or miss their contributions, and therefore the unique information first received during discussion is processed only superficially and not considered as fully as information studied before discussion. Second, information that supports prediscussion choices is more thoroughly processed and integrated into an individual's cognitive schema [36], while information challenging initial decisions is discounted [26, 58].

The processing of information is also affected by the presence of a majority opinion within the group. When faced by a group majority whose preferences are different from theirs, members assume the majority to be correct and focus on comparing their preferences with those of the majority. “The world of alternatives is reduced to two” [33, p. 25]. This stimulates a comparison process of convergent thought, of viewing the issues from the majority’s preference [31].

In contrast, when faced by a minority with a different preference, members are motivated to assume that minority members are incorrect and initially dismiss them [27, 33]. The minority position may stem from the use of different criteria or from the possession of unique information. Discussion therefore focuses on the relative importance of different criteria and the information that individual group members have. In order to change one's opinion (under this central route of information influence), individuals must first be convinced of the validity of the unique information or the different weighting of criteria before integrating them into their decision. In essence, the decision process becomes a series of smaller decisions on which information items will be accepted.

There are a number of obstacles to group acceptance of this unique information. Information that lacks demonstrability (i.e., whether an information item can be proven to be factual rather than opinion) is less likely to be accepted [35]. Information may also be rejected because of perceptions about members' characteristics such as low social status [21] or expertise [50, 57].

However, if the minority is consistent in presenting information and opinions, and does not yield to majority pressure, members realize that the minority is convinced of its position. They make attempts to understand it—a validation process of divergent thought $[31]$ . Information is reexamined from many perspectives, not just that of the minority, to understand why it has led to different conclusions $[27, 33]$ . In this case, information from minority members is processed more thoroughly than information from the majority because minority members are perceived as more independent or more dissimilar from one another than are members of the majority $[17]$ .

The second route by which information is processed is the “peripheral route,” in which members' preferences are shaped more by peripheral cues such the number of people arguing for a position or their attractiveness, rather than the quality of the information itself (also called social comparison or normative influence). Normative influence theory argues that members are motivated to conform to the opinions of others to preserve a favorable self-perception and self-presentation [32]. After obtaining information about others' preferences, members may change their preference to match others more closely, through either choice or coercion [15]. Also, publicly stating a preference may make that preference stronger; public commitment may make changing preferences more difficult without losing face [38] and may cause stronger internalization to reduce cognitive dissonance [32].

In a majority/minority situation, normative influence is usually quite strong [27]. Members may not actually change their preference but just state a more socially acceptable preference than their true preference. They may simply comply with the majority preference to avoid further stress, without examining any information [27].

## GSS Effects

GSS provide new opportunities for information exchange that may differ in important ways from non-GSS environments. One of these is electronic communication that can augment or replace verbal communication. This electronic communication provides a package of many different components, each of which may introduce new dynamics into the information exchange process. Three components in particular should affect information exchange and use: parallelism, group memory, and anonymity $[34]$ .

Parallelism is the ability of all group members to enter information at the same time. All group members have computer workstations, which enable them to contribute information and opinions by typing ideas, which are immediately shared with all other members. Since all members can type at the same time, no participant need wait for others to finish before contributing information. This parallelism mitigates the blocking that inhibits the exchange of information in verbally interacting groups $[53]$ .

The second component, group memory, means that all remarks typed into the computer are stored so that group members can refer to them later in the discussion. One of the key problems in verbal discussion is that listening to group discussion blocks processing of new information, and information processing blocks the reception of new information from discussion. A group memory enables members to enter or read information at their discretion; they can more easily pause to recall and process information without risk of missing information $[8]$ .

The third component is anonymity; group members can make contributions without attaching their names, which may motivate them to participate differently. Anonymity may reduce the reluctance to contribute information that contradicts the dominant group preference $[34]$ . Minorities often express their arguments more frequently and persistently when they communicate anonymously through GSS than when they communicate verbally or with a non-anonymous GSS $[29]$ .

## Effects on Information Exchange

GSS use may increase the amount of information exchanged because it provides parallelism (which reduces blocking) and anonymity (which may reduce the reluctance to contribute information challenging one's own preference or that of the majority). Previous empirical research presents a mixed set of conclusions. Studies of three- and four-member groups have found GSS groups to exchange less information than verbally interacting groups $[18, 21, 22, 51]$ . Another study of four-member groups found that members of the minority contributed more information when using an anonymous GSS than when discussing the issues verbally $[29]$ . In a study of six-member groups, GSS use neither increased nor decreased information exchange compared to face-to-face groups $[9]$ , while a study of ten-member groups found GSS use to increase information exchange $[8]$ .

To some extent, these results may be attributed to the need to type. Typing is slower than speaking $[56]$ and may require more effort. Therefore, in smaller groups, which experience less production blocking and evaluation apprehension, GSS use impairs performance $[10]$ . In larger groups, where production blocking and evaluation apprehension are more significant problems, GSS improves information exchange. These factors are likely to affect common and unique information equally. In this study, we focus on larger groups. Therefore:

H1a: Members of GSS groups will exchange more common information and more unique information than members of non-GSS groups.

In a majority/minority situation, there is more pressure on minority members to accede to the majority, encouraging less discussion of information; minority members may simply agree with the majority without much discussion because swaying the majority will be more difficult than swaying the few members required in the uniform situation. The shield provided by anonymity should be more strongly felt when there is a majority/minority within the group. Minority members who oppose the preference favored by the group majority will be more likely to oppose the majority viewpoint when they use a GSS. Because they will present their opinions more strongly and are more likely to persist in their opinions, they are likely to exchange more information. McLeod [29] found that members of the minority contributed more information when using an anonymous GSS than when discussing the issues verbally or using a non-anonymous GSS. Therefore:

H1b: The difference in information exchange between GSS and non-GSS groups will be the greatest in majority/minority groups.

## Information Processing and Use

GSS should enable group members to more thoroughly process information because it provides a group memory that enables members to review information at will $[37]$ . Previous GSS research has found that GSS use in general increases the use of factual information rather than members' preferences; that is, it increases information influence rather than normative influence [6]. However, GSS use appears to reduce the processing of information for hidden profile tasks [8, 9, 18]. Information contributed via GSS also appears to have less influence than information contributed verbally [21, 29]. One possible reason for these effects may be that information in the GSS is buried in pools of text and is more difficult to find [8]. In this study, we used the GSS in such a way that most information remained visible on the screen at the same time (see the Methods section below). We anticipated that this would overcome the problems encountered with information processing in previous research. Therefore:

H2a: Members of GSS groups are more likely to consider the information exchanged in making their decisions than are members of non-GSS groups.

The effects on information processing will be different in majority/minority groups and uniform groups. Minority members are more likely to accede to the majority without the equal opportunities provided by parallelism and the shield of anonymity. In GSS groups, minority members are likely to present their opinions more strongly and persist longer $[29]$ , and therefore are more likely to trigger the intensive information processing described by Moscovici $[31]$ . Therefore:

H2b: The difference in information use between GSS and non-GSS groups will be the greatest in majority/minority groups.

## Outcomes

The discussion and use of more information should increase the time required to reach a decision compared with non-GSS groups, unless the GSS enables groups to contribute and process information faster. There is evidence that GSS use takes longer, in part because GSS require participants to type, which is slower than talking $[2, 10, 56]$ . GSS groups have consistently taken longer than non-GSS groups for hidden profile tasks $[8, 9, 21]$ . Therefore:

## H3a: GSS groups will take longer to reach a decision than non-GSS groups.

In a majority/minority situation, there is more pressure on the minority members to accede to the majority, encouraging less discussion and less consideration of information. However, the equal time provided by parallelism and the shield of anonymity will encourage minority members to present their opinions more strongly and to persist in their opinions $[29]$ , requiring more time. Therefore:

H3b: The difference in time taken between GSS and non-GSS groups will be the greatest in majority/minority groups.

In certain situations, such as hidden profile tasks, greater use of unique information will enable groups to better identify the optimal alternative. Anonymity may also reduce the pressure to conform to others' preferences, and reduce the loss of face in backing down from previous positions, making it easier for members to change their preferences. Previous research with hidden profile tasks has found that GSS use impaired or had no effect on decision quality $[8, 9, 21, 29, 51]$ . We attribute these effects to a lack of information processing. Because we believed GSS use would improve information processing in this study, we hypothesized:

H4a: When information distribution is biased, members of GSS groups will make better decisions than members of non-GSS groups.

In a majority/minority situation, the minority members are more likely to accede to the group majority. The use of a GSS (with parallelism and anonymity) encourages more thorough exchange and use of the available information. Therefore:

H4b: The difference in decision quality between GSS and non-GSS groups will be the greatest in majority/minority groups.

## Method

## Subjects

ONE HUNDRED AND SEVENTY SOPHOMORE, JUNIOR, AND SENIOR business students drawn from a core business course participated as members of 17 ten-person groups (8 majority/minority task groups; 9 uniform task groups). The average age was 21; 59 percent were male.

## Independent Variables

This study used a $2 \times 2$ repeated measures design, crossing GSS use with the decision situation (majority/minority or uniform). Each group was assigned into either the majority/minority or the uniform condition and performed two tasks, one using a GSS and one not using a GSS. The order of the two tasks and the GSS and non-GSS treatments was reversed for half the groups.

In the non-GSS treatment, subjects interacted verbally. In the GSS treatment, subjects used TCBWorks, a GSS developed at the University of Georgia $[11]$ that provided electronic communication with parallelism, anonymity, and group memory. The software allowed subjects to exchange information with other group members. TCBWorks provides an outline structure and enables users to enter comments on pages “underneath” each topic (in much the same way as GroupSystems $[34]$ ). For this study, subjects were not permitted to enter comments underneath each topic; all comments appeared as topics so that they could be seen easily. Subjects were trained on the GSS before starting the task requiring its use.

The majority/minority task was designed so that seven subjects received a subset of information favoring the same suboptimal alternative, while three received a subset of information favoring the optimal alternative. In the uniform task, three subjects received a subset of information favoring the optimal alternative, four received a subset of information favoring a suboptimal alternative, and three received information favoring a different suboptimal alternative. We discarded any session in which the tasks did not produce the intended majority/minority (defined as at least five members favoring the suboptimal alternative and no more than three favoring the optimal) or a uniform split (defined as no more than four members favoring a suboptimal choice and two to four favoring the optimal choice) for both tasks. We studied 29 groups, but only 17 met these criteria and were used in the study; the other 12 groups were discarded (i.e., the intended task split failed in one of the two tasks for 12 groups—a failure rate of 12 in 58 tasks or 21 percent).

## Tasks

There were two tasks. Subjects performed both tasks, one in the GSS condition and one in the non-GSS condition. Subjects were informed at the start of each task that they each had only a subset of all available information and would have to share their information to make a good decision. Both tasks underwent six to ten rounds of redesign and pilot testing.

One task asked subjects to select one student from a set of four students for admission to the university. The complete information set included positive, negative, or neutral information on eighteen criteria for each alternative for a total of seventy-two pieces of information. Five pieces of information for all four alternatives (verbal SAT. math SAT, GPA, sex, major) were “common”—that is, given to all subjects (see Table 1). The remaining information was “unique,” given to only one subject. On average, each subject had five pieces of unique information plus the twenty pieces of common information (i.e., 5 criteria × 4 alternatives = 20 pieces of information).

This task was designed with the assistance of the university admissions office. The complete information version of the task was independently validated by three admissions officers (the director of admissions and two associate directors) to ensure that the alternative designed to be optimal was optimal. The complete information version was also validated by twenty-eight subjects drawn from the same subject pool but who did not participate in the study. Twenty-two (79 percent) chose the optimal choice; five (19 percent) chose next best alternative; one (2 percent) chose the third best alternative; no one chose the fourth alternative.

The second task asked subjects to select one computer from a set of four computers to be the “standard” computer required of all College of Business students. The complete information set included positive, negative, or neutral information on eighteen criteria for each alternative for a total of seventy-two pieces of information. Four pieces of information for all four alternatives (price, CPU, RAM, hard drive size) were “common” (Table 1). The remaining information was “unique.” On average, each subject had six pieces of unique information plus the sixteen pieces of common information (i.e., 4 criteria × 4 alternatives = 16 pieces of information).

This task was designed in conjunction with the college's computer lab staff. The chair of the college's computer users' committee, the college's computer service manager, and one faculty member independently validated the task to ensure that the alternative designed to be optimal was optimal. The complete information version of the task was validated by twenty-one subjects drawn from the same subject pool but who did not participate in the study. Sixteen (76 percent) chose the optimal choice; four (19 percent) chose the second best alternative; one (5 percent) chose the third best alternative; no one chose the fourth alternative.

## Procedures

The procedures followed those of Dennis [8]. Subjects first read the task and made an individual decision. The task information was removed and the subjects worked together as a group (using either a GSS or no GSS) to arrive at a unanimous group decision or until twenty-five minutes had elapsed. Subjects in the non-GSS treatment discussed the task verbally. Subjects in the GSS treatment were instructed to use the GSS to exchange information until their first vote; after the first vote they were instructed that they could use the GSS and/or discuss the issues verbally. Once the group reached a unanimous decision or twenty-five minutes elapsed, subjects individually completed two postsession questionnaires that asked them to make another individual decision (the same as or different from the group decision) and to report several perceptions. After a five-minute break, these steps were repeated for the second treatment.

## Measures

Data were analyzed at the group level or individual level depending upon the measure (e.g., time was measured at the group level, while satisfaction was recorded at the individual level). Group-level measures were analyzed using a repeated measures ANOVA with treatment order as a blocking factor. Individual-level measures were analyzed in the same manner, except that a group-nested-within-treatment term was also included because responses of subjects within the same group may be correlated to some extent (see [8]).

## Information Exchange

All sessions were videotaped and GSS transcripts recorded. The video camera failed during two sessions; these two sessions were included in the study, but all measures requiring use of the videotape were lost. The amount of information exchanged was measured by two raters, who counted the pieces of correct information in the group discussions (videotapes for non-GSS treatments; printed computer records and videotapes for GSS treatments). A set of six randomly selected groups (three uniform, three majority/minority) was selected to determine interrater reliability. The raters agreed on 0.94 of the ratings (measured as 1 – [number of disagreements/information exchanged]), indicating adequate agreement.

Subjects' statements also can be classified as exerting information influence (e.g., providing facts) or as exerting normative influence (e.g., stating a preference for one alternative). Statements were coded as exerting information influence or normative influence using the classification scheme of Kaplan and Miller [24] and Huang et al. [23]. For each group, we then calculated the proportion of statements expressing normative influence which was used as a measure of the degree of normative influence present in the group's discussion.

## Information Processing and Use

Determining information processing and use was done in two ways. First, a perceptual measure was used. The postsession questionnaire included two scales designed to measure perceived information usage (adapted from [8]): "I-thought" (i.e., the extent to which the respondent thought about the information contributed by others; 3 items,

Cronbach alpha = 0.84, 1 = low, 7 = high), and “others-thought” (i.e., the extent to which the respondent believed other group members thought about the information contributed by the respondent; 3 items, alpha = 0.85, 1 = low, 7 = high). While perceptions of information usage can be unreliable, they are one way of triangulating on this concept [8, 36].

Second, information use was considered by examining the information recalled by each group member at the end of the group discussions (see $[5, 8]$ ). Subjects were asked to list all information they remembered in a free recall question on the postsession questionnaire. Two raters coded the questionnaires and counted the number of correct pieces of information provided. Interrater agreement was again adequate (0.93).

The information recalled was categorized as either information known to the subject prior to group discussion or information unknown prior to discussion that had been learned from the discussion. Each category was analyzed separately. In order to be recalled, information must have been processed as a prerequisite to being stored in memory $[5, 8, 47]$ . Information learned must have been processed during discussion, while information known in advance may have been processed either in advance or during discussions.

One additional perceptual measure was also included on the postsession questionnaire. Information credibility, the degree of accuracy that subjects attributed to information contributed by others, was measured using Dennis's [8] four-item scale. Alpha was 0.82.

After the subjects completed the postsession questionnaire, they were given a second postsession questionnaire that asked them to rate the importance of the eighteen criteria present on a 1–7 scale (1 = not important; 7 = very important). This information was used for the analyses described below.

## Outcome Measures

Decision time was measured by the number of minutes required for the group to reach a decision. Decision quality was measured by the number of groups making the optimal decision. ## Information Processing and Use

We used two measures of information use. The first was perceptual data. Participants perceived of themselves as having thought about information to a greater extent when not using the GSS. There were no significant differences in the others-thought measure.

The second measure was the recall of information known prior to discussion and information unknown prior to discussion that was learned. There was a significant interaction effect for previously known information. Participants recalled the most previously known information when using the GSS in the majority/minority treatment and the least when using the GSS in the uniform treatment. Participants learned more previously unknown information in the non-GSS treatment and in the majority/minority treatment. Participants also reported less credibility in the information when using the GSS than when interacting verbally.

These measures present a mixed picture. One measure (others-think) is inconclusive. Two measures (I-think, information learned) suggest that participants were less likely to process information when using the GSS. ## Additional Analyses

The results show rather striking—and unexpected—differences between GSS use and no GSS use. This was a repeated measures study in which all groups made one decision with the GSS and one decision without the GSS, so these performance differences are due to changes in behavior of the same individuals. In the majority/minority treatment, five of eight groups made the optimal decision when using the GSS, but only two of these same eight groups made the optimal decision without the GSS. The reverse was true for the uniform treatment: Only one of nine groups made the optimal decision when using the GSS, while seven of these same nine groups made the optimal decision without the GSS. In short, GSS use significantly helped majority/minority groups but significantly hurt the uniform groups.

## Explanation of Analyses

To better understand what happened in each group, we conducted two additional analyses. The first was a qualitative analysis in which we attempted to gain a holistic understanding of the process differences among the different treatments. The second was a more quantitative analysis of the specific information exchange and use processes of the groups. Figure 2 shows a more detailed illustration of the processes that occurred in these groups (shown as boxes) and a series of measures (shown as circles) that can help pinpoint the breakdowns in each group's decision-making process.

In order to reach a decision, individual participants take the complete information set and contribute some subset of it to the group discussion. Likewise, participants develop a set of criteria they will use, which is usually a function of the correct decision criteria, undervaluing the importance of some criteria and overvaluing others. Individual participants attend to information exchanged and process it into their individual decision frameworks to form their individual preferences (information influence). Individuals also attend to the preferences of others and may change their actual or espoused preferences to match those of the group majority (normative influence).

These individual preferences are combined to form the group's decision.

Barring serendipity, groups must be successful in all of these processes (or at least most of them) to identify the optimal alternative: They must exchange information appropriately, must use appropriate criteria, must process and use the information and criteria, and must not be overly swayed by inappropriate normative influence. It is possible to develop measures to determine the extent to which each group successfully performed these processes.

We can determine whether a group has exchanged adequate information by applying the correct criteria for the task to the information the group has exchanged. $^{1}$ If this results in the optimal alternative, then the information exchange process was adequate. If the results are inconclusive or favor a suboptimal alternative, then information exchange was not adequate. Likewise, if we apply the group's criteria to the complete information, and this results in the optimal alternative, then the group's criteria were adequate. If the results are inconclusive or favor a suboptimal alternative, then the criteria the group used were not adequate.

One measure of information processing and use is possible by comparing the group's decision to the decision that the group should have made by applying their criteria to the information they exchanged (the “predicted” decision). If the predicted decision is the same as the actual decision, then the group likely processed the available information properly. However, if there is a mismatch between the predicted decision and the actual decision, then the group failed to process the information adequately, or normative influence dominated information influence.

## Uniform Groups

The opposite pattern was present in the uniform groups. When using the GSS, not one of the eight groups analyzed picked the optimal alternative (one of the nine groups in this treatment was omitted because of the videotape failure). Five groups failed to process the information adequately (their predicted decision was the optimal), while the other three had incorrect or inconclusive predicted decisions. All eight groups exerted high normative influence.

When not using the GSS, six of these same groups picked the optimal alternative. The remaining two groups either had poor or inconclusive processing and high normative influence.

The qualitative analyses again suggest that the groups used very different processes when using the GSS than when not using it. Without the GSS, most groups immediately began an information search to identify what information each person had. This typically began in a round-robin fashion but was often sidetracked when someone mentioned an important piece of information. This new information would trigger a recognition of the importance of the criterion and spawn a search for this type of information for the other alternatives (e.g., “High school quality is important. Anyone else have information on this?”). The discussion tended to focus on important criteria and probe for information.

With the GSS, this probing for information was not possible because information arrived as participants typed it, not in response to queries. While we saw attempts at this search behavior (e.g., “What was this person’s major?”) there were few immediate responses. Likewise, attempts to limit the scope of discussions (e.g., “[x] is gone, agreed?”) often went unanswered because there was no way to judge the extent of agreement to the proposal. The result was that a lot more information was entered about all three alternatives (or even all four). Much of the information typed into the GSS was “filler.” For example, “[x] is the most well rounded candidate,” “The one word that describes this man: slack,” and “[x] is obviously best at what she wants.” These comments added little factual information to the discussion to sway opinions. Focusing on the important information was therefore difficult. When participants found it difficult to influence others, they resorted to normative influence, which did not help matters (e.g., “Ruth, Ruth, Ruth, Go Team.”).

## Discussion

## Majority/Minority Groups

IN GROUPS THAT HAD A PREDISCUSSION MAJORITY FAVORING A SUBOPTIMAL alternative, verbal interaction without a GSS led to poor decisions: Six of the eight groups chose the sub-optimal alternative favored by the group majority. In general, the principal problem experienced by these groups was a poor exchange and processing of unique information key to the optimal decision. The majority overwhelmed the minority. The final decision depended more on the biased information members received before the meeting than on the unique information exchanged during the meeting.

For these same groups, the use of GSS added considerable value. When using the GSS, these groups exchanged more unique information and almost uniformly made the optimal decision in no more time. In the two cases in which the groups made incorrect decisions (see the GSS groups 6 and 7 in Table 4), the groups either did not pay attention to the information or did not exchange enough information, while normative influence dominated.

Without the GSS, the majority view was more likely to overwhelm the minority. The minority did not exchange their unique information, leading groups to favor their prediscussion choice. With the GSS, the minority was able to induce the majority to listen to the information presented and to convince them to change their minds to the optimal alternative. When using the GSS, all group members were able to discuss on the same playing field; that is, members of the minority were able to exchange their ideas and information and were listened to by the majority.

## Uniform Groups

The situation was quite different in the uniform treatment. Without the GSS, the groups quickly shared the critical unique information, thought about it, and identified the optimal decision (one group did so in less than two minutes). When using the GSS, members exchanged more information but appeared to think about it less, resorted to normative influence, took a longer time, and made a suboptimal decision or could not agree on a unanimous decision.

In short, in the uniform treatment, GSS use impaired the groups' decision-making process. The most common problem in these groups was a lack of information processing (see the GSS groups 8–12 in Table 4).

## Lack of Information Processing

Our results suggest that many groups did not adequately process the available information—those in the majority/minority treatment when they did not use the GSS, and those in the uniform treatment when they did. It has been argued that group performance is achieved in spite of, rather than as a result of, communication and its associated process losses $[49]$ .

Davis [7] suggests that group performance is largely a function of the quality of the members' prediscussion preferences. This was true of most of the groups in the majority/minority treatment when they did not use the GSS. The majority view dominated and the minority was unable—in most cases—to present its information convincingly enough to change the majority view. In Salazar's terms [39], the majority/minority groups experienced strong inertia toward the majority preference. This inertia inhibited information processing; members of the majority assumed the minority was incorrect and did not engage in information search [33, 39]. Without the GSS, the majority was unable to present enough disruptive obstacles (i.e., counterarguments) to deflect the inertia and trigger information processing among members of the majority (see [27, 33]). With the GSS, the minority raised enough obstacles to break the inertia and cause the majority to rethink its opinions; the majority considered the information favoring the alternate choice proposed by the minority and in most cases found it persuasive.

The uniform treatment presented a different situation. With no dominant majority, group members could not rest on their prediscussion choices; there was a lack of inertia toward one solution. This ambiguity in direction triggered an information search behavior $[39]$ . Without the GSS, the groups were very successful in this search. However, GSS use inhibited information processing in more than half of the groups and inhibited information exchange in the rest; all groups resorted to high levels of normative influence in an attempt to reach closure. The reasons for this are unclear. We offer four plausible interpretations for future research: information integration, information credibility, lack of focus, and coordination.

## Information Integration

One possibility may lie in group members' abilities to integrate newly received, unique information into their existing base of information. During discussion, group members must engage in three simultaneous activities (information recall, information exchange, and information use), and because people have limited cognitive resources, engaging in any one activity limits the ability to engage in the other two [3]. Factual information requires more cognitive processing than information about others' preferences (36), so when cognitive resources are limited, normative influence may dominate information influence.

Streufert and Swezey [52] argue that, if factual information arrives faster than one item every three minutes, people have difficulty integrating it with existing information. While we do not believe the volume of information in these studies was very high [47], it clearly required the processing of more than one piece of information every three minutes. In the uniform treatment, there were three competing alternatives (versus two in the majority/minority treatment), and no minority advocating its opinions to focus the analysis on just two alternatives. There was simply more information to consider, much of it unimportant.

When using the GSS in the uniform treatment, members generated about twice as much information as when not using it. Therefore, one plausible explanation for the lack of information processing may lie in the cognitive limitations of individuals' ability to integrate information. Participants may have suffered from information overload and an inability to focus their analysis on the dominant alternatives to a greater extent when using the GSS and therefore failed to process the information it contained as thoroughly as information received in the verbal discussions. By limiting the amount of information available, the production blocking in the non-GSS groups may actually have helped by filtering the available information $[20]$ .

Under this interpretation, there are two implications for future research. First, we need research varying the information load to determine if GSS use helps or hinders in situations of high and low information load across three or more dominant alternatives. Second, we need to better understand what group processes to use and how to structure the information in the GSS to minimize overload and enable better integration of information. These processes and structures need to reduce the amount of unimportant information the group considers and focus attention on important information.

## Information Credibility

Another possibility for the reduced processing of unique information in GSS groups may lie in the credibility of the information. To bring about a change in a previous decision (in this case, the individual decision made prior to group discussion), information must be important, novel (i.e., not previously processed), and credible [30]. In this case, the unique information received during discussion was both important and novel. However, because the information was unique, only one participant had it and could not “prove” its accuracy (unlike common information, which can be corroborated by others). Each time unique information was introduced into the discussion, participants had to determine what information to accept as fact and what to disregard [35]. The questionnaire results show that participants were less likely to find unique information credible when using the GSS. Thus, participants may have intentionally disregarded the information in the GSS. There are at least two plausible explanations for this reduced credibility.

The first is a negative anonymity effect on credibility. The credibility of the source of information can be important to the acceptance and processing of a message, particularly when the information is ambiguous or difficult to process [36]. It may be that the anonymity of the GSS used in this study made the information more suspect, because it was more difficult to verify the source's credibility. Anonymity also facilitated lying. Anyone could invent information at will to support his or her position and never have to suffer the consequences. While we have no evidence of increased lying, the mere ability to lie may have reduced credibility, in the same manner that the ability to check information increases credibility [36].

A second reason for reduced credibility may be a reduced ability to seek confirmation from the information's contributor. The ability to confirm a particular fact or set of facts has been shown to increase the amount of information discussed. Experiments in which participants retained the task during group discussion (e.g., [22]) or were merely able to access task sheets upon request (e.g., [36]) increased the amount of both common and unique information discussed. In most non-GSS groups, we observed at least one (usually several) occasion when unique information presented to the group was immediately challenged (e.g., "Really? I didn't get that."). The contributor would then confirm the validity of the information and discussion would move on. Similar challenges were seen in GSS groups, but the response would come much later or not at all. It may be that the reduced ability to provide immediate confirming feedback reduces the credibility of the information.

Under this interpretation, we need additional research to determine if anonymity is useful in this situation. Anonymity has been found to be important for groups whose members have different power and status but to have fewer benefits for peers $[34]$ . The experimental procedures used in this study may also have promoted this effect. As is traditional in hidden profile studies, we removed the task information sheets from the subjects prior to group discussion. Permitting subjects to keep the task sheets may reduce anonymity effect on credibility (see $[35]$ ).

## Information Focus

A third possibility may be the ease of ignoring the information because its presentation is unfocused. When a person speaks verbally and contributes information, the group's attention focuses on that information [17]—it can be difficult to ignore a person speaking right in front of you. In contrast, when using a GSS, information is contained in lines of text in a pool of information. Without a GSS, information is presented to you; with a GSS, you must actively peruse the group memory. When using the GSS, participants may not have actively searched out and reviewed all available information.

Under this interpretation, we need research to discover how GSS can better engage participants and encourage them actively to seek and use the information it contains (i.e., a GSS like a video game that draws participants in).

## Coordination

A final possibility may be a reduced ability to coordinate discussion. In both GSS and non-GSS groups, a lack of information about who has what information makes it difficult to coordinate effort. This coordination often occurs without any explicit discussion of task strategies $[16, 48]$ . Metaknowledge of who has what information has been shown to improve decision quality $[43, 50]$ . Group members form expectations about others' likely contributions to the group's task $[55]$ , but the GSS may hinder the ability to identify important information rapidly or to identify “experts” within the group (i.e., possessors of important unique information).

Under this interpretation, we need research to produce ways to identify expertise within the group. We also need ways to coordinate the group's information search activities so that members can easily identify what information is needed.

## Limitations

This study suffers from the usual limitations of laboratory experiments (see $[28]$ ). We studied ad-hoc groups of undergraduate students with little intrinsic motivation and little prior GSS or business experience. The tasks were comparatively simple in that they required less than thirty minutes of work. Our subjects might have been unfamiliar with the tasks, although we explicitly chose these tasks because we believed the subjects were familiar with the items under discussion. Therefore, additional basic evidence is needed to support or refute the conclusion of increased information exchange in GSS groups and reduced information processing with GSS in uniform groups.

## Implications for Practice

This study has several implications for managers striving to understand and improve the exchange and use of information in organizational problem solving. For groups that had a majority/minority, GSS use improved the exchange of information and the quality of the decision. However, for groups without a majority/minority, GSS use impaired the quality of decision making. One implication is that GSS might only be used when there are strong differences of opinion within a group, where the GSS's ability to focus the group on the information and break the group's inertia toward the majority viewpoint will be valuable.

We believe that the key problem for GSS use in the uniform treatment (where there was not a strong majority/minority in the group) was the groups' inability or unwillingness to process new information received during group discussion into their existing information base. While we await research on this, a second implication is therefore that group meetings might be structured to provide sufficient time for members to study, assess, and integrate this new information before they must make decisions. Separating information exchange from the decision making that uses it is one option. Members could hold one meeting to discuss a problem or opportunity, break for several hours or days to evaluate and integrate the information, and hold a second meeting to make the actual decision. This structure is similar to the “incubation” period recommended for idea generation.

In our study important information may have gotten lost in a sea of information. A simple technique would be for the group actively to identify the important criteria (either before or during the meeting) and intentionally highlight any information about those factors by moving it to the top of the lists of information for each alternative. Conversely, adding a little more structure to the topics in the GSS that explicitly require groups to identify important information might also improve performance.

## Lessons Learned

THE RESULTS OF THIS STUDY ARE SIMULTANEOUSLY ENCOURAGING and discouraging for developers and users of GSS in organizations. First, the results suggest that GSS is a useful tool in overcoming the biased decision making that often occurs when there is a majority within a group. The GSS enabled the group minority to deflect the group's inertia toward the majority viewpoint, something the minority was unable to do without the GSS.

Without the dominant group majority, the situation was very different. The groups engaged in information search, rather than attempting to force the minority to comply with the majority viewpoint. In this case, the GSS impaired the groups' ability to identify and focus quickly on the important information. The result was that group members did not process important information and incorporate it in their decisions.
