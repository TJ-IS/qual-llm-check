# Anonymity in Group Support Systems Research: A New Conceptualization, Measure, and Contingency Framework

ALAIN PINSONNEAULT AND NELSON HEPPEL

ALAIN PINSONNEAULT is an Associate Professor at Ecole des Hautes Etudes Commerciales de Montréal. He holds a Ph.D. in administration from the University of California at Irvine and an M.Sc. in management information systems from Ecole des Hautes Etudes Commerciales de Montréal. His current research interests include the organizational and individual impacts of information technology, the strategic alignment of information technology, and group support systems. He has published articles in Management Science, Journal of Management Information Systems, MIS Quarterly, Decision Support Systems, and European Journal of Operational Research. His doctoral dissertation won the International Center for Information Technology and MCI Communications Doctoral Award.

NELSON HEPPEL was a student in management information systems in the Master in Administrative Sciences at Ecole des Hautes Etudes Commerciales of Montreal.

ABSTRACT: Anonymity is a fundamental concept in group support systems (GSS) research. It is expected to reduce fear of social disapproval and of evaluation, and to lower inhibition and censorship. This is believed to create an environment that improves participation and communication, that promotes more objective and honest evaluation of ideas, and that enhances the productivity of groups and their decision-making process. However, empirical evidence about what are the effects of anonymity in GSS is inconclusive. This paper argues that there are two main reasons for the lack of consistent findings. First is the fact that anonymity has been defined too narrowly as the nonidentification of participants when, in fact, the literature in social psychology suggests that anonymity is multidimensional and subjective in nature. Second is the fact that the effects of anonymity interacts with other situational variables, making its relation to disinhibition complex. This paper argues that one critical situational variable is the importance of social evaluation of group members. A measure of the new conceptualization of anonymity and of the importance of social evaluation is proposed and the results of a preliminary test are presented. A contingency approach for studying the effects of anonymity is presented and the implications for future research and for practice are discussed.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: anonymity, computer-based collaborative work, group decision making, group support systems.

GROUP WORK IS INCREASINGLY IMPORTANT IN ORGANIZATIONS. Managers spend more than half of their work time in meetings $[21]$ , and most important decisions are made in groups $[19]$ . Yet, group work is often inefficient and unproductive, suffering from a lack of participation, social or production blocking, self- or socially imposed censorship, and political games $[21, 36]$ . By allowing anonymous communications, GSS are expected to reduce inhibition and evaluation apprehension, enhance participation, increase the number of ideas generated, and improve the quality of decisions $[39]$ . However, empirical evidence is unclear about what are the effects of anonymity in computer-based interactions $[12, 22, 41]$ . For example, anonymity was found to increase criticalness $[6, 23, 28, 49]$ , to have no effects on inhibition $[17, 20, 29]$ , on group communications $[46]$ , and on group performance $[17, 23, 49]$ , and, surprisingly, it was also found to lead to more conservative decisions in a conservative organizational context $[20]$ .

Some explanations can be advanced for this lack of convergence. The effects of anonymity might depend on some contextual and group factors such as the importance of power and status differences between group members $[39]$ ; the degree of evaluation apprehension and conformance pressures of groups $[39]$ ; the presence or absence of a leader in a group $[17, 25]$ , or the evaluative tone of group communications $[6]$ . Others argued that the effects of anonymity depend on the setting in which it is studied (i.e., laboratory or field) $[53]$ . The key commonality of these explanations is that the concept of anonymity seems much more complex than conceptualized in earlier studies. In addition, the literature in social psychology indicates that disinhibition depends, among other things, on both the subjective evaluation of the degree of anonymity offered and the importance of social evaluation of group members. The very concept of anonymity needs to be reconceptualized $[30, 39]$ . This paper attempts a theoretical explanation of the state of empirical findings by providing a new conceptualization and measure of anonymity and by analyzing its impacts in the broader context of disinhibition. It first presents the empirical evidence concerning the effects of anonymity in GSS and then analyzes the empirical evidence by drawing on research in social psychology. Finally, we propose a new conceptualization and measures of anonymity and present a contingency approach for studying its effects on groups. We conclude by presenting the results of preliminary tests of the measure and by discussing the study limitations and the implications of the new conceptualization for research and practice.

## Concept and Empirical Evidence

ANONYMITY IS DEFINED IN GSS RESEARCH AS THE INABILITY of group members to identify the origin of messages they receive and the destination of messages they send [17, 49, 50, 53]. It is conceptualized as a dichotomous and objective factor: It is either present or absent. Others have refined the concept of anonymity by distinguishing between process anonymity (the extent to which group members can determine whether or not another member is participating) and content anonymity (the extent to which group members can identify the source of a particular contribution to the group) [49].

It is commonly argued that by divorcing statements from their authors anonymity offers a low-threat environment, reduces evaluation apprehension, and breaks down social barriers and conformance pressures, which disinhibits behaviors, promotes more equal participation by group members, and leads to the generation of more ideas $[6, 17, 20, 23, 24, 25, 28, 40, 46, 53]$ . Anonymity also promotes more objective and honest evaluation of ideas and should improve the quality of decision and group performance $[6]$ . We think that the process gains described above are often countered by some process losses generated by anonymous communications. By reducing social comparison, social pressure to accomplish the task, and accountability, anonymity may induce social loafing and free riding $[20, 50]$ . In addition, anonymity might lead some group members to be overly critical and caustic $[22, 46]$ . The net effect of anonymity is the combination of the process gains and process losses inherent to anonymous communications. Hence, depending on the context in which anonymity is used, the process gains might offset the process losses, but the reverse might also be true in other contexts.

Table 1 presents the empirical studies on the effects of anonymity on groups. It indicates that, overall, anonymity has had limited impacts in settings with different technologies, different tasks, and subjects with different motivations. When it had an effect, it was not monolithic, but, rather, seemed to vary from one group to another. Anonymity was found both to have no effects on inhibition and to increase criticalness [6, 17, 20, 23, 25]. Anonymity was also found to increase the number of comments generated [6, 16, 23, 53], but Shepherd and colleagues [45] found this to be true only when social comparison of productivity is provided to group members. Furthermore, anonymity was found to have no significant effects on several variables where important effects were expected, such as the amount and equality of participation [17, 20, 25], consensus reaching and disagreement [17, 20], and quality of ideas or decisions [6, 17]. Anonymous groups were not more satisfied [53], except when dispersed [25] or with an assigned leader [17]. Anonymous groups generated more clarification efforts than identified ones [23, 25]. Anonymity was also found to have no effects on the number of ideas generated for sensitive and for non-sensitive topics [40], and to decrease the number of unique ideas generated by established groups working on nonsensitive topics [40]. Finally, surprisingly, anonymity was found to induce more conservative decision making within the context of a conservative corporate culture [20].

Several explanations have been offered to explain the state of inconclusive empirical evidence. First, the effects of anonymity might depend on contextual variables such as the importance of power and status differences in groups [39]. For example, anonymity was found to interact with other variables, such as leadership [17, 22], proximity [25], and evaluative tone [6]. Second, there might be lower evaluation

Anonymity and Disinhibition in Social Psychology:

Why Does Disinhibition Not Always Occur?

DISINHIBITION IS CAUSED BY TWO MAIN SUBJECTIVE STATES (public and private self-awareness), each of which has its own antecedent conditions (accountability and attentional cues, respectively) [42]. Public self-awareness involves attention to oneself as a social object and concerns appearance and the impressions made in social situations [42]. It reflects the importance given to being evaluated and judged by others and to social evaluation in general [1, 13, 15]. Lowering public self-awareness reduces concerns with social standards, conformity, and social evaluation. Not expecting retaliation and censure, individuals become disinhibited [10, 11, 55]. In these situations, people are aware of their actions but they do not expect to suffer any negative consequences for their actions since disinhibited behaviors will go unpunished. Public self-awareness is determined by accountability cues, or the extent to which people think they will be held accountable for their actions. These are made of group size, similarity of group members or knowledge of others [11], the presence of recording material and the number of observers in the room [4, 11, 14], diffused responsibility [31, 42, 43, 52, 55], and anonymity [42, 43].

All these factors have been found to affect public self-awareness and disinhibition. But anonymity was found to interact with other situational variables in unpredictable ways, making its effects quite varied and contradictory and its relationship with disinhibition very complex. In social psychology, anonymity was found to increase uninhibited behaviors $[27, 51]$ , but also to have no effects on inhibition $[11]$ , or even to decrease uninhibited behaviors $[55]$ . Diener $[11]$ argues that one possible explanation of these contradictory effects is that anonymity manipulations in experiments might convey the message to participants that the actions they are about to perform are undesirable and thus might heighten self-awareness. Also, anonymity was found to interact with other situational factors, such as group unity, in quite unpredictable ways. Third, the literature in social psychology indicates that anonymity will disinhibit behaviors only if threats of punishments is a major source of inhibition and if the condition is perceived by individuals as an effective method for protecting themselves from these threats. This might explain why anonymity has had limited effects on disinhibition in experiments with students working on fictitious cases. They were liberated from constraints that were not a major source of inhibition. Anonymity might increase or decrease inhibition when inhibition originates from internal standards (strong values, for example), depending on whether anonymity heightens or lessens self-awareness [11]. Then, the finding of Hiltz, Turoff, and Johnson [20] that anonymity increased conservativeness in a conservative organizational culture could be explained by the fact that individuals working in such a culture are more conservative by nature and that anonymity heightened self-awareness and conservativeness. Fourth, the concept of anonymity is multidimensional. It can be actively sought or imposed and there are different methods for becoming anonymous (costume, non-identification, pen name in written communications). Fifth, and most important, anonymity is much more than lack of identification. It is a condition that frees individuals from social evaluation or scrutiny [52]. There is an important subjective dimension to it, and it is the perception of whether the offered condition adequately frees individuals from social evaluation that really makes people feel anonymous and behave accordingly.

Disinhibition is also caused by private self-awareness, which refers to a focus on personal aspects of oneself, like perceptions, thoughts, and feelings $[42]$ . Lessening private self-awareness leads to deindividuation, that is, a group of individuals being transformed into a united entity that seem to develop a collective mind. People become immersed in a group rather than focusing on self-regulation $[42]$ . Diener $[11]$ argues that when private self-awareness is decreased, people are not as aware of their internal processes, standards, and values and are less able to retrieve them to compare and adjust their behavior. Group members thus regulate their behaviors based on group norms and standards rather than on interval values, self-regulation, and behavioral constraints. When deindividuated, behaviors depend on other emotional, motivational, and situational factors (like group norms) present at the time $[11]$ . Thus, loss of private self-awareness might lead to disinhibition of behaviors, when, for example, group norms and standards favor its emergence, or it might also lead to more conservative, prosocial, and altruistic behaviors. Private self-awareness is determined by attentional cues (fostering group cohesiveness and arousal, and focusing attention away from oneself), but not by accountability cues (of which anonymity is part). Thus, according to Prentice-Dunn and Rogers $[42]$ , anonymity will not be associated with deindividuation. Also, it is rather unlikely that deindividuation occurs in ad-hoc groups, unless there is a period of time before the experiment during which group members could become a cohesive group $[11]$ .

Non-anonymous computer-based communications have been found to heighten private self-awareness and to lessen public self-awareness $[33, 34, 35]$ . Sproull and Kiesler $[48]$ found that electronic mail attenuated social context cues, which substantially deregulated communications and favored the transmission of information that would not have been conveyed through another medium. Although the messages were clearly identified, the feeling of anonymity led to more uninhibited behaviors, such as flaming. Consequently, it may be argued that the marginal impact of anonymity in computer-based interactions is very limited because computer-based communications already lessen public self-awareness and diminish social context cues.

In sum, the following observations can be made based on research in social psychology. First, there are numerous factors leading to disinhibition, and anonymity is only one of them. Hence, its expected effect on disinhibition is likely to be marginal.

Also, social context cues are already attenuated and behaviors uninhibited by non-anonymous computer-based communications, leaving little to be gained from anonymity. Furthermore, anonymity only disinhibits behaviors when the major source of inhibition is fear of threats. Anonymity will have no significant effecta when inhibition is caused by internal personal standards or values (related to private self-awareness). Second, anonymity has been found to interact with other situational factors, making its relation to disinhibition quite unpredictable and complex. Depending on different factors, anonymity could increase or decrease inhibition. This heightens the importance of measuring and controlling such factors as private self-awareness, group unity, group size, presence of observers, and so forth. Finally, the very concept of anonymity is multidimensional, continuous, and subjective in nature. It is how individuals perceive the anonymous condition to protect themselves from threats that will lead to disinhibition of behaviors.

Sorting through these explanations remains difficult because most empirical research in computer-based interactions has focused on anonymity alone as a single cause of disinhibition. In the next section, we develop a new conceptualization of anonymity and a contingency framework based on research in social psychology.

Anonymity and Disinhibition in GSS Revisited

## The Anonymity Concept Revisited

RESEARCH IN SOCIAL PSYCHOLOGY INDICATES THAT ANONYMITY is the degree to which individuals feel liberated from social evaluation and from threats of punishment. Liberation from social evaluation is a multidimensional concept made of five components. First is the lack of identification associated with actions, comments, ideas, and so forth [11, 42, 43, 52]. This is an experimental condition and is not part of our perceptual measure of anonymity. People are either identified or nonidentified. The second component is diffused responsibility [31, 52, 55]. As discussed in the preceding section, empirical evidence in social psychology indicates that, when responsibility of behaviors is felt to be diffused to all members of the group, individuals feel more anonymous [11, 32, 55]. The third component of anonymity is proximity [11]. Proximity is especially important in the context of computer-based communications because it can greatly influence the degree to which people feel they are being observed or not by group members or by observers. For example, if people are seated too close to one another, they might feel people can read what they write on their screen or look at their keyboard, and thus identify their comments. Although there are no names attached to comments, anonymity is lessened in such a situation. The fourth component is knowledge of other group members [11]. Knowledge of other group members directly affects the feeling of being observed and the suspicion that others could recognize comments by their style, content, and wording [52]. Group size is related to this variable, because people feel it is more difficult to identify their comments in larger groups [11]. The fifth component of anonymity, the confidence

## Anonymity and Disinhibition: A Contingency Approach

Empirical evidence on the effects of anonymity in GSS is quite contradictory. Reconceptualizing anonymity as a degree of liberation from social evaluation, threats of punishment, and retaliation, and as a subjective, continuous, and multidimensional concept might shed more light on this phenomenon and how it relates to disinhibition. However, research in social psychology also indicates that public self-awareness comes into play as an intervening variable between anonymity and disinhibition. This is illustrated by the $2 \times 2$ framework presented in figure 1.

Anonymity can only significantly affect disinhibition, and other behaviors in general, when social evaluation is an important source of inhibition (public self-awareness) (high importance of social evaluation in figure 1) [11]. Liberating people from social evaluation when it is not an important source of inhibition is likely to have very little effect on disinhibition (low importance of social evaluation in figure 1). Hence, a given level of anonymity will have different effects on disinhibition depending on how important social evaluation is as a source of inhibition for individuals (public self-awareness). A high level of anonymity is likely to have limited effects on disinhibition for people with a low level of public self-awareness (for whom social evaluation is not an important source of inhibition) (high anonymity and low importance of social evaluation quadrant in figure 1). Conversely, it is likely to produce high disinhibition for people with a high level of public self-awareness (for whom social evaluation is a very important source of inhibition) (high anonymity and high social evaluation in figure 1). A low level of anonymity is likely to have no significant effects on disinhibition when public self-awareness is low (low-low quadrant in figure 1) and minimal effects when it is high because people will only feel partly liberated from their inhibiting factor (low anonymity and high social evaluation in figure 2).

It is important to note that, when public self-awareness is high, a correspondingly high level of anonymity will be required in order to liberate people from social evaluation. That is, when people fear social evaluation, disinhibition will occur only when they feel fully protected from it by anonymity. Conversely, a lower level of anonymity will be required to disinhibit behavior when fear of social evaluation is low because people will require less protection against something they do not fear as much (yet, as discussed above, the amount of potential disinhibition is limited because public self-awareness is not a major source of inhibition).

In sum, the literature in social psychology leads to expect that the effects of anonymity on disinhibition largely depend on public self-awareness. Although anonymity might interact with other situational factors, public self-awareness is likely to be an important variable to help better understand the complex relation between anonymity and disinhibition.

Measures of Anonymity and Public Self-Awareness:

A Preliminary Test

## Measures

TWO CONCEPTS ARE IMPORTANT IN THE STUDY OF THE EFFECTS of anonymity on groups: anonymity and public self-awareness. Recall that anonymity is made of five components (identification, diffused responsibility, proximity, knowledge of other group members, and confidence in the system). Identification, an experimental condition, is not included in the proposed perceptual measure of anonymity. Some work has been done in social psychology on anonymity, but there is no measure that could be applied in a GSS context. Table 2 presents the measures that were developed based on the conceptualization of anonymity proposed earlier and on the literature in social psychology [26, 32, 38, 44, 47].

Public self-awareness and public self-consciousness (a related concept) have been studied quite extensively in social psychology and scales have been developed and tested $[2, 3, 14, 18]$ . The two concepts are similar, except that public self-consciousness refers to a general awareness of the self as a social object, and public self-awareness refers to a state, that is, an awareness of the self as a social object as a result of transient situational variables $[14]$ . Measures of public self-awareness in social psychology are simply measures of public self-consciousness applied in a given context. Similarly, the measure proposed here is the application of the public self-consciousness measure to the specific context of computer-based interactions. Fenigstein, Scheier, and Buss $[14]$ were among the first to proposed a public self-consciousness scale. Their measure consisted of the following seven items:

"One of the last things I do before I leave my house is look in the mirror."

"I'm concerned about my style of doing things."

"I'm concerned about the way I present myself."

"I'm self-conscious about the way I look."

"I'm concerned about what other people think of me."

"I usually worry about making a good impression."

Each item was rated on a scale of 0 (extremely uncharacteristic) to 4 (extremely characteristic). Feningstein et al. [14] tested their scale with 432 students and retested it with 150 college undergraduates. Principal components analysis yielded strong factor loadings (all above 0.45), and had a test–retest reliability of 0.84. Burnkrant and Page [3] tested the same scale in two studies using confirmatory factor analysis performed by LISREL V. In the first study, 360 women answered the questionnaire and the confirmatory factor analysis yielded a chi-square goodness to fit measure of 37.50, p = 0.00, indicating a poor fit with the data. Two items, “One of the last things I do before I leave the house is look in the mirror” and “I’m usually aware of my appearance,” had very low reliabilities (less than 0.20) and were dropped from the scale. One hundred and ninety-eight college students participated in the second study, in which the reduced scale produced a chi-square of 7.74, p = 0.17, which is an acceptable fit. Gould [18] tested the five-item scale of Burnkrant and Page [3] with 169 adults in a large northeastern metropolitan area. Confirmatory factor analysis (LISREL VI) indicated that all items loaded on the construct with a score of over 0.558.

We adapted the five item public self-consciousness scale. Table 2 presents the five-item public self-awareness scale.

## Method

A preliminary test of the measures was conducted to assess construct validity and to identify ambiguous items. In order to achieve this, a method proposed by Davis [8, 9] and by Moore and Benbasat [37] was used. Fifteen graduate students in management were asked to sort the various items into categories that represented the constructs of interest. Each item presented in Table 2 was printed on one 4×6-inch index card randomly numbered from one to twenty-nine. Each respondent was provided with a set of 29 cards randomly shuffled, clear instructions describing the sorting procedure (with precise examples), and six cards indicating the grouping categories and the definition of the variables (“Diffused responsibility,” “Proximity,” “Knowledge of other group members,” “Confidence in the system,” “Public self-awareness,” “Ambiguous/Does not fit”). The “Ambiguous/Does not fit” category was provided to allow the respondents to disregard items they estimated did not belong to any particular category or that were confused. This prevented them from force-fitting items into categories. A sorting trial, unrelated to the present study, was performed prior to the actual sorting to insure they understood the procedure. The respondents were encouraged to ask as many questions they wanted to make sure they clearly understood the procedure. Then, they were asked to independently place items in categories that best reflected the underlying construct.

An item consistently classified in the same category by the different respondents is considered to indicate convergent validity with the related construct and discriminant validity with the other constructs [37]. Three indicators were used to assess the reliability of the scales. The first indicator is the number of items that were placed by the panel of respondents in the “appropriate” theoretical construct (Table 3). Each item was designed to measure a particular theoretical construct; this indicator assesses the frequency with which items were assigned to the intended construct. The higher the percentage of “appropriate” placements, the higher the degree of inter-respondent agreement. Moore and Benbasat [37] suggest that a high degree of items being appropriately placed in a category also indicates high construct validity. Second, the level of agreement was estimated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient [5]. An assessment was made by comparing the categorization of all pairs of respondents. Although there are no established guidelines for Kappa, scores greater than 0.65 have been considered acceptable in recent studies [37]. The third indicator we used is the frequency of “correct” placements for each item (Table 4). This is the percentage of time each item was placed in the correct category. This indicator can be used to determine whether an item is ambiguous or not.

## Discussion

## Scholarly and Practical Implications

THE NEW CONCEPTUALIZATION OF ANONYMITY PROPOSED IN THIS PAPER (as a subjective, multidimensional, and continuous construct: the degree of liberation from social evaluation) has several implications for research. First, because anonymity is subjective and context-dependent, its measurement has to be taken as soon as possible after the anonymous condition. For example, in the context of a GSS experiment with multiple tasks, this entails measuring anonymity after every anonymous task. Second, numerous factors affect the degree of anonymity (e.g., proximity, screen dispositions, etc.), and consequently greater care will be required to assure a sufficient degree of anonymity. Third, anonymity has been found to interact with other situational variables in social psychology, such as group unity, and these will have to be measured and controlled. Fourth, any study of anonymity and disinhibition has to take into account the fact that the effects of anonymity occur in a broader context where deindividuation, private self-awareness, accountability cues, and attentional cues are also important antecedents. Furthermore, the literature in social psychology indicates that anonymity does not affect disinhibition directly, but through public self-awareness.

The contingency framework proposed in this article might help better approach these phenomena and better explain their complex relationships. It heightens the importance of being careful when conducting experiments with students because public self-awareness might not be important for them in that context, and, consequently, anonymity might not have important effects. Furthermore, great care must be taken when generalizing results on anonymity from ad hoc groups to established ones.

Members of ad hoc groups are by nature more anonymous than members of established group because they do not know each other as well. In addition, disinhibition has been found in social psychology to be affected by deindividuation (the feeling of being immersed in a group), which is likely to be minimal in ad hoc groups and more pronounced in established ones.

The main implication of the present study for practice is that facilitators must be aware that “real” anonymity is not obtained simply by a lack of identification of the origin and destination of ideas. Lack of identification is only one of the five dimensions of anonymity that lead to disinhibition. When anonymity is required to disinhibit behaviors and improve group performance, a facilitator must make sure that all five dimensions of anonymity converge in that direction. A facilitator should be aware that anonymity entails both process gains and process losses. Group members working anonymously will benefit from a lower-threat environment, but anonymity might also lead to dysfunctional behaviors, such as flaming or being overly critical. The effects of anonymity on groups are the difference between the process gains and the process losses inherent in anonymous communications. A facilitator should be careful when using anonymity to minimize the process losses. Finally, the literature in social psychology indicates that anonymity will lead to disinhibition only when fear of social evaluation is an important source of inhibition. Consequently, some groups might not react at all to an anonymous condition. In fact, Pinsonneault and colleagues [40] found that, when working on a non-socially sensitive or noncontroversial topic (i.e., one that generates a low level of anxiety and apprehension when one expresses ideas), established groups generated more comments when working non-anonymously than when working anonymously. This result is consistent with Constant, Sproull, and Kiesler [7] who found that computer networks (non-anonymous) facilitated social observation of technical information exchanges, which encouraged people to contribute for personal benefits.

## Study Limitations and Future Research

The proposed conceptualization and scales of anonymity need to be further tested and refined. The preliminary tests indicate that construct validity is acceptable, but reliability and external validity should be further assessed. Heterogeneous samples should be sought to ensure generability. Also, the overall anonymity measure is relatively long; a shorter version of the scale would facilitate its usage in experiments and in real-world settings. In addition, the measure of anonymity was developed for a particular setting (i.e., text-based and same-time, same-place communications) and needs to be tested and, maybe, adapted to other group work settings.

The contingency framework proposed in this paper also needs to be tested empirically. Laboratory and field experiments should be conducted to determine the impact of anonymity on group processes and outcomes as reconceptualized here. In addition, more research is needed to determine the interaction between anonymity and public self-awareness. Also, the effects of anonymity in ad-hoc and established groups should be studied.

The bottom-line implication of this article is an alternative way of studying anonymity and understanding how it affects group work. Anonymity is a subjective and very complex construct that interacts with other situational variables in quite unpredictable ways. To understand anonymity better, more attention ought to be given to these situational variables, one of the most important of which is the importance of social evaluation (or public self-awareness).
