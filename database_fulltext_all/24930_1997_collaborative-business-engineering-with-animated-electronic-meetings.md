# Collaborative Business Engineering with Animated Electronic Meetings

GERT-JAN DE VREEDE

GERT-JAN DE VREEDE is an Assistant Professor at the School of Systems Engineering, Policy Analysis, and Management of Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands. He received his Ph.D. in systems engineering from the same university, where he is currently responsible for the GSS research program. His research interests include the application of collaborative technologies to facilitate organizational design activities, and the adoption and diffusion of GSS both in Western environments and in developing countries. His articles have appeared in Journal of Creativity and Innovation Management, Holland Management Review, and Journal of Simulation Practice and Theory.

ABSTRACT: In an action research study at the Amsterdam Municipal Police Force, a collaborative business engineering approach was employed to explore the joint application of group support systems and animation techniques to support stakeholder involvement in organizational change processes. Stakeholders constructed and evaluated static and dynamic models of the police force's current and future work processes. Findings illustrate the complimentary potential of the two technologies to support stakeholder involvement in terms of efficiency, resulting model quality, and stakeholder satisfaction with process and technologies. The study illustrates the value of an incremental modeling strategy, the involvement of a substantial number of stakeholders, and the need to keep the momentum in the process. Issues for further research concern the development of a collaborative modeling environment that allows for group enabled model construction and viewing and the automatic transformation between different types of models.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: collaborative business engineering, group modeling, group support systems, simulation.

GIVEN THE HIGH RATE OF CHANGE IN TECHNOLOGY AND WORKING environments, many organizations have evolved to the point where their structure, procedures, and technologies are no longer in keeping with the needs of the future [18]. This means that organizations need to study themselves to determine what should be changed. Their components must be reshaped and rearranged to form appropriately coupled new subsystems with greater simplicity and internal cohesiveness than the existing structures. Information systems need to be adapted or developed to meet the requirements of the new structures and processes. Recognition must also be given to relationships between the organization and its external environment.

An example of an organization that found itself in the situation described above is the Criminal Investigations Department (CID) of the Amsterdam Municipal Police Force. The CID has a difficult task of combating organized crime in the capital of the Netherlands. They have limited means (time, money, personnel) to use against too many suspect criminal organizations. In 1994, the CID management decided to reorganize the functional specialized department into a multidisciplinary project-based organization in order to utilize their means more effectively and efficiently. It was unclear, however, (1) how this transition had to take place, (2) how the project teams, so-called units, and their progress had to be managed, and (3) which technologies should be (developed and) employed to support the future organization.

The situation at the CID was an excellent opportunity to apply, evaluate, and further improve a design approach, Collaborative Business Engineering, which focuses on the collaborative analysis and design of organizational structures, processes, and information technology. Earlier studies investigated various methods, techniques, and tools to support organizational change processes. These studies showed the potential of object-oriented modeling, simulation and animation techniques, and group support systems $[14, 33, 34]$ . They also suggested that, during an organizational change process, one of the main issues is interaction:

1. A lot of interaction is required between and among stakeholders from the organization and analysts to exchange and discuss information and ideas with respect to the organization's current situation and various design alternatives.

2. Interaction between the people involved in the process is time consuming and difficult. Each stakeholder finds itself in a complex multi-actor situation where different (personal) goals, different perspectives on the functioning of the organization, different expertise, and hidden agendas exist.

The involvement of stakeholders in the actual shaping of the activities and the processes in which they are participants is often considered a prerequisite for the success of such change processes $[2, 15, 21, 29]$ . Stakeholder involvement means helping stakeholders decide how to act in a complex problem situation. The objective of supporting interaction among stakeholders and interaction with analysts is to elicit commitment for the decisions the group takes regarding problem perceptions and courses of actions for procedural and structural organizational changes $[13]$ .

In the study described here, the issue of interaction is addressed by marrying two techniques that, in separate situations, have been shown to facilitate interaction between and among members of an organization and analysts: GSS and the use of animated simulation models. Hence, the objective in this study was to explore the possibilities of the combined employment of GSS and animation techniques to facilitate organizational change in a collaborative fashion. “Collaborative fashion” means that stakeholders from the organization are actively involved and have a say in the various modeling and design activities during the change process. In Mumford’s terminology, this has both aspects of representative and consensus design $[20]$ .

## Research Approach

THIS STUDY FOLLOWED AN ACTION RESEARCH METHOD. Action research can be defined as “an inquiry into how human beings design and implement action in relation to one another” [1]. It is intended to have the dual outcomes of action (or change) and research (understanding). An action researcher participates or intervenes in the phenomenon studied in order to apply a theory to practice and evaluate its worth [6]. The intervention in the study consisted of organizing and facilitating group sessions, constructing prototypes of models of organizational processes, and building animated simulation models.

Action research can be seen as a subset of case study research $[16]$ . Benbasat et al. $[3]$ describe three criteria to determine whether case study research is appropriate. A case can be made that these three criteria are also applicable for action research. First, there is a need to study the phenomena in its natural setting. Especially with respect to GSS, various sources stress the importance to conduct more studies in a natural setting $[11, 23, 39]$ . A review by Pinsonneault and Kraemer $[24]$ shows that, up until 1988, about two-thirds of all GSS studies have been lab experiments. Of the more recent studies (1989–90), about one-third have been experimental research $[39]$ . Not more than 15 percent of the studies into GSS involve case studies or action research $[23]$ . Moreover, most of the case studies that are reported originate from a limited number of organizations, the most predominant being IBM $[17]$ , the IRS $[12]$ , Texaco $[28]$ , and the Department of Defense $[8]$ . This implies that one may be able to begin theorizing about the application of GSS in real-life settings but there is as yet little substantial evidence of their practical value. In addition, for this study it would be very hard to design a constructed environment—say, with student subjects—that is a valid representation of the complex reality in which this study is performed.

Second, there is an emphasis on interest in the “how” and “why” questions. The goal for this study is to explore the potential of GSS and animation techniques to support a collaborative design approach. Hence, it is more appropriate to explore the process of applying these technologies than to explore the resulting products.

Third, there is a lack of previous studies, and elaborate theoretical understanding. The author knows of no previous studies that have been carried out on the combined application of GSS and animated simulation models in the organizational change arena.

Finally, there is a fourth criterion peculiar to action research: The study involves phenomena that do not (yet) naturally occur $[36]$ . This study brings together two technologies, GSS and animation, that do not occur in an organizational environment. These technologies are specialized and their proper use requires trained experts; therefore many organizations would not have access to them. For this reason, a passive approach was not appropriate for this study.

During the study, both quantitative and qualitative data from various sources were collected to enable a rich representation of the phenomena under investigation and to permit comparison and contrast of the collected data. Quantitative data sources included system logs of each session and questionnaires completed by participants after each session. Qualitative data sources included verbal feedback from the participants before and after each session, open-ended questions on the session questionnaires, and our ongoing observations during each session and during the study as a whole.

## Collaborative Business Engineering for Organizational Change

EXPERIENCE SUGGESTS THAT ORGANIZATIONAL CHANGE IS EASIER SAID than done. It is a complex activity. First, the goals of the people involved may conflict with those of the organization. Second, there are many possible courses of action to change an organization. It is not always clear beforehand which course of action will be most efficient and effective. Third, there are usually many options or alternatives for change. Because these change alternatives are often dangerous or impossible to test in reality, the effects of these changes are seldom known in advance. Finally, apart from “rationale,” there may be other (conflicting) issues—political, cultural, social, and so on—that influence the actual change process. It is therefore not surprising that a considerable number of approaches and supporting tools have been proposed to facilitate organizational change efforts.

## Problem Solving for Organizational Change

Because bringing about organizational change can be considered as a process of solving an ill-structured problem, a problem-solving strategy needs to address the intricate attributes of such a process. Based on literature research and experiences from a large number of field studies, the problem-solving process depicted in figure 1 is proposed. This process consists of several design activities that can be carried out iteratively. First, the problem situation in an organization is conceptualized in order to structure the problem situation so as to focus and minimize the efforts for detailed, low-level data gathering to create the empirical model. Next, a descriptive empirical model is built that can be used to analyze and diagnose the problem situation. For this purpose, the empirical model is, for example, implemented in a simulation language. The third activity seeks solutions and captures them in a number of prescriptive models of possible solutions. These models can be compared in order to study the effects of the alternatives in more detail. The actual choice is made in the next phase. This may involve a combination of possible solutions or leaving the situation as it is. Finally, in order actually to solve the problem situation, the solution has to be implemented.

## Collaboration in Organizational Change Processes

In practice, many people are usually involved in the various design activities described above $[30, 31, 35]$ . First, a team of analysts is responsible for building and analyzing models of current and future organizational processes. Second, stakeholders actually execute the processes. They have to provide the analysts with relevant information on their working situation. Finally, other stakeholders supervise the change process in the organization. Equipped with the results of the analysts, they decide which changes the organization is going to make.

Frequent interaction among these parties is vital for the quality of the design process and its outcome [37]. Stakeholders' opinions and perspectives regarding the functioning of the organization may change during the design process, resulting in changes in organizational requirements with respect to processes and systems. Also, stakeholder participation is thought to enhance the acceptance of eventual changes in their work situation. Furthermore, organizational change processes are usually far too complex for a single person to understand all in their entirety.

The desire for a design process that is as collaborative in nature as possible presents us with a time/completeness paradox. On the one hand, collaborative design efforts need to be time effective. An organization cannot take too long to build models to plan changes if its competitive and financial positions are deteriorating. However, traditional methods for participative modeling are often time-consuming  [30] . They require analysts to move from small group to small group, gathering information, defining terms, resolving differences, trying to make a big picture from all the small fragments. On the other hand, collaborative design efforts pursue completeness. It could be argued that the process should involve as many stakeholders as possible. This would generate more ideas with respect to an organization's current and future situation. Collaborative design enables a more complete picture of an organizational situation. Also, broader participation promotes wider buy-in to the outcomes of a study. Finally, larger groups of stakeholders are able to evaluate ideas more extensively. However, the broader the stakeholder participation, the more time needed to carry out the design process.

Hence, the participative nature of organizational change efforts sets the requirement to use techniques, methods, and automated support that facilitate the collection, organization, and communication of various types of design information. This study investigates the proposition that the application of two technologies together meet the requirements identified above: group support systems and animated simulation models.

## Group Support Systems

Group support systems (GSS) represent information technology designed to enhance the productivity of group meetings and group decision making. Over the years a great deal of research effort has been devoted to the development and application of GSS. As a result of field studies and a large number of experiments, a substantial body of knowledge has emerged about the effects of GSS on group processes and outcomes, especially with respect to GSS used in same-time, same-place settings. Compared with “traditional” group settings, these types of GSS meetings appear to be more efficient and more effective, while the participants are more satisfied with the results and the process $[9, 22]$ . Several attributes have been identified that appear to bring about the added value of GSS meetings over non-GSS meetings, including:

\- Anonymity. By being able to enter ideas, comments, and votes anonymously, silent or shy participants are more encouraged to participate. Group members are hindered from dominating by position or personality. Ideas appear to be judged on their merit, not on the personality of the person who submitted it.

\- Parallelism. By generating ideas and communicating them in parallel, participants get equal “air time.” This prevents production blocking so that participants can spend more time generating new ideas. Furthermore, working in parallel allows groups to generate more ideas. In this respect, some argue that the number of high-quality ideas may be positively correlated to the total number of ideas [5, 27].

\- Group memory. During an electronic meeting, all ideas, comments, and votes are stored electronically. Hence, previous meeting results are readily available in a followup meeting. Little time is needed to produce meeting minutes. Moreover, there is a fundamental difference between the quality and completeness of electronic group memory and that of traditional minutes [7]. First, “traditional” minutes are likely to be more subjective, since they are written by an individual. The electronic group memory gives an objective history. Second, minutes rarely describe participants’ initial positions or interim postures. Group memory depicts the evolution of the group’s position over time. Finally, group memory makes each comment during a meeting public, to which others can refer at a later stage.

\- Group size. Findings suggest that GSS have a greater positive impact on large groups (eight members or more) in terms of productivity and participant satisfaction [8]. In other words, GSS seem to facilitate the effective and efficient participation of a larger number of people in collaborative processes.

## Animated Simulation Models

During the process of problem solving, a number of models of organizational processes are constructed and analyzed. Such models have to meet a number of requirements. First, models have to reflect different abstraction levels, as managers usually look at processes differently than operational staff. Second, models have to depict parallelism, since at least parts of processes are usually executed at the same time. Third, models have to describe dynamics, as the constituent activities of processes are often interdependent, both logically and in terms of time.

For analysts and stakeholders, models of current and future organizational processes are used as a vehicle of communication. Hence, these models must be easy to communicate. This means that a model has to closely resemble the mental model of the person to whom it is communicated [6]. Experience suggests that traditional diagramming techniques such as Entity Relationship Diagrams, Data Flow Diagrams, or IDEF0 models do not meet these requirements [35]:

\- Diagramming techniques may be able to model the dynamics of a situation, but the resulting diagrams are static in themselves. This makes it difficult to understand and analyze the dynamic characteristics of the modeled processes thoroughly.

\- Diagramming techniques are often difficult to communicate to stakeholders. They usually lack the required training and expertise to build and understand the resulting models. Also, diagrams seldom resemble a problem situation visually.

In this respect, simulation techniques appear to be more useful for developing models to analyze process dynamics $[26]$ . However, a simulation model itself can still be considered nothing more than a black box that hides the dynamics of the problem situation. It is difficult to investigate the modeled processes by only going through large sets of input and output data. Using animation techniques on top of simulation models may be able to overcome this problem. An animation model graphically portrays the dynamic behavior of a process by showing icons that visually resemble objects from the real world, moving around a computer screen, changing shape, color, and so on. Because of their “cartoon-like” behavior, animation models have the potential to resemble closely the mental models of both stakeholders and analysts. Animation models support their communication in various ways $[35]$ :

\- For an analyst, the use of animation models may decrease the development time of the simulation model because of the enhanced debugging possibilities. With animation, it may be quicker to find and localize mistakes than by going through output of traces or using a debugger.

\- Visual process information in an animation model is less abstract. Hence, it is easier to communicate to a group of stakeholders and increases their understanding of the organizational processes, allowing them to participate in the design process more actively. As a result, stakeholders may improve models and also appreciate and value their involvement more.

\- During the construction of a descriptive empirical model, using animation offers unique possibilities for face-validity tests. Structural mistakes in the model or deviant model behavior can be pointed out by the stakeholders.

\- In the solution-seeking activity, animation may help point out inadequacies of possible solutions which the stakeholders want to test. With animation, these shortcomings may be presented in such a way that they can be understood and accepted.

\- An animation model can illustrate the statistical results of a process analysis in an accessible way. There is no need for stakeholders to go through large amounts of printed, numerical data.

In the CID study, the general-purpose simulation language Arena was used, which supports both discrete event simulation and continuous simulation. This language can be used in both production and service environments. Apart from modeling and portraying the dynamics of organizational processes, the language offers facilities to design simulation experiments and to analyze numerical results.

## Conceptual Model

The conceptual model of the CID's current situation was built in three steps following an incremental modeling strategy. First, a number of short interviews with some key stakeholders in the CID were held. In these interviews, information was gathered on the relevant processes in the CID. Second, during the first two sessions, a first version (prototype) of the conceptual model was built in TeamGraphics with the assistance of a key CID stakeholder. To this end, the analysts explained the modeling technique $^{1}$ for the conceptual model to the CID stakeholder involved. Then, the TeamGraphics technology was introduced and the CID stakeholder worked with it for a while to get accustomed to it. Next, two subgroups were made, each focusing on a part of the processes that had to be included into the conceptual model. Each pair worked graphically on the same process, reviewing each other's work and making corrections and modifications if necessary. The CID stakeholder reviewed not only the work he did with his own subgroup colleague, but also the work of the other subgroup. At the same time, additional information about the CID processes was gathered using GroupSystems. This information included assumptions made by the modelers, which had to be discussed in the next session with the CID management, and problem indications in the modeled situation. Part of the resulting prototype model is depicted in figure 3.

![](/api/attachments/53ZATA4R/fulltext/images/50418ebc15ca1e07446a7213a088e2d43f13d1c63909981b1337a34665b2a6b5.jpg)  
Figure 3. Part of the Conceptual Model in TeamGraphics

Third, the prototype of the model was presented to a group of CID stakeholders, including the CID management. During this session (session 3 in Table 2), we used TeamGraphics in “facilitation mode” [38]. Participants could browse through the model. They did not, however, have privileges to make modifications themselves. For this purpose, the graduate student operated as a dedicated chauffeur, immediately following up on all participants’ requests for changes in the models.

Regarding the group modeling process supported by TeamGraphics, one can make number of observations. First, the active involvement of the CID employees in the modeling process facilitated the implicit verification of the resulting model. All participants agreed that the final model was a valid representation of the CID's current situation. In addition, the CID management requested that the model be used as a basis in other CID reorganization efforts.

Second, during the initial individual interviews carried out by the analysts, it became clear that the CID stakeholders sometimes had very different perceptions of their own and other colleagues' work situation. However, it was important for the CID stakeholders to have a shared view about the situation and the problems they face. In other words, their mental models of the problem situation had to be in harmony. This was considered important in order to support the collaborative diagnosis of the problem situation and to arrive at a proposed solution on which everybody could agree. By discussing and improving the prototype of the conceptual model as a group, it was thought that the mental models of the group members would converge. The fact that all participants stated that the resulting model was a valid representation of the CID's processes lends support to the proposition that, indeed, their mental models did converge.

Third, there is evidence that the group modeling process was more efficient than a traditional process where a model is built during an iterative series of individual interviews with stakeholders. The prototype of the conceptual model was constructed in six hours. The discussion and modification of this model took another three and a half hours. During this time, a great majority of the stakeholders' suggestions to modify and expand the model had been processed.

Finally, it was assumed that the stakeholders would have a tendency to overemphasize details with respect to their processes and hence would invest a disproportionate amount of their effort in modeling. Because they are so involved in their work, it would be natural for them to want to see a complete and correct representation of their work situation. Moreover, since the modeling effort was likely to have an impact on the stakeholders' future situation, it was important for them to ensure an appropriate representation of their current situation. However, a perfect, complete model of a certain situation, including every exceptional event that may occur, is too detailed and hence either is impossible to create or costs a disproportionate amount of modeling effort. An analysis showed that the model size only increased by about 18 percent (from 101 elements to 119 elements) [33]. This does not support the assumption that the model size would increase dramatically when the model was presented to the stakeholders. On the contrary, the stakeholders even deleted about 10 percent of the first version of the model. Hence, the modeling process remained manageable in terms of modeling focus and level of detail.

## Empirical Model

Like the conceptual model, the empirical model of the current situation was also constructed in two participative steps representing an incremental modeling strategy. First, during the same session (session 3 in Table 2) in which the conceptual model was evaluated and modified, the CID stakeholders used GroupSystems to identify and prioritize a number of organizational requirements (comparable to critical success factors). Second, the group was invited to brainstorm about known and believed problems that hindered the CID from meeting its organizational requirements. To stimulate this brainstorming activity, an initial list of problems was added to the outline before the stakeholders could enter their ideas. This list was composed of problems identified during the initial interviews of the study and during the construction of the conceptual model prototype.

![](/api/attachments/53ZATA4R/fulltext/images/d5b42594a954256b424ba476e26a434ca87dc161406c2124f6dcce5f784ea4dd.jpg)  
Figure 4. The Hierarchical Structure of the Conceptual Model in Group Outliner

For the brainstorming activity, GroupSystems V's Group Outliner module was used in combination with TeamGraphics. Every stakeholder had access to two adjacent terminals, one running TeamGraphics, the other one Group Outliner. The stakeholders could use TeamGraphics to “walk through” the conceptual model during the brainstorming exercise. In Group Outliner, the hierarchical structure of the conceptual model’s process models was outlined (figure 4). The stakeholders were asked to enter the problems they perceived as comments to the most appropriate part of the outline. This resulted in very specific problems being entered as comments to the task to which they were related, and more general problem statements being entered at the top level of the outline.

The initial problem list consisted of thirty-eight problems. The participants extended this list with another seventy-one problem statements. Since there was only a small overlap (nine problems) between the two sets of problems, one has to conclude that the stakeholders made a significant contribution to the analysis of the CID's current situation. The main difference between the two sets of problems related to their focus: strategy (initial list) versus operational (stakeholders). Clearly, these two focuses complemented each other well. In this sense, the stakeholders did not question the perceptions of the initial list, but accepted it as a basis for a more thorough assessment of the situation. Also, along with the problem descriptions, some stakeholders identified and described solution directions or even detailed solution proposals.

Identifying the problems of CID's current situation in a group setting brought about a rich picture of the problem situation. The stakeholders not only outperformed the analysts in terms of the number of problems generated, they also reacted to problem statements immediately, fueling a rich discussion about some problems and enforcing clearer, more detailed problem descriptions. This also saved time since the analysts did not have to confirm each problem statement with stakeholders individually.

## Models for Change

Based on the problem statements gathered during the previous activity, a number of potential solutions were identified by the analysts in cooperation with a number of key CID employees. These solutions entailed various structural modifications and extensions with respect to how the main processes in the CID were performed. The further design of the solutions was carried out in a number of steps. First, each new or modified main process was conceptualized by the analysts. Second, these conceptual models for change were transformed into animated simulation models in Arena. This was a time-consuming activity; three person-weeks were needed to complete these models. The animation models portrayed the dynamic behavior of the actors in the envisioned situation in terms of the order and the interdependencies of the activities they carried out. An example of an animation model is shown in figure 5. Note that this is a static representation of the animation model; in reality, the people depicted can be seen working, walking around, sending messages to each other, and so on.

Third, each animated model was presented to two groups of stakeholders (twelve people per group) during sessions 4 and 5 (see Table 2). During each session, the proposed process was introduced and explained to the stakeholders and the animation model was demonstrated. As soon as the model and the process it depicted were clear to the stakeholders, GroupSystems V's Topic Commenter module was used to discuss the proposed new or modified process. During this activity, the stakeholders were encouraged to be as critical and creative as possible. The stakeholders entered comments and held electronic discussions on five predefined discussion topics: "Expected problems with the proposed process," "Questions about the proposed process," "Alternative ways to overcome the problems addressed in this process proposal," "Ideas regarding a project management system," and "Remaining comments and ideas." Finally, based on the stakeholders' feedback and the subsequent group discussions, the definitive design of the process was made.

In total, the 24 stakeholders participating generated 399 comments, nearly 60 pages of output, covering the five predefined discussion topics with respect to the proposed processes. It took the analysts about two weeks to process all comments and present the modified design of the new processes. The CID management approved of this design and initiated a number of activities required to achieve the implementation of the proposed changes.

## GSS Technology

The application of GroupSystems was evaluated using questionnaires. The stakeholders gave a lot of positive feedback. In general, they liked the ability of GroupSystems to focus and structure a discussion and to work simultaneously and anonymously. The main questionnaire results are presented in Table 3.

The stakeholders felt that the quantity of session results was high (question 1). The stakeholders clearly attributed the sessions' high productivity to GroupSystems (question 2). With respect to the quality of the sessions' results (question 3), the participants also displayed a positive impression. The stakeholders expressed great enthusiasm about GroupSystems itself. ## Animation Technology

The decision to communicate the models for change to the stakeholders as animation models was predicated on the assumption that the proposed changes would be easier to understand fully, which would, in turn, facilitate a critical stakeholder evaluation. However, a previous study showed that stakeholders may feel overwhelmed by animation models and become interested viewers rather than critical evaluators. This is called the “Pacman effect” [19]. This risk was addressed by keeping the models as simple as possible and by consciously stimulating the stakeholders to assume a critical brainstorming attitude. During the introduction of the models, there was indeed a short initial period in which the stakeholders seemed a bit in awe of the models. However, as soon as they had the opportunity to react to the model and engage in an (electronic) discussion of it, their passivity gave way to a critical and assertive attitude. This was evident not only in the tone of their comments and the motivation with which they reacted to each other’s comments, but also in the number of comments, ideas, and suggestions submitted.

Hence, there is evidence suggesting that using animated models in a GSS environment does not seem to harm stakeholders' active involvement in the design and evaluation process. However, the stakeholders' motivation to participate to some extent seemed to be related to the “perceived correctness” of the animation models. During the sessions, small logical inconsistencies or impossibilities were found to trigger the stakeholders to study the proposed process very carefully. In other words, they were stimulated by the “imperfectness” of the models.

With respect to the animation models themselves, the stakeholders were surveyed about whether they felt the models sufficiently clarified the proposed processes. Their average response was 3.7 (std. 0.9, using the same five-point scale used in Tables 3 and 4). In general, the stakeholders found the animation models somewhat complex. They thought there were too many elements moving on the screen and too many interdependencies shown to comprehend the proposed situation easily. During the second animation session (session 5 in Table 2), we addressed this problem by taking more time to explain the models and by letting the participants get used to them. Still, compared with the diagrammatic conceptual process models, the stakeholders liked the animation models very much. As one stakeholder who attended both the conceptual modeling and the animation sessions remarked: “That animation model tells me more than ten pages of process models!”

This observation is consistent with the ACOST study, in which no animation techniques were used. In this study, the stakeholders discussed future work processes using conceptual process models only. Here, it sometimes appeared difficult for the stakeholders to grasp the intentions and consequences of change alternatives that were represented with process models. Although these models were easy to understand, they seemed to be insufficient to move stakeholders beyond understanding to the point where they could see the effects of change alternatives and subsequently come up with proposals for modifications. In order words, the main contribution of animation models compared to process models appeared to be that they not only help “understanding” but also provide “insight” into the modeled situation.

On the whole, the design sessions left a positive impression with the stakeholders. The results of the questionnaire are presented in Table 5. The stakeholders appeared to be very satisfied with the sessions (question 1). Their written and oral feedback suggests that they felt comfortable with the way the sessions were structured and the issues were addressed. Furthermore, they subscribed to the results of the session (question 2), while at the same time they thought that the sessions helped them to gain new insights (question 3). The stakeholders felt a lot of consensus was reached (question 4), which is generally believed to be of critical importance to the success of collaborative design efforts. ## Conclusions and Future Research

THE OBJECTIVE OF THIS STUDY WAS TO EXPLORE THE POSSIBILITIES of the combined employment of GSS and animation techniques to facilitate organizational change in a collaborative fashion. The situation at the Criminal Investigations Department (CID) of the Amsterdam Municipal Police Force represented a realistic environment to achieve this objective. GSS and animated simulation models were used to support a collaborative business engineering process in the CID: Together with several stakeholders' models of the CID's current and possible future work processes were developed, portrayed, evaluated, and modified. The description of the modeling activities and the feedback from the stakeholders involved indicate the potential of the technologies concerned. In particular, during the process:

1. Required design information was efficiently gathered and processed.

2. The stakeholders involved got highly motivated to actively take part in the design effort.

3. The constructed models were effective in the sense that they triggered detailed collaborative evaluations and resulted in implementation proposals that were accepted by the stakeholders.

4. The stakeholders were very satisfied with both the collaborative design process and the process support.

The main lessons learned from the study are fourfold. First, both the conceptual model of the current situation and the models for change were built using an incremental modeling strategy. The analysts, in cooperation with a few CID stakeholders, produced a first version of each model. These versions were subsequently discussed with a larger group of stakeholders. This modeling strategy was believed to be more efficient than a strategy that requires a group to start from scratch. Being confronted with a first version of a model, the stakeholders had the opportunity to grow accustomed to the modeling technique used and the abstraction level of the model. Also, as some of the stakeholders expressed, it is often easier to point out deficiencies of a model and improve it than it is to have to do it right oneself immediately. Especially in larger stakeholder groups, we believe a common starting point can save time and facilitate the group interactions by “walking through” the model in a structured way.

Second, the experiences in the CID study suggest that it is possible to effectively involve a substantial number of stakeholders in the design activities. Groups were limited to fourteen participants only because of the size of the group support facility. Both the analysts and the main CID stakeholders felt that the two sessions with the largest number of participants (twelve, sessions 4 and 5) could also have been organized as one session without sacrificing the manageability and quality of the group interactions. Hence, using the GSS technology not only made the collaborative activities more time-efficient because stakeholders were working together at the same time, it also seemed to allow a larger group of stakeholders to be involved. This is consistent with findings from other studies in which large groups used GSS in collaborative business engineering efforts [10].

Third, based on the experiences in the CID study, it can be argued that the GSS facilities and animation technologies are complementary in the process of collaborative business engineering. Using animation models triggered the stakeholders to discuss the modeled situation extensively, while the GSS enabled the analysts to structure, capture, and effectively build upon the discussions. However, in future collaborative business engineering efforts, it would be good to have access to:

1. More flexible animation environments. During the sessions, it was impossible or too cumbersome to modify the animation models. Nor was it possible for the stakeholders to manipulate the animation models themselves. In other words, the animation models needed to be “group enabled.” This would allow stakeholders to explore the models in more detail themselves, facilitating their insight in the modeled situation.

2. GSS technologies that combine text and graphical input. During the study, TeamGraphics and GroupSystems had to be used separately, even on separate computer stations. This was perceived as cumbersome. It was felt that it would be more true to the nature of collaborative modeling efforts, and result in higher-quality interactions, if the group meeting and modeling technology were integrated. This would allow stakeholders to comment easily on graphics or include graphics to clarify textual contributions.

Finally, it turned out to be difficult at certain stages in the study to keep the momentum. As the efficiency of stakeholder involvement increased and the amount of contributions increased as well, the analysts needed more time to process all contributions into one meaningful problem analysis and design. Also, some sessions required labor-intensive preparations, such as the aforementioned period of three person-weeks to build the animation models. During the sessions, however, the stakeholders experienced efficient information gathering and interactions and saw immediate results from their efforts. This resulted in (too) high expectations with respect to the momentum of the study. Participants expected to be able to move on from the construction of the model of the current situation into the design phase of the study in a couple of days. These experiences call for conscious planning of the collaborative design activities and accordingly for careful management of stakeholders' expectations.

These observations suggest a number of issues for future research. In particular, efforts should be aimed at developing a modeling environment that meets a number of requirements to effectively support collaborative business engineering projects. First, it should be possible to support a variety of conceptual modeling techniques that have proven easy to use with stakeholders who are not modeling experts. Second, it should allow for the automatic transformation of conceptual process models into animated simulation models. This would dramatically decrease the amount of time needed to construct animation models. Third, it should facilitate the collection of numerical data needed to “fill” the simulation model and hence achieve a realistic animation. Fourth, it should allow for group interaction with the animation model. Fifth, it should be possible to implement small and moderate modifications during breaks in a group session. Finally, it would be helpful if an animation model could accommodate different abstraction levels as some stakeholders are more interested in the details of a small part of a process while others prefer a broad overview depicting only the essential aspects of a process. Research initiatives are currently underway to develop new support tools that meet these requirements and to test them in the field.
