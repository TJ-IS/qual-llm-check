# Communication and Coordination in the Virtual Office

MARY BETH WATSON FRITZ, SRIDHAR NARASIMHAN, AND HYEUN-SUK RHEE

MARY BETH WATSON FRITZ is an Assistant Professor in the Decision and Information Systems Department in the Warrington College of Business Administration at the University of Florida. She received her Ph.D. in information technology management from Georgia Institute of Technology. Her current interests include the adoption and management of virtual work environments, telecommuting, computer-supported communications, and information technology and organizational communication. Her research papers have been published in Journal of Group Decision and Negotiation, Journal of Information and Systems Engineering, Proceedings of Association of Management Conference, and Proceedings of the Hawaii International Conference on Systems Sciences. She has over ten years of industry experience in telecommunications engineering and management.

HYEUN-SUK RHEE is an Assistant Professor of Information Technology Management at Georgia Institute of Technology. She received her Ph.D. from Ohio State University. Her research interests include information technology and organizational communication, electronic commerce, negotiation support systems, telework and distributed work arrangements, information technology in development countries, research methodologies in information systems. She has published in Communications of the ACM and proceedings of many leading IS conferences.

SRIDHAR NARASIMHAN is an Associate Professor and Area Coordinator of Information Technology Management at the DuPree School of Management, Georgia Institute of Technology. He received his Ph.D. from Ohio State University. Professor Narasimhan's research interests are in computer network design, distributed databases, telework and distributed work arrangements, and information technology outsourcing. He has published in Management Science, ORSA Journal on Computing, IEEE Transactions on Communications, IIE Transactions, Computer Communications, Decision Sciences, Computers and Operations Research, Computer Networks and ISDN Systems, Annals of Operations Research, Naval Research Logistics, Group Decision and Negotiation, Journal of Database Management, and Information and Management.

ABSTRACT: As information technology becomes more pervasive, the structure of the traditional work environment is changing. A number of alternatives are emerging where work is performed at remote locations. Existing work practices and managerial strategies are often not appropriate in this environment. In particular, traditional office communication with coworkers and management, which is often dependent on physical proximity, is disrupted. In this study, individual satisfaction with office communication in the telecommuting and conventional work environments is compared through a study of telecommuters and a comparison group of non-telecommuters in nine firms. We investigate the influence of certain organizational factors, such as job characteristics, IT support, and coordination methods, on satisfaction with office communication in the two work environments. We find telecommuters report higher satisfaction with office communication. Our findings indicate that task predictability, IT support, and electronic coordination have similar influences for both groups. We discuss implications of these findings for research and practice.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: distributed work, distributed work environments, electronic coordination, organizational communication, telecommuting.

MUCH OF THE WORK DONE IN ORGANIZATIONS IS ACCOMPLISHED through communication as people exchange information and coordinate work activities $[18, 20, 29]$ . The merging of computers and telecommunications (IT) is facilitating dramatic changes in how workers communicate with each other and perform work activities. "Physical offices are vaporizing into 'virtual offices' that spring into being wherever and whenever needed and disappear as quickly" $[12]$ . While the "virtual office" is not a reality in many organizations, new office environments are emerging where work is done at locations other than the conventional corporate office $[6]$ .

One new work environment, which is gaining popularity, is telecommuting. When telecommuting, employees work in their homes rather than in offices provided by the organization. Although telecommuting has been defined as “full-time employees who stay at home and work” [26], Gray [6] reports that telecommuters work best if they are at home only part-time, say, three days per week. Telecommuting has been found to be an effective work environment—employees often report increased ability to concentrate and improved productivity levels. In addition, telecommuting provides an attractive work alternative for many companies as it can lead to reduced costs, enable access to workers with key skills regardless of their location, and provide employees with the flexibility to meet changing life-style demands [22].

While these benefits appear very attractive, managers remain reluctant to implement telecommuting programs $[2]$ . A major problem faced by managers is the disruption to the communication channels in the conventional office $[22]$ . Communication with colleagues and management plays an integral role in the efficient performance of organizational work $[29]$ . Much of this communication has been dependent on physical proximity as employees working in conventional offices have attended meetings, conversed in the hallway, and interacted with coworkers sharing the same office space. In the telecommuting environment, opportunities for this type of interaction are reduced. However, there has been little empirical research to investigate the implications of these structural changes in the work environment.

Much of what we know about telecommuting is based on investigations of individual workers and the management of these individuals. Previous research has investigated factors that influence individual performance in the telecommuting environment, such as individual characteristics that are suitable for telecommuting, such as autonomy or self-management [15, 24], and that influence the worker's choice to telecommute [35]. Little research on telecommuting has addressed system-level factors, socialization, and communication among individuals in a distributed work environment.

Our objective in this study is to investigate office communication in the telecommuting work environment. We have identified a number of organizational variables that we expect to influence satisfaction with office communication. These variables are job characteristics of interdependence and predictability, information technology (IT) support, and coordination methods. To understand the influence of these variables more fully, we investigate both telecommuting and conventional office settings. Thus, our objectives are: to identify the relationships between organizational factors and office communication in the different work environments; to suggest practical implications of these findings for successful management and implementation of telecommuting programs; and to suggest theoretical implications for future research in this area.

## Background

ORGANIZATIONS ARE ESTABLISHED TO COORDINATE THE ACTIVITIES of a number of people in order to accomplish common work and attain organizational goals. In this study we view organizations as social systems whose basic structure consists of relationships between individuals $[16, 32]$ . We use coordination in a broad sense to represent the actions necessary to perform various cooperative activities harmoniously. Activities are organized through communication, as organizational members construct shared meaning and expectations to achieve work objectives $[13]$ . From this perspective, we explore some ways in which organizational work is accomplished through communication channels and, further, how changes in the work environment such as telecommuting may influence this work system.

Workers develop alliances, think through problems, and learn from each other through communication and conversation with coworkers as a way of getting work done. Sachs [31] cites evidence that work is accomplished through relationships and informal political systems, facilitating networks of human contact within the organization. Knowledge of work skills and procedures is transferred from one worker to another, facilitating effective work practices. In addition, individuals must communicate with colleagues to find out about and adjust to shifting organizational priorities and coordinate the performance of interdependent tasks [29]. Through regular communication, individuals construct shared meaning and develop common understanding of activities to be performed [13].

The formal coordination of performance in organizations is accomplished through the managerial process. In this framework, an imposed supervisory relationship exists as people are formally organized and structures are put in place to define individual rights and obligations. This formal relationship is also a human relationship developed through communication between supervisor and subordinate. One essential aspect of this communication is the creation of shared understanding of performance expectations. Both supervisor and subordinate must understand what the other expects for performance to be effectively coordinated $[13]$ .

Social, or non-task-related interaction among members is an important aspect of office communication. Authority, power, and role definitions, as well as interpersonal attraction and compatibility, are constructed through social interactions among group members [10]. Social interactions enable the development of common grounds for communication [18, 36], increasing communication effectiveness and enhancing the ability of individuals to work together.

The implicit assumption in much of the research discussed so far is that this communication takes place in the traditional office environment. In the telecommuting environment, the nature of interactions between group members changes. For example, employees in the same physical location develop relationships and coordinate work activities through chance encounters and informal discussion $[20]$ . This type of interaction is limited in the telecommuting environment.

A number of authors have speculated that changes in the nature of interactions due to telecommuting will negatively influence the performance of work activities $[8, 11, 21, 23]$ . In an extensive study by Huws, Korte, and Robinson $[11]$ , participants described the lack of social interaction with colleagues and dearth of work-related meetings as a disadvantage of remote work. Heilmann $[9]$ argues that informal information exchange and communication of office events are reduced in the “teleprogramming” work environment (this term describes a telecommuting environment for computer programmers).

Other authors, however, maintain that, while changes in communication may occur in the telecommuting environment, a number of different organizational factors can be managed to increase satisfaction with communication. For example, more formalized communication channels such as scheduled meetings can be implemented $[22]$ , or the use of information technology applications such as e-mail for communication and information gathering can be encouraged $[21, 22]$ . In addition, previous research on telecommuting has addressed suitable job characteristics for telecommuters $[3]$ . This research indicates that the choice of jobs for telecommuting is important, particularly with respect to communication needs: for example, jobs with minimal communication needs are most suited to telecommuting $[15]$ .

## Research Variables and Hypotheses

THE RESEARCH MODEL FOR THIS STUDY IS REPRESENTED in figure 1. The hypotheses in this study describe the relationships between organizational factors and satisfaction with office communication in the telecommuting and conventional work environments. We define office communication as the communication with colleagues and with management that is essential to the performance of organizational work activities. Based on previous research, we have chosen to investigate the following organizational factors in our study: job characteristics, IT support, and coordination methods.

## Characteristics of the Job Being Performed

Jobs can be described by certain objective characteristics (for example, see [8]). $^{1}$ We have chosen to investigate two characteristics of jobs that address relationships and interactions between workers necessary for the performance of work activities. These characteristics are interdependence $[25]$ and task predictability $[37]$ . We expect that these characteristics of the work being performed by telecommuters will influence their satisfaction with office communication.

Interdependence refers to the extent to which the performance of an individual's job depends on coworkers. Highly interdependent jobs require extensive integration of work activities. Workers must cooperate by sharing the task, that is, by collaborating on the performance of the task or by specifying subtasks and coordinating their performance [25]. Regular communication with coworkers is necessary to perform highly interdependent tasks effectively.

In the telecommuting environment, access to office communication is less readily available, and contact with colleagues is reduced. Studies have suggested that jobs most suitable for telecommuting are those where job activities can be performed in isolation $[15, 19, 26]$ , implying that low task interdependence is most suitable for the telecommuting environment.

Based on the above argument, we propose hypothesis 1:

H1: There will be a significant negative relationship between interdependence and satisfaction with office communication for telecommuters.

Jobs characterized by highly predictable tasks have established procedures and well-understood methods of performance. Performance generally involves gathering information in response to clearly defined questions, say, report preparation. Jobs with low task predictability do not have clearly defined procedures $[29]$ , and much of the information needed to answer questions and perform work activities must be determined as the task is being performed, as in research or strategic planning. Workers use judgment and creativity in the performance of unpredictable tasks and must search beyond existing or known task procedures.

In addition, jobs that are not predictable require more communication with coworkers to gather information and solve problems than jobs that are predictable [19, 34].

Extensive interaction with colleagues and access to shared organizational information is necessary for workers to construct procedures and perform tasks of this nature collaboratively. Alternatively, highly predictable tasks require less communication with colleagues and necessary access to office communication is not as extensive. This study hypothesizes the relationships among task predictability, office communication, and work environment as follows:

H2: There will be a stronger positive relationship between task predictability and satisfaction with office communication for telecommuters than for non-telecommuters.

## Information Technology

While individuals can telecommute using only a conventional telephone $[22]$ , more sophisticated information technologies can provide richer, more varied access to organizational information and communication. Telecommuters can use information technology for remote access to computerized information, such as corporate databases and electronic information archives, software applications, and electronic communication channels. Information technology enables workers to gather, process, share, and store organizational information regardless of physical location.

Use of information technology to access electronic communication channels is necessary as telecommuters substitute electronic communication with colleagues for face-to-face (FTF) communication. Groupware systems, with electronic discussion and advanced information sharing capabilities, increase connectivity in distributed organizations $[6]$ . In addition, as telecommuters become more dependent on information technology, it is crucial that technical assistance is available and that technical problems can be resolved quickly $[6, 22]$ .

Much of this technology, however, is not in place only for telecommuters, but is also used by those working in the conventional office. Groupware systems, computerized databases, and other software applications will be a part of the IT infrastructure of the organization. As such, the technology will be used by the entire work group, not just those who telecommute. In addition, technical support must be available for both groups.

In order to access and participate in office communication effectively, we then propose that telecommuters and those in the conventional office must be satisfied with the following aspects of IT support: the use information technology, access to computerized information, and the ability to obtain technical assistance when necessary. The worker in the telecommuting environment, however, is more dependent on information technology for access to information and communication; alternative access to information through other methods such as conversation with coworkers is not as readily available. Therefore, we hypothesize the relationships among IT support, office communication, and work environment as follows:

H3: There will be a stronger positive relationship between satisfaction with IT support and satisfaction with office communication for telecommuters than non-telecommuters.

## Coordination Methods

Coordination is the process of linking the work activities of employees. Work activities must be linked together in order for individuals to perform productively. For example, individuals working on joint projects must coordinate work activities in order to avoid performing redundant activities. In addition, through communication-based coordination, individuals build relationships and form alliances to share and gather organizational information. Much organizational coordination occurs through different types of communication between employees, and employees and management $[19, 34]$ . Studies indicate that organizational members who do not engage in routine interactions with colleagues may become dissatisfied with communication, perceiving communication in the organization to be closed to them and withholding information $[14]$ , thereby reducing coordination effectiveness.

Coordination can take place through different communication modes—FTF or telephone interaction, and electronic communication such as e-mail. Communication-based coordination methods include meetings and conversations between coworkers or between coworkers and managers. These meetings can be scheduled and planned ahead, or they may occur on an impromptu basis, as in casual conversation in the hallway. These interactions can take place through FTF communication or over the telephone. The use of electronic coordination methods, such as e-mail, electronic bulletin boards, or electronic project tracking tools, enables a different type of interaction. This communication is unscheduled and takes place in an asynchronous manner: People can participate as their schedule permits. Each of these methods of coordination is used often, and in various combinations, by individuals to integrate work activities $[19]$ .

The use of communication-based coordination is important for both telecommuters and those in the conventional office. However, when workers are telecommuting, there is less opportunity for regular interaction with colleagues in the office. Interactions between people must be programmed and planned in advance. In addition, electronic coordination methods, such as e-mail, may be used as workers exchange information and organize activities.

Based on the above arguments, we propose:

H4a: There will be a stronger positive relationship between frequency of use of scheduled meetings and satisfaction with office communication for telecommuters than for non-telecommuters.

H4b: There will be a stronger positive relationship between frequency of use of electronic coordination methods and satisfaction with office communication for telecommuters than for non-telecommuters.

## Research Method

WE CONDUCTED A SURVEY OF TELECOMMUTERS AND CONVENTIONAL OFFICE workers in nine companies to investigate these hypotheses. The survey questionnaire contained questions measuring the outcome variable of satisfaction with office communication and independent variables of job characteristics, IT support, and coordination method. Questions were also included to ascertain demographics characteristics of the participants, such as age, gender, and educational level.

## Operationalization

The questions used in the study are found in the appendix. All questions were pilot tested. Based on responses from participants in the pilot test, appropriate changes were made to the questionnaire. In addition, other researchers in the area reviewed the questions and provided feedback on the construction of the questionnaire. After data collection, all questions were subjected to reliability analysis. The results of this analysis are also shown in the appendix.

## Data Collection

Data were collected for this research in the third quarter of 1995 using a questionnaire distributed to workers in nine firms in the Atlanta area. The firms are all large, well-established organizations with ongoing telecommuting programs. Table 2 depicts the classification of these firms.

Copies of the questionnaire were mailed to a coordinator in the organizations participating in the study. The coordinator then distributed the questionnaire to all workers in the firm who were in job functions where telecommuting programs had been implemented. In order to maintain anonymity, the study participants then returned the questionnaire directly to the researchers. Six hundred questionnaires were distributed to workers. Two hundred and thirty completed questionnaires were returned for a 38 percent response rate.

## Study Participants

The participating organizations wished to remain anonymous. The organizations are classified in three categories: type I—high-tech sales and service, organizations 1, 2, 3, and 4; type II—federal government agencies, organizations 5 and 6; and type III—public utilities, organizations 7, 8, and 9.

All participants were asked to describe their current work situation as follows: "In a typical week, how much time do you spend working at home instead of coming to an office? (a) I do not work in a home office, (b) Less than 1 day per week total, (c) 1–2 days per week, (d) 3–4 days per week, (e) More than 4 days per week." Participants who reported their work environment was best described by (a) were classified as conventional office workers. All others were classified as telecommuters.

THE MEAN AGE OF THE TOTAL PARTICIPANTS IN THE STUDY was forty-one years. On average, respondents had a college education, had worked in their current job for about 53 months, or 4.5 years, and had worked in their current organization for 171 months, or approximately 14 years. Slightly more males than females participated in the study (125 males, 105 females). Table 3 describes the differences in the two groups in the sample.

## Data Analysis

The mean responses of telecommuters and conventional workers to the overall measure of satisfaction with office communication and to the individual questions are shown in Table 4. A t-test was performed to examine significant differences in the means. Overall, telecommuters were significantly more satisfied with office communication than were non-telecommuters. Analysis of the item-level responses indicated that telecommuters were significantly more satisfied with finding out about job-related changes than conventional office workers. No other significant differences were found between the mean responses in individual items.

Hypotheses were tested using multiple regression. Previous research has shown a relationship between job characteristics, such as predictability, use of the computer to access information, and performance effectiveness $[30]$ . Gasser $[5]$ has argued that the use of computing is “embedded” in the performance of tasks, shaping work routines, and the characteristics of computer use. In light of this research, the independent variables measuring job characteristics and IT support were regressed as a group on the outcome variables, and a separate equation was fitted for the coordination methods variables. Equations were estimated for both groups of respondents, telecommuters and conventional office workers.

In multiple regression, multicollinearity, or the correlation of the independent variables among themselves, can make the examination of the marginal effect of any one independent variable in the model unrealistic. To detect the presence of multicollinearity, variance inflation factors (VIF) for each independent variable were examined. VIF for all independent variables was substantially less than 10, indicating no evidence that multicollinearity is influencing the models [27].

This study has a number of limitations. First, the survey was distributed to individuals in organizational groups where telecommuting had been implemented. All respondents had chosen their work environment, either telecommuting or conventional. The sample is representative of many telecommuting situations, as workers are often given this choice $[22]$ . However, this sample may not be generalizable to situations where the work environment is not a choice for employees.

In addition, the frequency that respondents actually telecommuted was somewhat low—44 percent of the telecommuters in this study spent less than one day per week working at home and 42 percent spent one to two days per week at home. These findings may have been significantly different with a sample of telecommuters who worked at home more often.

Finally, the telecommuters in this study had been on their jobs significantly longer than conventional office workers had been on their jobs (58 months and 42 months, respectively, p = 0.021), which also may have influenced the results; for example, relationships with coworkers had already been built and were easier to maintain. Despite the limitations of this study, our results have implications for understanding the factors influencing satisfaction with office communication in the telecommuting environment.

Our findings suggest that increased task predictability was significantly associated with increased satisfaction with office communication for telecommuters. In performing tasks with high predictability, the procedures are known and clearly defined. Also, performance requirements are easier to measure and better defined, possibly making these tasks more portable and therefore suitable for the remote work environment $[35]$ . In addition, the identity of coworkers with whom communication must take place to perform work activities is known and often formalized.

Based on a literature survey, Gutek [7] reports studies showing that, as task structure (or predictability) increases, the structure of work groups becomes more centralized and formalized. In other words, workers with highly structured tasks are likely to be members of work groups with clearly defined membership and goals. Members of these groups will then be more likely to have mutual understanding of goals and shared team identification. This appears to be particularly important in the telecommuting environment where there is less opportunity for developing common understanding through impromptu interactions. It should be noted that we found very little difference in the predictive value of this variable between the two groups, indicating that both groups should be considered in the development of communication strategies.

As expected, satisfaction with IT support was a significant predictor of satisfaction with office communication for telecommuters. Telecommuting work environments depend heavily on the availability and use of IT by workers. This was also a significant predictor of satisfaction for conventional office workers (see Tables 5 and 6). Much of the computerized information, software applications, and electronic communication channels necessary for telecommuters will also be used by members of the work group who remain in the conventional office. These results indicate that the information technology needs and capabilities of both groups should be considered when implementing telecommuting programs.

## Implications

THIS STUDY HAS BOTH PRACTICAL AND THEORETICAL IMPLICATIONS. Much research on the social and communication aspects of the work environment has been conducted with the implicit assumption that people work in the same physical location. We have investigated the influence of the relationship between organizational factors—that is, job characteristics of predictability and interdependence, IT support, and coordination methods and satisfaction—and office communication in the telecommuting and conventional office environment. We found that telecommuters reported higher satisfaction with office communication than non-telecommuters. Our findings also suggest that predictability, IT support, and electronic coordination have significant but similar influences on satisfaction with communication for both groups.

## Implications for Practitioners

The results of this study have a number of significant implications for practitioners. Although our findings were not conclusive, they point to a number of strategies for maintaining effective communication and coordination channels in the telecommuting environment. In addition, these findings suggest that the needs of both telecommuters and those remaining in the office should be considered in the management and implementation of telecommuting programs. While telecommuters are in remote locations such as the home, those remaining in the office are likewise geographically separated from the telecommuters. Therefore, managers implementing telecommuting programs should develop strategies not just for managing telecommuters but for managing a network of workers in a distributed environment, that is, in a variety of different geographic locations.

We found that, as the predictability of the jobs of telecommuters increased, they were more likely to be satisfied with office communication. There were also indications that this is important for employees in the conventional office environment. Workers in highly predictable jobs have formalized communication channels and clearly defined group membership. This suggests that it is important for workers in a distributed environment, particularly those in less predictable jobs, to understand how their job is integrated with their coworkers. Managers in distributed work environments can develop strategies that explicitly emphasize and develop the connections among employees to develop common understanding of larger group or organizational goals.

For example, one of the managers in our survey, who was responsible for several departments with a number of telecommuters, had implemented interdepartmental teams to increase communication and understanding. She felt that these teams were necessary because the employees, who had formerly worked in the same building, were now much less likely to meet in the hallway or visit a coworker's office to discuss a problem. Because of this lack of communication with employees from different areas, workers seemed to have less understanding of the goals and objectives of departments other than their own.

Our study also indicates that use of IT plays an important role in maintaining office communication channels in the telecommuting environment. However, interviews conducted in these firms indicated that they had conducted little IT training before establishing telecommuting programs. Therefore, we suspect that workers already comfortable with the use of IT for accessing information and communication were most likely to choose this work environment. For firms implementing telecommuting, particularly those who want a large number of workers to participate, additional training and IT support for workers may be necessary. Also, as IT support is valuable for both telecommuters and conventional office workers (see Table 8), training is important for both groups.

Finally, our analysis suggests that undirected electronic communication (UEC), such as project tracking tools and electronic bulletin boards, is important for satisfaction with office communication in the distributed work environment. UEC differs from directed electronic communication (DEC) such as e-mail, where information is exchanged between specific individuals. UEC allows “lurking” where workers can monitor a wide variety of work-related events and discussions among colleagues. This suggests the need for managers to consider a number of different electronic communication methods when implementing telecommuting, such as groupware and workflow products.

## Implications for Researchers

The results of this study have significant implications for research as well. This is one of the few studies we are aware of that has rigorously examined communication and coordination in the telecommuting work environment from a theoretical and empirical perspective. A number of interesting issues have surfaced that could be considered for future research.

Data were collected for this study from a variety of organizations (nine different firms). In addition, study participants were in a wide variety of jobs. While we believe the variety of organizations and job types increases the generalizability of our findings, we are conducting additional research to understand the influence of these variables. For example, communication norms in different companies, and even functional areas, may have a significant influence on satisfaction with office communication in the distributed work environment.

Our findings also indicate that additional research examining the job characteristics of telecommuters would be interesting. While we found only weak indications of the influence of interdependence, this construct was measured with one item. This raises some question as to the reliability and validity of the measurement. Additional research to develop a more robust measure of this construct would be useful.

In addition, research to identify other objective characteristics of jobs would provide theoretical and practical value. A better understanding of characteristics of jobs that are suitable for the distributed environment would allow strategies to be developed to identify and, where possible, to redesign jobs to enable work to be performed more effectively (e.g., see $[8]$ for discussion of job redesign). In the study of emerging distributed work environments, we believe it is particularly important to identify characteristics of jobs that address relationships and interactions between workers.

Our research model had low explanatory power, suggesting that other variables may need to be included to better explain satisfaction with office communication in the distributed work environment. As we have discussed, the influence of organization, job type (e.g., financial analyst, procurement manager), and more objective job characteristics are important. In addition, understanding of the influence of different frequencies of telecommuting—for example, how often individuals actually work at home—is limited.

IT allows new conceptions of the work environment. A number of alternative work arrangements are emerging. Our study implies that work practices may change in the distributed environment. In particular, different strategies may be required to manage social and communication aspects of the distributed work environment. The results of this research provide only a start in investigating these questions.
