# Differences Between Novice and Expert Systems Analysts: What Do We Know and What Do We Do?

K.D. SCHENK, NICHOLAS P. VITALARI, AND K. SHANNON DAVIS

K.D. SCHENK is Assistant Professor of Information Systems in the College of Management at North Carolina State University. She received her Ph.D. and M.B.A. from the University of California, Irvine. Dr. Schenk has recently published in Interfaces and is a member of AIS, TIMS, and SIM. Her current research interests include management support systems, information systems and problem-solving behaviors, and human-computer interfaces.

NICHOLAS P. VITALARI is Vice-President and Director of the Summit Project for CSC, Index. He has been Associate Professor of Management at the University of California, Irvine, where his research areas included cognitive models in systems analysis, computing in the home, and the social impacts of computing. He has published articles in MIS Quarterly, Communications of the ACM, Management Science, and Computers, Environments and Urban Systems. He has also written a book entitled Structured BASIC for Business. He holds a Ph.D. and an M.B.A. from the University of Minnesota.

K. SHANNON DAVIS is Assistant Professor of Human Resources in the College of Management at North Carolina State University. She received her Ph.D. from the University of Maryland, College Park, and her M.B.A. from Virginia Polytechnic Institute and State University. Dr. Davis has published articles in the Journal of Applied Psychology, International Journal of Industrial Ergonomics, and Journal of Collective Negotiations in the Public Sector. Her current research interests include social cognition and managerial decision making, employee skills for the twenty-first century, and technology and managerial education.

ABSTRACT: Due to increased organizational dependence on information systems technology, the effectiveness of the systems analyst is more crucial today than ever. Despite technological advances that have increased the number and quality of tools available to systems analysts, the individual analyst's problem-solving skills remain key to defining good systems requirements. Understanding how expert analysts approach the requirements analysis task may provide insights for improving the effectiveness of novice analysts. This paper examines differences in the problem-solving approaches of novice and expert systems analysts during an information requirements task. Specific weaknesses that set novice and expert analysts apart are identified, and techniques that may be used to strengthen novice skills are suggested. This research supports the current literature on creativity techniques as a strategy for strengthening system analysis skills.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: creativity in problem solving, information requirements, information systems development, novice and expert problem-solving, systems analysis, systems analyst education.

AS RAPID CHANGES IN INFORMATION TECHNOLOGY AND THE IMPORTANCE of effectively managing organizational information increase in today's business environments, the process of creating information systems (IS) continues to be crucial. "The demand for systems analysts is high because the demand for timely and efficient systems development is high" [67]. As evidenced by unsatisfactory and even unused systems, defining good systems requirements continues to be a huge problem for IS development. Despite recent advances in systems analysis tools, the systems analyst continues to play a key role in the development process.

Rapid technological changes have brought about an increase in the variety of tools used for conducting systems analysis. After a lengthy delay, the IS field has focused finally on creating tools aimed at improving the development process. Technological advancements have provided us with system creation aids. Computer assisted software engineering (CASE) tools with data flow diagramming and code generators help systems analysts develop useful information systems. Methodologies such as Systems Development Life Cycle (SDLC), Joint Applications Development (JAD), and prototyping have been designed to provide guidance in systems development. As Wanninger and Dickson indicate: "Much progress has been made. The technology and processes have improved substantially over the last 25 years" [103].

The most valuable “tool,” however, still resides within the systems analyst. Technological advances cannot totally replace the analyst’s cognitive abilities. The development process hinges on the analyst’s ability to understand the problem and specify the necessary steps for resolution. Without a clear understanding of the problem, the development road map is likely to produce an incomplete and unsatisfactory system.

The most critical part of this development process is requirements analysis. The analyst's ability to develop accurate, complete, and clear information requirements is paramount for successful systems building [20, 98, 100, 105, 106]. Flaws in this initial process are costly. Errors early in the design process can have rippling effects, and these errors are expensive and time-consuming to correct after system completion. Increased use of analysis support tools such as CASE and code generators magnifies these problems. For example, research indicates that using support tools without adequate problem understanding can produce a flurry of superficial activity resulting in simplistic models [60]. Because these models are “computer-generated,” they may seem highly credible and work may continue further in the process before serious errors are detected. Thus, incomplete or faulty requirements analysis performed by systems analysts remain a tremendous problem that significantly affects subsequent systems development.

Given the critical importance of accurate requirements analysis, equipping analysts with the problem-solving skills required for this task is a crucial component of developing good systems analysts. Providing analysts with these skills becomes even more of an issue when we consider the differences between novice systems analysts and more experienced analysts. Previous research indicates that differences between novice and experienced/expert analysts can be found in both the number of cognitive processes utilized and the effectiveness of those processes for problem solving $[14, 18, 56, 60, 63]$ . Novices employ fewer cognitive processes and their knowledge structures are smaller and less detailed than those of experts. Experts are able to access information quickly where novices require great effort. In addition, because of differences in novice and expert knowledge organization, experts are more effective at recognizing appropriate problem-solving strategies for domain-specific issues than are novices.

Improved understanding of novice problem-solving behavior permits identification of specific error-prone behaviors that may limit the novice's ability to determine information systems requirements successfully. Error-proneness in novice problem solving suggests that their lack of expertise makes novices more likely than experts to make mistakes $[18, 88]$ . Error-proneness among systems analysts, particularly in requirements determination, could lead to incomplete, inaccurate, ambiguous, and/or incorrect information requirements.

In order to avoid these costly errors, it is important to facilitate the analyst's progression from novice to expert. This requires three steps: (1) understanding how a novice analyst functions, (2) understanding how an expert analyst functions, and (3) quickly and efficiently closing the gap between the two. Understanding these steps has long been an important task for educators and an important field of study for researchers [8, 15, 18, 25, 44, 49, 59, 60, 70]. Although systems analysis is an inexact skill and it is virtually impossible to avoid all requirements errors, understanding how to facilitate movement from novice to expert systems analyst provides clear direction for the education process. Educators of systems analysts need to fully understand the error-proneness of novice analysts. This knowledge would allow us to address these weaknesses and provide novices with better skills to increase their success rate earlier in their careers. As has been stated, errors occurring early in the systems analysis process are much more expensive and time-consuming to resolve than those that may occur at any other time in systems design. These errors, in turn, could lead to expensive reanalysis, system redesign, user dissatisfaction, and excessive or unnecessary maintenance. Thus, a focus on this initial requirements analysis phase is critical: The impact on systems development due to novices' limitations can be tremendous.

Prior research suggests factors that are crucial to the requirements analysis process. Much of this work focuses on experienced systems analysts: their cognitive processes $[50, 78, 79, 100, 101]$ , interpersonal communication styles $[39, 47, 82, 91]$ , and world views $[24, 53]$ . An understanding of approaches used by experienced analysts would facilitate educators, researchers, and analysts themselves in developing better strategies for requirements analysis. Requirements analysis is a problem-solving task. Systematic approaches, including creativity techniques, have been developed and shown to be very effective in enhancing problem-solving skills. For example, Couger's five-step Creative Problem Solving (CPS) model specifies techniques that focus on cognitive skills similar to those utilized in requirements analysis (including problem definition, compiling relevant information, and evaluating ideas) [22]. These techniques appear applicable to novices' error-proneness and the cognitive limitations addressed in this study. The current study also extends prior research on experienced systems analysts by incorporating literature on differences in expert and novice cognitive processes.

The purpose of this paper is to investigate the problem-solving behavior of novices vis-à-vis expert systems analysts during an information requirements determination task. The intention is to identify differences in the problem-solving approaches used by these analysts. Using protocol analysis, we examine quantitative and qualitative differences in problem-solving behaviors employed by novices and expert systems analysts. Possible techniques for facilitating the transition from novice to expert analyst are then suggested.

## Research Bases and Development of Hypotheses

A MAJOR OBJECTIVE OF THIS STUDY IS TO PROVIDE PRELIMINARY insight into the following research question:

How do observed novice behaviors compare to those exhibited by expert systems analysts?

Investigation of this question calls for the integration of several fields of study, or research bases: the nature of experience, differences in novice and expert cognitive processes, the nature of the information requirements task, and theories of problem understanding and problem-solving strategies. We draw upon the literature in these areas in order to explore the question and derive specific behavioral hypotheses. Following is a discussion of these research bases and the development of associated hypotheses (H1–H8).

## Research Bases

## Nature of Experience

It is clear that experience plays a role in the development of professional judgment expertise. Domain-specific experience is one key in the movement from novice to expert. Experience entails rehearsal of known judgment strategies, generation of new strategies in the face of new situations, general learning by trial and error, and an increasing awareness and familiarity with the types of domain problems and their structure [49, 83]. Through this experience accumulation process, the professional develops a repertoire of skills, knowledge, and meta-knowledge that leads to effective performance in a specific domain. In fact, the major goal of a novice is to develop increasing levels of proficiency and mastery over the task environment. This movement from the novice stage to the intermediate, and finally to the expert, is characterized by increasing levels of knowledge, adaptability, and confidence. However, before acquiring mastery over the task environment, novices tend to exhibit more error-proneness than do experts [18]. In other words, they are more likely to commit errors in the problem-solving process than are experts. Kolodner [49] notes that novices lack the specific trial-and-error experiences in real, domain-relevant situations from which experts gain much knowledge. In turn, this incomplete knowledge base of novice analysts results in more errors in the problem-solving situation than do the more developed knowledge structures of experts [56].

This lack of experience also limits other novice cognitive skills. A major aspect of experience is the learning and rehearsal processes. Rehearsal of mental behaviors has long been known to increase specific memory recall strength, assist in skill refinement and mastery, reduce performance anxiety, and increase confidence $[2, 5, 27]$ . The novice has rehearsed a set of skills in the context of practice problems, which are divorced from the actual domain. Thus, this “classroom” experience is reified and suffers from pronounced detail, depth, and realism anemia.

## Differences in Novice and Expert Cognitive Processes

Although research indicates that experience plays a role in the differences between novices and experts, it is clear that experience alone is insufficient to explain performance differences $[9, 37, 56, 63]$ . Neale and Northcraft view experience as feedback, whereas expertise is strategic conceptualization $[68]$ . Strategic conceptualization is not just feedback to our decision making but a constant monitoring and awareness of our decision-making processes. It is knowing how we do what we do and learning to avoid common decision-making biases $[68]$ . It is the examination of the basic mechanisms and skills that experience provides that sheds light on this issue. Differences in novice and expert cognitive processes have been studied in a number of professional domains, including accounting, EDP auditing, medicine, computer programming, and law $[1, 38, 52]$ . The basic goal in these studies was to identify the bases for expertise and the gaps between novice and expert behavior. These studies provide insight into the cognitive processes that underlie differences in novice and expert performance and confirm that such differences occur across a variety of problem-solving situations.

Kolodner [49] suggests that limited on-the-job exposure severely constrains the amount of episodic knowledge at the novice's disposal. Episodic knowledge refers to the organized collection of specific job-relevant events or situations (i.e., episodes) that becomes a source for future problem solutions. Moreover, this episodic knowledge, if it exists at all in the novice, is superficially organized compared with that of the experienced professional [37, 63]. Thus, while the novice may have a great store of semantic or factual knowledge, including the latest techniques, research results, and critical issues, he or she is sorely lacking in grounded, domain-specific knowledge [56].

Because of this limited episodic knowledge, the novice's procedural knowledge may lack external validity. Procedural knowledge encompasses the "how to" in performing an activity. Thus, a mismatch exists between the level of procedural knowledge required to solve artificial problems and that required to solve “real” problems. As a default, the novice relies on weak methods $[17, 69]$ . Weak methods are those acquired in the classroom or learned in other jobs or life situations that are insufficient to deal with more complex situations. For example, a novice systems analyst who has not had sufficient conflict situation experience may employ a “nice guy” heuristic to diffuse conflict situations because this may have been a successful procedure or strategy in school or daily life. However, this is a weak procedure in a requirements analysis setting because real conflicts do exist and strong methods for skilled conflict resolution are needed. Weak methods or incomplete procedural knowledge may also reduce the novice’s ability to construct and execute internally consistent reasoning sequences, or render the novice unable to complete a reasoning sequence. Effective causal reasoning and problem-solving planning activities rely heavily on complete procedural knowledge.

## Nature of the Information Requirements Task

Information systems analysis is a semantically rich professional domain largely characterized by ill-defined problems. In the requirements analysis phase of system construction, the analyst is faced with the task of making sense out of multiple user requests and perspectives and integrating this external information with their own technical knowledge and professional judgment. To further complicate the process, the analyst must often perform these tasks in a politicized and conflict-oriented context and must simultaneously assure some level of due process in soliciting ideas and opinions $[48, 61]$ . From a cognitive perspective, the analyst must identify important cues amid a sea of extraneous information, set goals, generate and test hypotheses concerning the system attributes, process dissimilar information from multiple sources, and distinguish between relevant and irrelevant information. He or she must then reformulate the problem into a common nomenclature for technical and nontechnical audiences and specify a consensus-based, error-free set of systems requirements $[101]$ . In addition, Vessey and Couger $[98]$ found synergistic effects of application and methodology knowledge by novices in determining information requirements. Thus, the impact of application knowledge becomes another factor to be considered in the requirements analysis process.

Clearly, requirements analysis is not trivial. To perform successfully in such a complex task environment requires a highly adapted set of cognitive skills with a deep structure knowledge of the domain. For the novice, this presents severe problems since it is difficult to simulate the complexity and impart the task structure multidimensionality through textbooks and toy problems $[18]$ . Moreover, as has been made clear in the development of CASE environments, this level of knowledge is difficult to imbed in computer aids for the systems analyst—especially at the requirements analysis stage $[102]$ .

Thus, it is evident upon examination of the literature that systems analysts have been researched through a variety of well-developed studies. Even so, the ill-structured nature and complexity of the systems analysis task suggest additional studies are necessary to provide guidance regarding this problem-solving activity and to increase our understanding of the process of becoming an expert systems analyst.

## Development of Hypotheses

In addition to the background research discussed above, we examine existing research on problem understanding and problem-solving strategies. These theories provide a basis for specifying behavioral expectations for both novice and expert analysts in this study.

## Problem Understanding

Before problem solvers can solve the problem at hand, they must attempt to understand the problem. Understanding consists of constructing a problem representation, determining the initial problem state, defining a desired goal state, and planning to move from the initial state to the goal state $[18]$ . Problem understanding is affected by knowledge availability and knowledge organization.

Knowledge availability: Knowledge availability affects the quality of the problem-solving effort. Available knowledge may be episodic or semantic. As stated earlier, novices have limited episodic, or situation-specific, knowledge $[49]$ . This limits their ability to relate new problem tasks to previous system experiences. Episodic knowledge plays an important role in analogical reasoning and learning. The accumulation of job events or episodes allows the professional to link multiple episodes over time and derive principles, create prototypes, and generate stereotypic or generalized responses to domain situations $[14, 45]$ . Flanagan $[35]$ was one of the first occupational psychologists to discover that job skills depended on the accumulation and distillation of “critical incidents” in the task domain $[81]$ .

Semantic knowledge has been described as generic facts, with little functional or operational knowledge associated with those facts. These facts may be arranged in categories, or schemata, such as “Visual Basic” belonging to the category “object-oriented tools” which in turn belongs to the category “software.” Another way to characterize this semantic knowledge base is to refer to it as a stereotypic collection of facts assembled from textbooks, research articles, and professorial admonitions reflecting current classroom trends.

Episodic knowledge also encodes prior experience. In contrast to semantic knowledge, however, the relationships between concepts in episodic knowledge are much richer and better defined. Limited episodic knowledge will compel novice systems analysts to rely on weak methods since they have only general problem-solving methods in their repertoire of skills. The use of weak methods may be manifested in a reduced quality of specified requirements and a reduced level of goal attainment (for a more complete discussion on episodic and semantic knowledge in novices and experts, see $[49]$ ).

Availability of domain-specific knowledge is another factor in problem understanding $[15, 49, 70]$ . Knowledge categories are often domain-specific. For example, physicians deal with disease types, mechanical engineers with thermodynamics, and physicists with physics. Similarly, systems analysts utilize a core set of knowledge categories. This domain-specific knowledge is acquired through formal education as well as job-related experiences. Given their lack of experience, the novice analysts' knowledge base is populated largely by semantic textbook knowledge. Thus, most of the knowledge available to the novice in a requirements analysis situation is likely to reflect the current state of the art in codified systems analysis knowledge. This knowledge base would include the latest admonitions from experts on how best to implement an information system, prototyping and structured analysis methods, the importance of user involvement, the importance of documenting information requirements, and so on.

The question here concerns how novices might differ from experts in knowledge availability and utilization in the requirements analysis task. It is expected that, because of novices' limited domain-specific knowledge, expert analysts will verbalize more domain-specific issues than will novices. Since novices have little experience on which to draw, they must rely on the textbook list of issues. Given more recent emphasis by educational programs on user issues in systems development, it is expected that novices will address user involvement, but at a superficial level. More specifically, it is expected that novices will verbalize the importance of user issues in the systems analysis process as evidenced by a large number of references to user involvement. However, superficial references to this important behavioral issue are likely, given novices' lack of experience in dealing with users in real job situations.

For these reasons, hypotheses 1 and 2 posit that:

H1: Novice analysts verbalize fewer domain-specific issues than do expert analysts.

H2: Novice analysts verbalize more user-involvement references than do expert analysts.

Knowledge organization: The organization of knowledge also affects problem understanding. Knowledge organization appears to be different for novices and experts $[15, 56, 63, 84]$ . Several studies have indicated that the organization of information within the cognitive structure is an important aspect of expertise. Atwood et al. $[4]$ found that the knowledge structures of novice systems designers (i.e., analysts working with a fully specified requirements set) were superficial and sparse compared with those of expert systems designers. Johnson et al. $[46]$ noted in a medical diagnosis study that the novices' disease schemata or templates "have an internal structure that is fairly imprecise" (p. 237). Their analysis suggests that this imprecision and lack of detail led to diagnostic reasoning error, especially in patient data cue interpretation, hypothesis generation, and hypothesis evaluation.

Mackay and Elam [60] concluded that “in general, the researchers find that experts are inclined to approach problems from the top down and are able to group problems with the same underlying structure. In contrast, novices tend to use a bottom-up procedure which lacks a comprehensive plan, and they usually group problems by surface characteristics” (p. 151). Experts are able to represent problems in a conceptual form as opposed to novices’ more syntactical style. Novices tend to focus on specific problem implementation issues while experts are better at generalizing the problem [15, 60, 63, 84]. The findings of Jeffries, Turner, Polson, and Atwood [44] suggest that, while experts consider multiple strategies for problem solving, novices attempt the first method that comes to mind. This existing research is the basis for hypothesis 3:

H3: Experts tend to structure the problem in a top-down manner while novices tend to use a bottom-up structuring process.

## Problem Solving

Once they understand the problem, problem solvers can begin to solve the problem. As stated above, cognitive structure is an important aspect of expertise. Problem-solving behaviors are affected by both the content and the organization of these cognitive structures, and differences in novice and expert performance have been attributed to differences in these structures $[15, 49, 56, 84]$ . Problem-solving behaviors contributing to performance differences between novices and experts are discussed below; these include trigger identification, hypothesis management, goal setting, strategy use, and heuristics.

Trigger identification: Presumably, both semantic and episodic knowledge are incorporated into a cognitive structure, or schematic framework. Some researchers have hypothesized that episodic knowledge is instrumental in developing cognitive schemata that permit experts to filter irrelevant information and identify important clues in the problem setting $[12, 14, 15, 25, 31, 55, 75, 80, 85, 101]$ . A cognitive schema is a memory organization that permits the problem solver to filter, process, and analyze environmental information. The identification of the correct cues, or triggers, in a problem situation leads to the development of working hypotheses, goals, and direction for further data collection $[46, 77]$ .

Given the expert's increased level of experience, the expert's cognitive schemata tend to be rich and detailed, fully elaborated, and well adapted to the task domain. Since the novice has neither the quantity nor the quality of the expert's domain-specific or episodic knowledge, his or her cognitive schemata are characterized by sparseness and general imprecision [56]. These characteristics are expected to play a role in novice problem-solving behavior. A sparse cognitive schema likely leads to several novice performance limitations. One major limitation could be seen in the analyst trigger (or cue) utilization behavior. Triggers are data in the task environment that the analyst deems important to the problem at hand. Since the novice's schema is sparse, imprecise, and poorly adapted, he or she may have difficulty identifying relevant triggers in the problem setting. In addition, limited schemata may also constrain the novice's ability to identify missing requirements. This condition may result in less search for trigger behaviors by novices. The novice analyst may be unable to recognize the omitted or missing triggers in a stream of information since novices cannot compare this problem situation with others and have not yet learned what is and is not relevant. Based on this work, we formulate hypotheses 4a and 4b:

H4a: Novices verbalize fewer triggers in the problem setting than do expert analysts.

## H4b: Novices verbalize less search for trigger behaviors than do expert analysts.

Hypothesis management: Hypothesis management is a problem-solving approach used by systems analysts as they attempt to make sense of the systems requirements task. It is a multistage process that includes hypothesis generation, testing, and retention/discard behaviors. Prior work on hypothesis management $[26, 46, 64, 70]$ forms the basis for three related hypotheses. With regard to hypothesis generation, research indicates that novices and experts tend to verbalize approximately the same number of problem-solving hypotheses $[26, 64]$ . Presumably, hypotheses are generated according to some partitioning of the world, an understanding of the role of specific problem components, and an underlying categorization scheme or cognitive schema $[46]$ . Novices with sparse cognitive schemata may be expected to have difficulty generating hypotheses since hypotheses are based on some previous understanding of a class of problems. The research cited above $[26, 64]$ , however, indicates that this is not the case: Novices and experts do verbalize about the same number of hypotheses (although hypotheses verbalized by novices do tend to be overgeneralized and simplistic in nature). Thus, we postulate that novices and experienced analysts will generate an equal number of hypotheses during problem solving.

## H5a: Novices and expert analysts verbalize an equal number of hypotheses.

In addition to hypothesis generation, hypothesis management involves hypothesis testing and retention/discard behaviors. Although the research discussed above does indicate that novices and experts generate an equal number of hypotheses, novices' sparse episodic knowledge makes it more difficult for them to evaluate and filter hypotheses $[14, 15, 25, 31, 75]$ . This limited knowledge constrains novices' ability to evaluate a presumption and subject it to a test. The term hypothesis testing refers here to the deductive process whereby an analyst attempts to verify a previously generated hypothesis. Hypothesis testing among novices is thus hindered by the limited nature of their cognitive schemata. Johnson et al. $[46]$ surmised that novices' limited knowledge base led to diagnostic errors in hypothesis testing and evaluation. On what basis will novices reason the correctness of a given set of hypotheses? Since hypotheses have been shown to play an important role in problem solving in an ill-defined domain $[30, 77, 101]$ , it is likely that novice systems analysts will have difficulty testing hypotheses and identifying important questions for further inquiry.

Likewise, we expect novices to discard hypotheses less frequently than will their more experienced counterparts. The decision to discard or retain active hypotheses requires the analyst to match existing knowledge with new task information. Because of their lack of episodic knowledge, novices find it difficult to distinguish between relevant and irrelevant information, further complicating the elimination of weak hypotheses. Failure to eliminate weak or unusable hypotheses increases the cognitive complexity of the task considerably $[70, 72, 92]$ . Frequent references in the research literature to the error-proneness of novices $[18, 49, 56]$ may be attributed in part to their difficulties in hypothesis testing and decisions to retain or discard hypotheses. Based on this discussion, we propose the following:

H5b: Novices verbalize fewer hypothesis-testing behaviors than do expert analysts.

H5c: Novices discard fewer hypotheses than do expert analysts.

Goal setting: Limitations in cognitive schemata content are also expected to limit novice goal-setting behavior severely. Goal setting plays a significant role in problem structuring and reducing the size of the problem space $[15, 18, 51]$ . Since goal setting provides direction for subsequent problem-solving behavior, decreased goal-setting behavior will severely constrain the problem solver's effectiveness. It is expected that the goal set specified by the novice analysts will be smaller and less comprehensive than that specified by expert analysts. Novice goals are expected to encompass fewer topic areas than those of expert systems analysts. Within the cognitive framework discussed above, it follows that these novice goals will likely be general and vague and fewer in number when compared with those of expert analysts. Thus, hypothesis 6 states:

H6: Novices verbalize fewer goals than do expert analysts.

Strategy use: Prior research indicates that novices and experts utilize different problem-solving strategies $[51, 88, 97]$ . For example, studies dealing with geometry and physics problem solving found that novices often use a strategy known as backward searches $[51]$ . A backward search involves starting at the unknown solution and working backward toward the problem givens. Experts tackling the same problems employ a forward search strategy, starting with the information given and working forward to the solution. Interestingly, these differences occurred only in easier, less complex, problems. In more difficult problem sets, both experts and novices utilized backward search strategies.

As indicated earlier, lack of expertise is expected to have a negative impact on procedural skill. Novice systems analysts are expected to employ fewer problem-solving strategies than expert systems analysts. Novices have difficulty identifying and specifying the next activity to be accomplished and identifying the specific steps necessary to carry out the problem-solving process. It is suggested that novice systems analysts “flounder” during the problem-solving process and exhibit uncertainty regarding the efficacies of particular problem-solving strategies $[49]$ . Thus,

H7: Novice systems analysts verbalize fewer problem-solving strategies than do expert systems analysts.

Heuristics: Because of a lack of experience and real-world learning, novice analysts have fewer “rules of thumb,” or heuristics, upon which to base their problem-solving strategies. Prior research indicates that heuristic behavior has been related to successful problem solving $[45, 46, 70]$ . Heuristics allow simplification of knowledge structures so as to reduce information-processing demands $[56, 95, 96]$ . In a cognitively taxing and complex task such as information requirements determination, the use of heuristics would appear to be beneficial. However, since novices have very limited episodic knowledge with regard to the systems analysis process, their use of heuristics would, most likely, be limited and ineffective. Thus, we expect novices to verbalize fewer heuristics in the problem setting than expert analysts, as posited below:

H8: Novices verbalize fewer heuristics in the problem setting than do expert analysts.

## Method

## Subjects and Basis for Categorization

THE STUDY PARTICIPANTS INCLUDED NOVICE AND EXPERIENCED SYSTEMS analysts. The seven novice participants (four men, three women) received academic instruction from the same AACSB-accredited M.B.A. program in order to maintain consistency in their academic training. All novice participants were information systems majors and had received traditional academic training in systems analysis theory, methods, and issues. Although these subjects had extensive academic training, their on-the-job systems analysis experience was minuscule. They had an average of two months equivalent job experience (ranging from one to six months) in a variety of organizations including financial services, aerospace, and consulting.

The comparison group of experienced systems analysts was divided into two groups based on level of expertise: high-rated and low-rated. As discussed earlier, years of experience is not the sole indicator of expertise $[56]$ . Whereas experience is acquired over time through repeated feedback, expertise requires a conceptual understanding of rational decision making in the domain area as well as the ability to recognize biases and error-proneness that may limit the decision-making process. It is conceivable that novices' emphasis on weak methods can lead to a fixation on those methods without proper on-the-job training or managerial guidance. Early rehearsal of these weak methods may give the illusion of success and thus become standard procedure for the analyst. This may explain why one can witness experienced analysts performing at minimal levels despite a long history of on-the-job projects. For this reason, experienced analysts in this study were divided into high-rated and low-rated groups. These groups consisted of nine pairs of analysts (one high-rated and one low-rated), each pair from a different organization.

Analysts were classified as high-rated or low-rated based on two different indicators of analyst performance. First, the analysts' supervisors used a rating scale developed by Arvey and Hoyle [3]. This rating instrument has been used to provide a standard performance measure across subjects and raters in different organizations and has been shown to be reliable and valid. Second, the supervisors, identifying the highest- and lowest-rated performers in each organization, completed a ten-point rating scale. Both rating measures agreed in all cases. Based on these two measures, the high- and low-rated analysts in each of the nine organizations were identified. All eighteen (sixteen men, two women) agreed to participate in this study. The experienced analysts averaged 10.3 years industry experience as systems analysts (range = 3–22 years), and were employed in a wide variety of industries including banking, publishing, medical technology, retail, and wholesale distribution. There was no significant correlation between number of years of experience and supervisor rating (r = 0.116).

## Task

Subjects participated in a problem-solving task similar to those performed on a regular basis by systems analysts. The subjects were requested to determine information requirements for an accounts receivable system. The experimental stimulus was a case study of a retail merchandising organization required to develop a new accounts receivable system. Subjects were provided additional information (e.g., background on the actors in the case, the budget, flow charts, an organizational chart) on a demand basis. Providing additional information on a demand basis closely simulates a real-world situation where the analyst usually receives limited initial information on the desired system and then must acquire additional information through documentation and inquiry in order to determine system requirements successfully. This case study and the supporting materials have been used in previous studies of systems analyst problem solving $[100, 101]$ . (See appendix A for task stimulus.)

## Procedure

Subjects participated in the study individually. Each subject was presented with the task materials and instructed to read the case problem. The subjects were then required (1) to determine the purpose of the system, (2) to determine the goals of the system, and (3) to specify the information requirements. Subjects were asked to verbalize all thought processes as they worked to solve the case and accomplish the stated goals. The researcher provided additional information only upon subject request. The subjects' verbalizations were collected via an audio tape recorder for subsequent transcription and analysis. The second author was present during the entire session to ensure uniformity of data collection across all subjects. Procedures were identical for all subjects in each of the groups. Each individual verbal protocol session was scheduled for two hours (maximum time utilized was ninety minutes), and no further limitations were placed on subjects for task completion.

## Protocol Analysis

Concurrent verbalization protocol analysis has been used extensively with problem-solving tasks, and researchers have concluded that this procedure produces an accurate and valid account of problem-solving processes $[8, 32, 60]$ . This methodology of “thinking aloud” during the problem-solving episode allows observation of the cognitive processes while the problem solving is actually occurring. The procedure requires subjects to verbalize their thought processes during the task performance. These verbalizations are subsequently transcribed, coded, and analyzed. For a more extensive review of studies that have utilized protocol analysis, see $[36]$ .

Verbal protocols were collected and transcribed for each subject separately. Following the collection of the verbal protocols, an assistant who was not familiar with the study transcribed the tape recordings. The transcriptions were broken into complete thoughts or sentences for subsequent coding and numbered for reference purposes. Two coders were trained and independently coded each of the verbal protocols according to the a priori problem-solving framework (see description of coding instrument and procedure below). The coded protocols were then analyzed for intercoder concordance using the Kappa coefficient. The Kappa coefficient indicated a degree of coder agreement of 0.89, indicating high interrater reliability. The codings of the more experienced coder were used for subsequent data analysis.

## Coding Instrument and Procedure

The coding scheme was based on previous research and pre-study interviews with practitioners, and was developed prior to data collection $[100, 101]$ . A pre-test was conducted and necessary adjustments were made prior to the use of the coding scheme on subject protocols. This evolutionary approach provides an established framework based on prior research, yet allows for refinements to accommodate actual behavior exhibited in the pre-test protocols $[99]$ . To ensure consistency in the coding process, coding rules and procedures were developed to facilitate and guide the process.

The coding scheme used in this study specifies expected components of systems analysts' domain-specific knowledge and problem-solving behaviors. Previous studies on system analysts' problem-solving processes identified specific categories of analysts' knowledge and behaviors [100, 101]. In these studies, eight categories of essential systems analysis knowledge were derived from a review of the research literature, consultation with systems researchers and expert systems analyst practitioners, and the codified body of systems analysis knowledge. While that analysis is not reconstituted here, the reader is encouraged to review the literature [20, 21, 57, 100, 104, 105, 106]. The eight knowledge categories specified were organizational structure, organizational behavior, organizational politics, functional requirements, systems development, information technology, individual behavior, and corporate characteristics. Detailed knowledge areas within each category were then identified, and definitions were developed to facilitate the coding process. These knowledge categories were used to test H1 and H2. (See appendix B for knowledge category codes.)

Existing categories representing types of problem-solving behaviors employed by systems analysts were also utilized $[100, 101]$ . These categories represented mental behaviors typically used by systems analysts to solve problems. The five categories of problem-solving behaviors were trigger identification, hypothesis management, goal setting, strategy use, and heuristics. Specific problem-solving behaviors associated with each category were specified and corresponding definitions were developed to facilitate coding. These behavioral categories were used to test hypotheses 4 through 8. (See appendix C for category codes and definitions.)

## Data Analysis

Prior to analyzing the data, the coded verbal protocols were converted to percentage frequencies. This procedure normalizes the protocols and allows for comparisons among subjects [87]. The use of percentage frequencies scales individual differences to a common denominator and permits the occurrence of each knowledge or behavior category in an individual subject's protocol to be used as a measure of the relative importance of that category in the subject's entire problem-solving episode. This procedure has been used in previous work with practicing systems analysts [100, 101]. The resulting measure is called the percent frequency measure of occurrence.

Both quantitative and qualitative analyses were used in this study. The quantitative analysis performed was the k-sample median test for comparisons of more than two groups. This measure was used to investigate differences in the number of verbalizations among the novice, high-rated experienced, and low-rated experienced groups. The k-sample median test is appropriate when measuring group differences where the underlying population distribution is unknown, the sample size is small, and the measures are nominal or ordinal in scale, thus making it an appropriate test for this study $[19, 87]$ . In addition to these quantitative comparisons, qualitative analysis was used to assess differences in the types of problem-solving behavior verbalized by novice and expert analysts.

Recall that the major goal of this study was to examine differences in the requirements analysis process between novice and expert systems analysts. Our comparisons, therefore, focus primarily on differences between novice and high-rated (or more expert) experienced analysts. Differences between the high-rated and low-rated experienced groups are discussed where appropriate. For additional discussion on differences in problem-solving approaches between high- and low-rated experienced systems analysts, see $[100, 101]$ .

## Findings and Discussion

General Findings

OVERALL, THE VERBAL PROTOCOLS INDICATED DIFFERENCES in the problem-solving processes used by novices and experienced systems analysts. Differences in the quantity of verbalizations and task duration, as well as level of detail, were evident.

## Quantity of Verbalizations/Task Duration

Prior research, using more structured tasks, has suggested that expert problem solving may be characterized by many automatic processes, resulting in less verbalization than occurs with novices $[88]$ . However, because of the complexity and unstructured nature of an information requirements task, it is not surprising that we found different results. The number of novice protocol statements ranged from 89 to 290 (median = 168), while the number of expert protocol statements ranged from 146–509 (median = 414). Observed differences in the number of protocol statements verbalized reflect variations in the duration of problem-solving episodes of subjects. Novice problem-solving episodes averaged 37 minutes (range = 23–47 minutes) in contrast to 58 minutes (range = 40–90 minutes) for experienced analysts.

## Findings Corresponding to Hypotheses

## Knowledge Availability (H1 and H2)

Recall that the first two hypotheses addressed knowledge availability, specifically, the verbalization of domain-specific issues (H1) and user involvement (H2). As posited in hypothesis 1, novices verbalized fewer domain-specific issues than did experts. Analysis of the protocols indicates that the percentage frequency of domain-specific issues verbalized by novices is 62 percent, compared with 84 percent by experts. Given novices' limited episodic knowledge, it is not surprising that they verbalized fewer domain-specific issues than experts, who have richer and more detailed cognitive schemata on which to draw [15, 44, 56]. Findings also show a larger number of null, or zero, frequency categories among novices than experts. Specifically, of the original 150 domain-specific knowledge categories (see appendix B), novices failed to verbalize 71 of the categories; experts failed to verbalized only 31. These results show a greater breadth of domain-specific knowledge among experts compared to novices.

The study findings were also consistent with the expectations set out in hypothesis 2, that is, novices verbalized user involvement more frequently than did experts (p < 0.01). Table 2 reports the percent frequency verbalizations for the most frequently verbalized knowledge categories, ranked by group. Ranking the knowledge categories allows for examination of the relative importance of each specific category. User involvement ranked fifth for novices and eighteenth for expert analysts.

To examine this finding in more detail, ordinal effects within the protocol statements were examined. The data were analyzed in blocks of 10 percent of total statements (10 percent, 20 percent, etc.). This analysis reveals that 57.9 percent of the user involvement verbalizations occurred in the first 20 percent of novice protocol statements. An additional 24.1 percent occurred in the second 20 percent. In contrast, only 17.4 percent of user involvement verbalizations occurred in the first 20 percent of expert protocol statements. Another 31 percent occurred in the second 20 percent of protocol statements, and 18.6 percent occurred in the third 20 percent of statements. Given the importance of the user relationship in the information requirements task, verbalization throughout the task would be expected if this concern were more than superficial. These findings indicate that, although novices focused on user involvement early in the information requirements task, this level of concern was not maintained. In contrast, expert analysts maintained an interest in user issues throughout the information requirements task.

Twenty-two knowledge categories account for over 50 percent of all knowledge categories appearing in the subjects' protocols. Comparisons between novices and experts reveal several interesting patterns. For example, whereas the experts focused heavily on functional requirements and information issues (e.g., report types, information requirements, database requirements), the novices jumped from very specific issues (e.g., source data) to more general issues (e.g., purpose of system, development issues).

Although the rankings of the knowledge categories by the high-rated/expert and low-rated experienced analysts were similar overall, two differences can be found across the three groups. First, novices ranked implementation thirteenth; high-rated/expert analysts ranked this issue twenty-fourth; low-rated analysts ranked it forty-eighth. Second, novices ranked database requirements thirty-fifth; high-rated/expert analysts ranked this issue seventh; low-rated analysts ranked it nineteenth. These rankings illustrate differences between high- and low-rated experienced analysts in the perceived importance of these particular issues. Although the rankings, overall, imply a high degree of agreement between high- and low-rated experienced analysts regarding the perceived importance of these knowledge categories, large differences pertaining to critical systems analysis knowledge do exist.

Findings in this study support, in part, Vessey and Conger's work [98] on the interaction of application knowledge and methodology and the major role that application knowledge plays in specifying information requirements. As expected, a larger percentage of application domain references were found in the experienced analysts' protocols. Although over 40 percent of experts' most frequently verbalized knowledge categories were application domain references, only 28 percent of novices' verbalizations referred to these issues. These findings indicate that novices are less likely than experts to have adequate application domain knowledge, even when addressing a familiar (accounts receivable system) application. Since this study did not address methodology issues, further comparisons with Vessey and Conger's work could not be made.

## Knowledge Organization (H3)

The third hypothesis addressed the organization of knowledge, specifically, problem structuring (H3). Analysis of the verbal protocols revealed recurring themes in both the novices' and experts' verbalizations during the problem-solving task. The experts' protocols indicate a strong focus on the accounts collection clerks and a more bottom-up approach to problem solving. The novices' protocols suggest more concern with determining management needs and employing a top-down approach. These differences exhibited in the protocols suggest that novices, by reason of their limitations, may rely on a top-down approach since they do not have a well-developed knowledge structure available to them from prior experience. This novice approach is the most visible and obvious structure to apply to an analysis problem because it mirrors the apparent structure of an organization and is consistent with stereotypic power and authority. Thus, novices, lacking proper knowledge organization, must begin at the top, given their limited and stereotypic understanding about how organizations operate (i.e., top management support is required, top-down movement assures success). However, the top-down approach without an overall model of problem structure is a weak method [17, 69]. Experts, on the other hand, already have an understanding of the problem's deep structures. They are looking for the most efficient way to populate their internal representation of the problem. This is not to imply that experts have no need to structure the problem. On the contrary, they add structure according to the unique cues and characteristics of the problem.

Prior research supports this view of novice and expert knowledge structures. For example, Vessey's [97] study of novice and expert programmers found that novices tended to employ weak methods while performing debugging tasks. Novice programmers took longer to debug programs, employed a breadth-first approach, and did not formulate an overall program structure model. This combination of using a breadth-first approach without an overall model was associated with a greater number of errors in the problem-solving process [71]. These findings are consistent with other programming studies on problem-solving strategies, problem models [65], and problem representations [10, 63]. Polson and Jeffries [1983] suggest that novices are less able to select among, or integrate, conflicting criteria into a coherent structure during the problem-solving process. These weaknesses lead to shallow understanding and the use of general strategies more akin to trial and error behavior [76].

## Hypothesis Management (H5a, H5b, and H5c)

These findings address three hypotheses based on problem-solving behaviors, specifically, hypothesis verbalization (H5a), hypothesis testing (H5b), and discarding hypotheses (H5c). Analysis indicates no significant quantitative differences between the novice and expert groups in hypothesis verbalization $p = 0.544$ . Both novices and experts verbalized hypotheses with similar frequency during the problem-solving episode. This finding is consistent with the expectations stated in H5a.

Quantitative differences between novices and experts and were found in both the test and discard hypothesis behaviors (p = 0.007 and 0.001, respectively). Experts verbalized hypothesis testing and discarding behaviors more frequently than did novice analysts. These findings are consistent with expectations stated in hypotheses H5b and H5c.

Interestingly, in the discard hypothesis behavior, high-rated/expert experienced analysts were also significantly different from low-rated experienced analysts. Experts verbalized a higher frequency of discarding hypotheses during the information requirements task than did low-rated experienced analysts. This reluctance to discard hypotheses is likely to present problems for low-rated experienced analysts as well as novices. Hypotheses that are not consciously discarded may eventually be forgotten or become accepted facts. Forgotten hypotheses are never tested and may lead to omitted systems requirements. Untested hypotheses that are incorporated into the requirements solution may become incorrect systems features, reflecting an incomplete or erroneous view of the user's problem.

Overall, in hypothesis management, the findings indicate several differences between novices and experienced analysts. Based on these findings and prior research, it is suggested that although novices appear capable of generating hypotheses based primarily on semantic knowledge, their ability to filter irrelevant information is limited. Sparse episodic knowledge makes it difficult for novices to evaluate hypotheses that they have generated but may not have the knowledge and cognitive skills necessary for conducting effective hypothesis assessment. This lack of knowledge limits novices' ability to evaluate a hunch and subject it to a test. These assessment skills are likely to depend on an ability to match hypotheses with existing knowledge or information obtained from the problem. If this interpretation is correct, it may explain novices' indecision and unwillingness to form conclusions and achieve closure. An inability to evaluate and subsequently discard unusable hypotheses (thereby retaining them in active memory) is cognitively taxing to analysts who are already dealing with a complex task. These limitations may also be a factor in low-rated experienced analysts' inability to move from experienced analysts to experts.

For example, subject K indicates that he or she would “go to the president to see if they’re really behind it.”

## Heuristics (H8)

Hypothesis H8 addressed problem-solving behavior, specifically, heuristics. In this study, heuristics behavior is surprising in its absence. Study findings indicate that heuristics accounted for only 1.9 percent of verbalizations for all subjects combined. No significant quantitative differences between novices and experts were evident in heuristic verbalizations (p = 0.850), nor were differences between groups found in the qualitative analysis. In this problem-solving task, neither experts nor novices indicated much use of heuristics. These results are surprising given that the use and value of heuristics in problem-solving situations have been established in the research literature [17, 95, 96]. One possible explanation for the low occurrence of heuristic verbalizations in all groups focuses on the verbalizations themselves. According to Sherman and Corty [86], heuristic use is often informal and occurs without awareness on the part of the problem solver. Thus, it is feasible that heuristic behavior did indeed occur in one or more groups, but because the subjects were not aware of this behavior, they did not verbalize the heuristics.

## Conclusions and Implications

THE RESULTS OF THIS STUDY SUGGEST MAJOR DIFFERENCES between novice and expert systems analysts' approaches to requirements analysis. These differences are evident in the areas of domain-specific knowledge, problem structuring, hypothesis management (i.e., actively testing and discarding hypotheses), goal setting and strategy use. Some of these findings are consistent with prior research on problem solving and with the expectations set forth in this study. Additional unexpected results help highlight differences in the cognitive processes used by novice and expert analysts. Taken together, these results emphasize the complex nature of the requirements analysis task.

Most important, the study indicates that both quantitative and qualitative differences are found in the problem-solving behaviors of novices and expert systems analysts. Quantitative differences indicate that novices perform some problem-solving behaviors to a greater or lesser degree than do experts. Where novices verbalized a higher frequency of strategies in their problem solving, they verbalized fewer goal, test hypothesis, and discard hypothesis behaviors. Qualitative differences are a value-added procedure for highlighting some important performance differences. Qualitative analysis indicates that novice analysts tend to work on problem components at a general level. Their protocols contain statements, assertions, and conclusions that lack the specificity and detail characteristics of the experts' protocols. Their strategic thinking tends to be characterized by lists of action items or tasks that should be done rather than attainable or detailed strategic steps.

As indicated earlier, a major distinction in the cognitive processing of novices and experienced/expert analysts is seen in their use of the knowledge categories. The novice systems analyst's knowledge base is characterized best by its sparseness, lack of breadth, and generality. Moreover, as shown in Table 2, the rank order indicates that the priority placed on various knowledge categories differs between novices and experienced systems analysts. Novices focus more on systems development activities and systems components and less on functional requirements, information requirements, systems procedures, and organizational issues than do more experienced analysts.

## Error-proneness

This study helps to develop a perspective on error-proneness in novice analyst behavior. These findings give some understanding of the role experience plays in developing systems analysis expertise. It is useful to extrapolate from the results of this study and identify potential areas where the novice might be error-prone. The following conclusions should be viewed as preliminary and suggestive of future directions for research.

Five observed differences between experienced analysts and novices suggest potential bases for error-proneness in novices: (1) less domain knowledge utilization, (2) superficial concern with user involvement, (3) use of general versus specific triggers, (4) less hypothesis testing and rejection, and (5) less goal generation.

1. Novices appear to have less knowledge about the problem domain. The large number of zero frequency categories suggests that the novice's knowledge base is much more constrained and less elaborate than that of experienced systems analysts. Because they rely on less elaborate knowledge bases, novices are less likely to perceive important problem components and may have difficulty asking the most appropriate questions during information gathering interviews. Although less domain knowledge is expected with novices, the systems analysts' role is often one of guidance when determining users' needs. Overlooking important questions during the requirements phase, can lead to missing critical components in systems construction.

2. Another problem-solving limitation for novices concerns user involvement. First, novices tended to verbalize about user involvement more than did expert analysts. Although there has been continued discussion concerning the measurement and/or terminology of user involvement, research literature identifies user involvement/participation as extremely important in systems development [16, 43, 90]. This lack of focus by novices is disconcerting. Although the novices spent considerable effort on user-analyst relationship issues, these concerns were stated early in the problem session and not carried throughout the process as might be expected for successful systems analysis. Indeed, very little consideration was given to several behavioral issues, particularly organizational politics. The importance of a variety of behavioral issues is often seen in traditional systems analysis and design texts and courses. Errors of omission concerning behavioral issues could lead to failure to identify correct information requirements and gain user confidence.

3. Novices in this study also tended to react to general rather than specific triggers in the problem-solving process. Although the study found no differences between the novices and experts in the frequency of verbalizations of perceive and search for triggers, the qualitative analysis revealed a noticeable lack of detail in the triggers employed by the novice analysts. The use of general triggers implies that novices derive less information from the task environment than do experts. This reliance on general triggers is consistent with the conceptual framework discussed earlier. Novices' lack of analyst knowledge and reliance on limited cognitive schemata could prevent them from recognizing triggers that require prior knowledge and experience. The recognition of cues or triggers in the task environment is vital to activating prior knowledge and to structuring the problem. This limitation could lead to less comprehensive requirements analysis or to a more time-consuming requirements analysis process, since novices begin with less information about the problem's potential scope. Moreover, novices verbalized a greater number of zero domain-specific categories and generated fewer goals, suggesting that they had more difficulty in identifying problem components and evaluating systems requirements than did the experts.

4. Although no differences were found between novices and experts in the number of state hypotheses behaviors during the problem-solving task, the novices in this study had less verbalization that they tested or that they discarded hypotheses than the experts had. As a result, it is reasonable to assume that novices may have retained more hypotheses throughout the problem-solving process. The failure to reject hypotheses can contribute to three potential errors. First, novices can forget to disconfirm a hypothetical requirement and thus erroneously include that hypothesis as a final specification requirement. Second, novices may forget a potentially correct requirement through lack of attention to the hypothesis generated. Third, novices reduce their problem-solving effectiveness by retaining active hypotheses. Active hypotheses increase cognitive load during the problem-solving process since analysts must retain all the hypotheses in active memory [70, 72, 92]. The lack of hypothesis rejection may also force novices to retain low-probability solution paths in the problem space, thus increasing unnecessary problem complexity. Discarding incorrect hypotheses is one way that problem solvers can reduce the size and complexity of the problem space, thus narrowing the search to fruitful areas of inquiry. This failure to test and reject hypotheses actively can lead to the inclusion of incorrect hypotheses as system requirements, incomplete requirements specification, and unnecessary complexity or time spent in the solution process. Interestingly, this failure to discard hypotheses, thereby increasing the analysts' cognitive load, was also found among the low-rated experienced analysts. Again, lack of hypothesis rejection may play an important role in the progression of novice to expert beyond increasing experience.

5. Novices tended to generate fewer goals than did experts. Goal generation is key to planning and strategy generation [15, 51]. Although novices generated more strategies than did experts, strategies without appropriate goals tend to be weak and, in the worst case, a random walk without an overall goal structure. Limited goal generation could also lead to an incompletely structured problem. Minimal goal generation implies that the problem has not been decomposed into subproblems. Decomposition aids in solution management and permits problem solvers to focus on particular problem aspects and identify appropriate knowledge domains [88, 89].

Little goal generation and incomplete problem structuring can lead to an ad-hoc method for requirements discovery because a requirements analysis plan is incomplete or virtually absent. One possible explanation is that novices, unlike experts, may use a more random and less structured problem-solving approach, overlooking the incremental development of the solution $[17]$ . The lack of goals exhibited by novices is likely to limit novices' ability to focus on fruitful solution regions. This supports conventional wisdom that often characterizes the novice as one who cannot focus or who flounders in the face of complexity. In terms of error-proneness, minimal goal generation and inadequate problem structuring could lead to incomplete requirements, excessive backtracking in the solution process, and perhaps in the worst case, failure to construct a satisfactory requirements document.

Examining areas of novice error-proneness found in this study helps us understand the incomplete knowledge base of novice analysts. This limited knowledge base prevents novices from deriving maximum information content from the task environment, discarding misleading or erroneous hypotheses, setting goals, and developing operational plans.

## Study Limitations

The greatest limitation of the current study is sample size. Small subject numbers decrease the statistical power for comparisons as well as the generalizability of the study's findings. The small sample size is primarily a function of the study methodology. Protocol analysis has been used extensively as an effective method for in-depth examination of cognitive behaviors in several related fields. This methodology is recognized as extremely labor-intensive, and the majority of protocol studies have utilized relatively small sample sizes. For example, Mackay and Elam [60] used a total of twelve subjects (four groups of three subjects each) for a novice-expert study on the interaction of domain knowledge and decision aid use. Adelson [1] compared expert and novice computer programmers using five subjects in each group. Bouwman [8] used three experts and five novices to compare accountants in a decision-making task. Thus, the sample size used in this study (seven novice and eighteen experienced systems analysts) is robust in comparison with prior studies. Despite the appropriateness of the sample size used in this study for protocol analysis, the small sample size does limit the generalizability of this study's findings. The high level of consistency of academic training among novices coupled with the wide variety of organizations represented among both the novice and experienced analyst groups, however, strengthens the generalizability of these findings.

A second limitation of the current study also has to do with the methodology. It is possible that the verbalization process itself interfered with analyst performance, possibly affecting novices to a greater extent than experienced analysts. First, the artificial nature of subject verbalizations during the problem-solving task should be recognized. Few analysts would verbalize aloud large portions of their problem-solving processes in a real-world situation. Second, previous research indicates that novices may have more difficulty verbalizing their thoughts than experts [49, 88]. Third, non-verbalizations cannot necessarily be equated with non-thought; non-verbalization may be evidence of heuristic information processing [88]. Thus, this study may provide an incomplete picture of novice problem-solving behavior. In light of these limitations, additional research is clearly warranted.

## Study Implications

Requirements analysis requires complex problem-solving behaviors that significantly affect subsequent systems development. The major objective of the study was to provide insight into similarities and differences in how novice and expert systems analysts approach the requirements analysis task. The findings of this study have important implications for educators, researchers, and systems analysts.

## Education

Understanding of these similarities and differences provides a foundation for educators to facilitate the transition of analysts from novice to expert. Recognizing that expertise is more than the accumulation of experience [68], educators are called upon to expand analysts' knowledge domain and enhance their problem-solving skills. The results of this study indicate that novices approach the requirements analysis task differently from experts. (For example, novices in this study discarded fewer hypotheses, tended to search for less specific triggers, and set fewer goals than did experts.) Given the nature of these findings, several educational techniques may prove useful to improve the performance of novice analysts. Creativity-enhancement tools and techniques appear particularly promising for improving the requirements analysis process. Requirements analysis is a complex problem-solving task: Successful requirements analysis depends on accurate problem definition and problem analysis. Couger [22] discusses numerous difficulties inherent in the problem-solving process, including failure (1) to recognize the existence of a problem, (2) to define the correct problem, (3) to use available information, (4) to recognize or question assumptions, (5) to consider a wide range of alternatives, and (6) to address implementation issues. In general, novices in this study experienced more of these “difficulties” than did experts. Creativity enhancement techniques, including Creative Problem Solving (CPS) and various creativity support systems appear promising in facilitating the novice-expert transition. For example, more and more software tools that help analysts focus on critical mental triggers are available [34, 66, 94]. Preliminary evidence of the benefits of creativity techniques is promising [58, 63]. The findings in this study also indicate differences in discard hypothesis behavior between high- and low-rated experienced systems analysts. Creativity-enhancing techniques may also prove beneficial for improving the performance of low-rated experienced analysts. These techniques may help low performers avoid errors and make more rational decisions, thus facilitating their transition from experienced to expert analyst. The work of Couger and others [22, 23, 33] suggests the value of teaching skills like creativity. Supporting this trend, the 1995 joint ACM-AIS-DPMA model IS curriculum explicitly includes creativity content.

Educators should also consider incorporating more “real-world” simulations, realistic cases, and student internships into the educational process in an effort to enhance analysts’ expertise and provide additional opportunities to deal with the complexity inherent in the requirements analysis task. Experience with domain-relevant situations would increase the analysts’ exposure to the complexity of the systems analysis task and allow them to practice domain-specific skills. This would encourage analysts to enhance their episodic knowledge—knowledge that is critical for the transition from novice to expert. These real-world experiences would provide for realistic problem solving that cannot be duplicated through traditional textbook approaches to systems analysis education.

## Research

These findings also suggest interesting directions for future research. The transition from novice to expert systems analyst is a complex, multifaceted process that demands additional study, both in exploring differences in problem-solving behaviors utilized by novices and experts and in developing specific techniques to facilitate the transition. This study identified specific weaknesses that divide novice and expert analysts—weaknesses that should be investigated further. First, trigger utilization appears to be a key factor in analyst expertise, especially in structuring the analysis task and identifying critical subproblems. More research on the differences in trigger utilization between novice and expert analysts, thus, is needed, as is further development of software support tools to aid analysts in the problem-structuring/solving process (e.g., Mindlink Problem Solver '95) [66]. Second, novices appear to be severely constrained in their ability to evaluate hypotheses. Since effective hypothesis management is critical to successful requirements analysis, more research is required to understand the mechanisms that limit novice hypothesis testing and evaluation. Third, the relationship between goal setting and strategy formulation deserves further attention. The results of this study indicate that novices generate a large number of strategies, but few specific goals: Strategy generation appears to be a compensatory mechanism for dealing with the lack of problem-structuring activities and goal setting. If so, then additional research on the goal-strategy link and its relationship to problem structuring and planning could provide needed insight on problem decomposition skills and the most effective use of strategies during the problem-solving process. Additional research into techniques to develop analyst skills in hypothesis management is warranted.

Given that we now recognize some typical weaknesses exhibited by novices, how do we equip new analysts with the problem-solving skills necessary for successful requirements analysis? Zmud, Anthony, and Stair [107] examined the use of techniques such as mental imagery to elicit information requirements. Additional research in this area may reveal techniques beneficial to requirements analysis as well. Another fruitful area of work appears to be in examining creativity tools and methods, including Creativity Support Systems (CSS). Preliminary findings of the benefits of incorporating creativity-enhancing techniques into software are promising and increased interest in creativity with regard to systems analysis is encouraging $[22, 23, 33, 58, 62]$ . So far, few studies in the IS literature have focused on integrating creativity and problem-solving processes in organizational settings $[23, 29, 58]$ ; future research should be designed to address this void. Systems analysis is an extremely complex, unstructured problem-solving task, and preliminary research exploring the benefits of using decision support systems (DSS) for such applications is encouraging. Guidelines for modifying the design of DSS to support creative problem-solving tasks have been recommended, as has a framework for investigating the impact of CSS on creative problem-solving processes $[28, 29, 54]$ . Continued research will be necessary to study novices who have experience with these techniques. We need to understand what impact these techniques have on novices' problem-solving skills and develop strategies for effectively teaching these skills.

Investigation of automated decision aids is also warranted. For example, as CASE systems become more complex and automated design systems offload the more tedious tasks (e.g., documentation, screen generation, data dictionary) to the computer, analysts will be expected to do more thorough analysis and generate better requirements. What will be the interaction between the analyst and the automated aid in the requirements process? Current studies that focus on the impact of decision aids, decision making, and level of expertise are important preliminary steps in that direction [60, 93]. Finally, additional research is necessary in the area of determining requirements for different applications. Vessey and Conger's [98] work on the synergistic effects of application and methodology indicates that more attention needs to focus on matching types of applications to specific methodologies. Successful requirements determination may depend on the match between application knowledge and process methodology.

## Systems Analysis

This study's findings also have interesting implications for systems analysts—both novice and experienced. Because requirements analysis is such a vital component of the systems analysis process, identifying and developing tools to facilitate requirements analysis is vital to developing good systems analysts. Promising tools and techniques including creativity enhancement, mental imagery, and automated decision aids have been discussed above. These aids may be particularly useful to novice analysts who have a difficult time defining the problem and managing the complexity of the problem-solving process. Similarly, the value to novice systems analysts of acquiring “real-world” domain-relevant experience has also been suggested. Academic-business partnerships designed to provide realistic problem-solving opportunities (e.g., internships, practicum experiences) offer novice analysts opportunities to develop domain-specific skills. Such opportunities also allow systems analysts to increase the interpersonal and communication skills that are critical to their ability to identify good user requirements.

Organizations can also provide training opportunities that focus on developing broad analytical skills among their analysts in addition to technical skills training $[67]$ . For example, company-sponsored training to enhance analysts' conceptual skills, problem-solving abilities, and creativity should increase experienced analysts' ability to better recognize biases and error-proneness in the requirements analysis process.

This study identified some specific weaknesses that differentiated novices and expert analysts engaged in a requirements analysis task. Further efforts to identify and develop systematic approaches to eliminate these weaknesses are critical, and incorporating these techniques into our systems analysis education and development plans should be a priority.

## Accounts Receivable Subsystem

Company A, a large retail merchandising company, has fifty-three low-margin stories throughout the Midwest. Annual revenues at the close of the third quarter were \$205 million. Company revenues are growing at an annual rate of 25 percent. Company A's success stems from its ability to monitor customer demand for consumer items closely. The items are sold at high volumes at discount prices. Moving the merchandise is key to their success. Credit sales constitute a large portion of sales, permitting the large-volume merchandising strategy. The company employs over 2,000 employees. Corporate offices are located in Chicago.

Company A recently decided to develop a new accounts-receivable system. The new system will replace an existing accounts-receivable system completed in 1973. The current system has been patched, repatched, and expanded through equipment generations. Besides these technical difficulties, a new system is required because both the internal and external auditors and management have proposed enhancements. The enhancements are incompatible with the current system's processing logic and program structure.

The new system will support the accounts-collection clerks, the assistant controller, the controller, the vice president of finance, and the assistant to the vice president of finance. The accounts-collection clerks monitor the status of particular accounts. The assistant controller and controller are responsible for collection procedures and monitoring the age of the receivables. The vice president of finance is responsible for establishing a sound credit policy. Tentative approval for the system has been secured from the systems planning committee, the director of information systems, and the vice president of finance.

## APPENDIX C

Category and Code Definitions for Problem-Solving Behaviors

Trigger—A trigger is a stimulus in the case situation which spurs the analyst to draw some conclusion, request additional information, generate hypotheses, set goals, develop strategies or apply heuristics.

TR01 Verbalize Trigger: This code is assigned when the behavior as recorded in the transcribed protocol indicates that the subject has drawn attention to a stimulus or clue in the case situation.

TR02 Search for Trigger: This code is assigned when the behavior as recorded in the transcribed protocol indicates that the subject is searching for or expects to find a particular unit of information or clue in the case situation.

Hypothesis—The hypothesis code refers to behavior wherein the subject posits a statement about a particular situation with intent to verify or test this presumption. The subject could be generating a hypothetical construction about the problem situation for later evaluation.

HY01 Verbalize Hypothesis: This code is assigned when the subject states, in advance, a presumption about the problem situation which is open to verification.

HY02 Test Hypothesis: This code is assigned when the subject seeks to verify or test a previously stated hypothesis.

HY03 Discard Hypothesis: This code is assigned when the subject determines that a particular hypothesis is invalid and rejects that hypothesis.

Goal—The goal code refers to behavior wherein the subject indicates a desired state and determines that the desired state should be attained.

GL01 Verbalize Goal: This code is assigned when the subject verbalizes a desired end state.

Strategy—The strategy code refers to behavior wherein the subject indicates an approach, a way to proceed as a plan of attack for solving the problem situation.

ST01 Verbalize Strategy: This code is assigned when the subject verbalizes an

approach to the solution of the problem.

Heuristic—The heuristic code refers to behavior wherein the subject incorporates the use of a rule based on prior experience or general guidelines based on episodic knowledge that provides a simplified way to make a choice. The rule is usually nondeducible.

HU01 Verbalize Heuristic: This code is assigned when the subject verbalizes a rule based upon experience.
