# A Theoretically Grounded Approach to Assist Memory Recall During Information Requirements Determination

JANETTE W. MOODY, J. ELLIS BLANTON, AND PAUL H. CHENEY

JANETTE W. MOODY is an Assistant Professor in the Business Administration Department at The Citadel in Charleston, South Carolina, where she teaches in the areas of management information systems and accounting. Prior to receiving her Ph.D. in information systems and decision sciences from the University of South Florida in Tampa, Florida, Dr. Moody worked in various corporations, including GTE, Price Waterhouse, CPAs, and Eastern Airlines. Her research interests include the behavioral and communication aspects of systems development and she has published in various journals, including MIS Quarterly, Journal of Systems Management, and Expert Systems with Applications.

J. ELLIS BLANTON is an Associate Professor in the College of Business Administration at the University of South Florida. He received a Ph.D. in management information systems from the University of Georgia and has published in various journals, including MIS Quarterly, Information and Management, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, and the Journal of Information Technology Management. His primary research interests include the strategic use of information technology and the impact of information technology on organizations.

PAUL H. CHENEY is a Professor in the College of Business Administration at the University of South Florida in Tampa. He was formerly the chairman at Texas Tech University and has taught at the University of Georgia and Iowa State University. He has published two books in the information systems field and over thirty articles in such journals as MIS Quarterly, Decision Sciences, Data Base, and the Academy of Management Journal. He has also consulted with over 100 organizations, including Exxon, Ford, Coca Cola, and GTE.

ABSTRACT: The interview has long been a part of the system professional's repertoire of elicitation techniques, used extensively in both requirements analysis and knowledge acquisition. Unfortunately, although interviewing is a widely recommended elicitation technique, the literature offers little in the way of theoretically grounded support or advice on exactly how the interview should be conducted and what format should be used.

This paper reports the findings of a study that compared the efficiency and effectiveness of the cognitive interview with the standard information requirements interview in an experiment using reference librarians as interviewees. The cognitive interview was found to be both more effective and more efficient than standard interviewing techniques in eliciting episodic knowledge from reference librarians. Use of the cognitive interview resulted in a richer recall, in terms of both breadth and depth, of details relevant to the task domain.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: cognitive interview, communication tools, information requirements determination, information systems development tools, interviewing.

THE IMPORTANCE AS WELL AS THE DIFFICULTY OF ELICITING user information needs during information systems development has been well recognized and documented [59]. Whether done by a systems analyst and called requirements analysis (RA), or by a knowledge engineer and called knowledge acquisition (KA), considerable overlap exists in the skills required and the techniques utilized [14]. One elicitation technique that has been widely advocated in both RA and KA is the interview [14, 31, 53]. Unfortunately, the interview has been characterized as a weak method that collects data that are “imprecise, incomplete, and inconsistent” [8, p. 132] and has not been theoretically grounded.

Other disciplines, such as criminology and epidemiology investigations, have had to face concerns about using interviewing as an elicitation technique. The outcome of these concerns has been the development of a theoretically grounded interview process called the cognitive interview [18]. The cognitive interview incorporates five principles of memory retrieval to aid in the interviewee's recall. This paper reports the findings of a study that compared the efficiency and effectiveness of the cognitive interview (CI) with the standard information requirements interview (SI) in an experiment using forty-two reference librarians as interviewees.

## Research Framework

## The Standard Information Requirements Interview

THE INTERVIEW IS A FREQUENTLY CITED ELICITATION TECHNIQUE [1, 14, 47, 53]. Unfortunately, although interviewing is widely recommended, the literature offers little in the way of theoretically grounded support or advice on exactly how the interview should be conducted and what type should be used. Boose [8] notes that the usual procedure is for the systems professional to use “common sense” when interviewing experts. Liou and Nunamaker [35] characterize unstructured interviews as starting with the question, “How do you solve this problem?” (p. 122). LaFrance [34] provides somewhat more guidance by offering a matrix of six question types designed to question the interviewee at various levels of specificity, while Kendall and Kendall [31] provide an overview of the mechanics of preparing and conducting an interview.

Although MIS researchers have recognized the need for “systematic probing interviews [to] pursue the relevant areas to a greater depth” [22, pp. 107–108], little has been done empirically to evaluate the interview as an elicitation technique. Instead, the literature is dominated by anecdotal reports on the usefulness of various elicitation techniques, including interviews, in developing specific systems [5, 11, 40].

Several studies have evaluated the interview in comparison with other techniques, with mixed results $[1, 12]$ . Burton et al. $[12]$ experimentally compared four techniques (interviews, protocol analysis, laddered grids, and card sorting) in terms of efficiency (time required to conduct a session and time required to convert transcripts to rules) and effectiveness (the completeness of an elicited rule as established relative to a senior expert's rule standard). They found that protocol analysis took longer and gave less information than did the structured interview.

Agarwal and Tanniru [1] conducted a field experiment to evaluate the effectiveness and efficiency of structured versus unstructured interviews, using both novice and experienced interviewers. The experimental results did not indicate that structured interviews enhanced recall. In addition, the knowledge elicited could not be compared with an objective criterion to assess its relative value, nor was there a theoretical basis for the selection of the interview method. The cognitive interview, discussed in the next section, represents one such candidate technique that would specifically address these elicitation needs.

## The Cognitive Interview

In order to understand how to assist interviewers in information retrieval, it is necessary to understand how humans store and use knowledge $[7]$ . Cognitive psychologists have identified two dimensions of knowledge. One dimension involves process aspects, such as procedural and declarative knowledge, while the other dimension involves organizational aspects, such as the semantic and the episodic. The cognitive interview is designed to tap episodic knowledge. Before explaining the cognitive interview in detail, we present an overview of these two dimensions of knowledge.

Procedural knowledge is described as “knowing how,” while declarative knowledge is “knowing that” [7, p. 8]. Procedural knowledge usually connotes the knowledge that facilitates skillful actions such as riding a bicycle or playing tennis. Declarative knowledge, on the other hand, consists of related facts that can be organized and reorganized as the occasion demands.

Episodic and semantic knowledge are distinctive in their organization in memory. Episodic knowledge is autobiographical and experiential, organized by time and place, and described by its perceptual characteristics $[6]$ . As such, it is highly individual—that is, autonoetic $[6]$ . For example, if one were asked where he was on the fourth Friday in November 1963, it is unlikely he could recall the information based on a mental “perpetual calendar” organization. However, if the question were “Where were you when John F. Kennedy was shot?,” retrieval could be activated more readily, based on its highly autobiographical nature.

Semantic knowledge, on the other hand, is composed of words, symbols, and facts organized in a hierarchy of relationships, untied to any temporal or spatial referents and thus not dependent upon context $[7, 48]$ . This organization provides the basis for a degree of universal commonality to the organization of word associations, despite the fact that no two persons have had identical experiences $[7]$ .

These four categories of knowledge, depicted in figure 1, have been identified as relevant to the development of information systems [37]. Episodic knowledge provides the organization of the events (or scenarios) in which end users participated while performing tasks and is therefore particularly relevant for providing specifics about the task domain. Constructing future scenarios through mental imagery, as advocated in [59], has been used in attempts to identify events that have not actually occurred. The cognitive interview, on the other hand, enhances the recall of actual scenarios—that is, real previous events.

The cognitive interview was designed to elicit episodic knowledge by incorporating principles of memory retrieval, cognition, and communication $[18, 21]$ . The cognitive interview has been found to provide enhanced recall compared with that produced by the standard interview (i.e., one with no mnemonic instructions) when used in controlled experiments in the lab and in the field $[19, 21, 23, 24]$ .

The cognitive interview focuses on two major components of the interview session: memory and communication [18]. When using the cognitive interview (CI), the systems professional (hereafter called the interviewer) uses five principles of memory retrieval to guide the end user (hereafter called the interviewee) in accessing stored information. Since it is the interviewee who must access and convey the desired information, it is important that the interviewer recognize that the interviewee is the one who holds the information to be accessed, and therefore is the central figure, with the interviewer playing a secondary role. Since the interview was initiated by the systems professional, there may be a tendency to see these roles as reversed. The interviewer's job is to use the principles of retrieval to guide the interviewee in retrieving the information (Table 1).

In addition to accessing the information, the interviewee must also be able to communicate it effectively. The CI enhances the communicative aspects of the interview by helping the interviewee articulate a complete response while helping the interviewer understand and record that response [18]. Establishing and sustaining rapport with the interviewee is enhanced by the CI's emphasis on the interviewee's unique mental representation of the events under investigation.

The CI is based on five principles of memory retrieval and their associated empirical support, as depicted in Table 1. The first and second principles, context recreation and focused concentration, set the stage for enhanced memory recall and are applicable throughout the interview. The remaining three principles provide specific guidance for activating recall through the use of appropriately directed cues.

The context recreation principle indicates that recall is enhanced if the physical and psychological stimuli surrounding the original event are recreated $[9, 10, 25]$ . We have all experienced going into a room to retrieve something only to find that once there we have forgotten the purpose of the trip. Often, by returning to the place of the thought's origin, we can have immediate recall of the purpose. In an interview, this principle is enacted by having the interviewee mentally replicate his physical surroundings and emotional state at the time of the event being recalled.

The principle of focused concentration posits that there are limits to our mental abilities such that task performance will decline if a difficult or unpracticed task is attempted simultaneously with other tasks [33]. Memory retrieval during information systems requirements determination is a mentally demanding task and any interference with the interviewee's concentration is an impediment [18, 22]. This principle is enacted by not interrupting the interviewee during a response, minimizing extraneous noises, gestures, and so forth.

Given that the interview conditions provide for context recreation and focused concentration, recall of event details still suffers if inappropriate search cues are used $[46]$ . Therefore, it is important to make multiple retrieval attempts $[18]$ . Because of the mental effort required in memory retrieval, many interviewees will indicate they have completed their recall after a few initial attempts. The principle of multiple retrieval is enacted by encouraging interviewees to search through memory even if they say they have thought of everything. Repeated attempts at recall require that the interviewer rephrase the question at least subtly in order to avoid the appearance of badgering. It is important that these additional questions not be just a repetition of the original questions but include variations in the wording to provide alternative retrieval routes. This leads into the fourth and fifth principles of the CI.

The remaining two principles of the CI, the principles of varied retrieval and multiple representation, are closely related. The principle of multiple representation attests to the concept that, when an event is experienced, it is stored in various formats and with different levels of precision, ranging from the very general to the very detailed $[17, 18]$ . The basis for this principle is found in schema theory $[2]$ . Schema theory can be summarized as follows:

Schemata are abstract knowledge structures whose elements are other schemata and slots, placeholders, or variables which can take on a restricted range of values. . . . A schema is structured in the sense that it indicates typical relationships among component elements. [2, p. 1]

This schemata facilitates information retrieval as well as information storage. Therefore, after people have stored information regarding an event, they will recall different information about that event, depending upon the schema being utilized at the time of the recall attempt.

The varied retrieval principle is enacted by using various cues to activate the multiple representations of memory of a single event. For example, an event may be represented in memory on the basis of its temporal order, its categorical features, and its sensory modalities $[17, 18, 50]$ . An interviewee recalling the event of handling a customer inquiry while on the help desk may recall certain details when probed regarding order of the events (“What was the customer’s opening question?” “Describe the solution steps you suggested in reverse order”). Other details of the same event would be recalled with questions about categories (“What was the customer’s most urgent question . . . most complex question . . . ?” etc.). Still further details may be recalled when the interviewee is probed regarding sensory modalities using questions such as, “During which question did the customer sound most agitated?” or “Can you remember seeing yourself reach for the hardware reference manual?”

As this discussion indicates, the principles of the CI are intended not to be a fixed, linear sequence of questions but rather to provide deeper insight into three basic concepts relevant to the recall of episodic knowledge: (1) the limitations of mental resources, (2) the variety of episodic representations in memory, and (3) appropriate cues (see appendix B and Table 1, application column) to activate memory retrieval. "The cognitive interview is not intended as a recipe" [18, p. 16]. The interviewer must be flexible enough to call upon these concepts as needed in the interview, each of which is unique. Although all of the details derived from these varied retrieval cues may not be required for the final information system, the very act of enhancing retrieval of the episodic memory results in a richer retrieval of pertinent details that might otherwise have been lost.

The next section describes a study comparing the cognitive interview with the standard information requirements interview to elicit recall of events that occurred in an organizational setting. The research question examined was:

Is the cognitive interview more efficient and effective in the recall of episodic knowledge than the standard information requirements interview?

## Method

## Field Experiment

THE RESEARCH PROJECT CONSISTED OF AN EXPERIMENT using forty-two reference librarians in eight academic library sites, in various private and public colleges and universities [41]. A reference librarian is trained to handle a wide range of patron inquiries. For example, the same librarian may handle an inquiry from a graduate student requiring elaborate and detailed citation searches on Elizabethan poets, and then may immediately have to assist a person from the community looking for information on laws regarding public access for the handicapped. The librarian's determination of how to resolve an inquiry will be based on an assessment of both the question and the questioner. This assessment can include the patron's age, educational level, native language, urgency, and other considerations [45].

The reference division has long utilized various manual methods of capturing this episodic knowledge of its staff members. Some units keep an index card file of problematic patron inquiries and the solutions. Others simply call upon more experienced staffers (if available) when handling a difficult question. The limitations of manual systems and the depletion of collective memory when staffers retire or relocate have led to increased development of computer-based information systems, including expert systems and neural networks $[13, 15, 32, 39, 58]$ .

The development of some of these information systems included having reference librarians write down the details of how they answered problem questions immediately after handling the problem [13]. This method is subject to varying degrees of success owing to the pressure of time from the next inquiring patron. Others suggested the use of interviews; however, little guidance is given regarding how to conduct the interviews and the interviewee's inability to articulate the process was a recurring concern [44].

The purpose of the study was to test the effectiveness and efficiency of the cognitive interview in eliciting episodic knowledge from professionals who are required to provide a customized solution to a variety of inquiries. Similar situations exist in the development of computer-based support for help desk personnel $[3, 4, 43]$ , front office workers in insurance companies $[36]$ , and property appraisers $[27]$ . In all these cases, each inquiry constitutes an event, an individual scenario, whose resolution (outcome), coupled with complete details of the event, is representative of the type of information needed to provide both domain knowledge and information items $[59]$ . The episodic knowledge to be recalled, therefore, is defined as an “event” that begins with the patron inquiry and ends with the librarian’s resolution of that inquiry.

Two hypotheses were tested. The first hypothesis tested the efficiency of the interview technique, that is, the number of events elicited per period of time:

H1: Use of the cognitive interview results in the recall of a greater number of events per interview than does use of the standard information requirements interview for a given period of time.

Hoffman [28] has stated that overall efficiency is “[p]erhaps the most important criterion in a practical sense” (p. 60) for a knowledge elicitation technique. The interviewee’s valuable time and the limited number of systems professionals are scarce resources that must be used efficiently.

The second hypothesis tested the effectiveness of the interview technique, that is, the completeness of details recalled per event.

H2: Use of the cognitive interview provides increased completeness of details recalled per event than does use of the standard information requirements interview.

In addition to providing efficiency, an elicitation technique should be effective; that is, it should provide “quality” information $[1, 12, 14]$ . This has also been called the “robustness” of the information items derived $[59]$ . Increased completeness of details recalled provide the systems analyst with enhanced domain knowledge, which in turn facilitates his or her ability to communicate with the user $[53]$ .

## Variables in the Study

The independent variable was the interview technique used to retrieve the reference librarian's episodic knowledge about past patron inquiries. Two interview techniques were used: the standard information requirements interview (SI) and the cognitive interview (CI). By random assignment, a reference librarian was interviewed using either the CI or the SI by an interviewer trained in both techniques. Each interviewer conducted an equal number of SIs and CIs, and each reference librarian was interviewed only once.

The SI is typically presented in the literature in very general terms $[47, 52]$ . Confirmatory evidence that systems analysts receive little or no formal training in interviewing techniques, often learning by observing others, was obtained by discussions with practicing systems analysts. For purposes of this study, the SI was consistent with Fisher and Quigley's $[21]$ definition of a standard interview as one that contains no mnemonic instructions. The SI was enacted by asking the reference librarian to recall and describe in as much detail as possible patron inquiries that he or she had handled. In the SI, the interviewer, following Waldron's $[52]$ suggested outline, did not use any cues to assist in memory recall (see appendix A for an outline of the SI). For example, when conducting a SI, the interviewer asked an open question, "Tell me about patron inquiries you have handled," a probing question, "What other details can you tell me about that inquiry and your resolution of it?," and a closed question, "How exactly is the OCLC used?," and provided a brief summary before moving on.

The CI focuses on reinstating the context of the original event, then using a variety of retrieval routes to activate recall $[18, 19]$ . These retrieval routes, discussed in the section on the cognitive interview, were adapted to meet the needs of the present task domain (appendix B illustrates this adaptation).

Two dependent variables were examined: (1) the number of events elicited per each interview (efficiency), and (2) the degree of completeness of details recalled per event (effectiveness). Details considered important in determining how to answer a patron inquiry include type of inquiry, length of encounter, evaluation of the patron's level of expertise and past attempts to resolve the question, time available to find a resolution, and so on [45, 56], plus the steps taken by the reference librarian to resolve the inquiry.

In prior research, efficiency has been operationalized as the number of facts, propositions, and if-then rules collected per task minute $[12, 28]$ and per page of interview transcript $[1]$ . In the proposed study, the efficiency of each interview technique was derived by counting the number of events elicited per interview session of controlled length.

The effectiveness of elicitation techniques has been operationalized in prior research in various ways. Zmud et al. [59] operationalized it as completeness of details (called “robustness”) in their research on scenario development for information requirements. In this study, the effectiveness of the interview technique was measured using a completeness of detail (COD) instrument, a seven-point rating scale indicating the degree of completeness of detail for each event.

In order for the cognitive interview to be considered an effective interviewing technique, more than one rater must determine that the degree of completeness of details of the events collected under the CI are higher than those collected under the SI. It is not necessary for both raters to agree on what constitutes “completeness of details”; rather, whatever their respective internal yardstick of this measurement, they must find events in the sample that reflect both ends of it. In fact, the hypothesis of significant differences would be more rigorously tested if put before one rater who uses a “liberal” standard and one who uses a more “conservative” standard. Significant differences found from a panel of raters with diverse views would support the generalizability of the findings to a wider population of raters.

A panel of two senior reference librarians (one man and one woman) who had no affiliations with the interviewees or each other independently evaluated each event using the COD instrument. Transcribed events were taken from the full-length interviews, coded for identification purposes, and presented to the panel in random order as stand-alone events. The COD instrument had been tested in a pilot study in which three raters used the COD to review five events. As a result of the pilot study, the COD's “Instructions to Raters” was modified to emphasize that only the completeness of relevant details of the event was being evaluated, not the manner in which the librarian in the event handled the inquiry. To avoid “learning” on the part of the raters, the pilot study raters were not used as raters or interviewees in the final study.

## Interviewees and Interviewers

Each of the forty-two interviewees (reference librarians) received only one interview treatment, randomly assigned and conducted when the librarian was not on duty. Demographics collected on interviewees confirmed no significant differences between the recipients of the SI versus recipients of the CI.

Neither the interviewees nor the supervisory librarians were made aware of the two interview techniques being tested. They were informed that the focus of the project was the collection of work events for the purpose of helping other universities plan and organize their reference areas.

The ten interviewers were students enrolled in a senior-level expert systems class who received training on the SI and the CI during regularly scheduled class time. The SI training consisted of the basic principles outlined in $[47]$ and $[31]$ (i.e., avoid interruptions, establish rapport, form simple and clear questions, etc.). The CI training incorporates these same basic principles, plus additional memory retrieval aides (see appendix B) based on the cognitive principles outlined earlier. The training sessions included role playing and mock interviews, as well as general background information on the knowledge domain of reference librarian tasks $[45, 51, 54, 55, 56, 57]$ .

Interviewers received course credit and monetary compensation for their participation. Each interviewer performed an equal number of CI and SI interviews (between two and four each), in a random order.

## Experimental Procedures

The interviews were conducted individually in a private room in the respective interviewee's academic institution and audio-recorded by the researcher. The researcher's presence was identified to the interviewee as necessary to handle the recording equipment, thereby leaving the interviewer free to concentrate on the interview process.

The interviewer, having been trained in both methods, had to exercise care in not mixing interview techniques. During the interview, the interviewer had access to a sheet of the highlights of the two techniques that he or she could reference as reminders (appendix B and Table 1). The researcher monitored the interviewer's conformance to either the SI or the CI as required. The interview length of one hour was fixed for both techniques. The interviews were transcribed by two transcribers, and each transcript was scored for the number of events recalled. Next, 32 events were randomly selected from the population of 118 events obtained from the 21 SI interviews and 32 from the 227 events obtained in the 21 CI interviews. A systematic random selection process was used [43]. This consisted of selecting, from a table of random numbers, two random numbers (one for the CI set and one for the SI set) to be used as starting points, then selecting every fourth event from the SI set and every seventh event from the CI set. Since more events were collected under the CI technique than under the SI technique, a proportionately higher percentage of the SI population of events had to be used for the raters' review set. The panel of raters reviewed this sample of 64 events presented in random order (32 SIs and 32 CIs), using the COD instrument to rate each event for completeness. The 64 events represented a sample size large enough to provide statistical significance yet small enough to avoid rater fatigue.

## Results

TESTS OF SIGNIFICANT DIFFERENCES BETWEEN THE DEPENDENT VARIABLES of (1) number of events collected per interview and (2) completeness of details per event were performed. The conservative approach of making no assumptions regarding the normal distribution or homogeneity of the underlying populations was taken. We therefore used the nonparametric Mann-Whitney test.

The first hypothesis tested the efficiency of the interview technique—that is, the number of events elicited per period of time. This hypothesis stated that the use of a theoretically grounded interview technique will provide a more efficient collection of information related to the problem domain.

The results of the Mann-Whitney test on the number of events elicited per treatment are presented in Table 2. The average number of events elicited per interview under the SI was 5.619, with a standard deviation of 2.974, while the average number of events elicited under the CI was 10.81, with a standard deviation of 4.65.

The null hypothesis is rejected as the number of events elicited using the CI is significantly higher than the number collected using the SI. This lends support to the alternative hypothesis that the memory retrieval techniques employed under the cognitive interview helped interviewees recall more events.

While establishing that the cognitive interview resulted in the recall of more events is important, it does not address whether or not this increased recall was gained at the sacrifice of completeness of recall of each event. Hypothesis 2 examined this question.

Hypothesis 2 was tested by the ratings of 32 randomly selected SI events (27 percent of the total 118 events collected from 21 interviewees) and 32 randomly selected CI events (14 percent of the total 227 events collected from 21 interviewees). The raters used the COD instrument, which has a scale of 7 for “very complete” and 1 for “very incomplete” (see appendix C for the complete rating scale). An analysis of the ratings found that rater 1 saw a slightly wider difference in the COD of the CI versus the SI than did rater 2. Rater 1 gave the CI events an average rating of 5.93, with a standard deviation of 1.43, and SI events an average rating of 4.02, with a standard deviation of 1.87. Rater 2 gave the CI events an average rating of 5.19, with a standard deviation of 1.51, and SI events an average rating of 4.06, with a standard deviation of 1.41.

Even given their diverse perspectives, both raters found significantly higher COD for the CI events versus the SI events, using the Mann-Whitney tests, as shown in Table 3.

The null hypothesis is rejected as the completeness of detail ratings by independent raters are significantly higher for events elicited during the CI than for those elicited during the SI. The rejection of the null hypothesis indicates that the CI provides a useful tool for the elicitation of episodic knowledge. Not only does it encourage the recall of an increased number of events over the SI, but the events recalled also provide more details.

In addition to the quantitative analysis just presented, a deeper understanding of the effectiveness of the CI was gained through qualitative analysis. While the completeness of details analysis measured the depth of information gathered, additional measurements were required for the breadth of details acquired. Therefore, supporting qualitative analysis was performed by using Q methodology as described below.

Researchers have identified five key categories of details, pertaining both to the reference question and to the patron, that are relevant to the librarian task of handling patron inquiries [45]:

1. Reason the patron seeks the information;

2. External variables such as patron's age, educational level, ability to read, native language, physical disabilities, status, urgency, etc.;

3. Type of question (simple or complex);

4. Level of task difficulty to provide answer;

5. Level of reference resources required to answer.

The contents of the sample of sixty-four events selected at random (thirty-two CI and thirty-two SI) were analyzed using Q methodology $[38]$ . Each event was reviewed by a senior reference librarian who determined the categories of detail contained in the event. ## Discussion

THE INTERVIEW HAS LONG BEEN PART OF THE SYSTEMS PROFESSIONAL'S repertoire of elicitation tools, used extensively in both requirements analysis and knowledge acquisition [14]. In the past, various drawbacks have been associated with the use of interviews. Open interviews are seen as ineffective in obtaining the necessary details because “uncued recall is often incomplete and unstructured” [14, p. 130]. On the other hand, structured interviewing techniques previously tested [1] are, by necessity, tied to a specific task domain. As such, they are not applicable to the development of systems for other task domains. The findings from this research study indicate that the cognitive interview, by utilizing theoretically grounded memory retrieval probes, provides richer details for a fixed amount of time than does an interview technique without specific mnemonic aids. These mnemonic aids include: context reinstatement, focused concentration, multiple retrievals, and varied retrievals. As such, the CI provides an enhanced tool for developing task domain overviews as well as for collecting detailed information about specific problem-solving scenarios.

Some of the limitations of this study are those inherent in an experiment in the field as opposed to one in a laboratory. These represent the tradeoffs found between internal validity gained through control of the variables in the laboratory versus enhanced generalizability from the field.

Also, a limited amount of training time was available to develop the skills necessary to use the five techniques of the cognitive interview to their fullest. Although the CI did produce higher levels of completeness of details recalled, further increases in these scores might be obtained if more experienced interviewers with additional training had been used.

Finally, the SI and the CI were both limited to one hour, as suggested by Kendall and Kendall [31], in order to minimize participant fatigue yet provide sufficient time to collect detailed events. In practice, this may be the maximum time a user, given his or her time demands, can give to the systems professional in one sitting. In practice, however, the information elicitation process would not be complete after one hour, and subsequent interviews would be needed.

## Conclusions

## Contributions

THIS EMPIRICAL INVESTIGATION OF THE USE OF THE COGNITIVE INTERVIEW in an organizational setting has provided additional support for the theories of memory retrieval used here. Fisher and Geiselman [18] theorized that using five basic principles of memory retrieval while interviewing would result in enhanced interviewee recall of specific events. Although these theories had previously been tested in both the laboratory and the field, they had not been tested on highly educated interviewees in an organizational setting. This study tested the hypothesis that, given a target interviewee population representative of that required for developing information systems, these theoretically grounded techniques would provide no advantage over the standard knowledge elicitation interview. The statistical findings, however, support Fisher and Geiselman's technique and extend its application to interviewee populations such as the one used in this experiment. Finally, this study responds to Byrd et al.'s [14] call for research efforts that would not only develop new elicitation techniques but also aid in the synthesis of the two research streams of requirements analysis and knowledge acquisition, both of which make extensive use of interviewing techniques.

## Future Research

Various research questions that concern all information elicitation techniques used in systems development should be explored. First, while both efficiency and effectiveness have been noted in the literature as important to the development of information systems $[1]$ , no determination has been made as to which is more important to the quality of the resulting system. The verification and validation of information systems $[16]$ and expert systems $[26]$ is an area of ongoing concern.

Second, it has been noted that, although the CI did produce higher levels of completeness of details, further increases in these scores might be obtained using experienced interviewers with additional training in the CI. Not only should the use of more extensive interview training be explored but also the question of who should be doing the interviewing. Is it better to have experts, who are specialists in the knowledge domain, trained to interview other experts, or to have knowledge engineers, who are specialists in interviewing and expert systems but unfamiliar with the knowledge domain, interview the expert?

Finally, the five principles of the CI are bundled as a package, with no attempt made to rank the relative importance of one over the others. This researcher observed that, in practice, the relative value of the components varied from one interviewing situation to another. Furthermore, the principle of focused retrieval was functioning, to the extent possible, in both the CI and the SI. Under both treatments, the interviewee was not interrupted by external demands by the interviewer or the environment. Therefore, although not statistically verified, it would seem reasonable to conclude that the majority of the differences noted were due to the other four principles. Future researchers might want to tease apart these components to determine their relative value $[20]$ .

## APPENDIX A

Example of Standard Knowledge Elicitation Interview, as seen in [52]

Do's

\- The success of the knowledge acquisition process depends on the knowledge engineer's preparation.

\- A knowledge acquisition interview should be structured and goal oriented.

\- It is up to the knowledge engineer to ensure that the knowledge acquisition interview remains directed.

\- The specific purpose of the interview should be made explicit by the knowledge engineer.

\- The knowledge engineer should use several strategies in assisting the expert in recalling relevant information.

\- The funnel technique of general-to-specific questions is the recommended structure of the interview.

\- The knowledge engineer follows an outline of prepared questions in this order: —Open questions are used to obtain a general description of the expert's approach to the task.

—Probing questions are used to delve into a specific section of the expert's description.

—Closed questions are used to verify and clarify the knowledge base.

—The interview is summarized and a transition moves the interview to the next subject area.

## Don'ts

\- Leading questions designate an appropriate response and should be avoided.

\- Closed questions are restrictive and have limited information value. They should only be used when the knowledge engineer is trying to clarify information.

Example of Standard Knowledge Elicitation Interview, as seen in [47, pp. 386–432]

1. Introduce a new topic.

2. Ask a question:

—Identify the information objective;

—Put the question into words;

—Voice the question to the expert;

—Correct the question if necessary.

3. Listen to the answer.

4. Respond to the answer:

—Ask a follow-up question;

—Confirm your understanding;

—Resolve contradictions;

—Learn how the expert uses unfamiliar terms;

—Update your agenda.

5. Close the topic.

## APPENDIX B: Model Cognitive Interview with Reference Librarian

INT: As you know, we want to review the details of a variety of patron inquiries that you have handled. You've handled so many events over the course of your experience as a reference librarian that often the details just all run together and it is difficult to remember specifics. But we'll be working together to help you recall them clearly.

REF. LIB: Okay

INT: Let's start with some recent inquiries. Think back to yesterday when you first went on duty at the reference desk. We'll start by putting you back into the same frame of mind as then—was it the morning shift?

REF. LIB: No, I came in at 3:00 P.M.

INT: O.K. How did you feel—rushed? relaxed?

REF. LIB: Pretty relaxed—it's summer so the crowds are lighter and we've caught up on things.

INT: Try to put yourself back to what you did when you first got to the desk—put away papers? look at memos?

REF. LIB: There wasn't much of that to do—I checked to see that LUIS was up and then looked through the regional library loan requests in-box.

INT: Try to visualize the first patron who came up—maybe close your eyes and think of who this person was.

REF. LIB: I think it was a student—he needed to find a literary criticism for an English class. I handled his question pretty easily—but I can't recall what author he was looking for.

INT: Let's take this from a different perspective. We'll start more toward the end and work backwards. Did you walk with him over to the literary criticism books? Multiple representations

REF. LIB: Yes.
INT: Did you pull out the correct volume or did he do it himself?

REF. LIB: I did—I wanted to show him how to use the index.
INT: Think about how the index page looked—were you looking at the first half of the alphabet or the last half?

REF. LIB: It was toward the end.
INT: Can you remember if you were thinking this is an unusual name or was it one you had seen many times? Varied retrieval

REF. LIB: I thought it was unusual—it was very short.
INT: Let's look at that index listing again—the name was toward the end and it was short and unusual. It may help to close your eyes and try to visualize the index page again and to read down to the author's name.

REF. LIB: That's it! It was Amy Tam—that was the author!
