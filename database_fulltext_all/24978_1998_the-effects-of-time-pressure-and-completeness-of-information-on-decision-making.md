# The Effects of Time Pressure and Completeness of Information on Decision Making

NIV AHITUV, MAGID IGBARIA, AND A VIEM SELLA

ABSTRACT: The Israeli Air Force (lAF) has developed a simulation system to train its top commanders in how to use defensive resources in the face of an aerial attack by enemy combat aircraft. During the simulation session, the commander in charge allocates airborne and standby resources and dispatches or diverts aircraft to intercept intruders. Seventy-four simulation sessions were conducted in order to examine the effects of time pressure and completeness of information on the performance of twenty-nine top IAF commanders. Variables examined were: (1) display of complete versus incomplete information, (2) time-constrained decision making versus unlimited decision time, and (3) the difference in performance between top strategic commanders and mid-level field commanders.

Our results show that complete information usually improved performance. However, field commanders (as opposed to top strategic commanders) did not improve their performance when presented with complete information under pressure of time. Time pressure usually, but not always, impaired performance. Top commanders tended to make fewer changes in previous decisions than did field commanders.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: decision making, effectiveness ofincomplete information, information effectiveness, time-constrained decision making, value of information.

THE REAL VALUE OF INFORMATION IS DERIVED FROM MEASURING DIFFERENCES in a decision maker's performance when he or she is provided with different information sets. Changing some characteristics of information pertaining to a certain decIsion problem will cause variations in the outcomes (i.e., the performance). The value of information derives from the differences in outcomes that result from different information sets made available to the decision makers. For example, all other things being equal, an investor in the stock exchange market will probably benefit more from having data on each individual firm than from having only aggregate data on the industrial sectors ofthe firms. The real value of such information can be measured by the difference in the values of the investment portfolios of the two investors over time. A discussion ofthe methods and difficulties of measuring the real value of information is presented in King and Schrems [23]. Banker, Kauffman, and Morey [6] provide a case study in which the real value of information was measured before and after a new apparatus was installed on the cash registers of a fast-food chain.

The purpose of this study was to assess the real value of information (as opposed to the normative value and the perceived value of information; see [1,2,3,4]). Its aim was to examine the effectiveness of information display on decision makers who have to react to a dynamic environment under very limiting time constraints, where decisions have to be made and implemented in time intervals of minutes or even seconds. This paper attempts to decide whether subjects should be presented with complete information, at the risk oflosing their way while trying to sort out and digest the information, or whether the system should be programmed to exhibit partial information, which is perceived at a faster rate, even though it might lead to more nsky decisions.

This paper reports on an experimental study conducted with top decision makers responsible for the operation of the air-defense system of the Israeli Air Force (IAF) when the country faces an aerial attack. Twenty-nine high-ranking officers, including some past chief commanders, as well as the present chief commander of the IAF, participated. Each, individually, was faced with a simulation system and required to respond promptly to information provided by the system simulating a hostile air attack by a neighboring enemy country. The simulation system is the one normally used for training of strategic commanders in war games and "live" exercises. The subjects of the experiments were not surrogates (e.g., M.B.A. students) but rather the people who would command the air defense on a rotational basis under actual circumstances.

Many studies center on data presentation modes (e.g., [36]). However, the effects of completeness of information have not been widely explored. In this study, we propose that complete information improves the commander's performance when no time pressure is involved. However, once the time factor is introduced, the combined effect of time and completeness is unclear, since more information requires more time to digest, and this mayor may not offset the disadvantage of coping with incomplete information.

## Background and Research Propositions

SEVERAL EXPERIMENTAL STUDIES OF THE REAL VALUE of information have been reported. Most are based on controlled experiments in which subjects are presented with various information sets and their decisions and actual performance are then recorded. A notable series of such studies was carried out in Minnesota in the mid-1970s. The series, known as the Minnesota Experiments, was reviewed in [14]. The studies were based on business games in which the information provided to players was modified according to the research objective of each study.

One of the first experiments in this area was conducted by Mock [28, 29], who measured how recency of data affects the performance of decision makers in production decisions and assessed the real value of information when decision makers were given more recent data. He found that recent information is better than old information in terms of reducing production costs. Edstrom [16] and Hedberg [17] also used a business game in their research, but they measured the effects of on-line systems on decision makers' performance. Their results indicate that on-line systems are more effective than reports being provided in a batch mode.

Prior research in this area tended to center on the effects of the mode of presentation on user performance. Such studies were usually conducted in a personal computer environment, where the mode of presentation (e.g., graphs versus tables, color versus monochrome, or windows' effectiveness) could be varied easily. Examples of such studies are [7,12,13,24,25,36,37].

Theoretical analysis of the value of information indicates that the normative value (that is, the value calculated through economic modeling) of complete information is greater than the value of partial information when rational behavior is assumed [27, ch. 5]. However, Kahneman and Tversky [21] established that, when a different pattern of behavior is assumed, more information can be ofless value. When rational behavior is not assumed, however, additional information is not necessarily better [30J. If we assume rational behavior on the part of the IAF commanders, then the subjects in this study would be expected to perform better with complete information than with incomplete information.

Time in decision making has been the subject of numerous studies in various disciplines [10, 11,34,35,38]. Svenson, Edland, and Siovic [33J reported that the quality of decision making depends heavily on time, and time is considered one of the primary resources upon which decision making and choice draw. Decision making under time pressure is a part of many people's daily lives. Time pressure in decision making is created by allowing individuals insufficient time to deliberate in finding a solution to a particular problem or to reach the most effective decision. Requiring individuals to make decisions within a limited time frame may create pressure and stress for them. Keinan, Friedland, and Ben-Porath [22J and Maule and Hockey [26J reported that time pressure may increase the level of arousal and psychological stress. Janis [19, 20J pointed out that, when the level of stress is very high, an individual may make decisions without generating all the available alternatives. Edland and Svenson [15J also concluded that "the accuracy of human judgment decreases under time pressure" (p. 36).

The negative effect of time pressure on decision-making effectiveness has been reported by many researchers, and the pattern of results obtained is fairly consistent. Ben-Zur and Breznitz [9J found that more weight is given to negative information as time pressure intensifies. This implies that people in their study became more riskaverse under time pressure. Further, Zakay [39J found that time pressure led to the use of simpler rules but did not change subjects' confidence in the correctness of their decisions. However, Smith, Mitchell, and Beach [32J found that confidence decreased under time pressure. Svenson et al. [33J found that common attributes, such as high school grades in Swedish, psychology, and natural sciences, were used less undertime pressure. Common attributes were used to liberate the decision makers from compensatory tradeoff judgment across attributes. These studies and others seem to indicate that decision makers undertime pressure, as compared with decision makers not under time pressure, use fewer but more important attributes, less complex decision rules, weight negative aspects more heavily, take fewer risks, and are less satisfied with their decisions. They also suggest that time pressure results in reduced information searching and processing, a decrease in the number of alternatives considered, failure to consider important data, and bad judgments.

Hwang [18J suggested that time constraints may be a factor affecting performance and decision making. Benbasat and Dexter [8J, as well as Hwang [18J, concluded that decision-making effectiveness is impaired by time pressure. However, no previous research has drawn any conclusions about the effect of both time pressure and information completeness on decision-making effectiveness and the decision maker's confidence in decisions made. The combination of time pressure and completeness of information together poses a very interesting research question: Does more information overcome the lack of time to make a decision, or does lack of time create an advantage for those provided with less information? The research cited suggests that the performance of IAF commanders will be adversely affected by time pressure.

An experiment dealing with information and time constraints was performed and reported by Benbasat and Dexter [8]. They related information representation modes (color versus monochrome, tabular versus graphical) and degree oftime constraint to decision makers' performance. The major findings showed that color and graphical presentations were advantageous under high time constraints (5 minutes versus 15 minutes allowed for the decision-making process). However, in our study, the time allotted for subjects to react to new information is much shorter-\~:me minute. Another difference between Benbasat and Dexter's study and the present work is that information completeness rather than mode of presentation is examined.

Finally, the need for more information depends to a great extent on the experience of the subjects involved. It may be argued that subjects who have gained much experience in strategic decisions (hereafter-experienced individuals) will not be as intimidated by information overload as middle-level (tactical) individuals (hereafterinexperienced individuals), since they know how to select the most relevant data and since they have a more coherent organization of information stored in memory. Consequently, they attend to greater amounts of information and process this information more extensively than do inexperienced individuals. Moreover, experienced individuals are more aware of the information relevant to performing a given task and, therefore, are more likely to attend to that information than are inexperienced individuals. Experienced individuals are also more likely to recognize the absence of relevant information in making judgments; thus, their judgmental confidence may be lower when information is incomplete [5, 31]. On the other hand, it may be argued that experienced individuals do not need complete information, since their intuition has been honed by many years of learning. They tend to adjust their decisions to compensate for the uncertainty created by incomplete information. It may be argued that experienced commanders are less affected by unexpected information and by breakout of sudden events. They manage to disregard insignificant events and to proceed with the long-range plan they have sketched in their minds. Inexperienced individuals, who are more involved with tactics than with strategy, would be affected more by sudden developments. Consequently, we anticipate that the experience of the decision makers will affect their performance, both when they are provided complete information and when the information provided is incomplete.

Based on the discussion above, we propose the following hypotheses:

1. IAF commanders who are given complete information will perform better than those given incomplete information.

2. IAF commanders who are allowed unlimited time will perform better than those who are subjected to time constraints (or time pressure).

3a. Experienced IAF commanders will perform better than less experienced commanders when provided with complete information.

3b. Experienced IAF commanders will perform better than less experienced commanders when provided with incomplete information.

## The Experimental Framework

## The Simulation System

THE IAF HAS DEVELOPED A SIMULATION SYSTEM TO TRAIN its top commanders in how to utilize defensive resources, should the country be subject to a massive aerial attack by enemy combat aircraft. The simulation tries to imitate a situation where enemy aircraft cross the border and attempt to attack ground targets. It was developed based on observations, interviews, and prior probabilities of intercepts' outcomes between various aircraft models. The commander first develops an initial strategy before the simulation commences. Such a strategy should include deployment of air patrols and standby forces, as well as conditions for their activation thereafter. While the attack is under way, the commander is expected to modify the strategy by diverting, adding, or withdrawing resources, in accordance with what he sees on the screens (all the participants of the study were men). The outcomes of aerial intercepts and combats are based on probability values installed in the simulation program. The defensive resources at the disposal of the commander are divided into three major categories:

I. Airborne combat aircraft patrolling the country's air space.

2. Combat aircraft parked in various airfields on standby.

3. Static resources, such as ground-to-air missiles and antiaircraft batteries.

Since static resources are not portable in a short time frame, the simulation centers mainly on activating airborne and parked aircraft. The first two categories consist of a variety of aircraft models. Some of the planes are technologically advanced and better suited for aerial intercept missions than other planes, which are used mostly for air-to-ground missions. The latter can also be dispatched to intercept enemy intruders, if necessary, but the chances of success are smaller. Patrolling aircraft can fly in the air for a limited time but must return to the ground after being airborne for that certain amount of time. The country's air space is divided into patrol zones, and the number of aircraft that can patrol in a certain zone is limited due to coordination and congestion constraints.

Because the country is very small (about the size of New Jersey) and the enemy can be right across the border, the IAF commander's reactions to an imminent attack both prior to and during its execution must be instantaneous. The hierarchy of reporting and control mechanisms is directed by a single IAF commander.

The aforementioned simulation was used in this study to learn the effects of complete and incomplete information on decision makers facing strong time pressure. This is not the normal use of the simulation system, but rather an application "tailored" for the experiment.

## Subjects

The subjects were IAF commanders. The whole population of top decision makers in that organization was sampled. In other words, all the present top commanders who might command the air defense under a real aerial attack (on a rotational basis) participated in this study; in addition, some newly retired top commanders who had been top commanders before their retirement were also included. A total of twentynine subjects participated. They can be divided into three groups:

G I: Eleven top commanders currently serving in the IAF, each of whom may alternate in commanding the country's air defense during war. Each commander had served for more than twenty years in the IAF.

G2: Five retired top IAF commanders who had been in top commanding positions in the air force while serving and who had retired recently, so that the experience was still fresh in their minds. Some of these commanders had served as IAF chief commander.

G3: Thirteen "mid-level" IAF commanders with at least twelve years of experience in field command but without any experience in strategic command. All members of this group hold senior field command positions and are experienced in tactical command in combat. However, they have never managed a "war game" from the point of view of a chief commander. These commanders are comparable to middle-level management in a business organization. This group was selected for comparative purposes.

Each individual simulation session was handled by a team of four assistants who also ran the simulation, recorded the decisions and reactions, and timed the decisions, when needed. Pilot runs were performed before the experimental trials began, in order to train the team and refine the experiments. The pilot study established that subjects did not have difficulty in using the simulation or in making decisions based on the simulation.

Seventy-four simulation sessions were carried out, each lasting between thirty minutes and one hour (elapsed time). Within that elapsed time, the time spent on the war game lasted fifteen to thirty minutes. The rest of the time was left for decision making (see below how the experiment was conducted).

## Information and Decisions

The information involved in the simulation consists of three types of data:

1. Background information (e.g., enemy tactics, aircraft performance and ammunition capacity load, topographical conditions, distances, airfield locations, flying times, intelligence reports, preferred ground targets, etc.). It is assumed that the necessary background information is known to the IAF commander before the commencement of the war game and need not be exhibited to him. In the event that the commander had a question, a prompt response was provided by support staff.

2. Probabilistic information about the chance of successful detection and intercept, air-battle outcomes, ground attack effectiveness, and the like. This information is embedded in the simulation system.

3. Real-time infonnation on the number of enemy aircraft in the air, their locations and paths, as well as the locations, status, and quantities of IAF resources in the air and on the ground at any moment.

Before the commencement of the war game, the IAF commander prepares a defense plan (i.e., an initial strategy) which is based mainly on the background infonnation. The plan detennines the allocation of resources between airborne patrols and on-theground standby, as well as the allocation of patrols to zones. The plan also lists a series of "IF ... THEN ... " instructions that specify repositioning of resources (moving patrols, reinforcing airborne forces with standby units, etc.), subject to the course of events. This plan is entered into the simulation by the operator of the program.

Theoretically, a perfect plan can prevail for the entire war game without the commander making a single real-time decision. A decision is defined here as a change in the prevailing strategy (e.g., dispatch standby aircraft although the circumstances in the initial plan do not call for that), or a change in previously made decisions (e.g., reposition a patrol after it had been positioned earlier). In practice, IAF commanders closely watch how the war game develops. As the circumstances change, they tend to divert resources and issue new commands.

## Perfonnance Measurement

The foremost objective of an IAF commander in charge of the country's air defense is to minimize the amount of damage and the number of casualties on the ground. This is achieved when the number of successful intercepts is maximized. A successful intercept is defined as an event in which enemy aircraft are detected on their way to a ground target, are met by IAF planes, and are then destroyed. A partial success was recorded if IAF planes were not hit but had to escape without hitting a ground target. The objective was to be accomplished with minimal cost in tenns of losing IAF aircraft.

Decision making was measured using three parameters: number of successful enemy air-to-ground attacks (the more, the worse); number of enemy aircraft hit (the more, the better); and number ofIAF aircraft hit (the more, the worse).

Decision making was transfonned into three perfonnance measurements:

I. GA-The number of ground targets attacked (the more, the worse).

2. EH-The number of enemy aircraft hit (the more, the better).

3. IH-The number of IAF aircraft hit (the more, the worse).

The simulation system provided these results throughout each session.

## Scenarios Tested in the Study

Five different scenarios were tested in this study. The scenarios differed in the amount of infonnation provided to the IAF commander and in the time interval during which he was allowed to make decisions. The five scenarios are delineated below:

I. Complete information and unlimited decision time (CU): The simulation ran for one minute of real time and was then paused. The commander was not constrained in the time he had to make decisions. The real-time information exhibited to him was complete. After he announced his decisions, the simulation ran for another minute and was again paused for possible decisions, and so forth.

2. Complete information and limited decision time (CL): This was the same as number I, except that the pause lasted only one minute. Right after the pause, the simulation ran for another minute, was paused for one minute, and so on.

3. Incomplete information on IAF airborne resources and limited decision time (IlL): A one-minute simulation run alternated with one minute of decisionmaking time (as in number 2); however, the status ofIAF airborne aircraft was not disclosed to the IAF commander.

4. Incomplete information on the number of enemy aircraft and Limited decision time (INL): A one-minute simulation run alternated with one minute of decision time (as above); however, the IAF commander was not informed of the number of enemy aircraft approaching the country and nearing ground targets.

5. Incomplete information on the directions of approach of enemy aircraft and limited decision time (IDL): A one-minute simulation run alternated with one minute of decision time (as above); however, the IAF commander was not presented with enemy aircraft flight directions.

Each subject underwent the experiment individually. Most of the subjects participated in two or three ofthe five scenarios. The scenarios were chosen in random order. None of the subjects underwent more than three simulation sessions (scenarios), in order to avoid possible learning effects.

The last four experiments imitate potential real circumstances; that is--time pres sure always exists, and display of partial information might occur. The first experiment was not realistic and was performed to "calibrate" the results of the other four.

A 2x2x2 (i.e., complete versus incomplete x limited versus unlimited x experienced commanders versus inexperienced commanders) quasi-experimental design was used to test our propositions. The combination of incomplete information and unlimited decision time was not included because this study focused on the combined effect of information completeness and time pressure.

The number of decisions made in each session was also recorded during the simulation sessions. A decision is defined as a modification of the initial strategy or a previous decision (e.g., changing the resource allocation or location through a command issued during a decision time interval). If the IAF commander did not make a decision during a certain time interval and instead let the war game proceed as planned (either following the initial strategy or according to previous decisions), no decision was recorded.

## Complete Versus Incomplete Information

In order to test the effects of infonnation completeness on perfonnance, the performance measures of scenario type CL were compared with those of scenario type IL. Both scenario types took place under time pressure (limited time), and the amount of infonnation (complete versus incomplete) was the only varying parameter. (We controIled for time pressure. Here the independent variable was infonnation completeness and the dependent variable was perfonnance.)

Experimental conditions were compared. As seen in Table 2, the perfonnance means among subjects with CL were not different from those with IL. Multiple paired t-tests were also used to test whether the differences between these two scenario types were statisticaIly significant. There were no significant differences in all perfonnance measures between CL and IL; that is, the quality of decisions and perfonnance did not differ significantly between the group with complete infonnation and the one with incomplete infonnation. Thus, infonnation completeness had no effect on performance when we controIled for time pressure.

## Discussion and Conclusions

THE STUDY REPORTED HERE WAS CONDUCTED ON DECISION MAKERS belonging to the top echelon of a military organization. Although the results are statistically valid only for the IAF commanders, the results may shed some light on four important attributes ofinfonnation and decision making: (I) the completeness of the information available, (2) the existence of time constraints, (3) the effect of seniority and experience on decision effectiveness, and (4) the effect of seniority and experience on the decision maker's confidence in the decision made (expressed by the frequency of decision modification).

The major findings of the study are summarized below:

1. In general, complete infonnation improves perfonnance, although the improvement is not always statistically significant. This result is consistent with analytical studies undertaken by infonnation economics research, which showed that under the assumption of rational behavior, more information improves decisions.

2. Time pressure usually, but not always, impairs perfonnance. This is consistent with the literature on human judgment under stress, which suggests that individuals perform more poorly under time pressure than they do when they are under no time constraints [15], because time pressure affects the accuracy and effectiveness of decision making and judgment.

It should be noted that the effect of time pressure on performance is more salient among less-experienced commanders. The performance of inexperienced commanders was affected more by time pressure than was the performance of experienced commanders. This suggests that time plays an important role in decision making, particularly among inexperienced commanders, who may need more time to process large amounts of information. Work under intense time pressure and information overload may contribute to overstimulation and stress in individuals and, eventually, may affect their performance.

3. Experienced top commanders digest additional information better than inexperienced commanders when decision time is constrained. Our results show that the long experience gained by active and retired top commanders affects their performance. They can use their prior experience to offset the missing information and limited time. They can also guess what information they lack, and thereby act accordingly. Less-experienced commanders tend to suffer from information overload. This suggests that experienced individuals may process information much faster by focusing on important attributes and altematives and by giving the appropriate weight to all attributes and alternatives. Further, experienced individuals may have experienced similar scenario types in the past and, as a result, they know how to cope with stressful circumstances.

4. Less-experienced commanders tend to make more decisions within a given time interval. In contrast, top commanders set an initial strategic plan and thereafter rely confidently on the plan. They are less influenced by seemingly transient developments. Their decision-making process is calmer. Fewer senior commanders tend to modify the initial plan frequently. Perhaps this is due to greater self-confidence.

5. All participants made more decisions during the early steps ofthe simulation session but later let the simulation develop as planned, except for minor adjustments. In other words, all participants were aware that it takes time for the impact of a decision to be felt. Hence, last-minute decisions would have little effect.

The difference in the performance of top commanders and field commanders raises an interesting "chicken and egg" question. The field commanders constitute the group of potential candidates for promotion to top-ranking officers. It is likely that in a decade some of them will reach the highest commanding positions, while others will retire. It is almost certain that within fifteen years one or more of them will be appointed as the chief commander of the IAF. We may have observed a "Darwinian" evolutionary process where those possessing some specific traits (e.g., calmness and confidence) are promoted, while others have to leave. It is hard to tell whether the differences between middle-level and experienced commanders are due to the additional experience acquired through the years or to inherent personality traits. This question remains open for further research.

In summary, this study suggests that experience is an important factor affecting the evaluation of information under time pressure. Since the subjects of this study were military commanders, readers should be cautioned that the results are not necessarily generalizable to a broader cross-section of organizations. The generalizability of these findings to other organizations, industries, and countries awaits confirmation through additional research. However, it is interesting to point out that, because of the early age of retirement from the military, many military commanders start new careers in industry when they are in their forties. The fact that these commanders are heavily recruited for management positions suggests that commanders' skills may also be useful in the business world.

This study provides insights into the importance of time and completeness of information on decision making. We hope that these findings will stimulate additIOnal research in other field settings.
