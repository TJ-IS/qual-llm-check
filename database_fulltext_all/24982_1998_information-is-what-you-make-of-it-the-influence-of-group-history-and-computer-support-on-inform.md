# Information Is What You Make of It: The Influence of Group History and Computer Support on Information Sharing, Decision Quality, and Member Perceptions

BRIAN E. MENNECKE AND JOSEPH S. V ALACICH

BRIAN E. MENNECKE is an Assistant Professor of Management Information Systems at East Carolina University. He received his Ph.D. in management information systems from Indiana University. His research interests include the diffusion of technology in organizations, geographic information systems, spatial decision support systems, spatial data warehousing, group decision support systems, and collaborative learning technologies. His past research has appeared in publications such as Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, Small Group Research, the International Journal of Human\~omputer Studies, Journal of Global Information Management, Journal of Computer Information Systems, and a number of national and international conference proceedings.

JOSEPH S. V ALACICH is an Associate Professor of Management Information Systems and is the George and Carolyn Hubman Distinguished Professor in Information Systems at Washington State University. He received a B.S. in computer science and M.B.A. from the University of Montana, and a Ph.D. in information systems from the University of Arizona. Dr. Valacich worked in the information systems field as a programmer, systems analyst, and technical product manager. His current research interests include technology-mediated group decision making, electronic commerce, the diffusion of technology in organizations, and distance learning. His past research has appeared in publications such as MIS Quarterly, Information Systems Research, Management Science, Academy of Management Journal, Communications of the ACM, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, and Journal of Applied Psychology. He is the author of Group Support Systems: New Perspectives and Modern Systems Analysis and Design. His third book on Information Technology Management is forthcoming in 1998.

ABSTRACT: Researchers have proposed that the contradictions observed between past group support system (GSS) laboratory and field research may be partially accounted for by the ad-hoc nature ofthe groups that are often used in the laboratory. To examine this, a laboratory experiment examining the influence of group history (i.e., established versus ad-hoc groups) and the level of computer support (i.e., communicating via a computer-mediated system versus face-to-face) was conducted. Dependent variables examined in the research include information-sharing performance, decision quality, and member perceptions. Subjects completed a hidden profile task--a task where some information is held by all group members prior to the meeting, while other information is held only by a subset of the group. As expected, established groups discussed less unique information than ad-hoc groups. In addition, information sharing was positively related to the quality of group decisions. Members of established groups were more satisfied than members of ad-hoc groups; members using the computermediated system were less satisfied than those communicating face-to-face. In addition, group cohesion was positively related to satisfaction and decision quality. The results are discussed in the context of prior theory and research. Opportunities for future research are also described.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: decision-group history, group cohesion, group support systems, information sharing.

THE GROWTH IN THE USE OF GROUP SUPPORT SYSTEMS (GSS) and related collaborative technologies has brought with it a need to understand how these technologies influence group behaviors and performance. Although a great deal of research has been conducted to explore these issues, additional research is needed to help clarifY how technology affects group behavior and performance. For example, many researchers have observed that prior GSS research has generated inconsistent or inconclusive results [6, 7, 8, 15]. Dennis, Easton et al. [6] pointed out that one reason for these inconsistencies involves the nature of the groups used in this research strea\~hat is, the almost universal use of ad-hoc groups in laboratory research. Because groups in organizational settings often have a history of working together (i.e., they are established), research on group history in the context of computer-supported groups is justified for both practical and theoretical reasons.

The literature on group development indicates that interacting groups progress through various phases as they develop [21, 32]. Several group development models assert that developmental phases are influenced by various contingencies such as the decision-making process, the characteristics of the communication tools, and task characteristics [29, 30, 38, 39, 40]. Likewise, groups at different stages of their development may also be influenced differently by these factors. For example, members of established groups will usually have significant knowledge about other members' skills and abilities, member roles may be better defined, and group cohesion may be more fully developed. Although the influence of these variables will vary depending on the context, their cumulative effect should be significant, leading to different behaviors and performance patterns in established groups than in ad-hoc groups.

Similarly, task has also been shown to be important in group research [8, 33]. For instance, Dennis and colleagues noted an important distinction that has often been made between tasks performed in the field and those completed in the laboratory:

Members of organizational groups in the field have often had asymmetrical (even conflicting) task information. Experimental groups, who in previous EMS [electronic meeting systerns] studies have generally worked from a common description of the experimental task, would have been more likely to have a common set of information. [8, p. 116]

Consequently, a growing body of group research has considered the role of tasks that possess an asymmetrical distribution of information. For example, Stasser and colleagues conducted several studies using tasks where each group member possessed unique, yet complementary information [45,48,49, 50]. Tasks with asymmetrical information are often referred to as "hidden-profile" tasks because the full nature of the objects described in the task is hidden from group members when they consider the task individually. Only when group members share information effectively can the true profile be discovered. Hidden-profile tasks are designed to simulate, at least in part, the type of task that groups often encounter in organizational settings; that is, where each group member possesses unique task-relevant knowledge [33]. As such, hidden-profile tasks are helpful for examining information sharing. Information sharing is often a critical function in organizations because it is through the sharing of information that managers and team members are able to sense and respond to the opportunities and threats that exist both within and outside the firm (see [41] for a discussion of the importance of information sharing in exploiting marketing opportunities). Interestingly, Stasser and colleagues [48] consistently found that groups are not likely to discover hidden profiles because individual members frequently failed to discuss initially unshared information.

There are theoretical reasons to believe that groups with a shared history wiII discuss and share information differently than newly formed groups [2, 45]. To address these issues, a hidden-profile task was used to examine how the history of a group and its use ofCMC technology influence information sharing, decision-making performance, and member perceptions. This research represents the first reported study that directly examines these variables. Research addressing these issues is important because groups often assemble with the goal of sharing data and information. However, if members ofthe group fail to share information effectively, they are likely to produce inferior decisions. Huber, Valacich, and Jessup [23] made this connection vis-a-vis organizational information sharing and GSS use when they noted that "by facilitating the sharing of information, ass increase the quality of the decision-making process, and that by aiding in the analysis of information within decision units, ass use increases the quality of decision making" (p. 267). Because collaborative technologies are increasingly being used for information sharing and decision making, research is needed to examine the impact of these technologies and the role of group characteristics, such as group history. The next section reviews the literature and sets out the hypotheses examined in this study.

## Prior Research and Study Hypotheses

## Information-Sharing Performance

STASSER AND COLLEAGUES [48,49,50] FOUND THAT AD-HOC GROUPS completing hidden-profile tasks often failed to pool initially unshared information effectively. While interpreting these findings, Stasser suggested that "decision-making groups that have a history of working together on similar kinds of tasks may develop an efficient and effective division of responsibility for ... communicating during discussions various kinds ofinfonnation" [45, p. 64]. This comment is worth greater consideration because of the unique differences between ad-hoc and established groups. For example, members of established groups will have a greater familiarity with other members' skills, abilities, and preferences than will members of ad-hoc groups. These and other factors (e.g., cohesion) have been shown to result in different patterns of communication and behaviors in established groups versus ad-hoc groups [3,20,31,43,44].

Yet, a close examination of the literature does not necessarily suggest that we should hold an optimistic view of the performance of established groups in sharing information. Although some research examining decision quality has shown that established groups make better solutions than ad-hoc groups [20], it is not clear whether they will share more information. In fact, much of the communication and small-group literature suggests the opposite: Established groups are more inclined to share less infonnation than ad-hoc groups.

For example, a seminal work on the role of group history in influencing knowledge acquisition and infonnation exchange is that of Carley [2].2 She argues that "the more established the group, the greater the potential for the underlying social structure to affect behavior vis-a-vis a particular task" [2, p. 413]. As a group develops, its members develop shared frames of reference that, in tum, affect their behaviors. In other words, as a group interacts over time, its members begin to view the world through a similar frame of reference. This often leads to the development ofa shared understanding of events and ideas, a shared vocabulary, and shared knowledge. This will affect communication in many circumstances. "Ironically," Carley [2, p. 424] notes, "the greater the common frame of reference, the less need there is for explicit communication. That is, given that there are a limited number of facts, the more facts the individuals share [prior to the meeting], the fewer they 'need' to communicate."

The implication of this shared perspective for communication between members of established groups is profound. For example, it is quite possible that established groups would develop a shared perspective on many issues and make the assumption that this shared perspective exists in future interactions. In the case of a task requiring infonnation sharing, this has the potential to affect infonnation-sharing perfonnance negatively. This is particularly important if the task is something that the subjects consider to be familiar or relevant to their situation. Thus, when established student groups process a task pertaining to their university, it is conceivable that this would generate an environment in which group members are more likely to assume that other members share their perspective about the issues presented in the task. However, this might not be as likely to happen when an established student group processes a less familiar task or one pertaining to topics that are unrelated to their university experiences.

Although little research has explicitly examined infonnation sharing among established group members, the research that does exist tends to support the perspective advanced by Carley [2]. For example, Zack [57] examined communication in ongoing management groups and found that groups possessing a greater shared-interpretive context rated lean media like e-mail as more expressive than did groups without a shared context. Thus, "shared context can substitute for bandwidth and social presence when needing to send a rich message" [57, p. 234], which implies that established groups will often perceive that they have less need to communicate certain types of information explicitly. Similarly, McGrath et al. [31] observed that the reduced ambiguity that established groups possessed tended to routinize the groups' division of labor and resource exchange patterns. This, they concluded, resulted in reduced member interdependence and lower information exchange [31].

In summary, it appears that established groups are more likely to assume that they understand one another, share similar viewpoints, and have similar information. These factors suggest that they would be less likely to share information. This leads to the following hypothesis:

## HI: Established groups will share less information than will ad-hoc groups.

Prior research has found that group members working on hidden-profile tasks without the support ofCMC frequently fail to discuss initially unshared information [49]. Normative influence theory [25] may help, at least in part, to explain these findings: Group members who hold unique information might be discouraged from discussing this information. For example, when individuals are presented with information that they did not read in their version of the task, this should create a perception of inconsistency. As a consequence, members may be reticent to discuss unique information (i.e., that no one else possesses), and focus instead on information that the group has already discussed. In other words, individuals who possess unique information may feel that they are in a minority position because they are the only group member possessing a particular piece of information. This minority status can have important negative impacts on information dissemination and processing [34].3

Depending on its configuration, CMC technology can create unique communication environments [36]. At one extreme, the CMC treatment can be configured so that CMC is the primary channel with little or no verbal communication. Configuring the communication channel so that groups use CMC as the primary or sole communication channel helps to directly evaluate the influence ofthe CMC channel for different types of groups relative to face-to-face, verbal-only groups (referred to as "no-tech" groups). The textual format may lead to reduced normative influences by helping groups focus on the informational content of the task. For example, Huang, Raman, and Wei [22] found that computer-supported communication has the potential to increase informational influences and attenuate normative influences. Furthermore, Huber and colleagues note in several oftheir concepts and propositions that GSS use is expected to increase information availability for the group because it facilitates the collection and presentation of information to individual group members and the group as a whole [23]. These factors, when considered together, suggest the following hypothesis:

H2: CMC groups will share more information than will no-tech groups.

## Decision Quality

Prior research on the relationship between information-sharing performance and the quality of a group's decision suggests that the more information a group possesses, the better will be its decision [47]. Drawing on the arguments used to guide the information-sharing hypotheses, we propose:

H3: Group decision-making performance will be positively related to group information-sharing peiformance.

H3a: Established groups will generate lower-quality decisions than will ad-hoc groups.

H3b: Groups using CMC will generate higher-quality decisions than will no-tech groups.

## Satisfaction

Group history is expected to have a significant influence on member satisfaction. Specifically, established groups should be more satisfied with the decision process and outcomes than members of ad-hoc groups. This prediction is derived from the expectation that the majority of established groups will have greater cohesion than ad\~hoc groups. This implies that individual members of cohesive established groups will possess a sense of unity and confidence in their group and in its performance capabilities [24]. Prior research has generally found that cohesion is positively related to satisfaction [II]. Thus, established groups should report greater satisfaction than ad-hoc groups. These ideas lead to the following hypotheses:

H4: Members of established groups will be more satisfied than ad-hoc group members.

H4a: Members of established groups will be more satisfied with the decision process than ad-hoc group members.

H4b: Members of established groups will be more satisfied with the quality of their decision than ad-hoc group members.

For the CMC manipulation, we expect that a number of variables will affect members' perceptions of satisfaction. For example, if subjects perceive that the CMC technology improves the group's decision-making process or that it helps them produce a better solution, satisfaction should be higher. In this context, the type of support available to group members and the nature of the task will collectively have important impacts on satisfaction. In particular, research has shown that the level of communication and process support influences satisfaction. Benbasat and Lim [1] report that users of GSS equipped with process structuring and modeling support (i.e., a level-2 GSS; [9]) consistently report greater satisfaction than do users of support technology that only provides communication support (i.e., level-! GSS). The CMC technology used in this research was configured to be more like a level-1 GSS than a level-2 system. Further, in a study designed to examine information sharing, Strauss [51] found that CMC groups reported lower satisfaction than no-tech groups. Thus, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H5: Members of CMC groups will be less satisfied than members of no-tech groups.

H5a: Members of CMC groups will be less satisfied with the decision process than members of no-tech groups.

H5b: Members of CMC groups will be less satisfied with the quality of their decision than members of no-tech groups.

## Cohesion

The influence of the independent variables will likely be moderated by the feelings members have for one another [II, 12,27]. For instance, when compared with ad-hoc groups, members of established groups should have well-developed feelings (both positive and negative) about other members and the group as a whole. The effects of these feelings on member behaviors could have several impacts on informationsharing performance, but, as noted earlier, we believe that greater cohesion will have a negative impact on information sharing. Similarly, since information sharing should be tied to the quality of the decision the group generates, we expect that cohesion will also be negatively related to decision quality. Finally, cohesion should also have a significant influence on member satisfaction. For example, group members who possess positive feelings toward other members should be more satisfied with both the process and the solution than members who do not have favorable feelings about their group. Consequently, the following is proposed:

H6: A relationship will exist between cohesion, peiformance, and perceptions of satisfaction.

H6a: A negative relationship will exist between cohesion and informationsharing peiformance.

H6b: A negative relationship will exist between cohesion and decision quality.

H6c: A positive relationship will exist between cohesion and satisfaction.

## Methods

THE RESEARCH USED A 2x2 FACTORIAL DESIGN. Two independent variables were examined: (I) group history (i.e., comparing ad-hoc groups with established groups) and (2) the level of computer support (i.e., comparing CMC groups with no-tech groups). A total of fifteen to seventeen four-member groups were included in each of the four cells. Each type of group, ad hoc and established, was randomly assigned to either the CMC orno-tech treatments. The task type, room, meeting agenda, time limit, and group size were held constant.

## Subjects

Sixty-four groups participated in the experimental sessions (256 subjects--118 females and 138 males with an average age of21 years). Subjects were recruited from a speech communications course at Indiana University.

## Established Groups

Students in the course were randomly assigned to groups at the beginning of the semester to work on group projects throughout the entire semester. Subjects participated in the experimental session after having completed three to four other major group projects and several meetings associated with a semester-long outside-of-class project. In addition, groups were required to complete at least two additional projects after the experimental session. At the time of the subject's exposure to the experimental treatments, the established groups could be characterized as intact, ongoing groups----that is, groups where members have had a significant history working together and they were likely to anticipate having a significant future together [28].

## Ad-hoc Groups

Students from the same speech course were used to represent both established groups and ad\~hoc groups. All subjects were selected from the same course so that participants in all treatments were drawn from the same population. Students were split into two samples: a total of 33 groups (132 students) were maintained in their established structure while the remaining students were randomly reassigned to 31 new experimental ad,hoc groups (124 students). Ad-hoc groups were structured so that they would be similar to the typical ad-hoc experimental groups used in the majority of prior group research. In other words, they were constructed so that members had no experience working together with other members and little or no expectation that they would work in a significant way together in the future. For example, ad-hoc groups were structured so that members of these groups did not participate with members of the groups they normally worked with in class. Furthermore, although some ad-hoc groups contained members from the same section ofthe course, an attempt was made to combine students into ad-hoc groups from different sections of the course. Summary statistics reporting on the interactions that subjects reported having with group members prior to the experimental session are presented in the results section below.

## Task

The task used for this experiment is a hidden profile task that was adapted from Stasser and Titus [49, 50] and Stasser et al. [48]. Hidden profiles were constructed by spreading unique and pertinent information about hypothetical student council presidential candidates among group members with the result that no one member had sufficient information to solve the case adequately (i.e., identify the true profile of each candidate).

The objective of the task is to rank three university student council presidential candidates. The task contains information about the candidates in the form of sixteen statements purportedly made by each candidate. These statements, which relate primarily to issues and policies facing students at the university, were rated prior to the experiment by over 250 undergraduate students who did not participate in the experiment. Student raters were instructed to score each statement for its importance and on how it would make them feel about a student council presidential candidate who made the statement. Based on the ratings, statements were combined to form a profile of each hypothetical candidate.

## Information Distribution

Profiles were constructed with the objective that each description be unequal in terms of desirability. However, the distribution of the information presented to the subjects was structured so that the true profiles of the three candidates were hidden from the subjects (see Table I). In addition, the information was distributed to create a bias that would make the best candidate appear to be less attractive. The bias was developed by initially sharing all or most of the positive information and less of the negative information about the less-desirable candidates. At the same time, only one or two of the group members initially shared the positive information about the best candidate. The information was distributed among each of the group members so that the group as a whole possessed all of the information that was available about each candidate. Furthermore, the statements were distributed so that one-quarter of the information was initially held by only one member, one-quarter was initially held by two members, one-quarter was initially held by three members, and one-quarter was initially held by all four members of the group. This information distribution was created to generate a prediscussion bias in individual group members [49] and to manipulate the percentage of information that was initially shared among group members [48, 50].

## Measurement of Information-Sharing Performance

Information-sharing performance was measured by counting the number of information elements from the task that were mentioned (or typed) by a group member during the discussion (interrater agreement was 84 percent; $K = 0 . 1 6 8 ; Z = 1 . 8 9 ; p = 0 . 0 2 9 4 )$ The proportions used in the analyses represent the percentage of information elements in each category that were mentioned at least once during the session. The percentage generated through this process represents the performance of the group in surfacing information-higher percentages indicate better performance in sharing information. The percentage of available information that was mentioned by each group was calculated for several categories of information: initially unshared information, partially shared information (information initially held by two or three people), and initially shared information.

## Dependent Variables

Four dependent measures were examined: the percentage of available information discussed during the meeting, group decision quality, perceptions of solution satisfaction, and perceptions of process satisfaction. In addition, cohesion was captured as a manipulation check for the group history treatment and to examine its relationship to each of the dependent variables in the study. All data were aggregated and examined at the group level by summing individual group member scores for each measure.

## Information-Sharing Performance

This variable was operationalized as the proportion of available information that was mentioned during the meeting-that is, the number of unique task-related information elements mentioned divided by the total number of information elements available to group members. The information that was mentioned during the meeting was captured by having raters code and count the number of information elements that were discussed.<sup>4</sup>

## Decision Quality

The quality of the group decision was operationalized by comparing the ranking of each group to the correct ranking of the alternatives in the case. The correct ranking was identified based on the distribution of positive, neutral, and negative information. Further, the task was tested with individuals who processed all of the information. To facilitate analyses using multivariate techniques (e.g., to examine the effects of presession individual preferences on group results), the rankings were converted into interval-level variables. This was accomplished by classifying group decisions into one of six categories ranging from complete agreement with the correct ranking (6) to complete disagreement with the correct ranking (1).

## Satisfaction

Both process satisfaction and decision outcome satisfaction were measured with Green and Taber's [19] satisfaction scale. Each of these factors is composed of a five-point scale that taps perceptions related to solution and process satisfaction. This satisfaction instrument has been used extensively in prior small-group research.

## Cohesion

Group cohesion was examined in conjunction with the dependent measures to identify the relationship between cohesion, decision quality, and satisfaction. Perceptions of cohesion were captured using the Group Attitude Scale (GAS) [13]. This scale is designed to assess group cohesion by identifying the level of attraction each group member has to his or her group. The cohesion instrument was administered before and after each session. Presession cohesion was used as a check for the group history manipulation, while the scores for postsession cohesion were used to examine the relationship between performance and satisfaction. Postsession cohesion was used for these analyses because these scores reflect member perceptions after their exposure to the experimental manipulations. Also, it is only after the session is completed that members of both ad-hoc and established groups will have had the opportunity to work with members of their group.

Experimental Setting and Procedures

## Experimental Setting

All groups completed the experimental task in the same setting. Members of computer-mediated groups were seated at four workstations in the same room while group members in the no-tech treatment sat in the same locations with the computers removed. The Topic Commenter tool in the GroupSystems GSS was used for information sharing, discussion, and voting [36]. The Topic Commenter tool is designed so that comments can be inserted by the user under one of several topics (in this case, four topics---three for the names of the three candidates and one for discussion and selection).

The CMC system was set so that no names or identification tags were automatically attached to messages; however, this did not prevent subjects from identifYing themselves in their messages. Further, because group size was rather small and subjects were seated close to one another in CMC and non-CMC conditions, complete anonymity could not be guaranteed in the CMC treatments. Nevertheless, groups using CMC were required to communicate using the computer; none violated this control by speaking or uttering task-related comments or ideas (which does not preclude the communication of nonverbal information).

## Experimental Procedures

After arriving at the experimental facility, all subjects were read an introduction to the experiment and were asked to read and sign a subject consent statement. Each was then asked to fill out several preliminary questionnaires, including the cohesion instrument. Following this, computer-mediated groups were provided with non-taskrelated training on the use of the CMC technology. Following the introduction and computer training (computer-mediated groups only), subjects were asked to read the case, rank the candidates based on their preferences, and then record those statements about the candidates that they could recall without looking at the task. Information such as presession preferences was captured to examine whether post- and presession preferences were related (see the results section below). Following this, all materials were removed from the subjects and they were asked to process the case based on what they could recall about the candidates.<sup>5</sup> All groups were instructed to use a structured heuristic to process the case. This heuristic requested that subjects first attempt to recall and discuss relevant information about the candidates without stating a preference or voting (fifteen minutes) and then discuss the case openly (forty-five minutes). All groups were told to continue to discuss and vote on the candidates until agreement was reached, deadlock occurred, or the time limit elapsed. Deadlock occurred when members of the group agreed that they were unable to achieve unanimity on the case. The agenda was used to structure the group discussion in a manner similar to that used by Stasser and Titus [50]. The group process was controlled and limited by the research assistant to delivering scripted instructions, assisting groups in the transition from the information-sharing phase to the discussion and selection phase, and correcting the group ifit strayed from the agenda. When groups completed the task, they were asked to complete several follow-up questionnaires, they were debriefed, and then they were dismissed.

## Discussion

THE RESULTS OF THIS STUDY HAVE SEVERAL IMPORTANT IMPLICATIONS for research on small groups in both CMC and no-tech settings. Chief among these implications is that both CMC use and group history will have important impacts on user performance and perceptions. These implications and other findings from this research are discussed below.

The results for information-sharing performance are mixed. As expected, established groups discussed significantly less initially unshared information than ad-hoc groups. However, no difference in information-sharing performance was observed for shared or partially shared information. Further, CMC had no impact on information sharing. Thus, these results provide only partial support for hypothesis 1 and no support for hypothesis 2.

The findings for the group history manipulation are consistent with the model advanced by Carley [2]. For example, she suggests that established groups will develop shared interpretive contexts and that this will influence the nature of communication and information sharing between group members. Specifically, established groups can be expected to be more likely to assume that their fellow group members hold similar views, perspectives, and information. Therefore, they would be less likely to communicate detailed information about the topic under discussion because they would assume that other group members approach the problem from a common perspective. The fact that established groups not only shared less information but also spent significantly less time working on the task strongly supports this "sharedcontext" effect.

One possible interpretation of these findings is that an established group's shared interpretive context causes group members to be less thorough in processing the task in which they are engaged. This lack of thoroughness has been observed and discussed in prior research examining established groups. For example, Janis [24] suggests that one potential consequence of Group think is that highly cohesive group members will refrain from engaging in vigilant decision-making activities. Vigilance is characterized by a more thorough examination of the issues and statements presented in the task. Almost by definition, vigilant groups should take more time examining and discussing case-relevant issues. The relationship between shorter session times for established groups and their poor information-sharing performance suggests that these groups were less vigilant during the decision-making process.

The results from the study fail to support the hypotheses for CMC and information sharing--no differences were observed between no-tech and CMC groups in information-sharing performance. These results are consistent with a growing body of literature that indicates that CMC does not help groups improve information-sharing performance. Recent work by Strauss [51] and Dennis [4, 5] showed that CMC groups shared no more information than no-tech groups. Why does this occur when CMC has been shown to be effective in supporting group brainstorming? A number of factors may contribute to this phenomenon. For example, it is likely that the parallel CMC communication channel that is so beneficial for brainstorming negatively affects group performance of hidden-profile tasks. Strauss suggests that CMC groups may suffer from something she labels as "attention blocking" [51]. This occurs in parallel communication when a group member is unable to attend to other participants comments because he or she is typing in his or her own comments. This is, in effect, the inverse of production blocking, which occurs when participants in a verbal discussion are unable to contribute and process their own ideas because they are busy listening to others. Parallel communication, therefore, creates an environment that is more likely to lead to divergent than to convergent communication. This could retard the information-sharing process because it may reduce the likelihood that group members will recognize that they have different information. Because an awareness of the presence of un shared information has been shown to have a positive impact on information sharing [42], the parallel nature of the CMC channel may actually retard infonnation sharing by making it less likely that group members will process the cues that other participants provide when they share unique infonnation.?

It is not clear, however, whether attention blocking would be a problem in all CMC environments. For example, groups using CMC over a longer time frame may not encounter this problem because they will generally have more time to process the contributions that were made by others. On the contrary, both our research and that of Dennis [4, 5] and Strauss [51] were conducted as one-time sessions that were constrained to a relatively short time (e.g., 1-2 hours). This line of reasoning suggests that future research should examine information sharing for groups that interact over a longer period of time (e.g., in geographically dispersed, asynchronous groups). If groups use the technology over a longer time period, the effects of attention blocking should dissipate, with the result that group members would be able to integrate information more effectively.

Another factor that may account for these findings is group size. With the exception of the study by Dennis [4], none of the groups in any of the studies that have examined CMC and information sharing has been larger than seven members. In prior CMC and GSS research, group size has been found to be an important factor affecting idea generation [52, 53]. In general, this research stream has shown that larger groups benefit more from computer support than do smaller groups. Furthermore, Valacich and colleagues [54] found that a critical group size (approximately seven members) exists for groups processing hidden-profile tasks. When group size was below this critical mass, GSS groups were not able to integrate the case-relevant facts effectively (i.e., the GSS did not assist in convergent task processing). Thus, the fact that the groups used in this research were smaller than this critical size may help account for the lack of significant findings. Nevertheless, Panko [37] found that most managerial meetings are quite small-in most cases between three and five members-thus, the size of our research groups is representative of the group sizes most often encountered in organizations.

Given the results for information sharing, it is not surprising that significant main effects for decision quality were not observed. For example, our a priori expectation was that decision quality would be related to information-sharing performance. Thus, because only one variable associated with information sharing was observed to be significantly different across the treatment conditions, differences in decision quality were likewise absent across these treatments. Nevertheless, the results did show that information-sharing performance for initially unshared information was significantly and positively related to decision quality. These results were observed across all of the treatment conditions and thus provide partial support for hypothesis 3.

These effects are noteworthy because of the importance of the initially unshared information in the task used in this study. Specifically, the initially unshared information generally included important information about the candidates that was designed to sway opinions about these individuals. For this task, unshared information for the best candidate was generally the most positive information about that individual, while the unshared information for the least preferable candidate was often the most negative information about that individual. Thus, groups that did a better job of surfacing unshared information should have been able to formulate a more accurate profile of each candidate and thereby generate a better solution. In general, these results are consistent with this expectation, since the results show that groups that were more effective at discussing un shared information also made better solutions. These results help to demonstrate the importance of using hidden-profile tasks for experimental research. Organizational groups often meet to share information that individual members did not possess prior to the meeting. Our research shows that both the premeeting distribution of information and its a.vailability to individual members during the meeting can have important impacts on decision quality.

The results for satisfaction indicate that established groups and no-tech groups were more satisfied with the process and solution than were ad-hoc groups and CMC groups, respectively. The results for the group history treatment confirm the hypothesis and suggest that group history is a significant factor in attitude formation. Likewise, the results for the CMC treatment support the satisfaction hypothesis. The restrictive implementation of the CMC treatment and the nature of the groups' interactions may help account for these findings. The lean CMC communication channel likely did not enable members to transmit the complex concepts and social cues that they would usually attempt to convey in processing a socially complex task such as this. In summary, these results are consistent with much of the prior laboratory-based research that has found technologically supported groups to be less satisfied than no-tech groups [14,16,55].

Cohesion was found to be positively related to satisfaction across all treatment conditions. Likewise, a significant positive relationship was observed between cohesion and decision quality. The results linking satisfaction, group outcomes, and cohesion are consistent with prior findings [II, 56] and indicate that not only are perceptions of cohesion related to feelings members have about the group itself, but they also reflect on the group's shared experiences, interpersonal feelings, and the relationships that exist within the group, irrespective of process interventions [26]. Gouran [18] suggests that perceptions related to the quality of the other participants contributions to the group effort have strong impacts on satisfaction. Given this, it seems reasonable that other relational issues, such as group cohesion, would also have the potential to influence satisfaction and performance. The results from this research clearly support this proposition. Furthermore, these results indicate that researchers should be cautious in their use and interpretation of satisfaction, particularly if the results are interpreted without consideration of group composition and cohesion.

Finally, the fact that more than 10 percent of the groups were deadlocked has implications for both this and future research. The fact that no ad-hoc no-tech groups were deadlocked while at least two groups in each of the other treatment conditions were deadlocked suggests an interaction between these variables, particularly because the bulk of the deadlocked groups were established and used computer-mediated communication. For example, it is likely that members of ad-hoc no-tech groups were more inclined to reach an agreement because of both the face-to-face format of their discussion and the early stage of their groups' development. This is supportive of the findings reported by Zack [57], who proposes that groups lacking a substantial history of working together need a rich and interactive communication environment to develop a shared interpretive context. The ad-hoc groups in our study lacked this context; therefore the contrast in the deadlock status between the ad-hoc groups using the CMC and those that did not may be related to this issue. Further, the richer communication environment of the face-to-face setting may have encouraged more compromise by ad-hoc group members as they attempted not only to come to a resolution of the task, but also, in effect, to put their best foot forward as they interacted with this new group of people. On the other hand, for members of established groups, this was one of several group meetings in which they would participate; therefore the pressure to resolve conflict immediately and accommodate other group members' opinions might have been less pronounced. These findings raise several questions about the interaction between the nature of the communication media and task-related conflict.

## Conclusions and Limitations

As IS OFTEN THE CASE, THIS INVESTIGATION GENERATED more questions than it answered. Nevertheless, there is much we can learn from this study. First, the results confinn that group history is an important variable in research, particularly in relation to membef perceptions. The finding that member satisfaction is related to cohesion suggests that further research is needed to examine the relationship between user satisfaction, group history, and interpersonal attraction. However, the finding that perfonnance differences did not exist between established and ad-hoc groups has important implications for research. The results of many laboratory studies involving ad-hoc groups have been questioned because of the nature of the experimental groups. Our results, which show no significant difference in perfonnance between established and ad-hoc groups, suggest that criticism of prior laboratory research may, at least in part, be unjustified.

This research also lends support to the view that a group's shared interpretive context is important and should be considered in interpreting research on intact groups. Specifically, our results call into question the assumption that the mere use of intact student groups in research is necessarily better than the use of ad-hoc student groups. Carley [2] suggests that, when established groups engage in a task that they perceive to be familiar or routine, group members may make assumptions about the task that will cause them to overlook important infonnation. Our results support this perspective. Given this, established groups may actually represent an inferior population from which to draw subjects for some types of research. Therefore, researchers should be aware of the problems they may encounter when they ask established groups to process a task with which group members are likely to be familiar.

These observations also have implications for meeting leaders and facilitators. For example, when established groups work on a task requiring infonnation sharing, meeting facilitators should look for potenti al problems associated with group members engaging in incomplete infonnation sharing. In these circumstances, facilitators should consider using strong facilitation and specific directions for avoiding incomplete infonnation sharing. For example, leaders may want to frequently remind members of their group to discuss issues and ideas explicitly that others might not possess. Also, it might be useful to build diversity into established groups purposely so that group members do not assume that infonnation is held by every group member. Finally, meeting procedures such as dialectic inquiry or devil' s advocacy--procedures that force participants to examine their assumptions and premises critically--might help established groups avoid these pitfalls.

The results from our research add to a growing list of findings that show that CMC appears to have no significant impact on infonnation sharing [5, 51]. While it is too early to conclude that CMC is not useful as a tool to improve infonnation shanng, it appears that the addition of the technology alone is not sufficient. More research is needed to examine both whether and how collaborative technologies can be used to improve infonnation-sharing perfonnance. For example, it is possible that over time infonnation sharing will improve as CMC users learn both to share their ideas and to listen to the messages and cues shared by others. Similarly, more sophisticated meeting interventions, such as dialectic inquiry, might be helpful for improving CMC users' infonnation-sharing perfonnance. Finally, it is possible that the CMC used in this research was too restrictive; additional research should look at the impact of richer media on infonnation-sharing perfonnance.

As collaborative technologies continue to increase in importance, so too will our need to understand how these technologies influence human interactions and behaviors. Research such as that reported here is important for improving our understanding of the nature of how groups behave when using unique or new communication technologies. In addition, although the theoretical focus of this research (e.g., a study conducted using student subjects in a controlled laboratory setting) can limit the usefulness of the findings for real-world applications, there is much to be learned about both the variables under scrutiny in this study and the methodological issues associated with conducting this type of research. In particular, the findings reported here should be useful for understanding and interpreting prior research that has examined ass use. In addition, it should be useful for guiding investigators in designing and executing research on these topics in the future.

Nevertheless, the nature of the experimental setting, the subject population, and the type of task used in this research limit generalizations of the results of this study to other contexts. For example, the use of students in experimental research has been criticized [17]. Although the established groups were ongoing (i.e., they would continue to work on other projects after the experimental session), they were scheduled to break up at the end of the semester. Furthermore, established groups in a field setting working on a task that has significant potential to affect their lives in substantive ways would be expected to respond quite differently than students. In addition, the way that the task was designed and delivered also has implications for interpreting these results. In particular, participants were required to recall all of the task-related information from memory. In most organizational meetings, participants would be expected to discuss information based on notes, computer reports, and other documentation. Further, this task was structured with partially shared information, while most of the prior research on hidden profiles has examined tasks that include only initially shared and initially unshared information. The nature of the task used in a research study must always be considered in interpreting the research findings [33].

The small size of the groups used in this research is also important. As noted earlier, it is possible that larger groups would benefit more from CMC than would smaller groups [7]. In many instances, organizational teams are quite large. This suggests not only that furtherresearch is needed to examine the impact of group size on information sharing, but also that these results should be considered in light of this fact. Finally, the one-time nature of this study also limits the generalizability of the findings to other contexts. In particular, over time, groups that use technology would be expected to adapt to and change the way they interact as they continue to use the technology [10, 21, 32]. Therefore, results are likely to differ for groups composed of nonstudent participants or groups that are studied in longitudinal research.

Further, asking subjects to discuss a socially relevant task using memory is much more consistent with how people would typically discuss this type of case (e.g., people do not typically bring notes with them when they are discussing a local election or social issues). Yet, within organizational or business groups, managers often do rely on notes or other written materials when discussing important issues. Alas, the influence of removing task information is an empirical question that warrants investigation.

6. The pre- and postsession cohesion scores show that the CMC did have an effect on cohesion. While no significant difference was observed in presession cohesion across the technology treatment, the difference between CMC and no-tech groups in postsession cohesion approached significance $( F ( 1 , 2 5 5 ) = 3 . 6 1 4 ; p = 0 . 0 5 8 )$ . A one-way ANOVA showed that after the session ad-hoc CMC groups were less cohesive than ad-hoc no-tech groups $( F ( 1 , 2 5 5 ) =$ $2 3 . 6 2 7 ; p < 0 . 0 0 1 )$ . Thus, although ad-hoc no-tech and ad-hoc CMC groups each started with the same relative perceptions of cohesion, the CMC appears to have impeded the groups that were using CMC from developing greater cohesion.

7. Attention blocking will likely have other impacts on the information-sharing process, as well. For example, it is possible that groups that suffer from attention blocking may be less efficient in sharing information by being more redundant in their information sharing. That is, if individuals are not paying attention to what is being presented by other members, they may repeat information that has already been shared. While the topic of redundant information sharing is important, it is beyond the scope of the current study. Further research should be designed to examine specifically whether attention blocking negatively affects group efficiency and performance.
