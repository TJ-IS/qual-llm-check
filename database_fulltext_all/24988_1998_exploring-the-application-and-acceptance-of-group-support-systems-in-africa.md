# Exploring the Application and Acceptance of Group Support Systems in Africa

GERT-JAN DE VREEDE, NOEL JONES, AND RABSON J. MGAYA

GERT-JAN DE VREEDE is an Assistant Professor at the Faculty of Technology, Policy, and Management of Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands. He received his Ph.D. in systems engineering from the same university, where he is currently responsible for the GSS research program. His research interests include the application of collaborative technologies to facilitate organizational design activities, and the adoption and diffusion of GSS in both Western environments as well as developing countries. His articles have appeared in Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Decision Systems, Journal of Creativity and Innovation Management, Holland Management Review, and Journal of Simulation Practice and Theory.

NOEL JONES is Vice President, Europe, for Capacity Building International (CBI), which specializes in capacity building for individuals, institutions, and countries. Prior to joining CBI, he worked at the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank, where he managed the Groupware Services. He has designed, developed, and implemented a wide range of strategic planning, organizational development, and management development programs throughout the world. He holds a Ph.D. in management and psychology from Manchester University and is the author of a number of books and articles on management, psychology, and work issues. Dr. Jones has delivered papers at three world conferences and many international and national conferences.

RABSON J. MGAYA holds an M.B.A. from the University of Dar es Salaam in Tanzania. He is a Lecturer at the Computing Centre of the University of Dar es Salaam. Currently he is pursuing a Ph.D. in group support systems at Delft University of Technology in the Netherlands. His research interests include group support systems, management information systems, and operations management.

ABSTRACT: Cross-cultural GSS field studies are scarce. Although the state of knowledge and theory development in this area warrants a focus on descriptive field explorations, most cross-cultural GSS research has taken place in laboratory environments. The study reported here represents the first detailed descriptive field study of GSS application in Africa. A grounded theory approach was used to collect and analyze data on eleven projects in which GSS meetings were organized in Malawi, Zimbabwe, and Tanzania. From the data emerged a model of GSS acceptance in the cultures investigated that extends the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) in terms of a specification of a number of relevant external factors. These factors include the endorsement of top management, computer literacy, oral communication preference, referent power, and satisfaction with use. Furthermore, the findings suggest that there is potential for applying GSS in Africa to support capacity-building efforts, which tops the agenda of international development agencies.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: cross-cultural studies, field study, group support systems, grounded theory research, technology acceptance.

GROUP SUPPORT SYSTEMS (GSS) HAVE BEEN AROUND FOR SOME TIME. Already at the end of the 1970s, experiments were carried out with the first GSS [52]. In the 1980s, the practical application of and academic inquiry into GSS increased dramatically. Although many questions about the added value and impact of GSS have been raised and answered, a lot of issues remain the subject of research efforts [5]. One particular area in which this is true is the application of GSS in settings outside of Western Europe, the United States, and Canada. Notable exceptions are studies conducted in Bulgaria, Mexico, and Southeast Asia [15, 27, 53].

This scarcity of GSS studies in non-Euro-American contexts represents a serious gap in our knowledge about the impact of GSS on group work. Technologies have built-in designers' assumptions on how people work. Since these assumptions may be culturally determined, it becomes dangerous to assume that research findings concerning the use and acceptance of technology can be unconditionally transferred across cultures [19].

There are indications that considerable differences exist in the way in which technologies are used and diffuse across cultures. For example, Straub found substantial differences with respect to the use and diffusion of electronic mail and fax in the United States and Japan $[40]$ . Griffith found that Bulgarian groups using a GSS were more likely to challenge authority than were U.S. groups $[15]$ . Based on the differences they found in the group decision-making behavior in Korean and U.S. business firms, Chung and Adams suggest different ways in which GSS should be employed in these environments $[6]$ . The fact that GSS technology has a predominantly positive impact on U.S. groups does not imply it will have such an impact on non-U.S. groups.

Understanding technology use in other cultural environments is becoming highly relevant considering the increasing global nature of organizational work and the growing mobility of organizational workers $[34]$ . The next century may see more and more multicultural groups working together through technology $[47]$ . Moreover, advanced (collaborative) technologies, especially when based on Internet technology, are becoming rapidly available for less-developed nations.

This paper reports on a field study on the application of GSS in various situations in a number of Southeast African countries. The study explored the perception of the African users of this type of technology. As described below, the cultural context of these countries is significantly different from those in which GSS research has been carried out so far. Hence, they represent a unique setting in which to investigate the cross-cultural application of GSS.

The academic objective of the study was to generate insight into the contextual issues surrounding the effective use and acceptance of GSS technology. We built on the work of Davis et al. concerning the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [8].

This model is useful in understanding and predicting how users accept and adopt new information technologies [41]. TAM posits that an individual's perception of a technology's usefulness and ease of use explains and predicts his or her acceptance and actual use of the technology. TAM also indicates that other external factors may influence a person's perception of usefulness and ease of use. Based on the insights from our study, we specified a number of these external factors in the context of GSS use in Africa. The practical objective of the study was to develop participative approaches and techniques to support capacity building in a number of underdeveloped countries.

## Capacity Building and Participative Development

CAPACITY AND CAPACITY BUILDING ARE TERMS WIDELY USED in the development arena [25, 51, 55]. Capacity is defined as the people, institutions, and practices that enable countries to achieve their development goals [55]. It follows that, in order to enable developing countries to guide and execute development for themselves, effective capacity-building programs are the most important prerequisite. As such, capacity building requires an investment in human capital, institutions, and practices extending over many areas: from the public sector to the private sector, to the civil society, and to education and training (see, e.g., [25, 51, 55]).

The predominant development paradigm is one in which development is either done to or done for those who are perceived to need development. It is only in recent years that there has been a shift toward doing development with or empowering those who need development to do it for themselves (see, e.g., [54, 55]). As development finance moved from investments in large infrastructure projects such as roads, bridges, and dams, to projects involving human resources, there has been a growing realization that development is complex. There also has been a growing recognition in recent years that successful development initiatives are those where the beneficiaries and other stakeholders in development have played an active part in the development process. The same arguments that are valid for group problem solving in general [3] are also applicable for participative development [54]:

\- Complexity: Development problems are often so complex that no single person has all the understanding, information, and resources to solve the problem alone.

\- Evaluation: A group of people is often more capable of providing a reality check for development issues (problem statements, solutions) than is the individual proposing the issues.

\- Acceptance: When all the stakeholders are represented in development activities, there is a better chance their interests will be accommodated in the solution. A choice that seems arbitrary to one participant may be critical to another.

\- Interest: Often many stakeholders have a strong interest in development problems and their implications, so it is appropriate to involve those people.

\- Synergy: People involved in creative problem solving processes often build on one another's ideas leveraging their different talents and knowledge. This synergy can lead to new, rich ideas that may not have otherwise occurred.

Many multilateral and bilateral donors, such as the World Bank, the Canadian International Development Agency, and the Commonwealth Secretariat, are seeking new ways to involve stakeholders and civil society at large in the development efforts they are financing in member countries. There are two basic assumptions underlying this intention:

1. Adequate capacity should be available in the environment that is receiving the development aid.

2. The available capacity should be mobilized as effectively as possible.

The two assumptions on capacity availability and mobilization mentioned above, however, do not hold in Africa. According to a recent investigation into the status of capacity building in Africa, a gloomy picture was presented [55]. $^{1}$ Among the various, interrelated, problems mentioned were:

\- Little attention is paid to staff or skills development in the civil service.

\- Short-term gains and opportunism outweigh long-term policies and vision.

\- Private entrepreneurs face weak infrastructures and insecure legal environments.

\- Governments often exclude or resist civil society (including academics, church groups, and welfare associations) as a full partner in development, while many actors in civil society lack basic skills in organization, communication, administration, and management.

\- Quality and relevance of education to meet the development needs of African countries are declining due to the poor facilities and the lack of qualified and motivated teachers.

In other words, the problems surrounding African capacity-building efforts make it imperative to find new ways to promote capacity building. The success of such new ways depends on the extent to which:

1. People are trained and educated in order to provide them with the necessary skills.

2. Relevant institutions are supported to become worthy players in the development activities.

3. Methods and tools are developed that support participative development efforts.

Although we address the first two issues as well, we focus on the third. We explored the application of GSS methods and tools to support participative development efforts. So far, many examples of such methods supporting capacity building have been employed, but few have proven successful $[22, 55]$ . From a practical point of view, our study investigated whether the application of GSS could alleviate these problems.

## Practical Reasons

THE NEED FOR A MORE PARTICIPATORY APPROACH TO DEVELOPMENT issues has been recognized for some time. However, experiences from the field show that the implementation of such approaches is not always straightforward [22]. Too often, external experts assist with the development of a country or sector (agriculture, education, etc.) with little consultation with the stakeholders or beneficiaries. Many approaches, such as surveys, interviews, or focus group meetings, have tried to involve stakeholders or beneficiaries at various stages of a development assignment. While these methods have been beneficial, there are downsides [22]:

\- Inadequate coverage—for example, in one African country, it took two weeks to conduct nineteen interviews to get stakeholder feedback.

\- A structure that cannot handle the target population's spontaneity. In one-on-one interviews, the interviewee may not speak freely, fearing retribution.

\- A lag between when data are gathered and when they are available for analysis.

The application of GSS may help overcome these problems. Group support systems (GSS) represent a specific type of groupware that support creative meeting activities in local and dispersed settings. Although research findings show that GSS are not an answer to every group problem, GSS meetings are often found to be more productive, more efficient, more effective, and more satisfying than non-GSS meetings $[12, 32, 35]$ . The success of GSS meetings is frequently attributed to specific GSS characteristics, such as the ability to communicate anonymously and in parallel, and to keep an electronic record of the meeting minutes $[31]$ . For example, in the situation mentioned above, GSS could provide considerable support. Instead of nineteen separate interviews, stakeholders could be invited to a single one-day workshop where they would answer the questions electronically. Aside from time reductions, protected (anonymous) communication, and immediate electronic availability of the interview data, the advantage of this setting would be that it enables creative cross-fertilization: The interviewees could see and respond to each others' ideas.

There is a substantial body of knowledge about the application of GSS in the field that shows the feasibility of such an application of GSS $[11, 12]$ . In fact, the majority of GSS field studies report successful applications of GSS $[49]$ . From an efficiency perspective, there have been reports of labor cost reductions averaging 50 percent and reductions of project calendar days up to 90 percent $[33, 36, 50]$ . Other positive findings involve issues such as $[49]$ :

• High participant satisfaction;

• Higher and more equal participation opportunity;

\- Improved meeting outcome quality;

\- Anonymity separating status, authority, and roles from ideas, resulting in little evaluation apprehension and cross-hierarchical communication support;

• Less emotional discussions, more negotiation.

In summary, we propose that the field experiences with GSS so far sufficiently indicate that employing the technology in Africa is potentially beneficial. The development arena that we described in the introduction consists of people from various backgrounds, with various status levels, and often with conflicting goals. Yet, there is a motive or urge to collaborate. GSS may effectively support this collaboration.

## Academic Reasons

GSS are now used on all continents except for Antarctica [32]. It is claimed that more than two million people worldwide have participated in thousands of GSS-supported meetings [3, 32]. However, as pointed out by Watson et al. [53], studies on GSS predominantly involve groups from Western cultures, such as the United States and Europe. It has been shown that theories with respect to group behavior, organizational behavior, and a variety of other disciplines are dependent on the culture of the groups or organizations studied [19]. Because most conceptual and theoretical foundations regarding GSS technology have been based on traditional North American perceptions on groups of people working together [53], one needs to be cautious about concluding that “Western” GSS findings are transferable to other cultures.

Up to 1988, almost all GSS research was carried out using American groups alone [37]. Even now, comparatively little GSS research data have been gathered on other cultures [32]. Exceptions are studies in the Asia-Pacific region [37] and in Mexico [27, 30]. Table 1 presents an overview of GSS research in non-Euro-American cultures. Although we do not claim that this overview is complete, we know of no other relevant studies.

The studies in this overview suggest that group decision making is always strongly influenced by the cultural norms surrounding the process. Culture seems to affect the satisfaction and participation equity of groups from different cultures, primarily because it affects the perception levels of the participants. However, given the relatively small number of studies, it is not surprising that no mature theory has yet emerged encompassing the various cultural dimensions and their effect on the use of GSS. Moreover, it appears that there are hardly any “real” experiences with other cultures using GSS. Most studies offer theoretical/argumentative discussions or concern laboratory experiments with student subjects. As such, hardly anything is known about the application of GSS in the cross-cultural field.

In other words, from a research perspective, we do not know a lot about the application of GSS in other cultures yet. If we are to capitalize successfully on the potential benefits of GSS technology and design better ways of employing it, we have to build this knowledge $[32, 37]$ . As far as we know, there have not yet been any studies that report on the practical application of GSS in an African context. ## Research Strategy

ONTOLOGICALLY, OUR RESEARCH FOLLOWS AN INTERPRETIVIST PHILOSOPHY, subjectively interpreting observations of reality. We focus on studying GSS and its application in its natural environment. In order to place our study in the context of investigating the application of GSS in other cultures, we describe our view on an overall research strategy for this broader research domain. We then elaborate on the particular strategy used in the study reported here.

## The Broader Context: A Strategy for Cross-Cultural GSS Research

Before describing in more detail how we carried out this study, we need to place it within its broader research context. Our study, investigating the application of GSS in Africa, is part of a research domain that may be labeled the cross-cultural application of GSS. Table 1 gave an overview of some other studies in this domain. Below, we present an overall research strategy (or inquiring system) for this research domain in terms of a sequence of data acquisition and analysis activities.

Cross-cultural GSS research deals with an ill-structured research problem: How can we effectively apply GSS in non-Euro-American cultures? It is difficult, if not impossible, to address this problem in a purely deductive way. Hence, we argue, it is imperative to identify inductively and to explore further the cross-cultural application of GSS in real-world situations and to develop theories accordingly. In order to do so, we propose an inductive-hypothetic model cycle as research strategy based on

Churchman's Singerian inquiring system [2, 7]. This research strategy consists of five activities, delineated in figure 1:

1. A number of empirical situations are investigated and described.

2. The essential aspects from the empirical situations are abstracted into a descriptive conceptual model.

3. From the descriptive conceptual model, a prescriptive conceptual model is derived in the form of a theory. This theory should be addressing the issues observed.

4. To test and validate the prescriptive conceptual model, the theory is applied in a number of prescriptive empirical situations. These may encompass both field situations and laboratory experiments.

5. Finally, the prescriptive empirical models are evaluated. Additional requirements for improving the prescriptive conceptual model may be identified. To further test and expand the theory inductively, it may be used as an initial theory in a number of new descriptive empirical situations.

The overview of past cross-cultural GSS research showed that descriptive empirical studies are rare and thus needed. Little is known about real groups in non-Euro-American cultures using GSS. This explains why the research efforts described here focus on the descriptive activities in figure 1.

## Strategy in This Study

Although the overall strategy presented above describes the order and interdependence of the research activities, it does not offer guidelines for the individual activities. Hence, we also need a strategy on a lower level of abstraction. For our study, which covered the descriptive research activities (activities 1 and 2 in figure 1), we used a grounded theory approach [14]. This approach aims to develop inductively derived grounded theories about a phenomenon [42]. A grounded theory is not built a priori; rather, it emerges during a study as data collection, analysis, and theory development occur in parallel.

We collected data using various qualitative and quantitative techniques. The combined use of techniques may (1) counterbalance their respective weaknesses and strengths, and (2) support the creation of a rich representation of reality. During our study, we used both open and semistructured interviews to converse with participants and meeting owners before, during, and after electronic meetings. Also, the electronic logs of the meetings were studied. Participants in the GSS meetings in Tanzania filled in satisfaction questionnaires after each meeting, consisting of open questions and questions on a five-point Likert scale [4]. These sources were augmented by our own observations during the group meetings and other interactions with the stakeholders involved. These observations concerned subjective interpretations of events. This subjectivity is often pointed out as a disadvantage of qualitative approaches. One way to counterbalance researcher bias is by using various data-collection techniques, as we did in this study. Another way is by using research teams to collect and analyze the data [17]. Reducing researcher bias can then be sought in combining the subjective interpretations of different observers [7]. In our study, several researchers kept observation notes. Comparison resulted in additional insights.

In general, data-collection activities may be guided by relevant existing theories. In our case, it can be argued that our data-collection efforts could have been prestructured using research domain relevant theories such as the cultural theories of Hofstede $[19]$ or the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) of Davis et al. $[8]$ . We decided not to do so in order to avoid a standard way of thinking about the phenomena observed. Having too much a priori theoretical guidance can block a researcher from seeing what is and is not really significant $[42]$ . We wanted to experience the unique reality of applying GSS in Africa in as broad a manner as possible, and to make sense of our observations without being constrained by the boundaries of a certain theory. After analyzing our observations, further insight was pursued by placing our findings in the context of the TAM and comparing them with GSS acceptance findings in Euro-American environments.

The specific technique we used for analyzing the collected data was open coding $[42]$ . This means that we closely examined all collected data, broke them into discrete parts, and labeled these parts. By comparing these labeled parts (called “concepts”) for similarities and differences, we could group them to the extent that they seemed to address the same phenomenon. In the end, a number of categories could be identified, which eventually represented the main constructs of the theory that emerged. In view of this analysis technique, the absence of the satisfaction questionnaire during the GSS meetings in Malawi and Zimbabwe did not represent a problem. Our data analysis techniques were not based on using exactly comparable collections of data. Rather, they focused on distilling insights from any collection of observations. Hence, in order to increase the potential of arriving at meaningful insights, we decided to include the Tanzanian questionnaire data in the analysis process.

The role of the researchers in the study was an active one, bearing similarities to action researchers [13, 56]. We acted as facilitators for the GSS meetings. This role covered various activities. We helped the meeting owners to prepare an agenda. We trained the participants in using the technology. We operated the master console—that is, we acted as technographers—and we moderated the meetings. Finally, we carried out some postmeeting activities, such as preparing reports, as desired. For none of the meetings was there any financial incentive on our part.

## Research Setting

IN THIS SECTION WE DESCRIBE THE SETTING OF OUR STUDY in more detail. We first address the three countries in which we collected data. Then we provide some background information on the number and type of meetings studied and give a detailed account of a number of these meetings. Finally, we describe a number of practical issues that we encountered during the various meetings.

## Background Information on the Countries Visited

The GSS meetings were conducted in three African countries over a two-year period: Malawi, Zimbabwe, and Tanzania, which are located in East and South Africa (see figure 2). Table 3 presents some demographic data from 1995 [45]. Tanzania and Malawi rank among the poorest ten countries in the world. Zimbabwe is slightly better off, with a national product per capita twice that of Tanzania and Malawi (\$US 1,500 versus \$US 750 in 1994). All countries are former English colonies so that English is one of the official languages. Economies largely depend on agriculture.

## Background Information on the Meetings Studied

We studied a total of eleven projects in which GSS meetings were organized. All meetings addressed real problems; the participants came together because they had a need to handle an undesirable situation or discuss an important issue. Table 4 summarizes the background information on these meetings. (Note that in Malawi and Zimbabwe a number of meetings were held for the same purpose, i.e., the same project.) Stakeholders from a variety of governmental bodies, civil society, and other relevant sectors participated in the meetings with a national focus. The meetings with an organizational focus (both public and private organizations) involved participants from various backgrounds ranging from management trainee to chairman of the board of directors. Most meetings addressed both strategic and operational issues with a strong focus on results that could be directly implemented.

The meetings in Malawi and Zimbabwe were carried out using a mobile GSS facility running GroupSystems V that was brought over from the World Bank's headquarters in Washington. The meetings in Tanzania used the GSS meeting facilities at the University of Dar es Salaam in the country's capital. The Tanzanian facilities include both a fixed facility and a mobile facility, each of which runs GroupSystems for Windows and can seat twelve participants (see figure 3).

## An Environmental Strategy for Malawi

In order to provide a context for the interpretations we present below, we elaborate on two projects in more detail. First, we discuss the development of an environmental strategy in Malawi. Following this discussion, we present a project that was aimed at finding solutions for capacity-building problems in Tanzania. Both projects can be considered representative for the type of projects that were supported with GSS.

The first project involves a two-day roundtable to help develop an environmental strategy for Malawi. There were forty-six to fifty participants present each day. In-country participants represented different stakeholders in Malawi who were involved with environmental issues: five different ministries, the private sector, community leaders, mayors, NGOs, and donors.

## Morning Day 1: Kicking Off

The host minister, permanent secretary, and the World Bank's resident representative opened the roundtable. This was followed by a set of presentations by various donors who were working on environmental programs in Malawi: UNDP, USAID, CIDA, ODA, EU, CURE, IDA, and NGOs.

## Afternoon Day 1: Identifying Main Topics

We set the room up with eight GroupSystems workstations. Six seats were arranged around each station. Participants were welcomed and asked to take their seats in a random manner. Next, the technology and the objectives of the afternoon activities were introduced. These objectives included the identification of the key environmental topics that had to be addressed during the roundtable and included in an environmental strategy for Malawi.

Using GroupSystems' Categorizer module, we opened up the meeting for each group around a workstation to enter their suggested topics. Forty-eight topics were identified; these were then merged down to twenty-six. The group worked to eliminate redundancy from the list by merging similar ideas. But if even one person objected to a proposed merger, it was not carried out, even if no reasons were given. We followed this procedure to show the participants that they owned and controlled the process. We felt it would be harmful to the process if they would get the impression that outside facilitators exercised influence on the agenda. However, in a Western group we would not have used this procedure. Our experiences with Western groups are that they have more discussion on merging topics. If someone disagrees with merging, he or she is expected to provide the rest of the group with arguments that can be (and often are) challenged.

To reduce the number of topics further, we requested participants to suggest more general headings. Eventually, the group reduced the list to eighteen unique topics. These were exported to the Vote module. Using the rank-ordering method, the group identified a clear block of seven topics with the highest votes and eleven topics with low votes. The top seven topics were: Participation, Capacity Building, Coordination among Ministries, Environmental Education, Water Resource Management, Land Degradation, and Urban Settlement. During the subsequent break, the prioritized list of eighteen topics was printed and handed out to each participant on their return.

We then asked each of the eight groups to work on all seven topics to identify the issues, challenges, and opportunities they posed. Three groups were asked to start at the top with the first topic and work down, while another three groups were asked to start at the last topic and work up. The remaining two groups could begin as they wished. Each group was asked to address all seven topics using the Categorizer module again. With this procedure (which is also common among GSS facilitators in Western settings), we ensured that each topic received good coverage. If all had started at the first topic, the latter few topics might not get the attention they deserved, because participants would have mostly engaged in discussions on the first topics.

This activity lasted about two hours. It generated twenty-five to thirty-five issues and challenges per topic, and an additional fifteen to twenty opportunities per topic. It appeared that the group had thoroughly examined each important, resulting in a detailed list of issues, challenges, and opportunities. The participants ensured that each topic received a balanced (pros and cons) and elaborate dose of facts, viewpoints, and opinions.

The use of small groups around each workstation was a great success. (This was also experienced the second day.) It left time for discussion and consensus building before entering data. Only one person per group did the typing, thus reducing the need for keyboard skills. (Some participants were not familiar with the Qwerty keyboard layout; this setup is rather uncommon in the Western meetings in which we participated. Usually more work stations are available, and Western meeting owners are often more inclined to break the group up into several subgroups that meet at different times so that everyone has an equal chance to participate.)

At the end of the first day, we informed the participants that they would receive a hard copy of the results next morning and that seven focus groups would be set up, one for each topic. Participants would be asked to select their own focus group on arrival next day. Overnight, we worked on the report, prepared macros, and converted it into a Microsoft Word document.

## Morning Day 2: Recommendations for the Future

Handing out the hard copy of the work done in the previous day clearly triggered an enthusiastic response from the group. Participants immediately saw their feedback formalized and used in subsequent discussions. They showed that they felt encouraged to further participate.

Participants formed seven focus groups by selecting their own work groups. The task of each focus group was to take the issues, challenges, and opportunities for their particular topic and come up with a list of recommendations, actions, and who should be responsible for implementing these. In some cases, particular donors who also wished to could express interest in committing funds to help support the implementation of particular recommendations.

This activity took the whole morning. Each focus group orally discussed the previous day's output and built on it, as instructed. Using the Categorizer module (which contained the meeting data from the previous day), they then typed in their results so that there was a running record of their agreements. At lunchtime, this report was again printed out and copied for everyone on his or her return. During the activities, it appeared that the participants were completely captivated by the issue at hand. The level of discussion, debate, and interest was great.

## Afternoon Day 2: Presentation and Consolidation

Each participant had a hard copy of the morning's work available to him or her as we went into the plenary meeting. Each group presented its report and this was then commented on, added to, and so on, as appropriate, both orally and using Categorizer. Two groups wanted to make some changes to their report, so we asked them to make these on the hard copy so that we could do a quick edit on the technographer workstation. Hard copies were essential so that participants did not have to rely solely on the central screen or the laptops for viewing reports.

After completing the plenary feedback, we asked each group to select just one recommendation using Vote. This last request was well received by participants. The selected recommendations were labeled in the reports of each focus group. At the conclusion of the meeting, the participants indicated that they felt that the results of the two-day meeting were remarkable. They identified and reached consensus on the most important topics with respect to an environmental strategy and composed a prioritized list with the most important recommendations to be implemented. We worked on the report the next morning and had a spell-checked forty-seven-page report ready to hand over to the permanent secretary of the host ministry for a 1.00 P.M. meeting.

## Aftermath

The project was a good example of computer-supported capacity building. First, lack of relevant participation is a major problem in traditional development projects $[55]$ . In this project, a comprehensive number of relevant stakeholders were actively involved in the formulation of the environmental strategy and had a key role in determining the agenda for environmental action plans. Second, the process and technology used were also responsible for keeping the available capacity effectively mobilized. A strategy development process that normally takes weeks to produce a final report was now finalized within three days. Third, the participants were exposed to and taught methods and techniques that enabled them to cooperate effectively on an important development issue. Such skills are important but often lacking in the development area $[54, 55]$ . Finally, the way the project was set up and executed became a blueprint for future roundtables.

## Solutions for Capacity-Building Problems in Tanzania

The second project reported in detail concerns a one-day meeting on capacity-building initiatives in Tanzania. Fourteen stakeholders from the government, the private sector, and the World Bank met to gain insight in the causes of problems frustrating capacity-building efforts in Tanzania and to identify a prioritized list of solutions to handle the most important problems.

## Morning: Cause Deliberations and Proposed Solutions

The World Bank resident representative opened the meeting. Next, a warming-up exercise was performed to introduce GroupSystems and acquaint participants with the user interface. We had fourteen workstations set up, so there was only workstation sharing when there was a (power) problem with one of the workstations.

The first activity concerned an hour-long electronic discussion/deliberation on a list of predefined causes for capacity-building problems. Using the Group Outliner module, the participants discussed a list of causes that were organized under headings such as Incentive related issues, Technical assistance issues, Educational/Learning issues, and Donors. This resulted in 135 comments being entered, ranging from brief expressions of agreements to more elaborate argumentations. During the activity, we turned on the “comment numbering” option so that participants could refer to each others’ comments.

The participants were very serious in addressing the agenda topic. It was clear that they were quite knowledgeable and involved with issues surrounding capacity building. However, the meeting atmosphere was also relaxed. Most participants seemed to know each other well. Also, some cracked a few jokes, which helped make the meeting more alive.

The second activity in the morning was the prioritization of the problem causes for each of the headings separately. This was done with the multiple selection method in the Vote module, which we consider one of the simplest vote techniques in Group-Systems. Initially, the outcomes did not show consensus on some issues. Before inviting the participants to a revote, these issues were discussed. Contrary to the Western meeting environment that we are most familiar with, this discussion seemed not to be about convincing other participants, but about explaining an issue to the facilitator. Participants engaged only in a few direct discussions with their colleagues. As facilitators, we had to be sensitive to this behavior by not forcing the participants into direct arguments, but by making sure various perspectives were presented during the course of the meeting.

We invited the participants to brainstorm about a number of solutions to handle the most important causes. Using Group Outlines, participants entered solution proposals as comments starting with “solution” (see table 5). It appeared that well-formulated and sometimes very detailed solutions were proposed. Nevertheless, within half an hour, this activity resulted in about one hundred solutions.

## Afternoon: Solution Elaboration and Discussion

Often, groups that we work with experience an “after-lunch-dip”: a period of about thirty minutes after lunch during which they need to get up to speed again and become productive. This group surprised us in that, immediately after lunch, every participant was very serious and active; they were really pounding the keyboards to elaborate on the solutions. At this point, it became clear that the participants generated so many solutions that we would not have time to discuss each one in detail. It was therefore decided to select a number of solutions to be the focus of oral discussion. Because some solutions appeared to address more than one cause, it was suggested and unanimously agreed that the list of solutions should be subjected to voting irrespective of the headings used during the cause deliberations in the morning. Using the multiple selection method again, twenty-two solutions were selected that scored four or more votes.

The remainder of the meeting was spent orally discussing and exploring these twenty-two solutions. Contrary to the morning discussion, this discussion was very hot and lively. A lot of issues and ideas were generated and were keyed in by the technographer. It was clear, however, that a number of outspoken and fearless participants dominated the discussion. Unlike our experiences in Western meetings, it was noteworthy that few participants took ownership of their ideas. During oral discussions in Western meetings, participants often mention that they entered a certain idea and then go on to elaborate on it. In this meeting, ideas were just discussed in general. Hence, it appears that in the African setting anonymity is more important to stimulate open and balanced discussions.

## Practical GSS Issues and Challenges in Africa

In this last section we describe a number of practical issues and challenges with respect to running GSS meetings in Africa. This account is meant to sketch the context and nature of the situation in which the GSS technology was applied.

## Candy for Bureaucrats

Taking computer equipment into Africa is no easy process. It requires lots of documentation, describing exactly what equipment is brought in and which institution owns it. Also, letters of invitation from a local institution are useful. It is especially important to carry many copies of these documents that can be handed out as candy to the employees of the various bureaucracies through which one must pass.

## Appointments Are Flexible

It is important to bear in mind that appointments are “flexible.” Timings and dates can be changed on very short notice, or even with no notice. This means that the preparation of GSS meetings can take a lot of time if several planning meetings are required with a problem owner. This situation is aggravated by the fact that phone communications are not reliable. The only reliable phone networks are for cellular phones, but not everyone has access to these owing to their cost.

## A Hostile Environment

Heat and humidity are considerable in the regions we visited. It is advisable to conduct GSS meetings in a room with air conditioning or another type of climate control. Computer equipment is not designed to operate in humid, over 40 degrees centigrade (115 Fahrenheit) conditions. For example, our projector switched off automatically during a number of meetings because it had a safety switch to prevent overheating. These conditions also have to be remembered with respect to the storage of the equipment. After some time in Tanzania, we noticed fungus growing on equipment that had not been stored in a dry place.

## A Confusion of Tongues

English is an official language in the countries where we conducted GSS meetings. However, the use of English is often limited to written, official documentation. Oral communication often takes place in a local language, such as Swahili. This may also happen during GSS meetings. Sometimes participants feel more comfortable explaining or discussing an issue in an indigenous language. For this reason, it is important that an external facilitator teams up with a local facilitator. Another issue involves the fact that certain words are considered inappropriate in Tanzania: for example, “fresh,” which is quite often used as slang, and “competitive,” which has a strongly negative connotation. This has to be taken into account not only when phrasing questions for the meeting agenda but also on postmeeting questionnaires.

## Electricity, a Blessing in Disguise

Electricity represents a very dynamic commodity in the regions we visited. First, there are significant periods in which no electricity is available. Second, power supply in the countries we visited is known for large fluctuations. This means that a number of precautions have to be taken. UPS units are hard to do without. Also, plan to have the batteries of all laptops charged to survive relatively short electricity outages. Bring lots of wire to get electricity from other rooms if necessary. To prevent irreparable damage, power stabilizers have to be used for equipment without adapters, such as hubs and LCD projectors. Finally, avoid connecting laptops directly into electricity sockets. Instead, connect them through fused and grounded strips that function as safety valves.

## Be Ready to Rock 'n Roll

In summary, one has to be prepared to improvise during a GSS meeting in the environment that we studied. Apart from electricity and climate problems, participants may show up late or not at all, and problem owners may decide on a different meeting agenda just before the start of the meeting. Clearly, some of the above issues are also relevant in Western GSS meetings; in that context, however, they seldom occur at the same time.

## Results

THE DATA WE COLLECTED WAS ANALYZED USING THE GROUNDED THEORY TECHNIQUES described above. Below, we introduce the theory that emerged from the data analysis and discuss each of the theory's factors in more detail. Our starting assumption for the data analysis was that, in order to enhance the capacity-building potential using GSS, GSS have to be accepted by the stakeholders that are regularly involved in capacity-building exercises.

Acceptance and use issues in MIS research are often studied using the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [41]. TAM describes how people's attitudes toward using information technology are influenced by their perception of the technology's usefulness and ease of use [8]. Specifically, the TAM model posits that the actual use of a technology can be predicted by the user's behavioral intention and his or her attitude toward use, which in turn are influenced by a technology's perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness (see figure 4).

TAM focuses on the use of IT over a longer period of time, for example, as in e-mail or word processors. Our study involved one-time meetings, lasting at most three days. In other words, the actual GSS use in all meetings was 100 percent. We could not investigate GSS acceptance and use over a longer period of time. In other words, it was not possible to shed light on how the actual use of GSS was influenced by the participants' behavioral intentions to use. However, from the analysis of our data, a number of concept categories emerged that appeared to represent what could be labeled "external factors." In the TAM, the external factors may be issues such as system features, training, documentation, user support consultants, situational constraints, and managerially controllable interventions impinging on behavior [8]. From our data, five factors emerged that appeared to influence the acceptance of the GSS technology by the African participants. The resulting model (theory) on the acceptance of GSS is depicted in figure 5. The five factors represent the "external factors" construct in TAM; "GSS acceptance" represents an abstraction of the other constructs in TAM. The data suggested that endorsement of the GSS technology by top management, computer literacy, and satisfaction with use would stimulate GSS acceptance. A preference for oral communication combined with referent power issues appeared to have negative impact on the acceptance of the technology.

## Endorsement by Top Management

It appeared that the extent to which the top management of the African organizations and institutions involved in the study endorsed the use of GSS had a significant impact on the acceptance of GSS by the groups as a whole. We believe the main reason for this is the strong sense of hierarchical relationships in the African cultures that we visited. Some of this hierarchical emphasis appears to come from local cultures, while some also appears to come from colonial experience in these African countries [24]. An interesting example is the custom in former British colonies that the chairman of a meeting does not come in until all other members are in, because no one is allowed to enter after the chairman is present. While this may be typical of some (former) British Commonwealth countries, it may not apply to all, or to African countries colonized by France or Portugal. A more common illustration of the strong sense of hierarchy is the fact that, if the chairman of a meeting proposes an idea, the other group members are very likely to agree with it. It is rare for someone to challenge the chairman's position or opinion. Therefore, sometimes ordinary meetings are just called by the chairman to get his own decisions accepted, or more precisely, to inform the group of his decision. The intention is not to get real feedback. Consequently, if the top management of an organization likes the GSS technology, his subordinates are likely to be more encouraged to use it. If, however, the top management does not buy into the technology, his subordinates are likely not to dare to touch it.

During the meetings that were organized, numerous top officials and top managers participated. The participants in both the Malawi and Zimbabwe meetings included, for example, government ministers, permanent secretaries, tribal chiefs, regional and district officers, and civic and religious leaders. The meetings in Tanzania included department heads, company directors and CEOs, project managers and coordinators, and deputy principal secretaries and commissioners from ministries. All but one of these “top” participants (including the chairmen of the meetings) openly endorsed using the technology. The exception was a Tanzanian chairman who decided to cancel his participation in the meeting at the last minute, leaving the other participants unsure what to do. Although the meeting went ahead, it was clear that the participants felt uncomfortable using the technology. It seemed they wanted to be polite by submitting a few global ideas into the system and asking a few technical questions, but the vice-chairman quickly concluded the meeting.

At the other meetings that proceeded as planned, hierarchical issues were clearly present. It was, for example, very notable that the oral discussions were carried out very carefully. Participants did not seem to address each other directly. They focused on the facilitator. If there was criticism, it was most often addressed to the facilitator. We believe that this was mainly to avoid facing the owner of the challenged idea. The electronic discussions, however, revealed a different picture. Reactions to ideas were very straightforward, very much to the point. Nevertheless, nobody seemed to feel personally insulted.

Our interaction with and observations of key participants and meeting owners suggested that they liked the GSS technology very much. One meeting owner in Tanzania stated that he was very happy with the potential of the system and would definitely consider using it again (which also happened at a later stage). Other chairmen conveyed similar sentiments. The only unsatisfied chairman also came from a Tanzanian meeting. We discovered that this was due to a discrepancy between his own meeting objective and that of the group. The chairman prepared a decision on a key issue and wanted a short, cursory electronic discussion to have it approved, which would be normal in a traditional meeting. However, the group seized the opportunity afforded by the electronic discussion: They felt the approach was wrong (they had not been involved earlier) and the decision outcome was wrong (their preferences were not accommodated). The ensuing electronic and verbal discussions helped the group and the chairman to reach consensus in this matter. Despite the chairman's dissatisfaction with the meeting, he planned to have more electronic meetings.

We propose two main reasons why the top managers and other high-level participants accepted the technology so well: First, it may be that the top managers were happy to get feedback on their ideas. Even if feedback was critical, it did not matter, because they did not have to take it personally. One chairman remarked that it was nice that one could be open toward every idea.

Second, it seemed that the top managers quickly recognized the potential danger of the technology to their way of running a meeting, but they also understood how to handle that danger. A few examples illustrate this. In one meeting, only the chairman of the meeting initiated the deletion of ideas; the other participants never made any suggestions in this respect. Another meeting demonstrated an interesting power play by the chairman. Just before the group was ready to vote on a list of strategic issues, the chairman suggested deleting two of them, because he “couldn’t see how these two ideas addressed the issue.” Apparently, he wanted to prevent the group from selecting them. Of course, nobody in the meeting was going to object at this point. A final example comes from a meeting in which each issue in a list was discussed verbally after being submitted electronically. When the group was ready to address one of the issues, a chief executive pointed out, “I wrote this . . . ,” and went on to elaborate. After that, there was no further discussion on the issue.

In conclusion, given the ease with which top managers and other high officials can determine a course of action in the organizations we visited, it can be expected that they would not want to surrender that power. This would be especially true if hidden agendas were in play and they wanted to push things through their way. Although GSS may promote more open exploration and decision making, allowing all participants to reflect on proposals and to carefully investigate issues from multiple perspectives, most top managers endorsed the GSS technology without noticeable hesitation and were eager to continue to use it for other issues. However, some managers quickly became quite sly in still being able to steer the course of the discussions and activities.

## Computer Literacy

It has been argued that familiarity with using computers will stimulate the successful acceptance of information technology $[8]$ . According to this argument, people who lack basic computer skills can be expected to have a hard time accepting an electronic meeting venue. The use of computers is still not widespread in the countries studied $[29, 44]$ . In particular, in groups with mixed computer literacy, people with poor typing skills may be at a disadvantage.

During the GSS meetings, we encountered different levels of computer literacy. For many Malawians, it was the first time they had even seen a computer, although some had previously used typewriters and had thus developed some keyboarding skills. The Tanzanian meetings involved both groups that were very proficient—namely, a computer department group—and groups that had never typed. The few groups with high levels of computer literacy were visibly very enthusiastic using the technology. Although most groups were familiar with computers or keyboards, the majority still had average typing skills; many were looking for keys. Very few people had mouse experience. Still, training exercises at the beginning of meetings never took more than twenty minutes. It appeared that participants with limited computer experience were not very enthusiastic in the beginning of the meetings; some even made a somewhat frustrated impression. Still, in most cases, they courageously hung on and got their ideas in. As a result, the meeting pace was a bit slower.

It was noticeable, however, that during the course of the meeting the less computer-literate participants seemed to grow more enthusiastic. In the end, the lack of typing and mouse skills did not seem to hinder them very much anymore. For example, a Tanzanian participant, who had very bad eyesight and could only type very slowly with one finger, said, “Nice that you can all enter your ideas this way at once without having to listen to each other and wait for your turn. Good to get the ideas on the table without interruptions. This is much faster!” Participants also seemed to concentrate on formulating their ideas much more to the point. In two Tanzanian meetings that involved the most computer-illiterate groups, the participants only used very short descriptions for their ideas, but this did not seem to harm the quality of the discussion or the participants’ satisfaction with the meeting. Furthermore, participants’ feedback suggested that the ability to communicate in parallel compensated for the lack of typing skills.

The second project in Malawi and the project in Zimbabwe used an alternative setup. The meeting environment consisted of a number of islands with one computer and some seats around it. Participants took their seats in a random manner. The rationale for this different arrangement was based on earlier experience in Malawi stakeholder consultations when each participant had a laptop, and therefore demanded more keyboarding skills. Furthermore, these tasks involved much larger groups and it was felt that, if each small group had time to discuss each issue before agreeing on what data to enter, it would help to improve the quality of data and also ensure greater consensus. This in fact proved to be supported by participants' feedback.

To conclude, various levels of computer literacy were encountered during the case studies. In general, participants were not very proficient using a computer. However, this did not seem to hinder the acceptance of the technology, despite initially low levels of enthusiasm. Giving the participants enough time to enter their ideas in combination with the alternative room setups and the ability to communicate in parallel seemed to counterbalance computer illiteracy effects on GSS acceptance.

## Referent Power

Referent power means that people do not hold a position in an organization based on their skills, but on their contacts. What powerful people are backing you becomes more important than your level of expertise. In some African settings, this is not uncommon and leads to erosion of the available capacity in a country, because it becomes more important to focus on establishing relations with institutional power brokers than with colleagues from one's own or other organizations to handle a problem $[55]$ . Hence, in these situations it seems that decisions are not always based on information, or even rational interests. More often, they are based on personal relations, on favors that have to be returned, or on fear of displeasing someone [23].

Although there are indications that this phenomenon is rather common $[55]$ , we did not observe many events that indicated referent power issues during the GSS meetings. In the electronic discussions, we observed that the level of criticism and open communication on various issues and submitted ideas did not suggest that participants were too careful or unwilling to share information. Even in meetings where government ministers were present, we did not witness any overt expressions of concern regarding the need to protect their power. There was one situation in a Tanzanian meeting where a referent power issue surfaced. The chairman of this group was not able to attend a meeting and appointed his assistant to assume that role. (This was not the meeting mentioned earlier where the chairman canceled his participation at the last minute.) Feedback suggested that the rest of the group did not completely accept the assistant because he was still very junior and only held a B.Sc. title whereas all others had an M.Sc. title. Only when the real chairman was there was the vice-chairman allowed to function. During the GSS meeting, the group electronically turned down and argued extensively about everything the assistant suggested (orally) in the meeting.

Still, based on some informal postmeeting interviews with participants and discussions with various other natives, we expect that GSS are not easily accepted in situations where issues of referent power are abundant. Interviewees suggested that people are mostly concerned about maintaining or improving their positions and the power that comes with it. They may be unwilling to share information in GSS meetings for fear of upsetting relations that have to be nurtured. The anonymity of the GSS communication prevents the identification of participants who “do favors.” Therefore, in view of the above, we can only make a tentative conclusion with respect to the negative effect of referent power on acceptance of GSS.

## Oral Communication Preference

In African cultures, oral interactions are very important. In meetings it often does not matter what you say as long as you say something. This means that, during discussions, it is important that everyone be able to have his or her turn. In this respect, it can be argued that GSS are more easily accepted in situations where written communication is a common form of interaction, such as use of office memoranda, written reports, or e-mail communication [40]. If people are more used to or have a strong preference for oral interaction, they are less likely to embrace GSS.

Because of the nature of the setup of the meetings in Malawi and Zimbabwe, there was sufficient opportunity for both oral and electronic communication. The Tanzanian meetings were varied with respect to the amount of oral communication: In some meetings, the technology was used about two-thirds of the time; in other meetings, as little as about one-third of the time. Participants spent most of the oral discussion on clarifying ideas, on cleaning up lists, and on voting results.

It was obvious in all meetings that most of the participants had a strong desire to discuss issues orally as well, especially after identifying the issues electronically. Facilitator suggestions to proceed with the next agenda topic were sometimes challenged by requesting more time for discussion. The oral discussions seldom brought new arguments to the table. For example, in one Tanzanian meeting, participants wanted to discuss two topics from the voting results on which the opinions seemed to diverge most. The arguments made electronically before the vote were exchanged again orally. In other words, often the participants did not communicate orally to gain additional information or insight, but just because they felt like it. Participants said they were impressed by the technology's capability to resolve conflicts by voting, but they also appeared to pursue oral consensus. Many participants suggested, however, that achieving consensus this way (i.e., first electronically, then orally) was much faster than when it has to be done completely orally.

Some participants remarked that the technology forces people to present their ideas in writing. For some people this may prove an extra threshold. They may find it easier to explain something while talking. Others may lack the skills to write clearly. However, other participants remarked that this effect was counterbalanced by the fact that some people are better at writing than at talking. Another participant found that the technology allowed more time to think: One can type something in, look at it, think about it a bit more, modify it, and then submit it. The same goes for other people's ideas: One can read them carefully, compose one's own thoughts, and then write them down. "It is a matter of taking control over your own behavior."

From our observations, we propose that the electronic communication was especially appreciated in meetings where there was disagreement on issues. Communicating electronically (anonymously) allowed the participants to get on with the discussion right away. Participants did not have to be overly careful as they were during oral discussions (see also the discussion on top management endorsement). They immediately presented their own position and explored the positions of their colleagues.

In conclusion, it appeared that oral communication was an important element of participants' comfort with the meeting setting and the technology. At the same time, electronic communication was appreciated and accepted easily, especially to explore consensus on issues and discuss issues on which disagreement existed. Finding the appropriate balance between oral and electronic communication therefore seems to be key.

## Satisfaction with Use

It can be argued that dissatisfied GSS meeting participants are less likely to accept the technology during the meeting or for future meetings [4]. If groups of stakeholders grow dissatisfied with GSS meetings, they may abandon the technology. If they grow dissatisfied with the processes provided by the GSS facilitator, they might abandon the facilitator's interventions.

Our data suggested that, on the whole, the participants were very satisfied with the meeting process, the technology, and the outcomes of the meetings. Their feedback suggested that this was because they felt they could now meaningfully function in the meetings. They identified the system's anonymity as the key asset because it gave them the ability to express their opinions on an idea without having to say anything up front. A number of observations and feedback from participants substantiate their satisfaction:

\- Over 97 percent of the participants in the Malawi and Zimbabwe meetings said they would recommend this technology to other groups. In these meetings, the usefulness of the technology for this type of consultation was rated on average 4.2 out of 5, where 5 is the most positive.

\- The majority of meetings in Tanzania ended with prolonged applause. On one occasion, the chairman made an enthusiastic and complimentary concluding speech.

\- One meeting in Tanzania lasted almost twice as long as planned. Although the meeting agenda had been completed in time, the group wanted to continue using the system to discuss other issues that emerged during the meeting.

\- Participants made comments such as “The technology has assisted us to identify issues we overlooked during previous meetings,” “Everyone’s ideas are taken up,” “Everyone is fully involved,” “You can all talk at the same time and understand each other,” “Freedom to think and express ideas without inhibition and fear of what others will say,” and “The ability to cross-reference with other participants’ views and ability to give views without bias.”

Not everyone was completely happy. We also collected more critical feedback, including statements such as, “It is too expensive for us,” “The setup was very expensive in terms of equipment,” “Doing it for the first time interfered with thought,” “Erosion of human element,” and “The success of a meeting seems to depend a lot on the facilitator. Though you do seem to have an increase in productivity, you have to have someone very skilled to keep the meeting on track.” The participants making these critical remarks thanked us for our efforts but did not mention anything with respect to followup activities.

One element with which the participants were especially satisfied was the perceived efficiency of the GSS meetings. Especially in meetings with more than ten participants, meeting owners and participants agreed that it would have taken several days to achieve the same results using traditional methods. Some observed that the quality of the results would probably not be as good; for example, after a Tanzanian meeting, a department director pointed out that the group achieved a major accomplishment. He had been involved in a lot of meetings with a similar objective. However, the time the group used was really small comparatively. He estimated that, to get the same results, the group would have needed at least four hours instead of the one hour used. A bystander added that it would even be questionable if the group would have reached the same type of consensus after four hours.

## Discussion

IN THE FOLLOWING ELABORATION OF OUR FINDINGS, we first describe the contribution of the GSS meetings to capacity-building issues. We then compare and contrast our findings with other GSS field studies for each of the external acceptance factors, except for “referent power,” since no GSS field study we know of discussed this issue. Because of the scarcity of cross-cultural GSS field studies, our discussion predominantly concerns Euro-American experiences.

## Capacity Building

From a substantive standpoint, the meetings we observed were a success. The participants' feedback and our own observations suggest that useful results were achieved that carried the endorsement of the participants. In the Malawi and Zimbabwe meetings, results were achieved in terms of a number of action plans that had been implemented at the time of writing up this study. The meetings in Tanzania also resulted in concrete plans, decisions, and options for further investigations. Still, it is too early to tell whether these results have yet been implemented.

The findings suggest a high level of participant acceptance with respect to the use of the GSS technology. This was especially the case during the meetings. There are also indications that acceptance may go beyond these first meetings. First, there was a lot of interest in future meetings. A number of groups requested more meetings soon after they became acquainted with the technology. Other groups were planning to use

GSS in a more long-term fashion to support the strategy formulation and restructuring of their organization. Second, there was interest in acquiring the technology on a permanent basis. Individual participants expressed a desire to implement the technology and methods in their organization. The extent to which these plans materialize, however, remains to be seen. The level of acceptance during and immediately after the observed meetings is no guarantee for the future use or adoption of the GSS technology.

The positive indications with respect to the acceptance of the GSS technology for the observed meetings do not automatically imply that capacity building was achieved. Hence, the question remains: To what extent did the GSS meeting support capacity building? As explained earlier, capacity-building efforts should be judged by their success in:

1. Training and educating people in order to provide them with the necessary skills;

2. Supporting relevant institutions to become worthy players in the development activities;

3. Developing methods and tools that support participative development efforts.

The various GSS meetings that were included in the study illustrated how capacity building and mobilization can be supported. It is difficult to determine exactly what the effect of these meetings was on capacity building because the impact of GSS on capacity building should be visible in the effect of capacity building itself—namely, in better educated people, more prominent roles for institutions in development activities, and effective development methods and tools. However, the effects of capacity-building efforts can only be seen in the long run since it is a slow, continuous process and the effects have to measured nationwide $[55]$ .

Nevertheless, several participants commented that the GSS meetings were capacity building in themselves, because of the process of consultation used and the empowering meeting design. Having gone through this type of exercise, they felt empowered to take more responsibility for their own country's or organization's development, rather than relying on external parties. Taking a closer look at the meeting from the perspective of each capacity building aspect separately, we first see that the meetings did train people in a number of necessary skills. The GSS meetings were used to train African colleagues to operate the GSS both as a technographer and as a facilitator. Basic group facilitation skills were also taught; these can be considered necessary in view of the need to address development problems participatively. Moreover, some participants also considered the fact that they learned how to operate a computer as an important improvement in their skills. Computer training is considered a key capacity to promote development [25]. Second, especially in the Malawi and Zimbabwe meetings, a large number of institutions were enabled to be meaningfully involved in participative development activities. The results of these meetings consisted of strategy formulations and action plans to which all participants had actively contributed. Third, we argue that the GSS meetings themselves were "proof by demonstration" that participative methods and tools can help address development issues in African countries. Through the GSS meetings, a variety of participative methods, such as brainstorming and focus groups, were introduced and successfully applied.

A recent World Bank report on capacity building in Africa calls for attention in two key areas: participative approaches by the people themselves and improving the decision-making environment of leaders and managers in key public sectors and civic sector institutions $[55]$ . These two areas present a paradox: One cannot automatically assume that leaders and managers will happily embrace participative approaches. Strong hierarchical relationships and referent power issues in many African cultures stand in the way of participation. Participation may be perceived as threatening, either because of the colonial legacy or because of traditional values. The employment of GSS as presented in this paper may provide a means to overcome this paradox. Therefore, in summary, we argue that the added value of applying GSS in the areas we visited extends along two timelines:

\- Immediate benefits: From a short-term perspective, a number of current problem areas and issues were addressed during the meetings that we observed.

\- Embedded benefits: From a long-term perspective, a number of sample approaches to address the issues in key problem areas may be institutionalized and may serve as examples (process blueprints) for other areas.

## GSS Acceptance

## Management Endorsement

In other field studies, management endorsement appears to be a key factor with respect to the acceptance of GSS. During the IBM studies, participants and meeting owners mentioned that getting the right management representatives in the GSS meeting room would have helped speed up the acceptance/adoption decision $[33, 46]$ . It was important that high levels of management championed and endorsed the use of GSS $[16]$ .

It is interesting to see that, in Euro-American situations, management endorsement is very much related to cost–benefit issues. In the IBM case, the management decision to extend GSS availability was predominantly based on time and cost savings. As the researchers note, there was a strong focus on the effective use of participants' time, and reduction in project time $[16]$ . Alavi found a similar focus in her three-month study of a Fortune 500 company that just acquired a GSS: The meeting owners most frequently mentioned speed and efficiency as the main benefits of GSS $[1]$ . With respect to the higher management levels, the same considerations were observed during a study at Nationale-Nederlanden, an insurance company in the Netherlands $[50]$ . Nevertheless, in this company, lower management levels appeared to be more interested in meeting effectiveness issues such as outcome quality than in cost reductions.

Contrary to these Euro-American experiences, the cost–benefit issue was not the primary concern of the African meeting owners with whom we worked. They seemed to be especially charmed by the depth and richness of the electronic discussions and the ability to seek consensus participatively on a number of issues without entering face-to-face discussions where one had to be overly diplomatic and respectful.

## Computer Literacy

Field studies report various experiences with respect to computer literacy. For example, during the IBM studies, results with respect to perceived effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction appeared to be independent of typing and computer skills $[33, 46]$ . The study at Nationale-Nederlanden found that participants with limited typing skills felt that they had a serious disadvantage $[50]$ . Alavi observed that all participants were able to interact easily with the GSS, despite differences in computer literacy and typing skills $[1]$ . In her study, the GSS's simple user interface and ease of use appeared to compensate for low levels of participant computer literacy and for typing skills. This last study appears to be most consistent with our experiences; we found short technology learning curves and parallel communication compensating for lack of typing skills.

Another important computer literacy issue was the alternative room setup in some African meetings where several participants shared a single workstation. In the Euro-American settings that we are familiar with, the sharing of workstations is rare. Most meeting owners would rather split the group and run a number of GSS meetings with different subgroups. During the IBM studies, the desire to work with larger groups resulted in a request for more workstations $[33]$ . Apparently, personal anonymity and equal participation opportunity are more important.

## Oral Communication

Field experiences have consistently indicated that oral discussion is desirable between electronic meeting activities to discuss intermediate brainstorming results and voting outcomes $[34, 37, 48, 50]$ . Alavi found that GSS do not replace but may augment verbal oral communications in face-to-face settings $[1]$ . In Mexico, Morales et al. $[30]$ found that participants who perceived themselves as very knowledgeable in the area of discussion might even favor face-to-face communication. Moreover, insufficient opportunities to discuss ideas and voting results orally may cause participants to perceive a GSS meeting as a bad meeting $[49]$ . Our experiences in Africa are consistent with these findings.

However, one can notice differences with respect to the nature of the oral communication that goes on between electronic activities. In the Euro-American settings with which we are familiar, oral discussions are used to clarify generated ideas or to challenge them. Voting results are often used as a trigger to debate certain issues in more detail. Alavi [1], for example, found that GSS even seem to invoke more oral discussion than in traditional meetings. In Mexico, participants appeared to be more expressive in terms of commenting on the session content and the opinions of others during oral communication than American participants [30]. However, our findings indicate that the nature of the African oral communication was very different. During oral discussions, African participants mostly repeated what was already said electronically. Also, the facilitator was often used as an intermediary to address the oral communication to instead of the group.

## Satisfaction

With a few exceptions [49], most field studies report high levels of participant satisfaction with meeting process and outcomes [1, 33, 48, 50]. The levels of participant satisfaction we observed in the African meetings are consistent with these findings.

If we look more closely at the questionnaire data obtained after the Tanzanian GSS meetings, the satisfaction levels in the Tanzanian meetings appear to be even higher than in a number of meetings in the United States and the Netherlands (see Table 7). ## Conclusion

THE STUDY DESCRIBED HERE REPRESENTS, AS FAR AS WE KNOW, the first in-depth analysis of the use of GSS in Africa and one of the few non Euro-American field studies. Its aim was to give a detailed account of the application of GSS in a number of real African groups and to shed light on some factors that appear to have an impact on the acceptance of a GSS technology that was developed based on a different set of cultural assumptions than those of the participants. As a result, we extended the TAM in terms of a specification of a number of external factors that are believed to influence the accepted and eventual adoption of a technology.

The limitations of this study are threefold. First, our study only involved English-speaking, South/East-African countries. It is known that there are many cultural and social differences across Africa. In fact, it is impossible to speak of one “African culture.” A large number of African countries encompass heterogeneous populations in which no cultural group is close to 50 percent [26]. Hence, whether our results are also applicable in other English-speaking (e.g., West African) countries or in countries with a different cultural or colonial heritage remains to be investigated.

Second, two different versions of the same GSS technology were used, GroupSystems for DOS and GroupSystems for Windows. One may argue that this harms the comparability of the insights across the meetings. On the other hand, one may also argue that, by using only one system, the results might have been system-specific. By getting similar results from different GSS, we can be more confident that our results may generalize across GSS. Moreover, the functionality of the two systems is basically the same. The main differences are in the user interface. During the meetings, participants made very few comments about operating the GSS technology itself other than that it was very easy to use. Most technical questions were about computer use in general. Even so, this was not because the participants were not able to operate the system easily; they just seemed eager to learn more about computer technology. Hence, we feel that the GSS itself, in terms of user interface, did not seem to matter much.

Finally, it appeared that the African participants we encountered were very polite to the extent that they were sometimes reluctant to show dissatisfaction. This may have implications for the interpretations of our data, for example, with respect to the satisfaction questionnaire results (Table 7). Since we were aware of this phenomenon, we tried to critically compare and counterbalance the feedback from private interviews with our own observations. Also, if participants appeared to have a more critical attitude toward the technology, we made a point of including them in the group of participants with whom we conducted postmeeting interviews.

As we have argued, research into the cross-cultural application of GSS is just beginning. Few descriptive field studies have been performed. Especially given the increasing global nature of organizational work, there clearly is a need for more research efforts in this area covering all five activities depicted in figure 1. In particular, our results suggest a number of directions for further research. First, longitudinal efforts have to be made to record what will actually happen with the results from the meetings. The implications of the meetings in the long run will provide us with a better insight into the effects of employing GSS technologies. In addition, acceptance is believed to precede adoption and diffusion processes [38]. Hence, one way to study the acceptance of the GSS technology in the long run is to investigate whether it is bought and used within organizations. Longitudinal efforts are also necessary to get real insight into the complex issue of referent power. We feel that, to observe this phenomenon more clearly, one must become acquainted with the history of an organization in terms of job appointments, interpersonal relationships, and other considerations.

Second, with the insight we have gained into some of the external factors that appear to have an impact on the acceptance of GSS, it seems worthwhile to study these factors in relation to the cultural background of the research setting. Hofstede's cultural dimensions may be one way to explain certain behavior by the groups and individuals studied [19, 20]. For example, what effect does the amount of power distance and uncertainty avoidance have on the endorsement of GSS by managers? To what extent do the various cultural dimensions explain why participants will be less or more satisfied with using the technology? Looking into these cultural aspects may help us better understand the mechanisms behind GSS acceptance and compare our findings with those in other cultures. In the end, this may lead to the development of theories to improve the application of GSS technology across various cultures.

Finally, if quantitative instruments such as surveys are going to become a more prominent data-collection instrument in cross-cultural GSS field research, we have to investigate carefully how to measure attitudes and perceptions in different cultures. The formulation of questions seems to be an important issue, especially if this is done by researchers with a different cultural background. Issues such as the extent to which respondents will take a critical attitude and the use of certain words are very relevant and determine the usability of collected data.
