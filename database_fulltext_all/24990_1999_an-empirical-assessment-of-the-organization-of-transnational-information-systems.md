# An Empirical Assessment of the Organization of Transnational Information Systems

WILLIAM R. KING AND VIKRAM SETHI

WILLIAM R. KING is University Professor in the Katz Graduate School of Business of the University of Pittsburgh. He has served as the Founding President of the Association for Information Systems, as President of the Institute of Management Sciences (now INFORMS), and as Editor-in-Chief of MIS Quarterly. He has published, lectured, and consulted widely in the field of MIS.

VIKRAM SETHI is an Associate Professor in the information systems area at Southwest Missouri State University in Springfield. His areas of interest include the training and development of information systems personnel, socialization practices, and the role adjustment of information systems employees. His articles have appeared in journals such as Journal of Management Information Systems, Omega, Journal of Information Technology, and Journal of High Technology Management Research.

ABSTRACT: The objective of this study is to aid our understanding of the organization of information systems in organizations whose activities cross national boundaries. The increasing globalization of business has led firms to seek new, and more appropriate, organizational structures, processes, and cultures. This has required the establishment of appropriate information technology platforms to coordinate business processes and to provide coalition mechanisms.

This study is based on five important dimensions of transnational strategy—the configuration of value chain activities, the coordination of value chain activities, centralization, strategic alliances, and market integration—that define a comprehensive taxonomy of transnational strategy. A basic proposition is that a firm's transnational strategy will be reflected in the design of its information systems. To address this proposition, a two-stage questionnaire study was conducted. Respondents included 150 multinational corporations from 20 countries and 25 industries. The five dimensions of transnational strategy were operationalized, and the analysis established the validity of a taxonomy that is better suited to the study of systems-related issues in MNCs. This taxonomy was used to test hypotheses related to the organization of transnational systems. These hypotheses were stated in terms of IS organizational, strategic, architectural, and personnel dimensions.

The results of the study support the proposition that the organizational characteristics of centralization, dispersal, and coordination are differentially reflected in the IT configurations of various kinds of MNCs. In a centrally coordinated business structure, IT is also globally centralized. In addition, local autonomy was shown to affect the deployment of IT in global firms.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: global information systems, multinational corporations, strategic information systems.

TRANSNATIONAL BUSINESS ACTIVITY IS FUELED BY THE FUNDAMENTAL economics of comparative advantage and by the growing realization that the strategic use of information can enable a firm to improve how it meets the needs of global products and customers. Operating transnationally brings the benefits of access to new markets and resources and the opportunity to utilize and develop knowledge wherever it may be located.

Information technology (IT) is fundamental to effective global operations in two primary ways. First, it makes globalization possible by providing a coordination mechanism for geographically dispersed activities. Second, it facilitates the reshaping of separate organizations into global cooperatives by providing a mechanism for enabling coalitions to be established.

This study focuses on the strategies for organizing the information technology (IT) and information systems (IS) activities of transnational enterprises. The term “transnational information systems (TNS)” is used to describe these systems and technologies. The focal question addressed is “How do various multinational corporations (MNCs) differentially organize their TNS operations?” Two basic assumptions are implicit in this inquiry: first, that MNCs have intrinsic differences that may be used to categorize them into meaningful groups, and, second, that differences among MNCs will be reflected in different TNS organizational strategies.

Thus, this study has two primary objectives:

1. To formulate and empirically validate a taxonomy of MNCs that will be useful in studying how various firms coordinate their activities and facilitate cooperation among organizational entities; and

2. To test hypotheses concerning the IS organizational strategies that are employed by the various kinds of MNCs.

## A Taxonomy of Multinational Corporations

RECENTLY, THERE HAS BEEN AN INCREASE IN RESEARCH on multinational firms. Firms have been described as “multinational” based on their operations, ownership, managerial attitudes, and social responsibility, among other criteria. A multinational firm may usefully be thought of as [46]:

1. A network of subsidiaries with an organizational structure to enable high coordination;

2. A global arrangement of activities with world-scale volume and the flexibility of arbitrage;

3. A firm with product diversity and a worldwide distribution system to enable cross-subsidization among products and markets.

Various measures have been applied to assessing the “degree of globality” of various firms [46]. Many of these definitions and measures are resource based [8]. However, they have led to many different formulations of differences in global strategies among firms [57, 58, 59].

The common thread in these various approaches is that the focus of management in MNCs is on developing the appropriate balance between global integration and local responsiveness—effectively meeting local demands while capitalizing on worldwide efficiencies and advantages.

The international strategy literature has long hypothesized a relationship between international strategy and implementation requirements. Several studies $[11, 12, 16, 17, 18, 56]$ have found that, as the strategy of an MNC changes, it is important to realign its organizational design in order to implement the new strategy successfully $[17]$ . However, the organizational design dimensions of these studies have focused on macroorganizational structures, such as worldwide product divisions, international divisions, and matrix structures.

Currently, there is an emerging line of thought and a general consensus that MNCs have additional design dimensions such as management systems, communication processes, and management philosophies $[2, 13, 20]$ . Since IT has only recently been recognized as a critical factor in maintaining the global–local balance $[60, 62]$ , existing MNC taxonomies are not well suited to the study of IT as it is employed in MNCs.

This study, therefore, begins with the development of a new taxonomy of MNCs—one that extends prior taxonomies to encompass the current issues related to coordination, formation, and maintenance of cooperative alliances, and other communication-intensive functions. We describe, in the next section, the different dimensions of globalization that are pertinent to this taxonomy. These dimensions—value chain configuration and coordination, strategic alliances, centralization, and market integration—are then shown to describe an MNC classification scheme.

## Dimensions of Globalization

Our assessment of the international strategy literature leads us to conclude that five dimensions will be most useful in differentiating multinational corporations in a manner that will facilitate the systems-related goals of this study: (1) value-chain configuration, (2) value-chain coordination, (3) strategic alliances, (4) centralization, and (5) market integration. The validity and utility of a taxonomy based on these five dimensions are empirically tested in this study.

## Value-Chain Configuration and Coordination

A firm's value-chain configuration refers to the geographic dispersal of its value-chain components. A firm's value-chain coordination refers to the manner in which these dispersed components are linked. These dimensions are based on Kogut [28], who focused on two questions:

1. Where should the value-added chain be broken across borders?

2. On which functional activities should a firm concentrate its resources?

The basis of his analysis is that value-chain components should be organized for maximum global advantage, and that this value-chain organization is a critical descriptor of a firm's global strategy [33, 42]. A company that is low in configuration has a high concentration of activities in one or a few countries. Alternatively, an organization may have a high configuration level (i.e., have dispersed activities), in which case an entire value chain is replicated in each country of business.

## Strategic Alliances

The strategic alliances dimension refers to the formal interdependencies that exist among global organizations. Strategic alliances are interorganizational relationships in which the partners make substantial investments to develop a long-term collaborative effort. The process of alliance formation is encouraged by increasing competition, accelerating technological change, the need for information, and the realization that alliances enable opportunities to be collectively pursued that might otherwise be unavailable to the participating organizations $[22, 31, 38, 43]$ .

## Centralization

Centralization refers to the central control of organizational resources by the corporate office [35]. According to Bartlett and Ghoshal [3], the two extremes along this dimension are the centralized hub, where decisions are concentrated, and the decentralized federation model, in which strategic decision making is distributed [21, 23, 40].

There is clearly no one best level of centralization since each MNC is subject to simultaneous and conflicting pressures. Foreign product diversity, outside ownership, growth through acquisitions, and the size of foreign operations have been identified as factors that can overload the decision-making capacity of the transnational hierarchy and pressure organizations to decentralize $[17]$ . At the same time, the dependency of the subsidiaries on the parent organization and competitive environmental change increases the need for central control of decision making.

## Market Integration

Market integration refers to the extent to which the parent corporation views the international market as a single competitive arena [39]. The importance of this characteristic is emphasized by Bartlett and Ghoshal [4], who note that a company cannot manage globally if its managers identify with local, parochial interests and objectives.

## An MNC Classification Scheme

Since each of the above five dimensions (value-chain configuration, value-chain coordination, strategic alliances, centralization, and market integration) has clear information requirements and IT implications, they can be used to define a taxonomy that may be useful in studying how MNCs differentially manage their information systems. Figure 1 shows the taxonomy used in this study. The four categories of MNCs described in the figure are not radically new in concept. However, defining the four varieties of firms in terms of the five dimensions represents a new way of viewing them that is particularly appropriate for studying IS issues.

The four varieties of MNCs are labeled as export-oriented, parent-child, global, and portfolio firms.

## Export-Oriented Firms

This category is similar to Leontiades's [30] export organization. Here, a corporation serves the international market through export departments organized along functional lines. The emphasis is on marketing and distribution effectiveness and on the success of channel members, such as trading companies, distributors, and marketing agencies. This category ranks low on value-chain configuration, value-chain coordination, and level of strategic alliances. Market integration is also low because the scope of markets is concentrated, focusing on specific segments only. Centralization is high because the parent corporation controls all resources and decision making.

## The Parent-Child Configuration

Firms in the parent-child configuration create structural units in the form of marketing and/or manufacturing subsidiaries in various countries. Each subsidiary interacts with the parent corporation through a flow of capital, providing remittances to the corporation and receiving developmental funds from it. This category ranks low on centralization and market integration because the functional areas of the subsidiaries, especially finance, marketing, production, and logistics, operate independently of each other as well as of the parent corporation. Strategic alliances and value-chain coordination are also low because each subsidiary is responsive only to local needs and maintains a simple organizational structure. However, since multiple units operate independently, these organizations rank high on value-chain configuration.

## Portfolio Management

Firms in the portfolio category approach the international market by focusing on a specific region, especially for production facilities, even though marketing and distribution activities are conducted globally. Some Japanese firms, for example, have transferred manufacturing bases to Pacific Rim countries such as Thailand and Singapore. The rise of regional trading blocks such as the EC and NAFTA have encouraged this process. Morrison, Ricks, and Roth [34] note several examples of portfolio firms such as Thomson Consumer Electronics, Inc., and Warner Lambert.

Portfolio firms share some characteristics with each of the other types of firms. Like global firms, their value-chain activities are concentrated and demonstrate high coordination and centralization. As Rugman and Verbeke [50] note, location-bound host-country advantages are achieved by portfolio firms as a result of governmental incentives, but this does not preclude the exploitation of firm-specific advantages. These organizations therefore rank low on configuration but high on coordination, centralization, and market integration. Strategic alliances are not the norm for these firms although such relationships may develop in their host countries during formation. Therefore, figure 1 ranks the level of strategic alliances in portfolio firms as medium.

## Global Firms

A firm belonging to the global category is one whose competitive position in one country is significantly affected by its position in other countries $[41]$ . The global firm targets the international market without distinguishing national or political boundaries. Thus, it ranks high on market integration. Its structural considerations also require high value-chain configuration and value-chain coordination. Centralization is an integral part of this configuration because all foreign operations are controlled by the parent corporation. However, in global companies, decision making is fairly decentralized $[60]$ . Strategic alliances are high because the corporation and its subsidiaries aim to achieve long-term profitability through entrenchment in host countries.

## Validating the MNC Taxonomy

If the MNC taxonomy of figure 1 is valid, it may be used further in the testing of IT-related hypotheses. Therefore, the first objective of this study is the testing of its validity. Data for the overall study (including the taxonomy validation and hypotheses test phases) were obtained using two surveys—one sent to the corporate CEO and the other to the executive responsible for IS planning and operations.

The sample was selected from companies listed in the following sources:

1. International Directory of Corporate Affiliations [36],

2. The World Directory of Multinational Enterprises [55], and

3. Principal International Businesses [15].

The principal criterion used for inclusion in the sample was the presence of at least one international subsidiary unit. Of the 3,600 companies selected, 1,539 were U.S.-based MNCs and 2,061 were non-American, representing a total of 43 countries and 28 industries.

The first questionnaire was intended:

1. To validate the IT-related issues to be studied in detail in the second questionnaire;

2. To request participation in the second phase of the project; and

3. To gather preliminary data concerning the participating organizations.

The first questionnaire was pretested using fifteen doctoral students representing five different countries of origin. It was then pilot-tested by mailing it to the CEOs of 300 companies that were selected at random from the full sample of 3,600. Of the

## Nonresponse Bias Analysis

A nonresponse bias test was conducted by comparing the characteristics of firms that responded to the final questionnaire (150) with those who did not (131 firms). This test showed that firms that did not respond had lower sales and fewer subsidiaries than the firms that did respond. There were no statistical differences in the number of employees and the number of countries between the two categories of firms. A second comparison was conducted between the 150 firms that responded and a random sample of 150 firms selected from the nonrespondents in the original sample of 3,600 firms. Again, the nonrespondents were firms with less multinational activity, as measured by the number of subsidiaries and the number of countries in which business was conducted. This implies that firms with smaller international and IT operations are not well represented in this study.

## Operationalization of MNC Dimensions

Five MNC dimensions were used for the purpose of validating the taxonomy in figure 1—value-chain coordination and configuration, strategic alliances, centralization, and market integration. The development of measures for these dimensions is described below.

## Value-Chain Configuration

This dimension is based on Porter [42] and has been operationalized by Roth and Morrison [48] and Roth, Schweiger, and Morrison [49]. These studies selected fourteen functional activities and, for each functional activity, asked respondents to indicate whether the activity was performed in a single country or in multiple countries for their business unit. In this study, the choices for number of countries were given as ranges: 1, 2–9, 10–25, 26–50, 51–100, and 100 or more. A configuration index was then calculated for each organization by averaging the responses across all value activities. Thus, a score of 1 would indicate that all activities were performed in one location and a score of 100 would indicate that all activities were performed in more than 100 countries. An overall index (CONFIGURATION) was derived from the above fourteen items (Cronbach's alpha = 0.95).

## Value-Chain Coordination

The value-chain activities used to measure configuration were also used to operationalize coordination. For each of the fourteen activities, executives were asked to indicate, on a five-point scale, the extent to which the activity was coordinated across countries. A coordination index was then calculated by summing each response. Thus, a score of 1 would indicate that the activities of the business were not coordinated and a score of 5 would indicate high coordination. An overall index (COORDINATION) was created by totaling responses across the fourteen coordination items (Cronbach's alpha = 0.86).

## Strategic Alliances

No instrument was available to measure the strength of strategic alliances. A measure was created based on Spekman and Sawhney [54], who specify the following dimensions of this construct: goal compatibility, strategic advantage, interdependence, commitment, communication, conflict resolution, coordination of work, and planning. Nine items were used to evaluate these characteristics. A factor analysis revealed that eight of the nine items loaded on one factor and accounted for 71 percent of the variance. Cronbach's alpha for these eight items was 0.94. These eight items were grouped into a strategic alliance scale (ALLIANCE).

## Centralization

Centralization was operationalized based on Egelhoff[17], who examined twenty-two decisions that have to be made as a part of the management and operation of most foreign subsidiaries. He measured the degree of centralization in a parent-subsidiary relationship by asking the respondents which hierarchical level in the organization would actually have to approve a decision.

Egelhoff's set of twenty-two decisions was used in this study with modifications to reflect decisions regarding both products and services, and five new decisions added based on a literature review. The twenty-seven items were factor-analyzed but did not reveal any clear pattern. Each was then correlated with the centralization response from the first questionnaire, and only those items that correlated with this response were retained. In the first questionnaire, respondents had been asked to rank on a scale of 1 to 5 the degree of centralization of decision making in their organization. Five items for which the correlations were not significant were dropped. An overall centralization scale was created (CENTRALIZATION) by averaging the remaining twenty-two decisions (Cronbach's alpha = 0.89).

## Market Integration

Market integration refers to the extent to which the parent company views the international market as a single competitive arena. No instrument was available for measuring this construct. A measure was created using four items based on Perlmutter [39]. One item was dropped after factor analysis and an overall index (INTEGRA-TION) was created using the remaining three items (Cronbach's alpha = 0.85).

With these five dimensions, cluster analysis was performed to classify the MNCs. The clustering strategy was based on Punj and Stewart [44], who suggest three steps for cluster analysis: (1) preliminary cluster solution, (2) selection of candidate number of clusters and clustering algorithm, and (3) final cluster solution.

The procedure resulted in four clusters whose frequencies are shown in Table 2. The table also shows the means of the clustering variables across clusters.

Examining other characteristic variables for each group provides a clearer picture of the meaning of the clusters. ## Results of the Validity Test

The clear correspondence between the results of the cluster analysis serves to validate the taxonomy of figure 1.

Several further steps were taken in order to assess the face validity of this empirical classification. First, an analysis of the annual reports of the firms who responded to the first questionnaire indicated that the four clusters had distinct characteristics.

It was found that export-oriented firms were smaller, conducted business in fewer countries, and their value chain was distributed in fewer countries. Portfolio firms also showed some of these characteristics—specifically, value-chain diversification—but coordinated their efforts to a much greater extent with their subsidiaries. These firms typically concentrated primary activities in a few countries but had highly dispersed marketing efforts. In a sense, they are regionally oriented firms. Parent-child configuration firms are large, diverse, and decentralized in nature, providing autonomy to their individual subsidiaries. The word “conglomerate” best describes this group of companies. Global firms, on the other hand, are as diverse as parent-child firms, but demonstrate greater coordination and, more important, exhibit extremely strong strategic alliances with other organizations in host countries.

These assessments further validate that the taxonomy of figure 1 provides a sound basis for testing TNS-related hypotheses.

## TNS-Related Hypotheses

THE TNS ISSUES STUDIED HERE ARE BASED ON STUDIES by Karimi and Konsynski [25], Jarvenpaa and Ives [24], Bradley, Hausman, and Nolan [6], and King and Sethi [26]. These studies have classified TNS issues as organizational, strategic, architectural, and personnel (described below). The hypotheses in this study follow these conceptualizations in that variations in the characteristics of these issues are treated as resulting from the organizational form that the MNC has adopted as reflected by its choices of the five MNC dimensions—that is, its category in the MNC taxonomy of figure 1.

Since the organizational environments for the four types of MNCs represent different levels of (1) diversification, (2) environmental uncertainty, (3) environmental instability, (4) limitations of organizational memory, and (5) need for information integration [9], two distinct research streams may be used to justify a number of the hypotheses relating MNC type to IS factors.

The information-processing research stream regards the organization as a collection of systems that gather, transform, store, and communicate information and seek to utilize this capacity to reduce environmental uncertainty $[19]$ . Effective organizations are those that match their information-processing capacities with the amount of uncertainty they face. Studies such as those by Burns and Stalker $[7]$ and Kmetz $[27]$ have used information processing as the central concept for linking an organizational design to contextual factors and strategic conditions. In the specific context of the MNC, Egelhoff $[17]$ used information processing as the key element to link different MNC structures (functional divisions, product divisions, international divisions, and global regions) with elements of strategy (foreign product diversity, product modification, product changes, size of foreign operations, size of foreign manufacturing, number of subsidiaries, and the extent of acquisitions).

Information processing has also been used to link a firm's global business orientation with specific elements of its IS design [24]. According to Jarvenpaa and Ives [24], decentralized firms require minimal information processing across subsidiaries. A decentralized firm will therefore pursue independent IT operations in each country. A global or centralized firm, on the other hand, must orient its IT configuration in a centralized manner.

The strategic management research stream has also discussed the manner in which MNCs structure business functions. In this literature, the presence of multiple autonomous units presents opportunities for profitable arbitrage through competitive moves, the influencing of governmental policy, and the movement of real factor and product prices. Worldwide dispersion allows a firm to shift production to more favorable locations or to minimize taxes through various mechanisms $[29]$ . The key strength of the multinational, therefore, is its ability to respond to country variance, which depends on the coordination of interfirm activities across subsidiaries. Since it is likely that firms differ in their abilities to respond to environmental events, the establishment of effective international managerial control and information mechanisms can provide a key source of competitive advantage for a firm.

A multinational firm may choose to allow a subsidiary to pursue autonomous growth and to advance local learning. Complete global flexibility, however, will require a mix of global and local perspectives. In this environment, an open exchange of information is necessary for one organizational unit to learn about and benefit from others. These information flows permit the MNC to develop the critical capabilities outlined by Doz, Prahalad, and Hamel [14]:

\- Efficiency in executing agreed-upon strategies through control of subsidiary actions;

\- Ability to adjust headquarters-subsidiary and subsidiary-subsidiary relationships to permit changes in strategic direction to take place; and

\- Flexibility to bring subsidiaries together to compete in a coordinated fashion.

Twelve hypotheses reasonably emanate from these arguments. These are discussed in terms of the categories of organizational issues, strategic issues, architectural issues, and personnel issues.

## Organizational Issues Hypotheses

These issues relate to the design of the subsidiary IS organizational structure, control systems, and reporting procedures, as well as the subsidiary's participation in the functions of the corporation as a whole. According to Alavi and Young [1], these issues concern the locus of information technology management (that is, the relative power exercised by information technology management in the headquarters and subsidiary organizations). As shown in figure 1, the center of control may be at the local or subsidiary level or at the HQ level or may be shared, depending on the structure and strategy of the MNC. As a result, the IT planning process and the roles of subsidiary IS groups vary across MNCs. Thompson et al. [60], Reck [47], and Alavi and Young [1] present a contingency framework where the degree, nature, and contribution of different IS groups to planning activities are enhanced as the firm becomes increasingly dependent on the activities of its subsidiary units. These issues form the first four hypotheses in this study (H1 through H4):

H1. Global firms exhibit greater levels of planning activity than do other types of MNCs.

H2. The TNS plan of global firms incorporates a broader set of planning areas than do plans of other MNC types.

H3. The role of the corporate IS group in formulating the TNS plan is greater in global firms than in other MNC types.

H4. The contribution of subsidiary IS groups to IS planning is greater in global firms than in the other MNC types.

## Strategic Issues Hypotheses

Strategic issues relate to the use of competitive advantage in MNCs. While several studies have examined how this occurs within a single national market, few have looked into leveraging global competitive or comparative advantages using IT. Commenting on the nature of IT's impact on global strategy, Senn [53] notes that, to support global competitive success, six issues must be addressed—the ability to interconnect across borders, the ability to reach across markets, the synchronization of policy and technology, the suitability of the technology platform, application locations, and common systems. In addition, environmental scanning has been shown to be a critical IT function [32].

Strategic issues, thus, relate to IS-business linkages and the balance between internal and external applications and information scanning. The linkage of the IS plan with the business plan and the role of IT in providing the firm with competitive advantage are the strategic areas of interest. Within the multinational context, Selig [52] provides a list of mechanisms for the integration of the business and IS plans, including joint planning and review sessions, integrating plan contents and the planning cycle, the use of executive and operational steering committees, and standards and guidelines. In addition, the participation of IS executives in business planning is an important integrative device [45]. Thus:

H5. There are more mechanisms linking the IS plan with the business plan in global firms than in the other MNC types.

H6. The IS orientation in all firms will be more toward internal applications than toward customer-oriented applications. A shift toward external applications will be detected in global firms.

H7. Environmental scanning will be more prominent in global firms than in other MNC types.

In addition, because pretesting and pilot testing indicated that some of the above issues might be different at the corporate and subsidiary levels, separate items were included to refer to the two different levels for H5 and H7.

## Architectural Issues Hypotheses

Architecture has been defined as a “framework for orchestrating effective integration of a set of systems applications, data resources, systems environments, and communications facilities to achieve the business objectives of the corporation” [63]. The purpose of an information architecture is to relate the information needs of an organization to applications, to share data, and to facilitate its integration within the organization.

The development of a suitable global architecture has been noted as a key IT management issue $[53]$ , especially since determining whether information technology and systems should be common globally or developed for local markets can be contentious. In addition, one of the more difficult policy issues is whether to rely on single-source suppliers globally or on multiple vendors in multiple countries [61].

The issues of compatibility in databases, applications development, use of global vendors, telecommunications technologies, and the standardization of technology are addressed in hypotheses 8 through 14.

H8. The compatibility of databases is greater in global firms than in other MNC types.

H9. Applications development tends to be more standardized in global firms than in other types of MNC.

H10. Global firms tend to use global vendors for applications development and support more than other types of MNCs do.

H11. Global firms tend to rely on private international networks more than do other types of MNC.

H12. Global firms tend to use new telecommunications technologies (e.g., EDI, EFT) to a greater extent than other types of MNC do.

H13. There will be greater standardization of IT technology in global firms than in other types of MNC.

H14. Global firms will rely on global vendors for hardware to a greater degree than will other types of MNC.

As was the case with two previous hypotheses (H5 and H7), pretesting indicated that it would be advisable to assess H12 separately at the corporate and subsidiary levels.

## Personnel Issues Hypotheses

Niederman [37] notes that human resource management for MNCs should address two issues—training and when or whether to use expatriates. The development of global training programs is key to creating an appropriate mix of executives from different cultures and also to ensuring that IS specialists can support a firm's internationalization efforts. Similarly, the use of expatriates plays an important role in transferring technological knowledge and managerial skills between the home and host countries. These issues are addressed as hypotheses H15 and H16:

H15. The number of corporate IS personnel in subsidiaries is lower in global firms than in other types of MNC.

H16. Global firms will tend to have a greater number of worldwide training programs than will other types of MNC.

## Testing the TNS Hypotheses

SEVERAL TNS VARIABLES ARE ASSOCIATED WITH EACH HYPOTHESIS. These variables are listed in Table 3, together with their respective hypotheses, the number of items used to measure them, and Cronbach's alpha if a scale was created for that variable. A sample item for each variable is also shown. For example, the variable of interest under H3 is the “role of the corporate IS group.” Eight items were used to measure this variable and combined to form a scale with an alpha value of 0.91. Similarly, H7 examined the presence of environmental scanning by asking respondents the extent to which their organizations gather information about environmental factors. Seven items were presented. Scales were created for the extent of environmental scanning at the corporate level (alpha = 0.95), and for the extent of scanning at the subsidiary level (alpha = 0.96).

The hypotheses relate the “global” form of organizations to the collection of all other forms. The taxonomy of MNCs was developed to facilitate the study of a wide variety of TNS issues, it was determined that a four-way analysis (corresponding to the categories of the taxonomy) would be used. This also facilitates the interpretation of the results of the analysis.

Multiple ANOVAs and chi-square tests were used to examine the hypotheses. $^{1}$ The chi-square test was used for testing H1 because a single item (shown in Table 3) using a categorical scale was used for this hypothesis. As shown in Table 3, single-item measures were also used for hypotheses H1, H10, and H11, because these hypotheses examine manifest properties of TNS strategy.

The TNS scales described in Table 3 were tested across the four clusters obtained from the cluster analysis. A summary of these tests and results is shown in Table 4. As can be seen from the table, eleven of the sixteen hypotheses were supported.

## Discussion and Conclusions

THESE RESULTS SUPPORT THE GENERAL PROPOSITIONS THAT THE ORGANIZATIONAL characteristics of centralization, dispersal, and coordination are differentially reflected in IT configurations of various kinds of MNCs. In a centrally coordinated business structure, IT is also globally centralized. Local autonomy appears to be a moderating variable, as can be seen from the differences in the IS characteristics of global and parent-child firms.

This study has sought to develop and validate a framework that will be useful in assessing the impact of IT on the operations of MNCs. A taxonomy of MNCs was developed based on well-accepted taxonomies that were created prior to the general acceptance of the critical importance of IT in managing global-scale firms. This taxonomy is then used as a basis for examining several hypotheses related to transnational systems. These hypotheses—operationalized in terms of IT organizational, strategic, architectural, and personnel issues—were generally supported by the results.

There were, however, several study hypotheses that were not supported by the data. These propositions were those relating to the use of TNS at the corporate level, including hypotheses concerning TNS planning areas at the corporate level, mechanisms linking the IS plan with the business plan at the corporate level, environmental scanning at the corporate level, and the use of telecommunications technologies at the corporate level. Corresponding hypotheses at the subsidiary level were found to be significant, however. This suggests that, at the headquarters level, there is no difference in the use of TNS among firm types. At the subsidiary level, however, TNS is being used consistent with the global strategies of the firm.
