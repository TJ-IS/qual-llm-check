# Effect of Partnership Quality on IS Outsourcing Success: Conceptual Framework and Empirical Validation

JAE-NAM LEE AND YOUNG-GUL KIM

JAE-NAM LEE is a Manager of LG-EDS Systems, which is one of the outsourcing service providers in Korea, and a Ph.D. candidate at the Graduate School of Management of the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology in Seoul. He received his M.S. degree in MIS from the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology. His research areas are information systems outsourcing, inter-organizational relationships, project management, and supply-chain management. He has published in Journal of Strategic Information Systems, and presented at a DSI conference.

YOUNG-GUL KIM is an Associate Professor at the Graduate School of Management of the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology in Seoul. He received his B.S. and M.S. in industrial engineering from the Seoul National University, Korea, and his Ph.D. degree in MIS from the University of Minnesota. His active research areas are IS architecture development, data and process modeling, IT management, and knowledge management. His publications have appeared in journals such as Communications of the ACM, Journal of Management Information Systems, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, Decision Support Systems, Information & Management, Information Systems Management, Database, and others. He has also presented several papers at ICIS, HICSS, and DSI conferences.

ABSTRACT: Recently, increasing attention has been paid to building successful partnerships in information systems (IS) outsourcing. This study establishes partnership quality as a key predictor of outsourcing success. We propose a theoretical framework for outsourcing partnership based on a social, rather than an economic, perspective. We identify the partnership-related variables from the relevant literature and clarify the concept of partnership quality by distinguishing between its components and its determinants. We then examine the impact of partnership quality on outsourcing success. Hypotheses on partnership quality were tested for 74 outsourcing relationships between 36 service receivers and 54 service providers. Results indicate that partnership quality may serve as a key predictor of outsourcing success. Partnership quality was found to be positively influenced by factors such as participation, communication, information sharing, and top management support, and negatively affected by age of relationship and mutual dependency.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: information systems outsourcing, outsourcing partnership, outsourcing success, partnership quality.

THE DECISION TO OUTSOURCE IS FUNCTIONS HAS BECOME more popular because organizations need diverse and high-quality information services to survive and excel in the rapidly changing external environment. This phenomenon shifts the traditional form of hierarchical IS governance to a newer market form of governance $[35]$ . In this process, various outsourcing options have developed based on the degree of outsourcing (total or selective) $[30]$ , period of outsourcing (long-term or short-term) $[45]$ , number of vendors (single or multiple) $[54]$ , and outsourcing types (service or asset) $[35]$ .

Many firms have addressed these issues, but mostly from the economic viewpoint $[29, 34, 39, 53]$ . Economic approaches, however, failed to address the fact that the economies of scale and scope achieved by IS outsourcing may vary significantly depending on the environment, structure, and strategy factors $[20, 43]$ . To overcome such problems, several firms have established intimate partnerships with service providers, as in the Kodak–IBM–DEC partnership $[4]$ and USAA–IBM partnership $[31]$ . They recognized the limitations of legal contracts and sought flexible relationships with their service providers based on mutual trust.

On the research side, much of the literature fails to distinguish between the components of partnership quality and its antecedent conditions. Many researchers have examined partnership quality through the correlation analysis among the partnership-related variables $[2, 24, 27]$ or between partnership success and related variables $[17, 20, 40]$ . Such studies, however, drew multiple, sometimes conflicting conclusions with few business implications. Despite the various theories related to partnership, such as resource-dependency theory, transaction-cost theory, agent-cost theory from the economic viewpoint $[34, 39, 43, 48]$ , and social-exchange theory and power-political theory from the social viewpoint $[14, 19, 27, 52]$ , there has been no integrated view to provide an in-depth analysis of the outsourcing partnership.

The objective of this study is to enhance the existing knowledge about outsourcing partnership by distinguishing the components of partnership quality from the variables that influence it. We also examine the relationship between partnership quality and outsourcing success through an integrated theoretical framework on outsourcing partnership based on social exchange theory and power-political theory. More specifically, this study intends to answer the following questions from the outsourcing customer's perspective:

• What factors influence outsourcing success?

\- What are the components of partnership quality in IS outsourcing between the service receiver and provider?

\- What are the determinants of partnership quality in IS outsourcing between the service receiver and provider?

\- Is there any relationship between partnership quality and outsourcing success?

## Literature Review

## Outsourcing Partnership

IS OUTSOURCING ORIGINATES FROM THE PROFESSIONAL AND FACILITY management services of the 1960s and 1970s in the areas of financial and operations support. While application packages, contract programming, and specific processing services comprised the major portion of IS outsourcing in the 1970 and 1980s, organizations in the 1990s outsource their IS functions through enterprise-wide systems integration, application development, and systems operation. In practice, IS outsourcing is an umbrella term for many information services such as transaction processing, facility management, systems integration, and more. In this study, we adopt the definition of IS outsourcing articulated by Grover, Cheon, and Teng [20]: “the practice of turning over part or all of an organization’s IS functions to external service provider(s).” IS outsourcing can be classified into two categories: asset outsourcing, which involves transfer of assets such as hardware, software, and people to service providers, and service outsourcing, which involves system integration and system management services without asset transfer.

In the 1990s, many organizations experienced difficulties in forming and managing a successful outsourcing relationship with service providers as the nature of outsourcing evolved from a contract relationship between the service receiver and provider to a partnership relationship [20, 38]. Prior studies emphasize the importance of partnership in IS outsourcing [16, 31].

Partnership is defined in this study as an interorganizational relationship to achieve the participants' shared goals. Partnership is not a new concept in the management area. Marketing and interorganization systems research has explored relationships between customer and vendor, buyer and seller, manufacturer and distributor, or auditor and client, and so on. A number of different views emerged concerning interorganizational relationships. Previous research classified the relationship between organizations into two types: transactional style and partnership style $[16, 20, 25]$ . A transactional style relationship develops through the formal contract in which rules of the game are well specified and the failure to deliver on commitments by either party should be resolved through litigation or penalty clauses in the contract. In contrast, the requirements of a partnership style relationship include risk and benefit sharing, the need to view the relationship as a series of exchanges without a definite endpoint, and the need to establish a range of mechanisms to monitor and execute its operations $[25]$ .

## Theoretical Background

In traditional IS management, the role of a service provider was limited in terms of the size of the contract and the type of service. Maintenance of hardware or program subcontracting has traditionally been the typical IS service provided. However, the type of relationship in outsourcing is changing from such buyer–seller relationships to the more strategic partnership relationship [20, 43]. Therefore, a necessary condition to move away from self-interest is a belief that the exchange relationship is a win-win situation for organizations to gain competitive advantages.

So far, many researchers have examined outsourcing partnership through a correlation analysis among the partnership-related variables, as in figure 1a [2, 24, 27, 42] or correlation analysis between partnership success and related variables as shown in figure 1b [17, 20, 40, 52]. However, these studies led to multiple, sometimes conflicting, conclusions without clear theory-driven explanation. Further, they failed to distinguish between process-oriented partnership success (e.g., high trust and commitment, benefit and risk sharing, fewer conflicts, etc.) and outcome-oriented outsourcing success (e.g., cost saving, competitive advantage gain, high user satisfaction, etc.).

## Theoretical Framework

RELEVANT THEORIES TO ANALYZE THE INTERORGANIZATIONAL RELATIONSHIP include the resource-dependency theory, transaction-cost theory, agent-cost theory from the economic viewpoint, and social exchange theory and power-political theory from the social viewpoint. Economic theories aim at explaining the characteristics of a governance or contract [23, 39]. They treat each sourcing decision as an independent event regardless of prior relationships that affect the ongoing sourcing decision [47, 53]. This treatment may be inappropriate where organizations repeatedly enter transactions with each other [43]. Explaining the relationship between organizations from a purely economic point of view is unjustifiable because interorganizational relationships form from the social learning experiences based on specific sequential interactions.

Social theorists assume that processes evolve over time as participants mutually and sequentially demonstrate their trustworthiness, whereas, in the economic perspective, the organization's exchange activities are enforceable [23, 27]. Social theorists understand relationship as a dynamic process through specific sequential interactions in which two participants carry out activities toward one another. However, a good relationship does not always bring about the participants' desired results [38, 46]. For this reason, we want to know what situations and factors determine the success of an outsourcing project.

According to the social theories, two mechanisms—trust and power—can explain the relationship between organizations $[9, 15]$ . Trust, a feature of relationship quality, has been conceptualized as “the firm’s belief that the other company will perform actions that will result in positive outcomes for the firm, and will not take unexpected actions that would result in negative outcomes for the firm” $[21]$ . Power is determined by the relative dependence between two actors in an exchange relationship, and the concept of power is only meaningful when compared with another organization $[5]$ . While social exchange theory uses the concept of trust to explain interactions between participants, power-political theory relies on the power derived from offering valuable resources that few other sources can provide.

To develop a conceptual model for outsourcing partnership, we integrate the relevant theoretical concepts from power-political and social exchange models. The first focus in our model is the relationship between partnership quality and its determinants. So far, past research has failed to notice this relationship because it has not distinguished between the elements of partnership quality and its antecedent conditions. The second focus of the model is the relationship between partnership quality and outsourcing success. The existing literature on partnership seems to take this relationship for granted. We should, however, validate whether outsourcing is successful when high-quality partnership exists. The partnership relationship between the service receiver and provider may not always be an appropriate one $[16, 25, 29]$ . Fitzgerald and Willcocks $[16]$ suggest that a partnership might only be appropriate under conditions of high uncertainty, when flexible contracts become important; a tight contractual relationship might be required under certain conditions.

## Research Model

PARTNERSHIP IS AN EFFECTIVE WAY TO IMPROVE ECONOMIES OF SCALE and scope provided by the traditional modes of organization [46]. However, partnership does not guarantee a desired outcome. Therefore, careful attention needs to be paid to the partnership problems that may lead to an unstable and conflicting relationship. The model developed here is composed of three major parts: partnership quality, determinants of partnership quality, and outsourcing success. We propose that partnership has its own factors to represent its quality, that several variables influence the degree of partnership quality, and that the degree of partnership quality is related to the outsourcing success.

## Partnership Quality

Quality is treated as having two dimensions: (1) fitness of use—Does the product or service do what it is supposed to do? Does it possess the features that meet the customer's needs? and (2) reliability—To what extent is the product free from deficiencies [18]? If we apply the first dimension to partnership, partnership quality may be expressed as how well the outcome of a partnership delivered matches the participants' expectations.

From this outset, this research views partnership quality as an antecedent of the outsourcing success. High partnership quality may be a necessary condition for outsourcing success, but not a sufficient condition. For instance, if the main objective of the outsourcing were cost reduction but the outsourcing vendor failed to meet the objective, such an outsourcing project would be a failure regardless of the partnership quality between the service receiver and provider. Thus, we distinguish the concept of partnership quality from that of outsourcing success, and empirically test whether outsourcing is successful when high-quality partnership exists. From the literature on interorganizational relationship and partnership, we identify five factors that make up partnership quality: trust, business understanding, benefit/risk share, conflict, and commitment. ## Determinants of Partnership Quality

Partnership quality is affected by organizational, human, and environmental factors. However, the existing literature does not explicitly distinguish the components of partnership quality from the factors that affect it. Here, we introduce the factors from the previous literature as potential determinants of partnership quality and present the hypotheses related to each factor. We expect to find a positive relationship between each of the hypothesized determinants of partnership quality and trust, business understanding, benefit/risk share, and commitment among the components of partnership quality, and a negative relationship between each of the hypothesized determinants of partnership quality and conflict.

## Participation

From a social perspective, participation is prescribed as “a remedy when there is conflict, frustration, and vacillation in the group” [10]. Active participation of the partnership members plays a major part in enhancing the sustainability of their partnerships over time [25]. When one partner's actions influence the ability of the other to compete effectively, the need for participation in specifying roles, responsibilities, and expectation increases [31]. Accordingly, the higher the degree of participation, the higher the quality of partnership:

## Joint Action

Heide and John [24] define joint action as “the degree of interpenetration of organizational boundaries.” It indicates that organizational boundaries become penetrated by the integration of activities such as long-range planning, product design, value analysis, design of quality control, training, and education. Therefore, it can provide a mechanism for negotiating and agreeing on mutual benefits, and for creating a common goal for participants [8, 25]. As the amount and scope of joint activities increase, we expect the organizations to become more effective partners in a relationship:

H2: There is a positive relationship between joint action and partnership quality.

## Communication Quality

According to the social exchange literature, effective communication between partners is essential in order to achieve the intended objectives $[1, 3]$ . Intensive communication should lead to better informed parties, which in turn should make each party more confident in the relationship and more willing to keep it alive $[3]$ . Communication quality is treated as an antecedent of trust in the previous literature $[1, 2, 40, 42]$ .

Accordingly, higher communication quality is believed to enhance the quality of partnership:

H3: There is a positive relationship between communication quality and partnership quality.

## Coordination

Good coordination is nearly invisible; we notice coordination most clearly when it is absent from the interorganizational relationship $[36]$ . Anderson and Narus $[2]$ suggest that successful working partnerships are marked by coordinated actions directed at mutual objectives. Coordination is needed to maintain stability between participants in a dynamic environment $[8, 28, 40]$ . Thus, quality of partnership will increase as the coordination effort between partners increases:

H4: There is a positive relationship between coordination and partnership quality.

## Information Sharing

Information sharing is the extent to which critical or proprietary information is communicated to one's partner [40, 42]. As described by Konsynski and McFarlan [28], partnerships can create a competitive advantage through the strategic sharing of organizations' key information. Many researchers report that closer relationships result from more frequent and relevant information exchanges among high-performance partners [25, 28]. Participants are expected to sustain more effective relationships over time by sharing information and by being knowledgeable about each other's organization:

H5: There is a positive relationship between information sharing and partnership quality.

## Age of Relationship

Participants will continue to interact with each other only if they perceive the exchange relationship as an attractive option $[3]$ . If not, they will find a more valuable partner with which to interact. Relationships that have lasted a long time are more likely to continue than younger relationships because participants have built mutual understanding and eliminated the need for detailed formal agreement through adjustment over time $[2, 14]$ . Thus, we expect quality of partnership to increase as the duration of prior relationship with the service providers increases:

H6: There is a positive relationship between age of relationship and partnership quality.

## Mutual Dependency

Dependency between organizations results from a relationship in which participants perceive mutual benefits from interactions [8, 15, 40]. The relative dependency suggested by Anderson and Narus [1] has to do with the firm's perception of its dependency on the partner firm relative to the partner's dependency on it. Mutual dependency between participants increases when the size of the exchange and importance of exchange are high, when participants consider their partner the best alternative, and when there are few alternatives or potential sources of exchange [17, 24, 52]. Therefore, the higher the degree of mutual dependency, we expect the higher quality of partnership:

H7: There is a positive relationship between mutual dependency and partnership quality.

## Cultural Similarity

Organizational culture is defined as “the pattern of shared values and beliefs that help individual understand organizational functioning and provide norms for behavior in the organizations” [13]. Partnership members with similar cultures should be more willing to trust their partners [3, 16, 31]. If the participants do not have similar organizational cultures, their relationship may create divergent values that make it difficult for them to trust one another and provide a fundamental cause to destroy the business relationship. Consequently, the existence of a cultural fit between participants is believed to improve the quality of partnership:

H8: There is a positive relationship between cultural similarity and partnership quality.

## Top Management Support

Top management support is considered a prerequisite for every successful outsourcing partnership $[3, 25, 28, 46]$ . If a partnership is to overcome the inevitable divergence of interests between participants, top executives have to share an understanding of the specific benefits of collaboration. The USAA–IBM partnership $[31]$ recognizes how important sponsorship and executive leadership are for a successful partnership. Rai, Borah, and Ramaprasad $[46]$ also observe that top management support is a critical factor to gain and sustain partnership success:

H9: There is a positive relationship between top management support and partnership quality.

## Outsourcing Success

Successful partnership enables participants to achieve organizational objectives and to build a competitive advantage that each organization could not easily attain by itself.

To gain these advantages of partnership, participants should try to enhance their partnership quality to reflect the extent of intimacy between partners. Therefore, a higher quality of partnership is likely to lead to a successful outsourcing relationship. Outsourcing success can be viewed as the level of fitness between the customer's requirements and the outsourcing outcomes. We measure outsourcing success in terms of both business and user perspectives in the following way: We expect to find a positive relationship between outsourcing success and components of partnership quality such as trust, business understanding, benefit/risk share, and commitment, and a negative relationship between outsourcing success and conflict.

## Business Perspective

Outsourcing is motivated by the promise of strategic, economic, and technological benefits. The success of outsourcing, then, should be assessed in terms of attainment of these benefits $[34, 38]$ . According to Grover, Cheon, and Teng $[20]$ , strategic benefits refer to the ability of a firm to focus on its core business by outsourcing routine information technology (IT) activities. Economic benefits refer to the ability of a firm to use expertise and economies of scale in human and technological resources of the service provider and to manage its cost structure through unambiguous contractual arrangements. Technological benefits refer to the ability of a firm to gain access to leading-edge IT and to avoid the risk of technological obsolescence that results from dynamic changes in IT.

## User Perspective

From a user perspective, outsourcing success may also be the level of quality of offered services $[22]$ . A decision to outsource on the basis of saving costs without analysis of the quality of service frequently leads to higher costs and lower user satisfaction. Therefore, it is imperative to conduct a proper analysis of the service quality before building a relationship with a service provider for a successful outsourcing project:

H10: There is a positive relationship between partnership quality and outsourcing success.

## Research Method

## Sample and Data Collection

THE UNIT OF ANALYSIS IN THIS STUDY IS THE OUTSOURCING relationship between a service receiver and a service provider. We focused on the service receiver's perceptions of the relationship. We selected thirty-six organizations in Korea that had outsourced their IS functions to external service providers. At our request, these organizations identified one to three service providers based on their past or current outsourcing projects. Then we interviewed two individuals involved in each relationship who were representatives in charge of the firms' IS operations, persons to manage each service provider, and end users of the outsourced information systems. To avoid the same subject bias in measuring partnership quality and outsourcing success, we interviewed outsourcing project personnel for partnership quality and its determinants while interviewing internal IS representatives for business satisfaction and end users for user satisfaction. The interviews were semistructured and detailed, following a questionnaire. Interviews were held individually or in a small group session for one to three hours per session. In sum, we surveyed 74 relationships between 36 service receivers and 54 service providers through 148 interviews.

## Measure Development

We conducted a series of personal interviews with seven IS professionals to confirm the external validity of the developed research framework. The interviews confirmed that our proposed research model was suitable for studying real world outsourcing phenomena. We then developed a five-point Likert-style questionnaire based on the literature and the authors' experience in the outsourcing industry, as well as the comments gathered from the interviews. Since one of the authors has worked for an IS outsourcing company for over five years, we were able to grasp an in-depth understanding of the IS outsourcing environment.

## Partnership Quality and Its Determinants

The operational definitions of the variables used and the relevant studies are summarized in appendix A. From the literature on partnership quality and its determinants, we adopted the constructs that have been used and validated by other researchers. Constructs that have not been used previously are evaluated and adopted from the relevant literature in terms of how they might be operationalized. In this research, we employed mostly perceptual measures for the variables of partnership quality and its determinants, except when objective measures were available, as in the case of age of relationship. The content validity of the questionnaire was established through a series of personal interviews with multiple IS professionals.

## Outsourcing Success

Outsourcing success, the dependent measure of our research, can be viewed as the level of fitness between customers' requirements and outsourcing outcomes. To overcome the traditional weaknesses of surrogate measures, we measured outsourcing's impact on business performance as well as on user satisfaction. For business performance, we adopted Grover, Cheon, and Teng's instrument to assess the degree of achieving the strategic, economic, and technological benefits of outsourcing [20]. User satisfaction was measured with an adapted version of the instrument used by Bailey and Pearson [6] and Baroudi and Olson [7].

## Reliability and Validity of the Measurement Instrument

Table 3 summarizes the number of items and the results of the reliability and validity tests. The content validity of the instruments was established through the adoption of the constructs that have been validated by other researchers and a pretest with seven IS professionals. We calculated the internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha), from 0.635 to 0.927, in order to assess the reliability of the measurement instrument [26]. For convergent validity, we evaluated the item-to-total correlation that is the correlation of each item to the sum of the remaining items. Items whose item-to-total correlation score was lower than 0.4 were dropped from further analysis. Discriminant validity was checked by means of a factor analysis [26].

Since each variable was measured by the multiitem constructs, factor analysis with varimax was conducted to check the unidimensionality of the items. Analysis was performed on the forty-seven items that measured the determinants and components of partnership quality, and on the thirty-nine items for outsourcing success. Items with factor loading values lower than 0.5 were abandoned from further analysis. Among them, eight items related to partnership quality had loading of less than 0.5 and were dropped. There were no items with a factor loading lower than 0.5 for outsourcing success. A final factor analysis for the determinants and components of partnership quality resulted in four factors, as shown in Table 4. All the measures used in this study are reported in appendix B, as well as the mean and standard deviation of each item, and Cronbach's alpha for each measure.

## Hypotheses Testing

The hypotheses were tested using a multiple regression analysis. For each hypothesis, models were run separately for each of the dependent variables: partnership quality and its components with its determinants, and outsourcing success with partnership quality and its components (figure 4). Since the proposed model is not meaningful if the correlation between partnership quality and outsourcing success is not significant, we first considered the quality of partnership as a summated variable and tested the relationship between outsourcing success and partnership quality. We then tested each hypothesis to find which factors were more important for outsourcing success.

The relationship between top management support and partnership quality was significant, so H9 is supported. Top management support also was significantly associated with trust and business understanding, while it was not related to benefit and risk share, conflict, or commitment.

## Outsourcing Success

As proposed in H10, the quality of outsourcing partnership shows a strong positive relationship with both business satisfaction and user satisfaction, as well as with overall outsourcing success. Trust showed a strong positive relationship with business satisfaction, while it had no effect on user satisfaction. This indicates that trust is a critical predictor of outsourcing success in terms of the business perspective, as opposed to the user perspective. Unlike the result with trust, business understanding was not a good predictor of business satisfaction while it significantly influenced user satisfaction. This means that the outsourcing outcome matched the users' requirements as understanding of its partner's business increased. Benefit and risk share showed a strong positive relationship with both business satisfaction and user satisfaction, as well as with overall outsourcing success. Although conflict was a predictor of business satisfaction, it had no effect on the overall outsourcing success and user satisfaction. Our finding for the conflict variable indicates that outsourcing success was not affected by the degree of conflict between the service receiver and provider. Our study also indicates that commitment was significantly associated with outsourcing success in terms of both the business and the user perspective.

## Discussion

ALL PARTNERSHIP QUALITY VARIABLES EXCEPT CONFLICT were significantly related to outsourcing success, as shown in Table 7. Previously, researchers have used the terms partnership quality and outsourcing success without clear conceptual distinction. To verify the relationship between the two, we proposed that partnership has its own factors representing its quality, while other variables determine the degree of the partnership quality. We also hypothesized that partnership quality affects outsourcing success. The result shows that partnership quality and outsourcing success have a strong relationship. It indicates that fostering a cooperative relationship based on trust, business understanding, benefit and risk share, and commitment is critical to reap the greatest benefits from outsourcing.

To examine the impact of partnership quality on outsourcing success, we introduced two concepts, business satisfaction and user satisfaction of outsourcing. It was the first attempt to measure outsourcing success in terms of both the business and user perspectives. The findings of this study indicate that partnership quality variables are significantly related to both business satisfaction and user satisfaction of outsourcing. That is, service receivers can achieve the strategic, economic, and technological benefits of outsourcing from high-quality partnership, while the end users of the outsourced information systems enjoy satisfactory outsourcing services when the quality of partnership is high.

The relationships between age of relationship and mutual dependency and partnership quality are interesting. They are both counterintuitive and inconsistent with the previous research $[2, 3]$ . Age of relationship had a significant negative effect on partnership quality. According to the marketing literature, a persevering relationship may or may not have content stability, depending on the environmental adjustments required and the willingness of the participants to make such adjustments $[14]$ . Normally, age of a relationship is an indicator of the relationship's strength, if the service receiver can exercise the renewal option at the end of the contract based on performance evaluation. The service receiver, however, may not have such an option if their initial outsourcing contract is long term (longer than five years) or if a significant business relationship exists between the service receiver and service provider firms that is not directly related to IS outsourcing $[32]$ . When such a situation prevails, service receivers may become less and less satisfied with their service providers, while their relationship period continues to be extended.

According to Fitzgerald and Willcocks [16], it is difficult to maintain partnerships in the field of outsourcing due to an asymmetry of resources and in the power relationship that favors the service provider. Service receivers initially have greater influence than service providers in their relationships. As the nature of the relationship between the service receiver and the provider changes from one that is relatively independent to one that is tightly coupled, service providers are likely to take more management responsibility and risk [20, 38]. And service receivers may fear that they become too dependent on their service providers, facing the significant switching costs [29, 47, 48]. This may explain the negative relationship between mutual dependency and partnership quality from the service receiver's perspective.

The nonsignificant findings in this study also bear some discussion. In general, we expected that the higher the degree of joint action, coordination, and cultural similarity, the higher the quality of partnership. However, these variables were not significantly related to the partnership quality. Joint action was defined as the degree of interpenetration of organizational boundaries $[24]$ , while coordination was defined as the managing of dependencies between activities $[36]$ . The activities of joint action and coordination may have been minimal in the prescribed project-level outsourcing relationships, reducing the role of those factors.

In prior studies on outsourcing partnership, cultural similarity was considered important to create convergent values, which lead to outsourcing success $[16, 25, 31]$ . Our own results on cultural similarity, however, indicate that it does not significantly increase the quality of partnership. Instead, in the repeated relationship, cultural similarity between the service receiver and provider is no longer a critical predictor once participants experience their partners' organizational culture during the initial relationship period.

As a rival model, we examined the association between the determinants of partnership quality and outsourcing success directly without the partnership quality construct.

This test of whether or not the concept of partnership quality is an important predictor of outsourcing success showed that most of the relations were not significant except for communication quality ( $\beta = 0.303, p < 0.05$ ) and top management support ( $\beta = 0.205, p < 0.10$ ). However, communication quality was not significantly related to user satisfaction, and top management support also was not significantly related to business satisfaction. As a result, even though there were some indirect effects between the determinants of partnership quality and outsourcing success, we can confirm that the concept of partnership quality is a significant predictor for outsourcing success.

A good deal of research has discussed partnership-based relationship on outsourcing, but such studies still reflect the nature of contractual relationship since the outsourcing partnerships in the real world were in the preliminary stage and the outcomes could not be properly evaluated. Thus, previous research has yielded a variety of sometimes conflicting conclusions. Recently, however, many organizations that have established intimate partnerships with service providers have begun to evaluate their performance relative to the contractual relationship $[37, 38, 50]$ . In this context, our study provides a meaningful conceptual structure for understanding and enhancing the quality of outsourcing partnerships.

## Conclusions and Limitations

THIS PAPER ESTABLISHES THE PARTNERSHIP QUALITY AS A CRITICAL success factor of IS outsourcing. The purpose of this study was to distinguish components of the partnership quality from its determinants and to investigate the effect of the partnership quality on outsourcing success based on an integrated theoretical framework from a social perspective. Partnership quality in the proposed research model consists of five components—trust, business understanding, benefit and risk share, conflict, and commitment. We found that partnership quality was influenced by several variables, including participation, communication quality, information sharing, age of relationship, mutual dependency, and top management support. We also found that trust, business understanding, benefit and risk share, and commitment had a significant effect on outsourcing success from both business and user perspectives. That is, the concept of partnership quality is not only critical to assure high-quality partnership, but also a key predictor for managing outsourcing for user and business satisfaction.

The identification of trust, business understanding, benefit and risk share, conflict, and commitment as the components of partnership quality is critical to the study and management of outsourcing partnership. For the researcher, if the components of partnership quality were considered merely as independent variables of outsourcing success, we would be likely to ignore their effects in studying outsourcing partnership. This failure to reflect their effects could produce flawed conclusions regarding not only the impact of the components of partnership quality on outsourcing success but also the impact of other antecedents. For the manager, our results imply that the components of partnership quality are key to understanding the process of outsourcing partnership development so that they can establish a high-quality partnership with the service provider.

If the concept of partnership quality is a key indicator of outsourcing success, how can such partnership characteristics be nurtured? We propose that they can be developed when the participants attend to outsourcing partnership by (1) increasing participation toward a cooperative relationship, (2) strengthening communication to achieve and monitor integrative agreements, (3) sharing information to build a competitive synergy that each organization cannot easily attain by itself, and (4) building participants' confidence so that their partners will not act opportunistically. Such actions will enable outsourcing participants to enjoy sustainable competitive advantages over their rivals.

This study has the following limitations: First, it was a kind of snapshot research that did not consider the feedback effect of partnership quality and outsourcing success over time. Ideally, we need a longitudinal research that tracks outsourcing partnership over time. Second, we used a convenient, not a random, sample in selecting firms that outsourced their IS functions. Third, the results of our study may have to be carefully interpreted since the sample was restricted to Korea. Among the seven significant relationships (H1, H3, H5, H6, H7, H9, and H10) of this study, two (H6 and H7) may not be generalizable to all outsourcing relationships.

On the basis of this research, we suggest the following future directions: First, this study examined the proposed research model only from the service receiver's perspective. Analysis of the relationship between partnership quality and outsourcing success from the service provider's perspective would be crucial for developing and sustaining high-quality partnership over time. Second, since studies for outsourcing partnership are still in a primitive stage, extension of this research is needed to include more specific and relevant factors to establish a high-quality partnership. Such studies can facilitate development of a theory base for outsourcing partnership. Third, longitudinal work to investigate the dynamic features of outsourcing partnership will provide more robust results than those of the snapshot research in the present study.
