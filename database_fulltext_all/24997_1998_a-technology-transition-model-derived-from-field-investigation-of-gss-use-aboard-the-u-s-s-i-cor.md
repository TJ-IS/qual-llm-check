JAY F. NUNAMAKER, JR. For biographical information, please see the Guest Editors' Introduction.

ABSTRACT: There are several thousand group support systems (GSS) installations worldwide, and, while that number is growing, GSS has not yet achieved critical mass. One reason may be that it can take one to three years for an organization to complete a transition to GSS. Studying GSS transition in the field could yield insights that would allow for faster, lower-risk transitions elsewhere. This article presents a thirty-two-month qualitative field investigation of an effort to introduce GSS into the daily work of the staff of the U.S. Navy's Commander, Third Fleet. Using the principles of action research, the project began with interventions based on the precepts of the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM). The goal of the interventions was to engender sufficient acceptance for GSS to create a self-sustaining, growing community of GSS users.

Throughout the study, building on a base of experience developed in other private-and public-sector transition projects, we revised and extended TAM based on insights that emerged in the field. The resulting model, the Technology Transition Model (TTM), frames acceptance as a multiplicative function of the magnitude and frequency of the perceived net value of a proposed change, moderated by the perceived net value associated with the transition period itself. TTM frames net value as having a number of dimensions, including cognitive, economic, political, social, affective, and physical. It posits that cognitive net value derives from at least three sources: changes in access, technical, and conceptual attention loads.

GSS transition proceeded at different speeds in different segments of the Third Fleet; the intelligence and battle staffs became self-sustaining within weeks, while others are still not self-sustaining. TTM appears to explain the differences that emerged in the Navy community.

The article presents TAM, then argues the propositions of TTM. It then presents background information about Third Fleet, and describes critical incidents in the transition effort that gave rise to the model. It summarizes the lessons learned in the field by comparing the differing transition trajectories among Fleet staff segments in light of the model.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: electronic meeting systems technology adoption, field studies, group support systems, qualitative research, technology acceptance, technology diffusion, technology transition, Technology Transition Model.

## Why Study GSS Transition?

support systems (GSS) are only just beginning to diffuse into the workplace.

A GSS is a suite of network-based software tools to support coordinated and concerted team efforts toward a goal. A GSS can produce two classes of outputs: visible shared objects and invisible, but no less tangible, group dynamics. The shared objects are texts, lists, trees, graphics, and so on. The participants may all contribute simultaneously to the creation of a shared object from their own workstations. Thus, people do not forget what they want to say while waiting for a turn to speak, nor do they ignore other speakers in order to remember what they want to say when they get the floor. The invisible group dynamics that may be created are convergence and divergence, depth and breadth, detail and abstraction, consensus and constructive conflict, to name but a few. Because a team may elect to contribute anonymously, people need not fear retribution from peers or superiors if they contribute unpopular ideas.

More than fifteen years of research in the lab and in the field have shown that, under certain conditions, GSS can substantially improve team productivity $[10, 11, 14, 15, 18, 21, 22, 28, 29, 31]$ . There are now several thousand GSS installations worldwide, and that number is growing, but GSS have not yet achieved widespread acceptance in the workplace. This may be explained, at least in part, by the fact that it can take three years or more for an organization to complete a transition to GSS.

We define “transition” as the period of time that starts when some person in an organization expresses interest in using a new technology and that ends when a community of users has become self-sustaining. There are several indicators that users may have become self-sustaining: They use the technology without intervention from a change agent; new members are trained and socialized to use the technology as a matter of course; and, if the technology breaks, the users find a way to get it fixed, rather than abandoning it.

Long transition times may inhibit the widespread use of GSS technology in several ways. The technology may evolve faster than it can be assimilated into daily work practices. Short-term financial constraints may dry up the resources required to sustain transition. Technology champions and experienced users may move on to other positions, leaving the technology to languish and die. Short transitions may mean more immediate benefit to the organization and more immediate return on the investment made by the technology creators, which may, in turn, lead to further technological advances. It may therefore be useful to understand why some technologies make the transition quickly, while others make the transition slowly, and why the same technology may make the transition quickly in some organizations and slowly in others. A longitudinal study of GSS transition in the field might give rise to insights that could allow researchers and practitioners to develop interventions to bring about more efficient, more complete transitions elsewhere.

## Overview of the Investigation

This article presents a thirty-two-month field investigation of an effort to introduce GSS into the daily work processes of the staff of the U.S. Navy's Third Fleet (COMTHIRDFLT) aboard the U.S.S. CORONADO. The principles of action research guided the investigation. With action research, one starts with a theory and intervenes in a situation to improve both the situation and the theory [4, 9]. This project began with interventions based on the precepts of Davis's Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [8]. The goal of the interventions was to engender sufficient acceptance for GSS to create a self-sustaining, growing community of GSS users.

The members of the research team had extensive experience with GSS. Beyond that, they came from a wide range of backgrounds. One specialized in collaborative workspace design. Another was a registered professional engineer with, among other things, heating, ventilation, and air conditioning design experience. A third specialized in team productivity issues; a fourth had expertise in group dynamics, communication, and facilitation. Several team members researched decision making under time pressure. Most had software development experience. Several had military experience. The team had extensive experience with previous GSS transition efforts at many organizations, including IBM, Burr Brown, Boeing, Hughes Missile, the World Bank, the U.S. Army and Air Force, and other private, government, and military organizations. The team members had facilitated thousands of GSS-supported meetings before the study presented here began. During this study, some members of the research team accrued more days at sea than did many of the Fleet staff.

The value of TAM for predicting user acceptance and use has been supported by a number of empirical studies $[1, 6, 7, 8, 19, 20, 23, 27, 30]$ . TAM was originally developed to predict future technology use after the first hour of exposure. We therefore did not expect it to offer a complete model for a complex, long-term technology transition project. Throughout the investigation, we revised and extended TAM using insights that emerged in the field. The result was the Technology Transition Model (TTM), a broader model of technology acceptance that may be useful for reducing the duration and risk of technology transition projects. We used a variety of qualitative methods to inform the model, including surveys, structured and unstructured interviews, observations, archival research, case studies, systems development, and active intervention.

This article briefly presents TAM, then argues the propositions of TTM. It provides background information about Third Fleet and describes how critical incidents in the GSS transition effort informed the development of the theoretical model. We then compare differing transition trajectories in two segments of the Fleet in light of TTM and summarize the lessons learned during the investigation.

## The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)

TAM IS A CAUSAL MODEL OF ACTUAL SYSTEM USE, the key indicator of success for technology transition. Figure 1 illustrates the propositions of TAM [6, 8]. It posits that actual technology use (AU) is directly caused by behavioral intentions (BI), a measure of the strength of one's intentions to perform a specific behavior. Intention is a useful construct because it can be measured well in advance of actual use.

TAM further posits that BI will be determined by two attitudes: perceived usefulness (U) of the technology for getting the job done and perceived ease of use (E), or the degree to which using the technology will be free of effort.

![](/api/attachments/JVHZ6VMN/fulltext/images/a0f5646961e74c9a51d0ae007faada91f2ed6cd9d351613f3861c23f06786e65.jpg)  
Figure 1. The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) [6, 7, 8]

U and E may seem at first to be very similar, but they are quite distinct. U is the degree to which one believes that using the technology will lead to improved job performance: "Will I do my job faster? Will my boss be happier with my results?" E, on the other hand, is the degree to which one believes the technology will leave one's mind free to work: "Will I remember how these menus work? Will I have to fight with the network?"

TAM proposes that myriad external variables $(X)$ , such as system design characteristics and self-efficacy, may combine to change one's perception of usefulness and ease of use. The model also posits that an increase in $E$ should cause an increase in $U$ . The unspoken assumption underlying this proposition may be that cognitive resources are limited; therefore the cognitive load imposed by the tool will interfere with task performance. If the tool is easy to use, it will be more useful for the task than if it is hard to use. TAM does not explore the mechanisms by which $\dot{X}$ may affect $U$ and $E$ . The model may summarized as follows:

$$
A U \approx f (B I) \approx f (U, E, X),
$$

where AU is actual use; BI is behavioral intention; U is perceived usefulness; E is perceived ease of use; and X is external variables.

During this and previous studies, following the precepts of action research, we began with a theory, TAM, which guided our intention to influence the perceptions of ease of use and usefulness among the staff at COMTHIRDFLT. During this project a new, somewhat richer model emerged. As the project progressed, we extended the model to explain events for which TAM did not account. The result is the Technology Transition Model (TTM). While TTM springs from TAM, it does not replace it. TAM predicts and explains a state of mind achieved after a one-hour exposure to technology; TTM attempts to explain what causes a group of technology users to become self-sustaining.

## Perceived Net Value

TTM posits that prospective users contemplate how life might be different should they choose to use a new technology. During this contemplation, they may consider how the proposed technology will affect many large and small elements of their lives, from the long-term viability of their organization to the length of their coffee breaks. The prospective users will form an attitude, a valenced subjective assessment of each element they consider. In TTM, these valenced responses are labeled as the perceived values ( $v_{i}$ ).

A perceived value has both a magnitude and a direction. It can be small or large, it can be positive, neutral, or negative. For example, prospective users may suspect that a proposed system will slightly reduce their influence with senior management. They might therefore perceive a slightly negative subjective assessment when they consider the political impact of changing to the new system, which is to say they perceive that the new system has at least one small negative political value. However, the same prospective users may perceive that the new technology will substantially increase their influence within their local work units. Thus, they might perceive that the new system also has at least one large positive political value.

TTM posits that the human mind tends to synthesize the myriad of perceived values through a nonvolitional, nonrational parallel process that produces an overall perceived net value (V). We define perceived net value as an attitude, a valenced subjective assessment in response to all the perceived likely consequences of changing from existing technology to the proposed technology. Note that V is not a measure of how different life might be with the new system, but of how the prospective user feels about the likely differences. Expected differences will evoke a holistic, valenced affective judgment, for example, on the whole, this will be good for me, or perhaps, life is going to get a lot worse.

We call the process of synthesizing perceived net value nonrational because the prospective user does not necessarily conduct a deliberate cost–benefit analysis. However, the concepts of perceived cost and perceived benefit are subsumed in the perceived net value construct. They are directions from neutral along a perceived value continuum. Anything above neutral (zero) may be framed as a benefit, anything below neutral (zero) may be labeled a cost.

Because humans can only hold a few items simultaneously in short term, TTM posits that prospective users remember their perceived net value more easily than they remember the specific details that gave rise to it. With each additional element of consequence they consider, they may modify their existing perceived net value without necessarily having full awareness of how the new element might interact with previously considered, but temporarily forgotten elements. Over the long term it tends to be the overall V that causes B, rather than the specific instances of $v_{i}$ .

Sometimes prospective users may be able to articulate the roots of their perceived net value with rational expectations that compare weighted costs and benefits, but TTM does not require this prerequisite for the occurrence of V. However, the act of trying to articulate a rational explanation of perceived net value may change the user's understanding of the consequences of changing to the new system, which may, in turn, cause changes in V. Sometimes the complexities of interactions and conflicts among the perceived values may make it impossible to articulate a rational explanation for perceived net value. The prospective user may only have a hunch or a “gut feeling.”

## Dimensions of Net Value

Perceived net value may vary along a number of dimensions. Davis $[6, 7, 8]$ identifies a most prominent instance of perceived value as usefulness, the degree to which the user believes the technology will enhance job performance. If the user thinks the new tool will greatly improve job performance, this might be an instance of a positive perceived value. However, there are other dimensions of perceived value.

Affective Value: Changing to a new technology may engender positive or negative emotional responses to which the prospective user may attach value. For example, the new system might engender less frustration (positive affective value) or more feelings of loneliness (negative affective value).

Economic Value: Changing to a new technology may cause changes in the economic status of the individual or the organization. For example, the new system might lead to more efficiency and thus higher profits (positive economic value), or it might cause a prospective user to lose a job (negative economic value).

Physical Value: A new system might affect the health and well-being of the prospective user in a variety of ways. For example, the new system might impose far less eye strain (positive physical value), or it might increase the noise level (negative physical value).

Political Value: By changing the way people access information and work together, a new information system may cause power shifts in an organization. For example, a new system might allow prospective users at remote locations to participate in decision-making meetings at headquarters (a positive political value), or it might increase pressure from outside constituencies (a negative political value).

Social Value: Adopting a new system could affect the personal relationships of prospective users. For example, the new system might reduce the amount of overtime work required, which could leave more time for family and friends (positive social value). On the other hand, the new system might eliminate the need for some face-to-face teamwork, reducing the hours a cohesive team spends together (negative social value).

Cognitive Value: A proposed system may cause some change in the attention demanded to accomplish a task. We define attention as cognitive effort over time. Human attention resources are limited. Any attention spent on operating a new technology interferes with problem solving and goal attainment. If the proposed system would reduce demands on the attention of the prospective user, this might be perceived as a positive cognitive value. If it would increase demands on attention, this might be perceived as a negative cognitive value. We have identified at least three dimensions of cognitive value: technical, access, and conceptual.

Technical value derives from changes in the difficulty of manipulating the technology. For example, if a proposed systems uses a GUI, while the existing system required the user to remember an arcane command set, the prospective user might perceive a positive technical cognitive value. If the new system required the prospective user to execute a tricky sequence of button-clicks not required by the existing system, this might be perceived as negative technical cognitive value.

Access value derives from changes in the cognitive load of gaining access to and permission to use a proposed technology—the so-called hassle factor. Users must expend some degree of mental effort each time they want to gain access to a new system. For example, prospective users might experience a negative access cognitive value if a new GSS were situated four floors below them, behind three locked doors for which they had to borrow keys. The access value might be even lower if the prospective users had to spend four hours on the phone trying to locate a facilitator every time they want to check whether the GSS facility were available for a particular date and time. On the other hand, if users already have a difficult-to-access system, and they perceive that accessing the new system will require substantially less effort, they will perceive a positive net access value. Thus, behavioral intentions to use a new technology will be determined in part by the degree to which access hassles will increase or decrease once the new system comes on line.

Conceptual value derives from changes in the cognitive load of understanding what the tool is supposed to do for the user. The more flexible and pwerful a technology becomes, the more generally useful it may be. However, more power brings with it a higher cognitive load for understanding what the system can do, and for deciding which of its many functions is the most appropriate for the task at hand. For example, a simple Internet chat might have only three features—chat, print, and save. Such a tool would present very low conceptual load. On the other hand, a GSS might have five or ten tools, each of which has dozens of features, and each combination of features might cause subtle yet powerful differences in group dynamics. Thus, the GSS presents a higher conceptual load than does the Internet chat tool.

TTM posits that any number of factors external to the individual may be perceived as creating positive or negative value along one or more dimensions in one or more directions simultaneously. Thus, in TTM, the dimensions of value subsume and explain the effects of the external factors, so there is no separate construct in the model to represent them. Further, the dimensions of value also subsume the concept of perceived usefulness, so it has no separate construct in the model.

## Perceived Frequency of Net Value

Users also consider how frequently $(F)$ they expect to derive the net value they perceive. Will they derive value from moment to moment? Daily? Twice a year? TTM posits that F and V combine multiplicatively to cause B. F may be zero or positive, it cannot have a negative value because there is no frequency less than zero occurrences per time unit.

No matter how high V becomes, if F is zero, B will be zero. Likewise, no matter how high F becomes, if V is zero, B will be zero. A small positive perceived net value obtained frequently may lead to a positive B. Likewise a large positive V and a low F may lead to a positive B. If V becomes negative, B may also become negative, and the user may actively avoid system use.

Thus, the relationship between F, V, and B may be expressed:

$$
B \approx f (F * V).
$$

Among other things, this model suggests that a frequent minor irritation, say, having to reset a server twice a day, may be sufficient to outweigh larger, but less-frequent benefits.

Note that F pertains to V, the overall perception of value. It does not pertain to $v_{i}$ , the individual instances of perceived value. Each element of perceived value will have its own frequency ( $f_{i}$ ). For example, a user might perceive that a proposed system will reduce the difficulty of a weekly task (positive cognitive value once a week), but it will require extensive maintenance about twice a year (negative cognitive value semiannually). Thus, one might argue that $V \cong f(\Sigma v_{i} * f_{i})$ . However, because attention resources are limited, the prospective user may not be able to remember all the myriad individual values and frequencies that went into the synthesis of V. TTM therefore posits that, as people form an overall V, they will also form an overall F. Their assessment may take the form, “On the whole, this system is going to do me a lot of good fairly frequently.”

## Perceived Net Value of Transition

But what of a technology that engenders a small positive V and a low F? Will it be accepted? That may depend on perceptions of switching costs and benefits. TTM posits that users also attend to the perceived net value of transition (T) when deciding whether to accept a new technology. While F and V involve a comparison of the existing system and the proposed system, T represents the value derived from the transition activity itself, apart from the value the new system will deliver. T represents a valenced synthesis of subjective responses to switching costs and switching benefits. For example, the learning curve for the new system would represent a negative cognitive value. On the other hand, a trip to San Francisco for training classes might be perceived as having positive economic, affective, and social values. Being regarded by one's boss as the project champion for new technology might offer positive political and social values, while having to shepherd the multitude of technical difficulties of establishing the new system might be a negative cognitive value. In the end, the prospective user will synthesize the perceived values associated with effecting the change into a subjective judgment of the net value of transition (T). They will weigh T against the value of $(F * V)$ . If T is larger, the user may elect not to use the system. Thus a low F, low V system may not be accepted unless T is also very low. The future benefits must outweigh the switching costs. Thus, the relationship among F, V, and B will be moderated by T:

$$
B \approx f (F ^ {*} V + T).
$$

## Certainty

People develop their attitudes toward a new technology based on their exposure to it. We identified three kinds of exposure: testimony, observation, and experience. Testimony may be as informal as a conversation at a water cooler or as formal as a refereed academic article. Observation may range from a glimpse of a video clip to several days of watching over people's shoulders as they use the technology. Experience may range from a few minutes of hands-on playing to days of intensive use for mission-critical applications.

Whatever the form of exposure, the prospective user will use it to form some assessment not only of a perceived net value, but also of some degree of certainty (C)

that their valenced subjective assessment is accurate. Certainty is a subjective probability that the net value a prospective user perceives will actually be obtained. A subjective probability is a seat-of-the-pants sense of the likelihood that one's knowledge or judgment is actually correct. Thus, a person may read a research paper on group support systems and develop a perception of high net value for switching to the technology. However, the person may not be certain that the high net value can be obtained. The less certain the person is of the perception of high net value for GSS, the less willing the person will be to use the technology.

The upper and lower bounds of certainty are easily quantified: It may take on a value from 0.0 to 1.0. It may be more difficult to quantify certainty when it falls between those values. How does one know whether one is 35 percent certain or 46 percent certain? Further research is required to find a reliable and valid way to measure certainty. Nonetheless, it remains an important concept for explaining why groups of users become self-sustaining with a technology.

Certainty acts as a multiplicative moderator on V with respect to B, thus:

$$
B \cong f \left(\left(\left(V ^ {*} C\right) ^ {*} F\right)\right) + T),
$$

which may be rewritten:

$$
B \cong f (V C F + T).
$$

Like TAM, TTM posits that actual use (A) is a function of behavioral intention (B). Thus, the complete model would read:

$$
A \cong f (B) \cong f (B C F + T).
$$

Having laid out the arguments of TTM, we now explore the derivation of the model. The next section presents the context and critical incidents of the qualitative investigation from which the model emerged. It is beyond the scope of this paper to present a detailed account of all the events that transpired, so we shall focus on those events that influenced the development of TTM.

## The Third Fleet Context

AT THE HEIGHT OF THE COLD WAR, the United States military faced a known enemy that used conventional strategies and tactics. In 1989 the world situation quickly and unexpectedly shifted. The Soviet bloc broke into smaller components, whose posture toward outside countries changed. Small, unknown terrorist groups, terrorist nations, and small civil wars emerged as the primary threats to international stability. At the same time, the U.S. military establishment began to broaden its role in international disaster relief efforts. Both of these objectives demanded that the U.S. military be able to respond more rapidly in less known or anticipated situations.

Faced with a growing need for rapid response to crises, the U.S. Department of Defense (DoD) began to foster collaboration among the four services by forming joint task forces (JTF). A JTF Commander (CJTF) is typically a three-star admiral or general who might come from any of the four armed services. When a crisis erupts, the national command authority appoints a CJTF. The CJTF designates one- or two-star admirals and generals as Component Commanders for the air, ground, and naval resources. These component commanders, in turn, draw resources from all four services in support of their mission. When the crisis is over, the JTF stands down and the components return to the chains of command within their respective services.

By the mid-1990s the number of U.S. military bases located on foreign soil dropped from a cold war high of 172 to just 27. Significant additional restrictions were placed on the types of activities that could be carried out at the remaining bases. In part to overcome diminishing resources overseas, the U.S. Navy has established a series of Command Ships, equipped to support a CJTF, the component commanders, and their staffs. The Command Ships provide a fully equipped and staffed mobile joint command center on sovereign U.S. territory that can be moved on-station into position within twelve miles of any coastline on short notice. These ships can be positioned without many of the formalities required to move troops into foreign territories; no diplomatic negotiations and no concessions are required.

## A Radical Change of Mission for Third Fleet

In February of 1996, Third Fleet was in the middle of a radical transition. Its cold war raison d'être had been to train battle group and ship commanders for duty off the coast of Siberia. By the early 1990s that mission was no longer relevant.

Third Fleet's flagship, the U.S.S. CORONADO (AGF 11) (figure 3), was an aging amphibious assault ship already slated to be decommissioned. It rarely left the dock. People began to refer to it as “Building 11, and U.S.S. Neversail.” Some people opted for assignments to Third Fleet so they could spend less time at sea and more time with their families. The organization became known as a sleepy “pineapple fleet.” There were high-level discussions in the Department of the Navy about whether to disband Third Fleet entirely.

In the mid-1990s, life at Third Fleet changed radically when it was assigned operational responsibility for the defense of the Eastern Pacific, an area encompassing 50 million square miles. The pace of work sped up dramatically. In 1995 Third Fleet was designated as an alternate Commander, Joint Task Force (CJTF), and the decision was made to rework the old flagship into a new, state-of-the-art command ship.

The mission of a command ship is fundamentally different from that of a flagship. A flagship is often the permanent home base for an admiral and a fleet staff. The flagship deploys to meet the needs of the fleet. A command ship is a floating military command center that awaits the arrival of several temporarily embarked flag and general staffs who will respond to an emerging crisis. Because of its role as a floating command center, a command ship offers two primary resources to a CJTF: floor space—office and meeting spaces, berthing and mess decks, and so on—and C4ISR infrastructure. C4ISR stands for Command, Control, Communications, Computing, Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance. The acronym stands for the infrastructure, for the processes supported by that infrastructure. Thus, an intelligence officer would acquire and analyze information using sensors, computers, and data communications channels provided and maintained by a C4ISR team. The command ship quickly deploys to the center of gravity for the crisis. The command ship may also serve as the home base for a fleet staff, as it does at Third Fleet, but that is not its primary mission.

These facts make a command staff a good candidate for the use of GSS.

## Staffing at Third Fleet

With approximately 50 officers and 120 enlisted personnel, Third Fleet is chronically understaffed. At any given time a dozen or more officer billets go unfilled for lack of qualified personnel. Service-wide retention rates for junior officers and enlisted personnel have dropped substantially in the past decade, in part because the skills they develop while in the Navy can sometimes bring twice the salary in the civilian market. Further, since the end of the cold war, the Navy has intentionally reduced its numbers, although its mission workload has not declined. Where it used to be able to focus its resources on the Eastern bloc, the Navy now must spread its declining resources among many hotspots across the world.

When the project began in early 1996, our efforts to introduce GSS to COM-THIRDFLT were guided by the precepts of TAM. We therefore focused on issues relating to usefulness and ease of use. The following sections describe the critical incidents in the project that informed the evolution from TAM to TTM.

## The Physical Work Environment at Third Fleet

The initial survey of the CORONADO revealed that there were no appropriate spaces for using collaborative technology. Noise in the workspaces from the ship's ventilation system averaged higher than 84 dBa in many places and it peaked above 110 dBa in a few (louder than a rock concert or the flight deck of an aircraft carrier). In the admiral's briefing room, the staff would shut down the cooling system so they could hear one another speak, but the metal-walled rooms would then quickly overheat. Further, a group of more than six or eight people had no meeting place on the ship that did not have a constant stream of other people with legitimate needs to walk through it frequently, which disrupted collaborative work. Finally, there was no place on the ship where a team could sit down and see both one another and their computers. All the computers on the ship were either in tall workstations that blocked the user's view or on desks facing walls.

The inadequate decision spaces posed a serious perceived ease of use barrier, so the first GSS sessions for the fleet's senior leadership were held at a nearby university. Those meetings are summarized in the next section.

## Prediffusion Ad-Hoc GSS Activities

THE USEFUL BUT INVISIBLE GROUP DYNAMICS that a GSS can create do not emerge unless the group is working on something substantial, something in which they perceive a vested interest. Demonstrations based on trivial, hypothetical tasks tend to leave prospective users with the perception that the technology is trivial. Further, groups tend to resent being convened to work on trivial tasks while important issues go unattended. In such cases, anonymous on-line discussions occasionally devolve into buffoonery or even hostility. For this reason, the transition team refrained from demonstrations, opting instead to find real tasks to which the GSS could be applied.

## Preparing for a Change of Command

In February 1996, the Fleet J6 officer (the person in charge strategically and tactically, wielding data and human-communication channels for the fleet) observed that, even if all available billets were to be filled, by shore-based standards the CJTF and the Component commanders aboard the ship would be permanently understaffed. A typical land-based command staff numbers in the thousands, but the command ship, which would embark as many as three command staffs, had berthing and food service for fewer than 1,000 people, including the 250 members of the ship's crew. The individual staff members would therefore have to operate much more efficiently than did their land-based counterparts. J6 thought collaborative technology might help ease the staff workload. Further, he believed that, because the staff was sea-based, it was isolated from other groups with whom it ought to be interacting, such as the other numbered fleets, the war colleges, and the other military services. J6 was therefore intrigued by GSS and decided to use it as a one-time experiment.

Because there were not yet GSS facilities aboard the ship, the meeting convened at a university about five miles from the ship. The chief of staff (COS), the other J code ACOS, the fleet marine officer, and the fleet science advisor met for two hours to prepare for an impending change of command; a new admiral was to take over the fleet one month hence. The group used an electronic brainstorming and voting tool $^{1}$ to address the question, “What are the key issues facing the fleet?” In half an hour, the group contributed approximately 210 comments. Then, working orally, they extracted from their comments a list of potential action items that they placed on an electronic ballot. Working anonymously, the group rated each ballot item. This was the first time any of the participants had used GSS.

There was mixed reaction to GSS among the ACOS. They generally agreed that they had accomplished the task much faster than they would have without the technology. The chief of staff and J6 expressed enthusiasm for the efficiency enabled by GSS, and both chose to use GSS again for subsequent tasks. However, during anonymous on-line feedback, and later during oral discussions and interviews, several of the participants expressed a lack of enthusiasm for the way their oral interactions had been curtailed during the activities supported by GSS.

During this meeting, the senior leadership also reviewed photographs of GSS-equipped facilities at the Pentagon, at Army facilities, and at civilian sites. All expressed strong interest in having similar facilities aboard the command ship.

## Moving the Ship to a New Home Pier

The U.S.S. CORONADO was to move to a new mooring site. Before the ship could move, the site would have to be configured to provide electricity, steam, fuel, communication, sanitation, crane service, parking, and shore-based offices, to name but a few items. In March of 1996, the chief of staff, five of the six ACOS, and the fleet science advisor once again convened at the nearby university to address the task.

They used an electronic brainstorming tool to respond to the question, “What steps must we take before we can move the ship to Ballast Point?” Within half an hour, the group had generated and discussed about one hundred potential action items. The team then extracted the items they considered most important and placed them on an electronic ballot. They evaluated each item by time sensitivity: Was action required this week? This month? This quarter? The poll revealed a high degree of consensus on some issues, and a broad range of opinions on others. In subsequent on-line discussion, the participants surfaced their assumptions, clarified meanings, and shared new information. A second poll revealed acceptable consensus on all issues.

The staff had allocated two days for the task. They completed their work in just over two hours, leaving with a list of twenty-six prioritized action items. One among them pointed out that they had previously held a two-day meeting on the same subject, and that the previous meeting had faltered and closed with no result. They agreed to find other opportunities to use the technology. However, several argued and all agreed that it was too inconvenient to get from the ship to the university. They requested that all subsequent GSS activity be conducted aboard the ship, despite its inadequate meeting spaces.

## Extending Perceived Ease of Use

TAM's ease-of-use construct offered only a general explanation for the staff's reluctance to use the technology at the university. The GSS was technically easy to use, but it was not easy to access. Clearly, the participants were counting the costs beyond the effort demanded by the technology itself. As the project progressed, we observed three distinct components of perceived ease of use: technical load access load, and conceptual load. Technical load relates to the attention demands imposed by the system itself. Arcane command sets, illogically arranged menus and controls, balky networks, sticky keyboards, and bad mice are all examples that would increase the technical load of using a proposed system. Access load pertains to the attention demands required to gain permission for and access to GSS facilities. One user referred to this as the “hassle factor.” Having to go to a different building, or to check out a key, or to sign up a week in advance might be instances of high access loads. Conceptual load pertains to the cognitive effort required to understand what a proposed system is supposed to do for the prospective user. The more features and functions a tool has, the higher the conceptual load may be for the prospective user. Throughout the study, all three of these ease-of-use constructs appeared to have a substantial impact on the GSS transition. These three constructs eventually became part of TTM as dimensions of conceptual value.

Testimony, Observation, Experience: Early GSS

Exposure at Third Fleet

In the Spring of 1996, design work began for five new collaborative decision spaces to be built aboard the CORONADO. Construction on the first space began in late March. While construction was underway, the transition team attempted to demonstrate the usefulness of GSS to more of the fleet staff by working aboard the ship and watching for opportunities to support collaborative efforts with GSS. The result was a series of ad-hoc GSS meetings using a network of twenty laptop computers that came to be known humorously as the FlyAway LAN. The transition team wrestled the FlyAway LAN up and down the ship's ladders in eighty-pound crates to set it up wherever space could be found, both on and off the ship. Exemplars of these ad-hoc activities are described in the next section.

## Spending \$10 Million

The next opportunity to use GSS at Third Fleet arose on rather short notice in March of 1996. J6 was informed that \$10 million dollars had suddenly been freed up when work on another ship was delayed. If J6 could obligate the funds within twenty-four hours, he could have the money for the CORONADO project. J6 called a meeting of twenty-five officers, senior enlisted, civilian engineers, designers, shipbuilders, and administrators to generate recommendations how the funds should be allocated.

The group used twelve laptop workstations in the ship's wardroom. Some participants sat at the GSS terminals to play an active role, while others sat behind them in an advisory capacity. J6 made it clear he was looking for recommendations from the group, not for decisions. Each participant used an electronic ballot to express a preference for how the \$10 million should be allocated among the budget items at hand. The GSS prioritized the budget items by averaging the allocations from the individual ballots. J6 moderated an oral discussion of the results and rearranged some of the items for practical reasons—order of construction, political impact, and so on. If there was substantial consensus about an item, there was little discussion. If there was a disparity of opinions, the active group members went on-line to explain the reasoning behind their votes. At the conclusion of each discussion, J6 assigned a final dollar amount to the item. The system kept a running tally of how much had been spent and how much remained. When the \$10 million had been allocated, the group browsed the unfunded items to see if any critical items had been missed. They found four such items and defunded several other items to cover the costs.

The session lasted just under three hours. When asked how long the task might have taken without GSS, the participants just laughed (heartily) and began to tell war stories about the arguments and digressions in previous meetings. With the success of this effort, the J6 became an open and vocal champion for GSS adoption by the fleet. Over the next few weeks, J6 attended dozens of meetings with staff members, funding agencies, and visiting dignitaries. In order to raise perceived benefits, he continuously pointed out that the ship had moved to the cutting edge of collaborative decision making and was already deriving great benefit.

## Frequency of Need: Long Lags Between Experiences

The next opportunity for GSS did not emerge for several more weeks. Indeed, throughout the first year of the project, opportunities to use GSS were sparse, always separated by two or more weeks. Participants reported being satisfied with the outcomes, the processes, and the tools, but their existing business practices were designed to minimize the need for collaborative effort. They only used the GSS for problems when they could not avoid a meeting. It therefore took many months to give them enough exposure that they could perceive the benefits. This factor accounted for a substantial chunk of the time required to accomplish the transition.

Just as a restaurant patron needs no training as a chef to enjoy a fine meal, so most GSS participants need no knowledge of GSS to benefit fully from using the tools. So long as one person among them knows how to wield the technology, all can benefit. Early in the project, the transition team provided the requisite expertise. The long intervals between uses also meant that participants felt there would be little payoff for learning to wield the technology for themselves. A few started to learn to run it out of curiosity, but they tended to forget the details between events. They perceived the system as useful, but did not use it often enough to make it worth learning.

TAM did not offer an explanation for this phenomenon, so we sought to extend the model beyond perceived usefulness to incorporate perceived frequency of usefulness. People with small, frequent needs might accept a technology, as might people with strong, infrequent needs, but people with a small, infrequent need might not.

Throughout this period, we also observed that, not surprisingly, the nearly continuous stream of testimonials presented to the staff by J6, the GSS technology champion, was a significant factor in raising perceived benefits, even among people who had not yet used the technology. We sought to expand the model to include the propositions that perceived usefulness was a function of testimony.

As the project progressed, we realized the testimony was actually an instance of exposure. People formed their assessments of the value of GSS based on three kinds of exposure: testimony, observation, and experience. The exposure allowed the prospective users to develop an assessment of the net value that would derive from the new system, and the exposure engendered some degree of certainty that their assessments of value were accurate. We therefore sought to incorporate certainty into our model of technology acceptance. We posited that the more exposure one had, the more certain one would be of one's value judgments about the new technology.

At first, we believed that exposure also caused perceived net value. However, on further consideration we noticed that we could not assert that “the more exposure one has, the more value one perceives.” It was entirely conceivable that the more exposure one had, the less value one would perceive. It was even possible that users could at first perceive small positive net value, then large negative net value, then large positive net value as exposure increased. Nonetheless, some level of exposure must logically be required for a prospective user to form an attitude about the technology. Therefore, we frame exposure as a boundary on TTM. The model only applies to prospective users who have been exposed to the technology. With increased exposure, their perceptions of value may change dramatically one way or another, but their certainty about whatever they perceive should increase.

## Increasing Testimonial Exposure: External GSS Activities

J6, now the technology champion, sought to increase the attractiveness of the technology by engaging the GSS team in several higher-profile, higher-risk external settings. The first such event took place in October 1996 at a two-day biannual command ship summit. Seventy-five senior officers and civilians representing all four command ships met to develop a unified set of prioritized funding issues. During the next external event in June 1996, a group of ten senior civilians at Pacific Fleet headquarters developed a set of action items for creating a paperless office. In August 1996, in a third external event, a group of thirty-four senior military and civilian officials from the Department of the Navy met for three days to review and revise a constitution for the new office of Department of the Navy chief information officer.

After each of these meetings, the most senior participant sent a letter to Third Fleet thanking them for the technology support. The technology champion circulated the letters among the fleet staff, may have raised the credibility of the GSS team at Third Fleet, and created a more favorable climate for transition.

## Beyond Job Performance: Perceived Political Value

The remote sites in the connectivity experiments agreed to participate partly because they were curious about the technology, but mostly because they wanted exposure and access to the senior leadership who would view the trials. None chose to use the technology because they thought it would improve their job performance. This was an instance of perceived benefit that had little to do with the perceived usefulness construct. Throughout the project, we observed a variety of such cases. Indeed, the earliest moments of the project provided such a case.

The project began when a Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) sponsor introduced a GSS researcher to a member of Third Fleet staff, and asked that the two of them attempt to introduce GSS to the fleet. The staff member embraced the project and arranged for the senior leadership to try the technology. Many months later, however, he admitted that he started with no expectations one way or another about the usefulness of the technology. Rather, he wanted to establish a good working relationship with DARPA and the researcher. He therefore agreed to use the technology in a few venues where there could be little political fallout from a failed effort. It was only later that he became convinced that the technology had value.

Throughout the project, a number of people chose to use (and not to use) the GSS for reasons that had little to do with a perception that it might improve their job performance (perceived usefulness). We therefore sought to expand the model to encompass benefits beyond perceived usefulness, so we adopted the construct perceived net value.

We define perceived net value as an affectively valenced subjective assessment of the likely consequences of changing to a new technology. Perceived net value may range from very positive to very negative. There may be many dimensions of perceived net value. Throughout the project, we observed that perceived usefulness remained a primary consideration, but people also chose to use (or not to use) the GSS technology for social, political, cognitive, economic, affective, and physical reasons that appeared to be orthogonal to perceived usefulness. We posit that it does not derive from a linear, rational process of considering and weighing each dimension separately. Rather, it derives from some parallel processing of all salient values simultaneously. By the end of the project, we noted that perceived usefulness had been explained by the other dimensions of perceived net value, so we dropped it.

## Not Perceiving Net Value: The Flag Summit

While the negative values of changing to a new technology are usually quite salient, participants often overlook the positive values they derive from GSS. Consider an example that occurred early in the project: In August 1996, a group of twenty-five admirals and generals used the GSS for a two-day meeting where they heard briefings and discussed issues of mutual concern. As the briefers spoke, the officers typed feedback into the GSS. They could read one another's comments and respond on-line. After each briefing, the participants addressed questions aloud to the presenters and discussed the issues aloud with one another.

In a postmeeting interview, one of the admirals reported that the GSS experience had been fun, but that it had contributed little value to the meeting. He speculated that perhaps a group of twenty-five was too large a group for effective use of GSS. He then observed that the group had produced hundreds of comments and responses without actually interrupting the presenters. He reflected that, in previous years, political posturing and long-winded opining had often sidetracked the meeting and brought it to a grinding halt. Without prompting, he stated that it might have taken days to get through all the discussion they had done on-line during the briefings. By the end of the interview he had changed his mind about the value of GSS in the meeting and about using GSS for large groups. Still without prompting, he concluded that the GSS might be even more useful in a group of forty or fifty people, especially if they all wanted to talk at once.

This experience demonstrates that the participants in a GSS session might not be immediately aware of the benefits they derive from GSS. If the technology is working well, the participants give their attention to the task at hand and do not think about the tools. This suggested that participants needed to hear their own testimony as well as that of others in order to perceive the benefits of the technology. At the end of all subsequent meetings, the participants were asked to assess what value, if any, the technology had provided, in an effort to increase the salience of their exposure, to increase its impact on their perceptions of value.

## Collaborative Spaces as a Catalyst for Diffusion

WHILE THE CORONADO WAS UNDER RECONSTRUCTION, she still had to meet her operational obligations, which meant that every few weeks the ship put to sea for two to three weeks at a time. The GSS team took advantage of these at-sea periods to try to develop concepts of operations that would work GSS into the daily life of the fleet. In part because of their own experiences, and in part because they had received positive reports from other sites, the command staff was willing to try GSS for shipboard tasks during underway periods. Before the new workstations arrived, workers welded a temporary table to the deck and the transition team set up the FlyAway LAN.

Until the first of the new collaborative workspaces became operational, all GSS activities were initiated by the transition team, and few groups used the system more than once. There was no apparent diffusion of the technology. When the first permanent collaborative facility became available on the ship, there was a substantial shift in the patterns of GSS use within the fleet.

## Civil Military Operations Center

The first facility was the Civil Military Operations Center (CMOC, pronounced see-mock) was completed in June 1996. It was a small space, measuring only $14 \times 20$ feet, with workstations for only eight participants. The CMOC was to be the workspace for representatives of foreign and nonmilitary organizations who might embark to help address a crisis. The space was laid out with a clear power seat at the head of the table, to help heterogeneous teams establish a group structure quickly. Because the space was not conceived to support critical military missions, the research team was able to experiment with a number of innovative, even radical design concepts before rolling them out to the rest of the ship. Figure 5 shows the CMOC.

There was a certain amount of skepticism about the CMOC design before it was built. The CMOC was tucked just under the flight deck along the starboard side of the ship. By the conventional wisdom, this location was undesirable because of noise from helicopters and heat from the sun-baked deck above. Experienced shipbuilders also expressed skepticism about both the need for and the possibility of creating a silent ventilation system. Said one, “You can’t control the temperature, you can’t control the noise, what’s the big deal? It’s a ship! Ships have always been like this!”

We used a concurrent engineering approach to the CMOC design. It addressed all the physical affordances that might affect collaborative work, including heating and cooling, passive ambient noise suppression, lighting, furniture, computer and telecommunication equipment, and multimedia display devices. The CMOC was fitted with a radical shock-mounted heating and cooling system with a series of three sound-dampening plenums and noise insulation inside the ducts. It used a low-velocity, high-volume approach that dropped cool air onto participants and then pulled it through the workstations past the monitors and computers and into a return duct under the deck.

Eleven inches of fiberglass insulation and a sound-baffled aluminum drop ceiling eliminated both heat and noise. When the space was finished, the ambient noise averaged 54 dBa with the ventilation system on full, and with all computers and projectors running. People could clearly understand one another while speaking in hushed tones. Most of the remaining ambient noise was transmitted through the metal bulkheads from a diesel-powered pump two decks below. A sound survey revealed that the quietest place in the space was inside the air plenum above the workstations.

Despite its small size, as a prototype for larger spaces in the rest of the ship, the CMOC was equipped with every technology that might be useful to collaborating teams, including radios, secret and unclassified telephone lines and computer networks, video teleconferencing equipment, blackboards, multimedia presentation gear, and two single-lens projectors. Besides being a general-purpose workspace, it was also designed to be a pedagogical tool. Its visual design made it easy to explain why its innovative components were configured as they were.

Soon after becoming operational, the CMOC was in demand by fleet staff. At first this appeared to have less to do with the perceived value of the GSS and more to do with the perceived value of the space. It was quiet, it had no through traffic, it had GSS and all the latest collaborative technology, and it had “these university geeks who would help you plan and execute a meeting.” The participants were at first tolerant of the GSS and then came to value it for some tasks. During its first year of operation, the CMOC came to be used twelve to fourteen hours per day whenever the ship was at sea. Teams as large as twenty people began to use the small facility on a regular basis. Eight people would sit at the permanent workstations, the rest would stand behind in the narrow aisles and contribute aloud. People seated at the workstation would add key issues from the oral discussion to the on-line interaction. The “strap-hangers,” as they became known, would follow the on-line discussion on the public screens at the front of the space.

Staff members began regularly to seek out the transition team to request GSS support for their activities. The term CMOC became a synonym for collaborative technology. The following paragraphs describe several exemplars of collaborative activities in the CMOC.

## Joint Experiment ROMEO

In April 1996, the CORONADO hosted a Joint Exercise with the code name ROMEO. $^{2}$ The exercise had two purposes: to prepare a carrier battle group for deployment, and to experiment with twenty-three new C4ISR technologies. The experiment lasted ten days. In past exercises, it had been difficult to consolidate and report outcomes of such experiments. People were busy running the ship, running the exercise, and running the experiments, so final reports tended to be cursory and vague. In the final two days of ROMEO, about fifty experimenters and warfighters were required to come to the CMOC in small groups for hourlong on-line debriefing sessions. They used an electronic questionnaire tool to respond to about twenty closed-ended and eleven open-ended questions about the results of the experiments. They provided feedback on a broad set of issues. Fleet staff rolled the output from the questionnaire into a group outline tool and drafted a report around the results. Before the ship reached port, they had prepared a seventy-five-page preliminary report on the experiments, with analysis of current results and recommendations for future directions.

## Joint Exercise YANKEE

In October 1996, during a joint exercise code-named YANKEE, a two-week-long evolution whose purpose was to prepare a carrier battle group for duty overseas, the J5 Plans group began to experiment with collaborative techniques for planning under time pressure. They met to analyze the enemy's most likely, least likely, and most dangerous Courses of Action (COAs).

Standard brainstorming techniques encourage people to contribute any idea, good or bad, in hopes that the juxtaposition of many ideas will stimulate creativity. As a result, when brainstorming is finished, it can take a great deal of effort to sift the useful ideas from the noise. This team had to work under intense time pressure. As much as possible, they wanted to eliminate noise from their contributions. We therefore developed a brainstorming technique called comparative brainstorming that encouraged focused, high-quality responses. With comparative brainstorming, before the team generates ideas, it develops a list of criteria for judging the idea quality. The leader then asks each member to contribute the best idea of which they can conceive. All subsequent contributions come in response to directive prompts from the leader. The participants must only contribute new ideas that are better than the existing ideas along some dimension.

For example, J5 Plans wanted to identify enemy COAs that were either very likely or very unlikely, and that were very dangerous. Eight officers used an electronic brainstorming tool to respond to a series of comparative prompts, such as:

Now give me a course of action that would be more dangerous than any you see on the screen in front of you.

What could they do that would be more surprising than anything proposed so far?

Now give me something that would be more likely than the ideas we already have.

Using these cues, the participants generated fifty-six courses of action in seven minutes. They moved the list categorizer tool where they could click and drag ideas from the list into “buckets” bearing the labels Most Likely, Least Likely, and Most Dangerous. Participants worked in parallel to drag COAs into the buckets. Categorization required six minutes. The participants reviewed the contents of each bucket, then prioritized the ideas in the most likely and most dangerous buckets. Participants were ready to report to the commander in under half an hour. The task typically took three times that long with standard means. Participants reported that, even though they spent one-third the time on the task, they had explored the problem and solution spaces in far more depth and detail than had been possible by conventional means.

## Joint Experiment ZULU

Up to this point, the champion and the GSS team had instigated most of the GSS interactions. In October 1996, the ship got underway for Joint Experiment ZULU, a ten-day-long test of new joint battle concepts in cooperation with the Marine Corps. During this under-way period, Third Fleet staff members began to request the GSS without prompting.

Just as the CORONADO got under way for Joint Experiment ZULU, Third Fleet received a request from CINCPACFLT to solicit input and recommendations from enlisted crew members about the quality of life aboard the ship. The chief of staff directed that the GSS be used to execute the task. Crew members came to the CMOC as their schedules allowed over the next day. They contributed their comments anonymously using a group outline tool. Their comments, some of which were positive, some of which were both negative and direct, were forwarded unfiltered to the chief of staff, who, in an unprecedented move, sent them on unfiltered to CINCPACFLT, a four-star admiral. $^{3}$

## Joint Exercise X-RAY

Following ZULU, there was a notable pause in the diffusion process. J6 finished his tour with Third Fleet and left the ship. The new J6 did not take on the role of champion. By this time, however, the transition team had become an accepted fixture in the fleet, so they were able to assume the role of internal champion. When the ship was in port between exercises, there was no use of GSS, in part because the ship's spaces were still under construction. In June 1997, the ship got underway again for Joint Exercise X-RAY, the final exercise before an extended overseas deployment. The two-wekk exercise was conducted to prepare a battle group for overseas deployment.

During X-RAY, a group that seemed poised to become self-sustaining instead stopped using the technology and then disbanded. The fleet staff created a joint planning group (JPG) with representatives from all the J codes. Their task was to develop possible courses of action for a seventy-two-hour time horizon. Pilot tests before the exercise suggested that the group had a good concept of operations for using GSS, and the members were enthusiastic.

The JPG was to meet in the CMOC three times a day for fourteen days. However, they abandoned the use of the GSS almost as soon as the exercise started. Several factors appeared to have contributed. First, subtle differences in the way the tools are configured and used can cause large differences in group dynamics. It is therefore vital intentionally to choose the desired group dynamics and use the tools accordingly $[2]$ . Most experienced GSS users prefer to plan a group process well in advance of the event. During the premeeting planning, it is important to identify clearly specific deliverables for the meeting, and explicitly to design the group dynamics that will allow the group to achieve those deliverables. Before the exercise, transition team and J5 held several intense planning sessions where they laid out a structured process for the group to follow. Pilot tests suggested that the JPG could cut their task time by two-thirds by using the GSS.

Unfortunately, just as the first JPG meeting started, leadership shifted to a new person who had not participated in the preexercise planning. The new leader had been an open supporter of the GSS project but did not know the deliverable planned for the meeting and did not know the process by which the deliverable was to be achieved. To further exacerbate matters, the new leader had other intensely challenging duties that caused him a high cognitive load for sixteen to eighteen hours a day.

Before each of the first three JPG meetings, a transition team member offered to explain the premeeting plan to the new leader. However, the felt he had a general sense of the group's task, and time was short. Without fully understanding the risks, the new leader began the first three meetings without a plan. At two points during the first meeting, he asked the group to address questions on-line. Because the questions were not carefully designed, the responses that came in were at a different level of abstraction than was useful for the task at hand. The new leader concluded aloud that the technology caused people to give far too much detail, and he abandoned the technology.

The GSS team feared the early difficulties might turn the JPG against GSS altogether. However, twice during the exercise they asked the transition team to run an ad-hoc GSS course of action development process for them. It was a far cry from the expected three times per day for fourteen days, but it was encouraging nonetheless. By the eleventh day of the exercise, the JPG stopped meeting altogether, in part because the leader was truly too busy to attend.

This event may be framed as an instance of cognitive costs relating to concept difficulty. The leader could not focus on learning what the tool was supposed to do for him because he was overloaded by other tasks. He was therefore reluctant to change from the standard processes he knew.

## Deployment to the Western Pacific

Four weeks after X-RAY, in August 1997, the CORONADO was to leave for a three-month deployment to Hawaii, Japan, Korea, Vancouver, British Columbia, Seattle, and Monterey, California. Many of the Third Fleet staff took leave before the deployment, and the rest worked long hours to prepare the ship, so no GSS activity took place during that month. Workers tore the CMOC network and video systems down to their component parts and rebuilt them from scratch to correct difficulties that had cropped up during the exercises.

When the deployment began, the transition team expected to run a few meetings per week and hoped to train several Third Fleet staff members as facilitators. Only one member of the transition team rode deployed with the ship. He was overwhelmed by an onslaught of demand for GSS and the CMOC almost as soon as the ship weighed anchor. Each J code designated a GSS facilitator and sent them to the CMOC for eight weeks of training. In addition, there were no fewer than three meetings a day in the CMOC with participants from all parts of the staff wrestling with an endless variety of tasks. The CMOC was in use from about 0730 to 2200 daily.

This dramatic increase may have occurred in part because this was the first time since the project began that the workload decreased from its heretofore grueling pace. Until this deployment, the staff was fully consumed with the dual tasks of refitting the ship and conducting scheduled exercises. With deployment, there were several weeks in transit with no construction and no exercises to run. People felt they had time to begin to catch up on their work, and they explored the possibility that the CMOC and its GSS might help.

The training sessions exposed staff members to the philosophy of facilitation, hands-on use of collaboration technology, meeting planning techniques, GSS software usage, and the experience of using collaborative technology in an actual meeting. It appeared that critical mass had been achieved at last.

## The Unexpected Punch Line: No Self-Sustaining Community of CMOC Users

Despite its apparent success, a self-sustaining community of GSS users did not coalesce around the CMOC. Quite the opposite: After a whirlwind year of continuous use, if a member of the transition team were not available to plan and execute the GSS task, the GSS still got no use. None of the officers who had been trained to use the CMOC and the GSS became an active facilitator; all continued to call on members of the transition team to run GSS-supported projects.

Interviews with the staff revealed that perceived usefulness was high, but perceived costs were also high, particularly perceived technical difficulty and perceived concept difficulty. The perceived technical difficulty arose not from the GSS, but from all the gadgets in the CMOC. The devices were controlled by an array of buttons and switches, levers and blinking lights. People who did not use the room daily quickly forgot which buttons controlled which devices. Even the room designers had difficulty if they did not use the space for a week or two. Subsequent spaces were designed with simpler control systems.

Staff members also reported concept difficulty with respect to the GSS. Their training had focused on a rich array of group dynamics that could be created by subtle manipulations of GSS features. With little experience, they were dismayed by the possibility of accidentally and publicly creating a group-dynamics disaster. Subsequent training efforts focused on a few simple, useful, and predictable processes as a starting point. The trainers also began to socialize the trainees to expect and accept the need to restart a process if it seemed to be going astray. They pointed out that there was very little cost to a GSS misstep. If the group started contributing too much detail, or too little substance, or misunderstood the question, the leader would know it almost immediately. Within moments, the leader could reframe the question and restart the group. The loss of a few minutes was minor compared with the loss of hours that often accompanied standard meetings.

Another difficulty related to the intervals between GSS exposures, which continued to be long. Although the staff was using the CMOC nearly continuously, its use was spread across a number of subgroups. A particular subgroup would only use the GSS every few days or weeks. In the interval between uses, users would forget the details of how to operate the CMOC and the GSS. They would therefore seek out the transition team to support each new task.

It was several months before a group emerged that needed to use the GSS daily. The J2 intelligence group wanted to use it as a collaborative logbook. The course of the J2 GSS transition, described in the next section, was different from that for the rest of the fleet.

## J2 Intelligence: A Rapid and Complete GSS Transition

THE J2 INTELLIGENCE COMMUNITY ABOARD THE SHIP was a cohesive unit with a good-natured camaraderie. They were well liked by the other staff members, but they were set apart because they worked in top-secret classified spaces where most others could not go.

J2 operated as a geographically distributed team because they were scattered among six small, noisy spaces across several different decks of the ship (figure 6).

Their group consisted of three key components: cryptologists, analysts, and watch officers. The cryptologists monitored a variety of information sources. If something of significance occurred, they were supposed to make an entry in a paper logbook. However, cryptologists were evaluated, in part, on the neatness of their logbooks. Therefore, as events unfolded, the cryptologists would retrieve paper from the trashcan and jot log entries on them. Later, when they had time, and if they could find their notes, they would transfer the entries neatly to the log.

The analysts would circulate among the cryptology spaces to read the logbooks and talk with the cryptologists. Their task was to interpret the raw data. For, example, an analyst might conclude that a particular radar signature indicated the presence of an enemy submarine. The analysts would record their findings in their own logbooks.

The watch officers would circulate among the analysts to read their logbooks and synthesize a situational awareness. For example, a watch officer might conclude that the presence of a submarine, combined with a massing of troops along a border, indicated an attack was eminent. The watch officer would keep the JTF commander and the battle staff abreast of the emerging situation.

The senior intelligence watch officers found it challenging to maintain an up-to-the-minute awareness of the battle situation with staff members spread across the ship.

A new intelligence watch officer arrived at Third Fleet just as the exercise began. He did not know about the collaborative logbook and received no training. He reported that, when he took the watch at midnight, he found the unfamiliar laptop on his desk running the unfamiliar program. Its purpose was clear from the log entries, so he simply began using it.

A senior chief on the J2 staff volunteered to provide technical support for the system and received three hours of formal training. After that, he supported the J2 team without further intervention by the transition team except once to help him to recover from a network crash.

At the end of the first exercise, the J2 ACOS declared publicly and repeatedly that the collaborative logbook was the single most important tool to come into the intelligence spaces during his career. Shortly afterwards, he received a new assignment away from the fleet. His replacement chose to continue using the collaborative logbook. She asked that the system be transferred from its stand-alone laptop LAN to the PCs attached to the ship's backbone. The role of the collaborative log expanded quickly from its initial simple record of events and analyses. It became a forum for assigning and accepting tasks, for requesting information proactively, and tracking important administrative and operational issues and for maintaining a sense of group cohesion through banter and discussion. The transition was complete: elapsed time, three months.

Several factors appeared to contribute to the rapid transition of GSS in the J2 community. The benefits were obvious, substantial, and immediate: no more running up and down ladders, and a dramatically reduced situational awareness cycle. The cognitive costs were low, particularly for perceived technical difficulty and perceived concept difficulty. Users had only to click an icon to run the system. They were using the tool for an easy-to-understand task, keeping a logbook, not the conceptually difficult task of managing group dynamics. Further, the users needed the technology minute-to-minute, all day long rather than once every few days or weeks. Finally, the collaborative log provided a forum for increased social interaction among the J2 staff, which some reported as a perceived benefit.

When the CORONADO's Joint Operations Center (JOC, pronounced jock) became operational, the collaborative logbooks diffused quickly from J2 into other parts of the fleet staff. This, in turn led to and adoption of GSS by the fleet. The following section describes the JOC and the events leading up to adoption.

## The Joint Operations Center

The Joint Operations Center (JOC) became the catalyst for the diffusion of GSS throughout the CORONADO. The JOC was designed to be the nerve center for the JTF. The CJTF needs a centralized clearinghouse for all the information that comes from the staff, the national command structure, and the component commanders. At any time, the CJTF can enter the JOC to get a briefing from the battle watch captain (BWC) about the status of any aspect of an emerging crisis. This enables the CJTF quickly to make informed decisions about mission-critical events and resources. The BWC acts on behalf of the CJTF to prosecute the crises when the CJTF is away from the JOC.

The JOC is equipped with two rows of workstations facing the forward wall. The front row has eight workstations that are used by tactical specialists to track the positions of friendly and enemy sea, air, and land forces (figure 7).

Most modern command centers have such tactical displays. However, the six workstations, or anchor desks, in the back row of the JOC are the first of their type among military command centers (figure 8). Experienced, battle-qualified officers with authority over key resources and processes stand watches at the anchor desks. Each desk attends to a different set of issues—C4ISR, Logistics, Intelligence, Weather, Undersea Warfare, and Plans. The desks are linked via collaborative technology to staff in other parts of the command ship and across the fleet. This makes the anchor an agent for obtaining and filtering information to be fed to the battle watch captain.

The anchors gather information from their staffs and from on-line resources such as the SIPRNET, a secret intranet maintained by the military. Because the anchors are in the JOC, each maintains a fairly complete battle picture as the operation unfolds. They are therefore able to anticipate the commander's information needs and to direct the efforts of staff not present in the JOC.

## Adoption

NAVY PERSONNEL HAVE A SAYING: "If the boss is interested, I'm excited." In a morning staff meeting during Operation Pax, the admiral asked a senior officer whether the courses of action being briefed had been generated using the GSS in the CMOC. The officer replied, "No Sir." Said the admiral, "I'd like to know why not? We have a fine capability there and we ought to be using it to our best advantage." The transition team marked this event as the moment when GSS was formally adopted by the fleet.

A short time later a Special Operations Forces (SOF) officer broke into the CMOC with a small group and commandeered it to write an operations order. A transition team member found them struggling with the system and gave them about five minutes of assistance, after which they were able to finish on their own. The SOF officer had previously been something of a vocal technophobe. A few days later he returned to conduct another short collaborative writing project.

A liaison officer from another branch of the military who had been negative about GSS from the start of the project asked to have a collaborative log established for the Coalition Exercise Control Group (CECG, pronounced keg). The CECG was the team that controlled the progress of the wargaming exercise in which the fleet was participating. They fed information to the forces on both sides of the wargame to ensure that all of the scheduled training events would take place as the scenario played out. The CECG leader objected to the small amount of visual real estate available on a computer screen. He preferred wall-sized white boards and rolls of butcher paper. More important, though, he objected to an overdependence on technology. He asserted that war was a messy, chaotic, brutal business, and that people had to be ready to win without technology, because technology would fail when the bullets started flying. When he asked for the CECG log, he said, "I still don't like it, but I need it." After the exercise, the liaison officer reported that he found COMMAND-NET useful, and he suggested several improvements. He reported that he was looking forward to beta-testing the next generation of software.

As Pax ramped up, several more groups asked the transition team to assist with collaborative decision making and planning activities. Examples of those activities are described in the next section.

## Postadoption Diffusion

DURING PAX, THE CMOC WAS IN REGULAR USE. At this point all collaborative activities were run at the request of the users; the technology transition team no longer had to sell each session.

## Current Operations Team

All the officers who had been part of the ill-fated JPG had since finished their tours of duty and left Third Fleet; with them went the institutional perception of technology gone wrong. The fleet established a new Current Operations (COPS) team that met every day. COPS had members from all participants of the exercise including the single-digit "C" codes: special forces, undersea warfare, coalition forces air command and control, public affairs, battle watch captains, helicopter scheduling, ship's company, Army ground forces, Marine ground forces, coalition exercise control group, and Korean, Australian, Japanese, Canadian, Chilean, and Mexican officers.

The COPS team was charged to swiftly develop and execute solutions to significant real-life issues that emerged during the exercise. For example, when a hurricane threatened to move into the exercise area, the team quickly developed action lists and contingencies for dealing with the possible consequences of the storm. In the course of the exercise, the team developed and refined a GSS method for rapidly developing and thoroughly examining possible courses of action (COAs). COPS typically sent eight people to the CMOC for such an activity:

1. An operations specialist,

2. A logistics expert,

3. A legal representation,

4. One or more air specialist,

5. One or more ground warfighters, and

6. One or more planning experts.

The process began with a review of the commander's intent, the rules of engagement, and the current situation. Then each participant contributed one or two potential COAs to the group via the GSS. Once the list was established, the group used oral discussion to merge, refine, and synthesize the COAs down to a list of six. Next they used the GSS to generate a list of criteria for selecting among the COAs. They also refined and reduced this list orally. The team then went on-line simultaneously to develop and argue a set of pros and cons for each COA. Finally, the COAs with their supporting arguments were moved to a multicriteria voting tool with which each participant rated each COA against each criteria. The polling tool automatically ranked the COAs in order from the highest rated to the lowest rated. The team examined the results to see if they made sense. When they were satisfied, they rolled the prioritized COAs with their pro-and-con arguments into a briefing for the admiral.

Without the GSS, the team typically spent about an hour and a half developing three COAs. One of those three would always be a throwaway “do nothing” option; another would be a carefully considered option, and the third would be a less-developed, often less-useful option. Using the GSS, the team created six options, considered their pros and cons, and rank-ordered them from most useful to least useful, all in half an hour. The head of the fleet planning department declared at a morning meeting that the technology was invaluable.

## COPS Team Writing

Toward the end of Pax, the COPS used the CMOC to compose a comprehensive document about the lessons learned during the exercise. A ten-person multinational subteam used the group outline tool to create eleven major headings for the document. Then, in approximately forty-five minutes, they recorded and organized 142 lessons learned. For half an hour they orally discussed any items in the document about which they had questions or comments. Then they presented the results to the admiral. In seventy-five minutes the team completed a task on which they expected to spend a day and a half.

The fleet's J5 Planning group created a less-involved COA development method for use by their planners. The J4 Logistics group used the CMOC and the GSS to make plans for taking the ship into the shipyard for repairs and maintenance. Coalition forces used the CMOC for a variety of problem-solving and planning tasks. In each case, the participants reported dramatic reductions of time on task and substantial improvements in product quality.

## State of the Project

THE COMMAND-NET INSTALLATIONS ARE CLEARLY SELF-SUSTAINING. The intelligence and battle watch logs serve as ready examples for each new component commander who comes aboard. New logs stand up, then disappear as the demands of each exercise require.

Is the CMOC transition complete? It is hard to say. Fleet personnel now regard the CMOC and its technology as part of their standard work practices. And yet, they still depend heavily on the transition team to support them with the technology rather than supporting it for themselves. From the point of view of the staff, the transition is complete. They openly refer to the transition team as members of the Third Fleet staff. After three years, the transition team has been aboard the ship longer than most members of the staff.

However, in order to declare the project a success, the transition team must pull out and leave behind a self-sustaining community of users. The multimedia display technology and the networks in the CMOC are still too complex and too unstable to be left without the ministrations of an experienced technologist. To date, the transition team has not been able to find or train such a person from the fleet staff.

The next section compares the COMMAND-NET transition with the CMOC transition in light of TTM to develop a list of lessons learned from the investigation. These lessons may be useful guidelines for future GSS transition projects.

## Comparing COMMAND-NET and CMOC in Light of TTM

HAVING DERIVED TTM FROM THE CRITICAL INCIDENTS OF THIS STUDY and others, we find it useful to reflect on the experience in light of the model. This section compares the COMMAND-NET and CMOC transitions in order to catalog the lessons we learned about GSS transition. In 1996, Nunamaker et al. [21] published an exposition of fifty-three lessons learned about GSS from experience in the field and the lab. Events in this investigation were consistent with most of those lessons. The investigation provided useful refinements for a few of those lessons and provided insights for a few new lessons.

## Perceived Frequency of Net Value

Lesson 1.0. Infrequent ad-hoc use hinders self-sustenance.

TTM posits that acceptance will depend in part on the frequency with which prospective users expect to derive value from a new technology. The COMMAND-NET users perceived benefits from hour to hour and moment to moment, while individual CMOC users typically needed the technology once or twice a month. Sometimes embarked guests would need and use the CMOC daily, but when they debarked at the end of an exercise they took their experience and acceptance with them. They left behind the stories of their exposure but did not leave a self-sustaining community of users.

Lesson 1.1. If possible, find a specific, repeated process to support with GSS.

The Third Fleet intelligence community had a clearly defined situational awareness process that cycled many times per day. CMOC users did not. Experience at other sites has shown similar transition successes for teams working on specific repeated processes. For example, a company in the Netherlands uses the technology to support routine inspections of computer code $[29]$ . The Air Force instituted a GSS-supported strategic planning process that is now self-sustaining $[3]$ . IBM Corporation established a total quality (TQ) process that quickly became self-sustaining $[17]$ . That success led to the establishment of 165 sites around the world to support ad-hoc problem solving rather than the more predictable, more structured TQ process. However, after ten years, only one-third of the sites for ad-hoc problem solving remain, while the TQ sites are still self-sustaining.

Lesson 1.2. If you want to do ad-hoc problem solving, find the resources for GSS specialists to support it.

The CMOC was designed for ad-hoc problem solving, a critical function for the fleet. However, because the CMOC serves the needs of many subgroups, no one subgroup has the resources to provide a full-time specialist to support it. The transition team has provided long-term support for fleet problem solvers. This created a permanent community of users, but they were not yet self-sustaining. The transition team must now focus on replacing themselves with personnel from within the fleet. It may be that GSS technologists will have to create intelligent agents to guide ad-hoc users on developing group processes in order to reduce the need for GSS specialist.

## Perceived Cognitive Value

Lesson 2.0. Keep it simple.

GSS transition is complicated by the fact that it is designed to produce both visible and invisible products. The visible products of a GSS are fairly straightforward shared objects—lists, trees, discussions, documents, and graphics. Conceptual cognitive load is low for understanding these objects. These visible objects are only remarkable in that everybody in the group, regardless of physical location, can contribute to the same objects at the same time. The COMMAND-NET users required only the visible products of the GSS. They reported very low conceptual load for their GSS use. They also reported low access loads because they used their own machines, and low technical difficulty because they had no extra multimedia equipment with which to wrestle.

The invisible products of a GSS may be even more important than the visible objects, but they impose higher conceptual cognitive loads—it may be more difficult to think about creating and maintaining group dynamics than about creating a list or an outline. Subtle differences in the way the GSS is configured and in the way users are directed can create large and useful differences in group dynamics $[26]$ . The concepts of operation for GSS are as diverse as the needs of the groups that use them.

CMOC users typically needed both the visible and invisible products of the GSS. Sometimes they had to diverge, searching further and further afield for new ideas. Other times they needed to converge quickly on key issues. Sometimes they needed to push for breadth, other times they needed to push for depth and detail. Sometimes the groups had to move from vague generalities into the details, other times they needed to move “up from the weeds” to achieve a unified big-picture understanding. Depending on how the GSS is configured, it is possible to create all of these dynamics, and many others. CMOC users reported that they found it challenging to understand and control the nuances of the GSS to create and maintain the group dynamics they needed. Indeed, they found it difficult to know which dynamics they needed, much less how to create them. This was a particular problem since they did not use the GSS regularly. It appears that the conceptual variety and complexity of the CMOC tasks may have caused a spillover perception that the software itself was technically difficult to use.

## Lesson 2.1. Err on the side of simplicity when equipping collaborative workspaces.

We found that, when the hardware was too hard to use, people would not use the software either. Furthermore, It may also be possible that technical difficulties with CMOC equipment led to increased perceptions of GSS technical difficulty. While the space was equipped with every modern convenience, its multilevel switching scheme made it hard to use, which was cited by a number of users as a reason for not becoming self-sustaining.

## Lesson 2.3. The premeeting plan is vital, but don't go overboard.

It is accepted as given among experienced GSS users that a preactivity plan is vital to the success of a GSS activity. This truth has been called GSS Law Zero. Said one expert, “With a GSS you’re going to get some dynamic. You’d better make sure it’s the one you need.”

Many users can recite stories about activities gone quickly awry due to vague planning. This lesson continued to hold at Third Fleet. Early in the project, the intensely busy fleet users were reluctant to conduct preplanning sessions. It seemed time-consuming and burdensome to some who were not yet familiar with the benefits they could derive from the system. To overcome this, the transition team focused the training heavily on the importance of the preplan, and on the group-dynamics disasters that could result from bad plans. In response, the prospective users became gun-shy. Perhaps it was better to let the transition team run the tools than to risk public disaster.

In fact, very few GSS activities result in disaster. Rather, a vaguely planned activity typically leads to a few minutes of confusion and lost effort. It quickly becomes clear when a group is headed the wrong direction, and the leader can quickly redirect the group and restart an activity. In subsequent training, the transition team made sure to focus on the corollary to Law Zero: No matter how well you plan, you'll have to change your plan on the fly; so be it; relax.

## Lesson 2.4. People don't always notice the benefits they derive.

Airline passengers may complain about a long, uneventful flight, not remembering that the same trip might have taken their ancestors three months instead of a day. In like manner, during this project, GSS users did not always notice the benefits they were deriving. When asked how the GSS was working out, they would sometimes respond with something like, “It’s okay, I guess.” When the GSS team asked whether they could have accomplished the same about of work without the GSS, the users would become aware of and sometimes excited about the value they had derived. The GSS team began explicitly to call people’s attention to the benefits of every interaction they supported, which seemed to increase the number of GSS war stories being told in the mess. This may, in turn, have improved the speed of GSS transition.

## Lesson 2.5. The nontypist is dead, long live the typist.

It was not necessary to simplify the technology to the point of eliminating the keyboard. Since the infancy of GSS, people have raised concerns that senior managers may be unwilling to type. During this study, a number of officers and civilians again raised the issue by asking whether the admiral and captains would be willing to type. On two occasions prospective users became adamant on the issue and urged that voice recognition be added to the system immediately. Throughout the project, more than one hundred admirals, generals, captains, and colonels used the technology. Not one expressed any reservations about using a computer keyboard.

## Lesson 3.0. Sometimes usefulness takes a backseat to other considerations.

For COMMAND-NET users, who primarily needed the visible products of the GSS, the utility of the technology clearly drove their acceptance. For CMOC users, who needed the more socially and politically sensitive invisible products, utility remained a primary concern. However, as noted earlier, usefulness was not the only consideration. Social, economic, political, physical, and cognitive considerations that had little to do with utility were often substantial acceptance issues. It behooves one who studies or practices GSS transition to attend to these issues.

## Lesson 3.1. Usefulness varies with time pressure.

Earlier field investigations have reported that, under some circumstances, GSS usage can reduce labor costs by 50 percent and project cycle times by 90 percent (e.g., [11, 17, 25]). We found that, when teams worked to solve problems under time pressure, their project cycle times typically decreased by two-thirds, rather than by 90 percent. It appeared that they had already compressed their work cycles significantly before the GSS became available, so there was less slack to be taken out of their processes.

## Directions for Future Research

THIS PAPER HAS DESCRIBED THE DERIVATION OF TTM from action research in the field. The model may help explain technology acceptance among groups of users in groups and organizations. It may be useful for increasing the speed and reducing the risk of technology transition. However, much work remains to be done before TTM can be judged useful.

## Measurement Issues

While TTM was derived using qualitative methods, it must be tested with rigorous quantitative methods. The next important step for this model must be to develop and validate measures for the crucial causal relationships proposed by the model. Davis [8] has already developed and validated measures of perceived usefulness. However, more general measures of perceived magnitude of net value and more specific measures of the other dimensions of perceived net value have yet to be derived. Further, measures of certainty, perceived frequency of net value, and perceived net value of transition must be developed and tested.

## Testing the Model

TTM must be tested empirically with experiments in the lab and with field studies to see whether new data support the key relationships it proposes. Until it has been tested in a variety of settings and a variety of technologies, it must be used with caution.

## Integrating TTM with Other Models

Several authors have framed the value of GSS in terms of process losses and process gains $[5, 12, 13, 15, 16, 22, 24]$ . Process losses are factors that inhibit group productivity, including but not limited to attention blocking, distraction, and hidden agendas. Process gains are factors that enhance group performance. For example, GSS allows for parallel communication and anonymity, both of which produce demonstrable benefits for some group processes $[22]$ . It may well be that process gains and losses can be mapped into the dimensions of perceived net value. TTM may offer causal explanations for how perceptions of process gains and losses will affect transition. The earlier works, in turn, may inform perceived net value, resulting in a richer transition model.

## Moving to an Organizational Unit of Analysis

Like TAM, TTM is a model of individual technology acceptance. However, TTM extends the individual into a group or organizational setting. For example, political and social considerations only emerge in multiple-person environments. As the model stands, one could only predict and explain organizational acceptance as an aggregate of individual acceptance. Future research may give rise to a model with a group or organizational unit of analysis. Until that time, TTM may prove a useful tool for researchers and practitioners who wish to study and engage in technology transition.

## Conclusions

This study was instrumental in reshaping our thinking about how to build a self-sustaining and growing community of GSS users. It was particularly intriguing to discover that ad-hoc problem solving with GSS hinders the transition process. This suggests that one could focus on identifying and supporting repeatable processes early in the transition process. One might choose to focus on ad-hoc problems only after a community has been established. The Technology Transition Model that emerged from this study may also be useful for technologies other than GSS.
