# The Creative Process: The Effects of Group Memory on Individual Idea Generation

JOHN W. SATZINGER, MONICA J. GARFIELD, AND MURLI NAGASUNDARAM

JOHN W. SATZINGER is an Associate Professor in the CIS Department of Southwest Missouri State University. He holds a Ph.D. in MIS from the Claremont Graduate University and was an Assistant Professor of MIS at the University of Georgia from 1991 to 1996. Dr. Satzinger has published articles on user interface design, group support systems, and end-user training in journals such as Journal of Management Information Systems, Database, Accounting, Management, and Information Technologies, and Information Systems Management. He is also coauthor of The Object-Oriented Approach.

MONICA J. GARFIELD is a Ph.D. student in the Department of Management at the University of Georgia. She has a bachelor's degree in cognitive science from Vassar College, and an M.B.A. and a masters of science in MIS from Boston University. She has published articles in Information Systems Research, Journal of Strategic Information Systems, and Information Systems Management. Her current research interests focus on knowledge creation at both the macro (state and national infrastructure and telemedicine policy) and micro (individual idea generation and creative problem solving) levels.

MURLI NAGASUNDARAM is Assistant Professor of Computer Information Systems in the College of Business at Boise State University. He has a bachelor's degree in engineering from the University of Madras, an M.B.A. from the Indian Institute of Management, Calcutta, and a Ph.D. in MIS from the University of Georgia. He has several years of managerial and entrepreneurial experience in the information technology and services industry. He helped found Collaborative Technologies Corporation and designed a group support system called VisionQuest. His research and teaching interests include group support systems, creativity and creative problem solving, organizational teamwork and collaboration, and telecommunications and the Internet.

ABSTRACT: To compete in today's rapidly paced business environment, many companies are increasingly relying on their employees to generate creative solutions to business problems. Specifically, through the use of idea-generation techniques, business owners hope to promote creative idea generation by their employees. Several leading corporations (and, concomitantly, researchers) have become interested in the use of group support systems (GSS) to enhance idea generation. A key component of the GSS-based idea-generation process is group memory, a repository of ideas created by users of GSS. Group memory provides stimuli, in the form of ideas, to the individuals using the software. This study seeks to better understand the manner in which the contents of the group memory influence the type of ideas generated by individuals in the group. We report the results of a laboratory experiment that investigated whether the stimuli contained within a GSS group memory (i.e., ideas) systematically influenced the extent of paradigm shift represented in the ideas generated by individuals. A GSS simulator designed for this study was used to allow for the manipulation of the stimuli in the group memory. The results suggest that individuals tend to generate ideas that match the paradigm-relatedness of ideas provided to them as stimulation.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: brainstorming, creativity, group memory, group support systems, idea generation.

IN A MARKETPLACE TEEMING WITH "ME-TOO" PRODUCTS AND SERVICES, businesses are constantly searching for a means to differentiate themselves from their competition. To do this, they frequently need to carve out new and creative paths $[10]$ . Many do this by tapping into the creativity of their employees $[31]$ . Creative idea-generation techniques are an important means of harnessing employee creativity. Often, companies look for ideas of a particular nature in these sessions. For instance, a company implementing a Total Quality Management (TQM) program is usually seeking incremental change to support continuous improvement, whereas a company that is reengineering its business processes may want its employees to step outside the current paradigm to explore new ways to approach its current business problems.

In addition to creative idea-generation techniques, organizations also have group support systems (GSS) available to them to assist in the idea-generation process. Considerable evidence exists showing that the use of GSS for idea generation results in an increase in both the quantity and quality of ideas generated $[12, 18, 20, 45]$ . Other than quality (defined in a number of ways) and quantity of ideas, however, there is little research that has investigated other attributes of ideas generated. For instance, few researchers (e.g., $[22, 34]$ ) have considered whether the idea-generation processes can be used to influence the type of ideas generated or to produce different degrees of paradigmatic shift in thinking as represented in the ideas generated. These ideas can be categorized in terms of being: (1) paradigm preserving (PP)—how well they retain the current paradigm (framework) presented in the business problem, or (2) paradigm modifying (PM)—how much they reflect a fundamental change in the underlying paradigm (framework) of the existing problem. Ideas that reflect paradigm shifts in thought (e.g., PM ideas) are those that break away from the standard way of perceiving the problem at hand.

There are a number of factors that could play a role in paradigmatic shifts in thinking during idea generation, one of which is by providing external stimuli [34]. $^{1}$ A key source of external stimulation during a GSS session is the group memory, the growing repository of ideas generated by participants that is displayed on each participant's screen. Group memory allows participants to review each other's ideas during the idea-generation process; these ideas may stimulate the production of more or of different types of ideas. Despite the potentially key role played by group memory in idea generation, the manner in which its contents influence the type of ideas generated by participants is presently unknown. Therefore, we conducted a laboratory experiment to determine whether the contents of group memory systematically influenced the paradigm shift represented in the ideas generated by participants. For the purposes of the study, a GSS simulator was designed to allow for the manipulation of the group memory contents.

## Research Framework

RESEARCH IN THE INTERDISCIPLINARY FIELD OF CREATIVITY suggests that there are four key factors, known as the Four P's—product, person, process, and press, involved in the execution of creative acts (figure 1). The creative product is the “thing” or object that draws praise or appreciation. It can be an idea, a new design, a strategy, or a plan. The creative person or persons are responsible for bringing the creative product into being, whether directly (e.g., by formulating a design or idea) or indirectly (e.g., managers and supervisors supporting and encouraging subordinates who are more directly responsible for the creative product). Creative processes are procedures or methods used by individuals and groups to bring creative ideas to life. There are many different processes that have been used to stimulate the generation of creative ideas. For instance, one may use the power of analogies or metaphors to aid in the generation of ideas to solve a problem (see [9] for a listing of twenty-two techniques). The creative press or environment is the context in which creative ideas are produced and explored. The press may include such environmental factors as evaluation, surveillance, competition, and restricted choice [1]. It is widely accepted that each of the Four P's influences each other [17, 40]. The creative process is comprised of structures (for instance, different creativity techniques) that influence the person, who in turn has an impact on the creative product (idea) generated [34].

This study focuses on the impact of creative processes on the product (in this case, the specific ideas generated). We hold constant the creative environment and use randomization in an attempt to reduce the impact of individual differences. We examine how the creative process of idea generation in the context of GSS influences the ideas that are generated by individuals. A key component of a GSS is the group memory containing ideas entered by participants during an idea-generation session. We asked the question:

## How do the contents of group memory in a GSS stimulate or influence the ideas generated by individuals in an idea generation session?

There are many ways of viewing the creativity of a product or idea. They can be measured by their degree of originality, feasibility $[32, 43]$ , or fitness $[5]$ . Ideas and other creative products can also be classified in a way that highlights the differences between them without making a judgment about the extent to which they are creative. This allows for different types of ideas to be equally creative yet different in form. One such classification scheme reflects how closely an idea conforms to the overriding paradigm of the problem or situation presented $[34]$ . In this classification scheme, ideas are classified on a continuum based on the amount they alter the current paradigm. Those ideas that do not alter the current paradigm or underlying assumptions of a problem are considered paradigm preserving (PP), those that do alter the current paradigm are considered paradigm modifying (PM) (figure 2). Because an idea can alter the underlying paradigm and assumptions of a presented problem to varying degrees, this scheme is a continuum.

Ideas that are PP tend to be directly related to the problem and its prevailing paradigm. In other words, they work within the underlying assumptions of the problem. By contrast, PM ideas tend to be more tangential to the original problem, and they often do not support common underlying assumptions of the presented problem. For example, if the problem being solved is how to use excess capacity of a teabag machine, a PP solution would not attempt to alter the underlying framework of the question (i.e., a machine that makes teabags needs to be used more). An example of a PP solution would be to put coffee in the teabags. A PM solution may alter the framework by considering how to increase demand of the teabags, so one possible solution would be to market bathing suits made out of teabag material, thus using the material produced by a teabag machine, but in a different form.

This PM and PP continuum allows us to modify our research question more pointedly as:

Do the contents of group memory in a GSS stimulate or influence the paradigm relatedness of ideas generated by individuals in an idea-generation session?

## Creativity and GSS

To answer the research question, creativity and GSS research must be integrated. A substantial portion of GSS research has focused on the idea-generation task (for some exceptions, see [41]). Brainstorming, GSS, and group memory as they apply to creativity research are reviewed next.

## Brainstorming

Brainstorming [38] is a common idea-generation technique that is used by a wide range of corporations. The general premise of brainstorming is to generate ideas without being constrained by the applicability of the idea. This technique can be performed by individuals acting alone or by groups of people. One of the general principles underlying brainstorming is that external stimuli for ideas will influence the potential ideas generated [23]. Group brainstorming provides a forum for group members to generate ideas and allows for each participant's ideas to be a stimulus for others [35]. The ideas generated by members of the group can be used by others to stimulate their own thinking and to produce new ideas. These brainstorming sessions can be conducted in many fashions, including manually, verbally, or electronically. Recently, group support systems (GSS) have become a popular tool for electronic brainstorming.

## Group Support Systems

Group-based information technologies such as group support systems are relatively new social technologies with a variety of features that have the potential to fundamentally change the nature of information exchange in group discussions $[15, 37]$ . From a theoretical perspective, electronic communication can provide three components that may significantly change information exchange: parallelism, anonymity, and group memory $[37]$ .

The impacts of parallelism $[19, 45]$ and anonymity $[8, 25]$ on group brainstorming have been well studied. Parallelism is the ability for all members to exchange information simultaneously. This allows a member of the group to “speak” (via input into a computer) when he or she has an idea without having to wait his or her turn to talk. For instance, if there were ten people in a verbal meeting that lasted sixty minutes, each person could speak for an average of six minutes (given the norms of most meetings wherein people do not try to outshout one another). Given the same constraints for an electronic meeting, however, it would be possible for each person to “speak” (or input ideas into a computer) for the full sixty minutes. Most studies have found that GSS groups using parallel brainstorming techniques produce more ideas, experience increased equality of participation, and report a higher level of satisfaction than groups that did not use GSS $[11, 18, 20, 44]$ .

With GSS, the identity of a group member can be better hidden than when ideas are generated verbally. Anonymity enables members to make contributions without attaching their names. Consequently, people may experience lower degrees of evaluation anxiety, since they need not worry about the group thinking poorly of them because of their ideas (the group will not know who entered each of the ideas). Empirical studies have found significant anonymity effects in field studies with groups that are highly critical of one another and have hierarchical relationships among members [37]. It has also been found that anonymity leads to increased equality of individual participation in groups [44].

## Group Memory

Unlike parallelism and anonymity, little research has been done on the impact of group memory on idea generation. Group memory is the electronic capture of the group's work, which is available for review by the group. Group memory records all remarks typed into the computer so that members can refer to them later in the discussion. It may be kept for future reference after the meeting(s) is over. All members of a group thus have a common, shared memory that can be used during or after the meeting. Having group memory accessible to group members also allows them to view a wide range of ideas suggested by all group members, thereby helping participants explore a larger share of the problem space $[13]$ .

In a GSS environment, the group memory provides much of the feedback and stimulation a user receives in the form of other group members' ideas. Frequently, the group memory is the only form of feedback or stimulation the user will receive during the brainstorming portion of a meeting. One of the underlying assumptions of the impact of group memory on group idea generation is that this feedback will have an impact on the ideas generated by an individual $[13]$ , which is also an assumption of the brainstorming technique discussed above $[38]$ . This assumption, though widely assumed in the GSS literature, has not been empirically tested with GSS. Those studies that have looked at the impact of feedback on an individual have been in the form of the tone of the feedback $[8]$ or the use of cognitive feedback to control the decision-making process and reach convergence among group members $[42]$ . The tone of feedback has been found to affect the number of ideas produced, the originality of the ideas produced, and the satisfaction level of the participants $[8]$ . The use of cognitive feedback has been found to improve the control over the decision-making process as group members' decision strategies converged $[42]$ .

## Impact of Group Memory on Idea Generation

The effects of the contents of group memory on the type or paradigm-relatedness of participant ideas is unstudied at this point. There are two theoretical underpinnings that may identify the potential impact of group memory on the type of idea an individual will generate: the cognitive processing of the individual and the social structures that affect that individual.

## Cognitive Processing

Humans share some basic cognitive structural characteristics [36]. Human cognitive processing is controlled by production rules—rules specifying the steps of cognition—that, when activated, produce ideas [2, 3]. Rules are activated automatically by input stimuli without conscious control [4]. A stimulus will activate a rule or set of rules, each of which has its own weight (i.e., likelihood of being activated), based on past experiences. Individuals tend to recall and cluster together items that have been encoded in similar ways [30]. As activation spreads through memory, rules related to the stimuli and to each other have the greatest strength and thus are most likely to be activated [14].

The cognitive processing of the human information system implies that external stimuli in the form of an idea will evoke the production of closely related ideas $[14]$ . Therefore, if an individual is exposed to a certain type of idea (PP or PM), he or she is more likely to generate that same type of idea.

## Social Interaction

Social factors may also influence the ideas that an individual generates in a group setting. As group members work together, they establish structures or traditions that constrain how they act by defining “normal” and “unacceptable” behavior $[21, 24]$ . These “habitual norms” or traditions $[21, 24]$ influence how members work together.

One norm typically found in group interaction is that of “not changing the subject” [27, p. 382]. Thus, members are motivated to focus on the same general topic, resulting in the production of similar ideas [29]. Furthermore, group members may follow piggybacking rules—building on the ideas of others [38]—thus producing more similar ideas. Therefore, due to the social norm of not changing the subject and the tendency of piggybacking ideas, individuals are apt to produce ideas similar to those to which they are exposed (i.e., PP or PM).

We are interested in understanding whether the specific type of stimuli an individual receives has an impact on the paradigm-relatedness (i.e., PP or PM) of the ideas generated. Cognitive processing and social interaction theories suggest that individuals will generate ideas similar to those to which they are exposed. Therefore we hypothesize that:

H1: Individuals exposed to paradigm-modifying (PM) ideas via group memory will generate more PM ideas than will individuals who are exposed to paradigm-preserving (PP) ideas.

H2: Individuals exposed to PP ideas via group memory will generate more PP ideas than will individuals who are exposed to PM ideas.

## Methodology

THE IMPACT OF GROUP MEMORY AS A SOURCE FOR STIMULI for brainstorming was explored through a laboratory experiment using a group simulator GSS.

## Participants

Participants included 185 junior and senior undergraduate business students, including ninety women and ninety-five men. All participants were enrolled in a core business course at a large state university and received course credit for their participation in the experiment.

## Session and Task

Participants self-selected their session and were randomly assigned to one of two treatment conditions (either receiving PM or PP ideas as stimulus). There were, on average, ten participants in each session, working alone at their own workstations. Session participants were told that they were part of a fictitious group of five participants. However, each session participant was the only real person in their group; the other four participants were simulated by the computer.

Sessions ran for an average of one hour (including introduction, brainstorming tasks such as warm-up and experimental tasks, switchover between the two tasks, and debriefing). Each session began with an introduction, followed by a warm-up task to allow participants to become familiar with the technology and technique. Then the next task (the experimental task used in $[8]$ and $[14]$ ) was introduced to the participants. This task asked individuals to generate responses to the experimental task question: What can be done to improve the parking situation on campus?

Ideas entered by the participants were added to the group memory, just as in a real group. The ideas that were presented to each participant in the group memory were precoded ideas that were to represent the ideas of the four fictitious members. The warm-up task and the parking task were each limited to fifteen minutes. The session ended with a debriefing of the subjects. The experimenter used a script during all sessions.

## Group Simulator GSS

One way to manipulate the paradigm-relatedness of ideas in group memory is to control what the members of the group enter into the memory. This can be accomplished by using a group comprised of one test participant, with the rest being confederates working from a script. This method has been used to understand the impact of positive and negative evaluative feedback on brainstorming $[8]$ . While effective, this method is very costly and may be subject to human error. A more efficient method is to create the illusion of a group for the lone test participant through the use of “phantom” participants simulated by a custom GSS. The GSS simulator displays ideas on the subjects’ screens as if other participants were entering the ideas into the group memory. Such a system was developed as a Visual Basic Windows application specifically for this study.

The GSS simulator implemented the Electronic Brainwriting Technique wherein participants entered ideas on a simulated “sheet of paper” on the computer screen that were drawn at random from six (number of “participants” + 1) electronic “folders” (figure 3). It is standard procedure in the Brainwriting Technique used in this experiment to use one more sheet of paper than the number of participants in the group. Each time a sheet was displayed to the participant, it included new ideas entered by the phantom participants. The new ideas were actually read in from a database of ideas generated by real participants who had worked on the same problem [33].

## Independent Variable

The independent variable was the type of stimulation the individual was exposed to (either PP or PM ideas). The ideas in the database were coded as PP or PM prior to the beginning of the experiment. The coding of the seed ideas was conducted by three coders who reached 100 percent agreement after reconciliation efforts. Depending on the treatment administered, the ideas presented to a participant were either all PM or all PP ideas. There were no other differences across experimental conditions—the same script was always used.

## Dependent Variables

The dependent variables were the count of ideas generated by participants, either PP or PM. The ideas entered by participants were captured in electronic session logs that identified the origin of each idea (the idea database or participant). Each idea was later coded as either a PP idea or a PM idea by two coders who were trained in identifying paradigm-relatedness in ideas. The coders used a categorization scheme $[33]$ (see

## Results

ONE HUNDRED AND EIGHTY-FIVE PEOPLE PARTICIPATED IN THE STUDY, with ninety-five receiving only PM feedback and ninety receiving only PP feedback. Table 1 shows the means and standard deviations for the number of PM ideas and PP ideas produced by the participants in the two experimental feedback conditions.

The statistical model used to analyze the data was a repeated measures approach to Manova using SPSS's general linear model. The count of each of the two types of ideas (PP and PM) for each participant was the within-subjects factor, named Idea-Type. The between-subjects factor was the type of feedback provided—either PM or PP. The analysis involved first testing the overall IdeaType within-subjects effect and then its interaction with the feedback effect. A significant interaction effect would demonstrate that the production of ideas differed depending on the type of feedback provided. Following a significant result on the interaction, a simple effect test (univariate Anova) on each of the two dependent variables was tested for each hypothesis.

A log transformation was applied to the data to satisfy the Manova equality of covariance assumption (Box's $M = 5.005$ , $F = 1.65$ , $p < 0.176$ ). A log transformation was necessary because the standard deviations in the raw data tended to be proportional to the treatment means, thus making the variance unequal [26, p. 83]. Stem and leaf plots revealed a normal distribution with the transformed scores.

Finally, a post-hoc test was conducted on the total number of ideas produced by participants. Since most idea-generation studies measure the total number of ideas without considering the types of ideas, it seemed interesting that the total number of ideas produced by participants in each experimental condition was about the same (12.61 when given PM feedback compared with 12.48 when given PP feedback). ## Discussion and Conclusions

THE GROUP MEMORY USED IN THIS STUDY SIMULATED a creative climate that appeared to affect the brainstorming process of the participants. Participants working with a group memory containing only PP ideas tended to generate PP ideas, while those exposed to PM ideas tended to generate PM ideas. Since the study used randomized groups, individuals with different creativity styles appear to have been influenced by the paradigm-relatedness of ideas in the group memory more than their own preferred styles.

Through the manipulation of the process (specifically, the stimuli to which the subjects were exposed), the subjects were affected in such a way that the creative product was altered. This supports the Four P's model and the implied interaction between the process, person, and product components of the model. This leaves open many more components of the model yet to explore: for instance, how the press (environment) affects the person and the creative product.

The findings of this study support both the social interaction stream of research and the cognitive processing stream of research, since individuals conformed to the type of feedback provided and the ideas they generated were similar to the ideas presented to them. It is unclear which stream of research may have been more responsible for the outcomes of this study. Because participants were told they were part of a simulated group, it is likely that cognitive processing was the greater influence, although unconsciously the participants may still have been influenced by the social context of the experiment.

Past GSS studies in brainstorming have focused on the quality and quantity of ideas generated. While all treatments may have resulted in the same quantity of ideas, they may have differed in the paradigm-relatedness of ideas. By focusing on the quantity of ideas, an interesting and different phenomenon might have been obscured. This study suggests that even among processes generating the same quantity of ideas (as seen in the post-hoc analysis), one could create conditions that result in differences in the paradigm-relatedness of ideas. Therefore, this study has introduced a new perspective on effects of group memory and how its use could actively influence a group brainstorming process.

## Implications for Practice

The manner in which group memory influences a group brainstorming process is very relevant to organizations or problem-solving groups. When bringing together a group to generate ideas, a manager may have a certain type of solution or approach in mind. For instance, a company that has put into place a Total Quality Management (TQM) plan with established goals may want its employees to generate ideas that would incrementally improve a specific process. In this case the manager may wish the group to generate PP ideas. On the other hand, a manager may be more interested in fully redesigning a delivery process and may therefore wish to develop PM solutions. Either way, the manager has in mind the type of idea he or she wishes the members of the group to generate.

Given a goal for brainstorming tasks such as the above, this study suggests that, by manipulating the contents of group memory, one could systematically influence the paradigm-relatedness of ideas generated by the group. One way to manipulate the contents of the group memory would be to seed it with either PP or PM ideas prior to the idea-generation task (as we did in this study). A more practical way to try to manipulate the group memory would be to select a group of people who tend to generate a specific type of idea when generating ideas alone. By ensuring the inclusion of specific types of individuals to take part in the brainstorming task, managers may be able to influence the type of ideas other members of the group are likely to generate. The use of a GSS could further facilitate the effect of an individual on the ideas generated by the remainder of the group because it can give group members instantaneous access to the ideas produced by other members. Alternatively, the manager or facilitator may be part of the brainstorming process and could enter either PP or PM ideas into the group memory to encourage further generation of similar ideas. Such ideas could be entered into the group memory at any point in time to change the general direction of ideas generated by the group.

For corporations, understanding how to tap into the various creativity abilities and styles of their employees could lead to new ways of competing in the marketplace. With the rapidly changing business environment and the globalization of many economies, it will become even more important for companies to find ways to differentiate themselves from their competitors. Creativity aids a company in differentiating itself from its competitors by helping it examine its current paradigm and consider ways to improve within this paradigm or to modify the paradigm in a fundamentally new way. It is widely believed that we are no longer in the era of manufacturing and industrialization but are instead in the era of knowledge creation  [6, 16, 39]  where a corporation's competitive advantage stems from the ideas of its management and employees.

## Implications for GSS System Developers

As GSS grow in popularity and system developers continue to improve on the various tools incorporated in the GSS, developers may wish to consider tools that will aid the user in generating specific types of ideas. For instance, they may want to include a screen that briefly describes the various types of ideas one may generate (i.e., PM or PP). Then they may add a simple tool to the tool bar to allow the user to tag their ideas as either PM or PP. They may also consider designing a tool that would allow the user to place their ideas into a specific folder that contains similar ideas, thus placing all PM ideas together and all PP ideas together. With similar ideas placed together in a folder, a manager could ask users to submit only one type of idea and keep the brainstorming process more focused.

## Limitations

The way in which we selected our participants and assigned the treatments to the individuals may have two potential flaws: randomization and use of college students. It is always possible that random assignment is an insufficient form of control for individual differences. We also elected to use college students, whose thought processes may or may not reflect the thought processes of corporate employees. We selected college students because the ideas that we used to seed the simulator were generated by college students and thus were more likely to match the level of understanding and complexity of thought that similar students would achieve.

As with many experiments of this variety, it is always possible that the subjects were guessing at what the experimenter wanted. However, since participants were not rewarded for their work (only their presence in the lab), this is unlikely. But, if this were the case, the findings may not hold true for other forms of teams (i.e., project teams working together to create a plan for a director).

## Future Research

This study investigated only one piece of the creativity puzzle. It focused on the impact of a specific process (group memory with specific types of ideas present) on the product (ideas) generated by individuals. There are a variety of avenues available for future research. By manipulating one aspect of the software used for brainstorming, we were able to affect the types of ideas participants generated. How would the manipulation of other aspects of the software affect the ideas generated by an individual? Does the user interface, for example, affect the ideas an individual generates?

Other aspects of the creative process must also be investigated. For example, does a specific type of creativity training affect the types of ideas an individual produces? Does a different creativity technique (e.g., problem reversal, manipulative verbs, boundary examination) alter the type of ideas an individual produces? Does the prolonged exposure to PP or PM ideas change the kinds of ideas people generate?

Other studies may focus not on the process element of the creativity model but on the press. The transferability of this study's findings to a different press or environment is uncertain. Would similar ideas be produced in a work environment? Since it was doubtful that many of the participants felt their ideas would ever be implemented, were the types of ideas they produced altered? Was the task (resolving the school's parking problems) one that lent itself equally to PP or PM solutions? Perhaps a different task would lead to a different ratio of results. What is the impact of different work or societal cultures on the types of ideas generated?

It is also possible that the impact of the process on the individual will differ according to the person's creativity style. These styles could be assessed by using one of a variety of tests for personality traits (e.g., Kirton, Torrance, Myers-Briggs, or Driskell). There may also be an amplification effect, similar to the one found by MacCrimmon and Wagner (1994), whereby the performance of some individuals was affected significantly more than the performance of others.

Further, it may be of interest to understand how the feedback a user receives affects his or her idea generation. Is he or she using it to stimulate the creation of similar ideas (as we found), or to make sense of the task? Using a more complex task in the design may help differentiate between the two types of uses for the feedback.

Finally, it would be of great interest to gain a better understanding of the impact of the cognitive processing theory and the social interaction theory on idea generation. Which of these two theories better explains how ideas are generated and why individuals tend to generate ideas similar to the ideas to which they are exposed? Since different cultures have different norms for social interaction, replicating this study in varying cultures may aid in understanding the impact of social interaction on idea generation.

## Conclusion

This study helps us understand how external stimuli, in the form of ideas, affect the type of ideas a person may generate. This is key to understanding how to channel a person's creativity toward the type of ideas a corporation wishes to generate when using a GSS. Although this study sheds light on how the exposure to specific types of ideas affects the ideas generated by an individual, it also opens the door to additional research questions. As corporations continue to explore the impact of the creative process on the development of future ideas, more research will be necessary to understand just how the creative energies of individuals can be further dampened or amplified in order to meet their goals.
