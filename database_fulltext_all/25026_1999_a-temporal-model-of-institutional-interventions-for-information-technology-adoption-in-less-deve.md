# A Temporal Model of Institutional Interventions for Information Technology Adoption in Less-Developed Countries

RAMIRO MONTEALEGRE

RAMIRO MONTEALEGRE is an Assistant Professor of Information Systems at the University of Colorado, Boulder. He received his doctorate in business administration from the Harvard Business School in the area of management information systems. His master's degree in computer science is from Carleton University, Canada. He holds a bachelor in engineering degree from the Francisco Marroquin University, Guatemala. Dr. Montealegre is a regularly invited lecturer at the Case Western Reserve University, the Instituto de Centro America de Administracion de Empresas (INCAE) in Costa Rica, the Instituto Tecnologico y de Estudios Superiores de Monterrey in Mexico, Austral and Torcuato DiTella Universities in Argentina, and Carlos III and Pablo Olavides Universities in Spain. Dr. Montealegre's research focuses on the interplay between information technology and organization transformation in highly uncertain environments. He has been involved in studying projects of organizational change in the United States, Canada, Mexico, and Central and South America. His research findings have been presented internationally.

ABSTRACT: This empirical study of Internet adoption in four Latin American countries delineates a gradual but progressive course of institutional actions and suggests a temporal ordering of the actions—including knowledge building, subsidy, knowledge deployment, innovation directive, and standard setting. The temporal model reveals how each country sustained the momentum of its evolving strategy, grew in competence to forge technological solutions, and gained access to the Internet. The four countries' original goals changed, but through experience they perceived new opportunities and established evolving Internet strategies that form the bases of new technological services provided at the national level.

KEY WORDS AND PHRASES: adoption of information technology, global information technology infrastructure, information technology in less-developed countries, network development and implementation.

THIS PAPER EXPLORES THE ROLE THAT INSTITUTIONS PLAY in the adoption of information technology (IT) in less-developed countries (LDCs). In particular the paths taken by four Latin American LDCs—Chile, Costa Rica, Ecuador, and Peru—that have recently established or are in the process of establishing an Internet presence are studied. In all the cases investigated, Internet adoption required support from many sectors, including the government, the academic community, the private sector, telecommunication providers, and the international community. This finding is in accordance with the work of King et al. [24], who suggest that, in contrast with the adoption of stand-alone IT innovations (such as personal computers), the adoption of more complex and sophisticated IT innovations that cut across firm boundaries (such as the Internet) cannot be adequately understood without an in-depth analysis of the role played by institutions.

Gaining access to the Internet by these four Latin American LDCs was an evolving process that was both cumulative and expansive. Each of these LDCs searched for a solution to connect academic and research institutions in the country with homologous institutions in the world. The leaders did not possess a grand plan or a complete picture of their final destination—merely a general goal of acquiring access. They took just a few steps at a time toward solving immediate problems, such as a weak global connection or a limiting infrastructure. Each successful solution improved their global connectivity and created a new infrastructure with latent potential for new functionality. The groups grew beyond each challenge to perceive a broader set of opportunities and establish an evolving Internet strategy. This is consistent with McKenney's [37] perception that pioneer adopters of strategic IT systems in developed countries followed a “cascade” process.

This study empirically validates the institutional actions in the adoption of IT identified by King et al. [24], who review strategies of institutional intervention in IT adoption to identify categories of institutional actions that can stimulate or retard IT diffusion, namely knowledge building, knowledge deployment, subsidy, innovation directive, mobilization, and standard setting. Although their work facilitates a broad understanding of the multifaceted role of institutions in the adoption process, they present little empirical evidence to associate specific interventions with specific IT innovations. The current study extends their theoretical work by suggesting a temporal ordering of the actions. As figure 1 shows, knowledge building contributed the most in the early phases of the adoption, subsidies contributed the most in the middle stages, and knowledge deployment and innovation directive contributed the most in the late or ending phase of the adoption process. Standard setting contributed the most in the early or late phases of the process. On the other hand, institutional actions that encourage thinking in a particular way with respect to an innovation, identified by King et al. as mobilization, were not found to have contributed in these particular cases. Although the Internet theme was found very often in the popular press in the countries investigated, none of the institutions studied embarked on initiatives or devoted resources to mobilize a particular view of the Internet.

## Research Context and Background

THIS STUDY GREW OUT OF AN INVITATION MADE TO THE AUTHOR by the U.S. Agency for International Development and the American Embassy in Peru to participate in a working team to evaluate all the Latin American and Caribbean networks. From a comprehensive evaluation of these networks, Chile, Costa Rica, Ecuador, and Peru were chosen on the basis of similarities and differences in their adoption conditions.

These countries share location, culture, and a significant level of support from a regional institution, the Organization of American States (OAS); nevertheless, each country has had a very different experience with Internet adoption. Costa Rica, which had strong government support, became the regional leader in Central America, acting as an access point for other Central American countries. Peru's Internet access began with grass roots efforts that had little government support, yet it has had the highest rate of Internet growth in Latin America for several years. At the time of the study, Chile had the largest number of hosts $^{1}$ per capita in South America and had an extremely competitive telecommunications environment. In Ecuador, the force driving the Internet initiative was a commercial enterprise rather than the government or an educational institution, either of which is more typical.

The general research questions that guided the investigation were: What created the need and opportunity for nations to adopt the Internet and how did they accomplish this? What level of understanding about the adoption process did Internet champions possess at the time? Why did change come when it did and take the form that it did? Who were the primary stakeholders in the adoption process? Although the evidence, and not a priori theory, drove the inquiry, some theoretical concepts from the existing literature on the adoption of IT were used to focus the questions and to gather and organize the evidence, as suggested by Eisenhardt [14] and by Mason, McKenney, and Copeland [34].

## The Role of Institutions in the Adoption of Information Technology

The adoption of IT in developed countries is well documented from the broad managerial action perspective $[16, 28]$ , the innovation diffusion perspective $[9]$ , the critical mass perspective $[31]$ , the organizational evolution perspective $[21, 36, 39, 47, 48]$ , the institutional perspective $[15, 25, 27, 29]$ , and the historical perspective $[34, 37]$ . The existing research, however, has lacked attention to interinstitutional factors that shape the process of IT adoption $[24]$ . An exception is the work by King et al. $[24]$ , which emphasizes the need to bridge between amorphous social concepts such as “society” and “culture” on one hand and more specifiable and tractable social entities such as firms, groups, and individuals on the other hand. Their work suggests that institutions form the vital boundary layers between these two worlds of discourse, and it facilitates an understanding of the multifaceted role of institutions in the adoption of technology.

King et al. claim that institutional intervention in IT adoption can be constructed at the intersection of the influence and regulatory powers of institutions and the ideologies of supply-push and demand-pull models of innovation. Institutions can proactively use their nonlegal influence to spearhead the development of IT; they can also use legal forces to affect IT development. They can stimulate supply by encouraging investment in production and promoting the transfer of foreign technology into a country. They can also stimulate demand by creating a need for innovative products and processes, or enhancing the efficiency and effectiveness with which they are used. The authors then review strategies of institutional intervention to produce a scheme of six categories of institutional actions that can stimulate or retard IT adoption: knowledge building, knowledge deployment, subsidy, mobilization, standard setting, and innovation directives.

\- Knowledge building consists of the institutional actions undertaken to provide the base of scientific and technical knowledge required to produce and exploit innovations. Uses of influence include sponsoring research that helps build the base of knowledge necessary for innovative activity, supporting basic research with limited immediate application potential, or supporting applied research to yield particular payoffs.

\- Knowledge deployment involves institutional actions that stimulate the dissemination of new knowledge, either in the form of knowledgeable individuals and institutions, or in the form of archives and libraries of scientific and technical facts. The most obvious form of knowledge deployment is the general provision of education to the population. Another form of knowledge deployment encourages already knowledgeable individuals and institutions to come into the country or region and establish operations. Still another form of knowledge deployment stimulates the use of innovations by training a cadre of potential users.

\- Subsidies are institutional actions designed to produce specific innovation outcomes and take a variety of forms: funding prototype development and demonstration projects; encouraging capital markets to make funds available for innovative activity and acquisition of innovative products or processes through loan guarantees, provision of tax breaks, subsidizing the cost of external expertise, providing low-interest loans, and so on; purchasing innovations and locally produced goods and services containing those innovations; supporting the provision of necessary complements to be used with innovative products or processes; and reducing barriers to production of innovations by individuals and institutions.

\- Mobilization refers to institutional actions that encourage decentralized actors to think in a particular way with respect to an innovation. By encouraging a positive or negative view of an innovation, for example, through promotional and awareness campaigns, institutions can affect the diffusion of IT [26].

\- Standard setting comprises actions that regulate the operation of decentralized actors and institutions to bring them into line with larger social or institutional objectives. Standards are socially constructed; they are agreements or “treaties” among interested parties to describe one way of doing things as “preferable.” They can be completely voluntary, as are many standards promulgated by professional and trade associations, or they can have the force of law.

\- Innovation directives are institutional actions commanding to produce innovations, to use them, or to engage in some activity that will specifically facilitate production and/or use. An institution may produce and use its own innovation, may require that other institutions invest given amounts of their research and development in activities presumed to lead to innovation, may require that other institutions use particular products or processes wherever they can be applied, or may require other institutions to alter their structures or operations in ways that indirectly affect innovation and diffusion.

These categories illuminate the roles played by institutions studied here and elucidate some of the dynamics at work.

## The Process of Information Technology Adoption

Process models focus on sequences of events over time in order to explain how and why particular outcomes are reached $[1, 40]$ . Several process models have been conceived to explain IT adoption by identifying the sequence of generic stages, each of which must be attended to if adoption is to occur $[23, 32, 37, 45, 54, 56]$ . A positive feature of process models is their faithful account of actual experiences, although they can become cumbersome and analytically complex $[25, 33, 44]$ .

In analyzing the process of IT adoption followed by the four countries studied here, triangulation of the findings and existing literature in this realm pointed toward McKenney's work on IT adoption, as shown in figure 1. McKenney [37] characterizes the adoption of IT as a process of movement through five overlapping phases: searching for a solution, building competence, expanding the solution, enabling changes, and evolving strategy. He conducted an in-depth study of U.S. companies that implemented strategic information systems that became dominant designs in their industries. McKenney suggests that the effects of a new information system are felt initially in the internal structure of the institution and then proliferate throughout the external competitive environment, a pattern he calls a “cascade process.”

\- Phase 1: Searching for a solution: The first condition for the model is a perceived need of sufficient proportions to capture the attention of a sponsor group. Individuals, groups, or institutions seek solutions in the enhanced information processing capability provided by a new IT.

\- Phase 2: Building competence: This phase begins when individuals form project teams to analyze and conceptualize a new IT solution. Ultimately, implementation of a system, typically a prototype or a limited application, exerts an impact on a reduced domain. The changes necessitated by the implementation lead to deeper involvement and greater collaboration among the parties who are involved as users or providers. If the system works and yields the projected benefits, leaders are encouraged to expand the IT-based system and continue the search for applications.

\- Phase 3: Expanding the solution: Prompted by growing understanding of the solution's potential, the leading groups search for partners with competence and resources to build an expanded design. They strive assiduously to realize the most robust design possible, given the state of the technology. The project grows to encompass and exert influence over a broad spectrum of institutions.

\- Phase 4: Enabling changes: The operational design emerges as the IT system is tested and resolves the processing crisis, providing improved services and a cost reduction. A significant effort is made to exploit the IT's productivity and provide a dynamic basis for augmenting the service provided.

\- Phase 5: Evolving strategy: As the evolution of the IT solution continues, competitors begin to emulate the new design. The leader typically enjoys a comfortable short-term lead and begins to introduce new services and more effective marketing programs. Competitors that have been experimenting on their own expand on the original design and improve the system's capability, which levels the playing field and initiates another round of IT innovation. In traversing the phases of the cascade, a leader gains competence that usually enables it to prevail over competitors' advances.

Like McKenney's subjects, the innovators in the present study assimilated an IT-based solution gradually, and, as they came to recognize the medium as a competitive market force, their strategies evolved. National Internet network designs, like the strategic IT systems investigated by McKenney, are not well understood at the beginning of the development cycle, but evolve with experience into a reasonable, well-understood vision that continues to shift with experience and changing opportunities. Furthermore, a remarkable consistency is that, even in an era in which the technology and professional talent existed elsewhere and were available, it still took five to seven years for these countries to arrive at an innovative design. In all the countries studied, institutional and interinstitutional issues required longer lead times than system development efforts. Thus, McKenney's cascade was used in this study as a conceptual model to describe recurring events and to reveal the similarities and differences among the four case studies.

## Research Strategy

THE RESEARCH STRATEGY FOR THIS STUDY CAN BE BROADLY CLASSIFIED as an interpretive epistemology [50, 61] using a holistic multiple case study design [62] to build theory [14, 17]. The design choice is consistent with the exploratory nature of the research questions; the focus on the global nature of the phenomena, rather than on the operational details; and the contemporary nature of the subject, which meant that extensive documentation was available and that the major actors could be located for interviewing.

The research involved both a historical background reconstruction of the adoption of the Internet in these countries and interviews with individuals who had been the champions as well as managers of the institutions that sponsored the adoption. Hence, our study involved retrospective, albeit first-hand, accounts of relevant events. The documentation review entailed analyzing, coding, and classifying reports related to Internet connectivity, annual reports, project proposals and progress reports, and design documents. Primary data sources were, appropriately, found on the Internet itself. Such resources included Web sites for individual countries and for international institutions sponsoring these efforts, and sites focusing on the Internet and development. Individuals directly involved with implementing the Internet in the selected countries were also contacted through Internet newsgroups and e-mail.

In addition, as part of the Latin American and Caribbean Internet evaluation project, the author attended a ten-day workshop sponsored by the institutions that had been participating in the construction of these networks. The leaders of the Internet adoption as well as managers of the sponsoring institutions were available for interviews, which complemented the historical reconstruction data compiled from the Internet. All interviews were semistructured, with open-ended questions focused on the interviewee's perceptions of the environment, the vision for adoption, the history of the connectivity developments, the rationale behind the technological solutions, the implementation strategy and schedule, stakeholders' involvement, individual and team experiences with networks, training, and changes associated with the adoption of the connectivity solutions. To capture the variety of perspectives inherent in a complex macro diffusion, a total of thirty-nine formal interviews were conducted with individuals representing governmental agencies, academic and private institutions, and international institutions involved in the adoption of the Internet in each of the study countries (see Table 1). Transcription and initial coding of the interviews were completed within thirty-six hours to ensure that the interpretation of responses was accurately recorded and available for planning subsequent interviews. Other interviews (for example, the ones with other members of the Latin American and Caribbean networks evaluation team) were also conducted as informal discussions on an ad-hoc basis.

Data analysis was conducted in several steps. First, notes taken from the documentation review were used to outline a broad picture of the background, the general path of the adoption process, and the major decisions over time. Second, the interview transcripts were verified against the tapes. Third, data sources (multiple informants associated with the Internet adoption initiative in each country) and data-collection methods (documentation and interviews) were triangulated to write the initial case descriptions. For example, since individuals were interviewed about events that had transpired years earlier, there may have been some failures in memory, and, more importantly, their interpretation of events may have changed with the passage of time. Therefore, every effort was made in the historical reconstruction to distinguish between participants' views at some point in the past and their recollection of those views at a later period. $^{2}$

Following Eisenhardt's roadmap [14], the case studies were used to build theory, testing emerging explanations against possible alternative ones by searching for contrasting evidence. Triangulation with existing literature on IT adoption and the role of institutions in IT adoption pointed toward McKenney's work on IT adoption [37] and King et al.'s categorization of institutional actions that stimulate or retard IT adoption [24]. McKenney's "cascade" model of IT adoption helped to illuminate the process of Internet adoption followed by each country and to reveal the similarities and differences between the four case studies. From this conceptual model, the initial case analyses were written. Then, the categories of institutional actions identified by King et al. were used as theoretical constructs to ground the emergent temporal model [14], analyzing the transcripts for specific actions that supported, refuted, or extended these categories. In particular, comments were identified that pertained to the history of institutional actions that were perceived as enabling Internet adoption in each country. The temporal model of these categories evolved as research proceeded. Finally, the people directly associated with the Internet adoption in each of the countries provided comments, corrections, and elaborations on drafts of the case study reports and findings.

## Analysis of Internet Adoption

THIS SECTION DETAILS THE SITUATION, STRATEGY, INSTITUTIONAL ACTIONS, technological changes, and mutations in the adoption of the Internet for each study country. All information is woven into a set of patterns that reflect their progress through the cascade of institutional activities (italicized in the text) that yielded, or is in the process of yielding, adoption of the technology.

## Chile

## Phase 1: Searching for a Solution (1985–86)

In 1985, ENTEL, the state-owned telecommunication agency, was privatized and limited competition was allowed (standard setting). This prompted dramatic reductions in service prices and increased investment from abroad [2]. In 1986 the computer science departments of the universities in Antofagasta, Concepcion, Valparaiso, and Santiago began searching (knowledge building) for means to be connected to each other and to similar institutions around the world.

## Phase 2: Building Competence (1987–88)

By 1987, the four computer science departments joined resources to build a UUCP network $^{3}$ to create an academic e-mail network (subsidy). At the same time, IBM donated the hardware, software, and leased lines to establish an SNA $^{4}$ network among these universities (subsidy). Plans were made for a gateway between the two systems, and the work was conducted by an informal institutional entity named REUNA (Red Universitaria Nacional or National University Network) [4] (knowledge building). The gateway became operational in April 1987 [59].

## Phase 3: Expanding the Solution (1988–90)

In 1988, the University of Chile reached an agreement with NASA that allowed Chilean universities to share the use of a voice line connecting NASA's Santiago Tracking Station (operated by the University of Chile) and Goddard Space Flight Center in Bethesda, Maryland (subsidy). A switch installed and operated by NASA personnel in Santiago and the United States enabled the line to be used during nights and weekends to connect to BITNET $^{5}$ through the University of Maryland [59]. This arrangement allowed the four Chilean universities to experiment with a BITNET connection (knowledge building) while avoiding the high communication costs by using NASA's excess capacity at no charge [4].

## Phase 4: Enabling Changes (1991–92)

In 1991 REUNA evolved into a formal consortium when the president of the Chilean Science and Technology Research Commission (CONICYT) formed a committee to establish the foundation for more permanent development of an academic network. All of the major universities were asked to appoint official representatives to the committee (standard setting). The result was a reorganized REUNA in charge of the development, administration, operation, and commercialization of a TCP/IP network connected to the Internet. REUNA was to remain a nonprofit institution to preserve the academic spirit of the network and was self-funded through the sale of its services to other institutions in Chile [59].

The cost of a 56 Kbps link to the United States at that time, which was necessary to start the TCP/IP network, was approximately \$12,000 per month. The financial support for this network came from a newly established National Fund for Private Sector–University Relations (FONDEF), $^{6}$ which offered a grant for a maximum of four years (subsidy). The grant required that demonstrable economic value be provided. In addition, CONICYT supplied funding to cover the cost of the Internet connection during its first year of operation (subsidy).

## Phase 5: Evolving Strategy (1992–95)

According to Florencio Utrera, REUNA's general manager, “disagreements about the distribution of costs and control of the network among the universities led to the development of two separate and competing networks, UNIRED and REUNA.” After some delay caused by the National Science Foundation’s (NSF) policy of “one country one link,” both REUNA and UNIRED were added to the MERIT’s Internet routing tables in February 1992. REUNA’s first year was essentially devoted to completing the network, installing the operation centers, and training technicians from member institutions in the use and administration of the Internet (knowledge deployment). REUNA began offering commercial services in 1993. UNIRED, directed by the private Catholic University, also began to offer commercial services, by actively marketing its network and fostering the interest of commercial enterprises to be connected to the Internet [55] (knowledge deployment). As a result of this competition between UNIRED and REUNA, Chile had two networks connected to the Internet via international links, but these networks were locally disconnected. Chilean traffic was flowing to the United States and back to Chile to reach another network in the same country—even the same city. In 1995 REUNA became involved in the development of a nationwide Internet network (national backbone) (innovation directive).

The high growth rate of Chilean Internet hosts in the first few years can be attributed not only to UNIRED's marketing strategies and REUNA's technical quality [55], but also to the evolution of the telecommunications infrastructure [59] (innovation directive) during this phase. In 1993, Chile became the second country in the world to have a totally digital telephone network; an integrated service digital network (ISDN) also became available in that year, and by 1994 frame relay networks were operational. The final deregulatory step occurred in January 1995, when the local telephone systems were opened to competition. Internet services became free of regulation, and new Internet service providers began to appear.

## Costa Rica

## Phase 1: Searching for a Solution (1990–91)

In the 1980s Costa Rica was considered to have one of the best domestic telephone services in all of Latin America [58]. Instituto Costarricense de Electricidad (ICE), the state electricity company, was responsible for domestic and international telephone service (standard setting). Radiografica Costarricense (RACSA), a subsidiary of ICE, was in charge of data transmission and value-added services, including telegraph, telex, data communications, and leased-line services. RACSA provided X.25, frame relay, and asynchronous transfer mode (ATM) services (standard setting). In 1990, researchers at the University of Costa Rica began searching for ways to connect to each other and to other academic and research institutions around the world (knowledge building).

## Phase 2: Building Competence (1991–92)

By the end of 1990, the University of Costa Rica had gained access to the global BITNET network and had begun experimenting on its use (knowledge building). To provide this connection, a local technical team used equipment already located at the University of Costa Rica. Guy de Teramond, the leader of the Internet access effort at the University of Costa Rica, recalled, “A connection to the nearest BITNET node, located in the United States, required the lease of a 19.2 Kbps digital channel for \$40,000 per year and an additional \$5,000 installation expense. Supporters encountered difficulties obtaining financing for a system which very few people knew how to use or exploit.” Finally, the Interamerican Development Bank decided to finance the first year of the connection (subsidy), and the University of Costa Rica financed the second year (subsidy). IBM also donated two protocol converters that enabled BITNET access from any personal computer over telephone lines or the Costa Rican X.25 network (subsidy).

## Phase 3: Expanding the solution (1992–93)

Costa Rica's second global network connection came in 1992 under the auspices of the United Nations Development Program (UNDP) Project Hurricane (subsidy). The project established a UUCP e-mail service, using X.25 network and telephone access to a U.S. gateway [60]. In January 1993, two years after connecting to BITNET, Costa Rica became the first country in Central America to establish an Internet node. The node, sponsored by the NSF and located at the University of Costa Rica (subsidy), connected through a 64 Kbps satellite link to the NSF-Sprint-PanAmSat gateway in Homestead, Florida. The Internet was new territory for the team in charge of the project, which soon discovered that its complexity, decentralization, and technologies demanded use of high-capacity channels and specialized routers to become more efficient (knowledge building).

## Phase 4: Enabling Changes (1993–94)

In 1993, the Ministry of Science and Technology created the National Research Network of Costa Rica (CRNet), as a formal institution in charge of administering the Internet national connectivity (standard setting). This initiative, supported by the University of Costa Rica, gave the project sufficient status to receive support inside and outside the country, as well as the ability to obtain funding [60]. CRNet established as a digital network providing high-speed local interconnectivity between scientists at universities, research laboratories, industries with a technological component, and other national institutions. It received support from a number of parties, including the U.S. Agency for International Development (US/AID) and OAS. Approval from US/AID allowed the acquisition of high-tech equipment and payment for the national backbone and satellite connection (subsidy). A donation of equipment from OAS increased the system's routing capacity (subsidy). OAS funds were instrumental in covering the high costs of connection. Since there were few institutions initially connected, it was difficult to share the costs among them. The project also used a CISCO router on loan from the University of Wisconsin that was installed at the University of Costa Rica (subsidy). The Omar Dengo Foundation became part of the network and contributed an additional router to facilitate the incorporation of new networks into the national network (subsidy).

## Phase 5: Evolving Strategy (1994–96)

In 1994, RACSA began operating an Internet network for the commercial sector (knowledge deployment). In 1995, an Internet network connected twelve government ministries, facilitating direct access to information inside and outside the government sector [11] (knowledge deployment). In addition, CRNet became an anchor for the OAS Central American networking project, RedCACyT (innovation directive). Links were developed between the University of Costa Rica and the National Engineering University of Managua, Nicaragua, as well as other Nicaraguan universities and research institutions. In 1994 Panama connected to the Internet through CRNet via microwave links [49].

## Ecuador

## Phase 1: Searching for a Solution (1990–91)

In the 1980s, Ecuador's domestic telecommunications facilities were provided by EMETEL, the state-owned telephone company. Because these facilities were generally known to be inadequate and unreliable [10], a number of banks and petroleum companies negotiated with EMETEL and began to operate private satellite networks using very small aperture terminals (VSATs).

In 1990, the Ecuadorian Information Corporation, a nonprofit association, was formed (standard setting) by the Escuela Politecnia del Litoral, the Universidad Catolica Santiago de Guayaquil, the Junta de Beneficencia de Guayaquil, the Fundacion Privada Ecuatoriana, and the Banco del Pacifico, the country's largest commercial bank and the operator of its largest private satellite network. The objective of the Ecuadorian Information Corporation was to install and manage a computer network that would enable students, researchers, and the general public to communicate with others throughout the world, and would provide access to libraries and scientific databases worldwide [12] (knowledge building).

## Phase 2: Building Competence (1991–92)

The project of developing an Internet connection and national network, EcuaNet, came into being after the Banco del Pacifico offered Ecuadorian Information Corporation the use of its national and international communications system at no charge (subsidy).

EcuaNet was given bandwidth on the bank's satellite and microwave-based domestic networking infrastructure, which was primarily used for automatic teller machines and interbranch/intercity voice and data communication, and on its satellite link to Miami [18]. The first stage of the EcuaNet project was completed in March 1991 when connection to the BITNET network was made, enabling its members to experiment with that type of connectivity (knowledge building).

## Phase 3: Expanding the Solution (1992–93)

In October 1992, Ecuador was connected to the Internet through SURAnet. $^{7}$ Ecuadorians sent their traffic through this network for many months, while EcuaNet gained experience and maturity (knowledge building). Technical and financial support for this connection came from the Escuela Politecnica del Litoral and the Universidad Catolica de Santiago de Guayaquil (subsidy). Private institutions also contributed equipment and funding (subsidy), including Almacenadora del Agro, Factor del Pacifico, IBM del Ecuador, L.E.A.S.I.N.G. del Pacifico, MasterCard, Seguros Sucre, and the U.S. Pacific National Bank.

## Phase 4: Enabling Changes (1993–94)

In February 1993, EcuaNet received financial support from NSF and began sending traffic through the NSF-Sprint/ICM router in Homestead, Florida [5] (subsidy). The Ecuadorian Information Corporation began to offer several different courses on topics related to the Internet to members of academic, public, and private institutions (knowledge deployment).

## Phase 5: Evolving Strategy (1994–96)

EcuaNet/Ecuadorian Information Corporation grew, and by 1996 it was composed of diverse groups, including universities, secondary schools, research centers and foundations, government and public institutions, members of the armed forces, professional associations, business, clinics, and hospitals (knowledge deployment). At that time several private Internet providers also began to appear (innovation directive).

## Peru

## Phase 1: Searching for a Solution (1990–91)

In the 1980s the Peruvian telecommunications network was one of the least developed in Latin America [10]. Beginning in 1985, several attempts were made to start a national network in Peru, but some barely reached the take-off point and others did not achieve a significant user base [52]. At the end of 1991, Peruvian universities and nongovernmental institutions formed the Red Cientifica Peruana (RCP) (standard setting), a nonprofit institution in charge of investigating different ways to develop a national academic and research network [57] (knowledge building).

## Phase 2: Building Competence (1991–92)

RCP began its activities after receiving a small grant for basic networking equipment from UNDP and three telephone lines and an X.25 connection from the Peruvian university ESAN (subsidies). Private institutions were not confident that RCP could be self-supporting, and the government did not want to participate in a consortium that was not directly controlled by a governmental agency. In particular, the state-owned Postal Telegraph and Telephone (PTT) agency, ENTEL, refused to cooperate because it believed that RCP would compete with its X.25 service.

RCP also encountered a shortage of engineers and technicians with experience in connectivity. To provide technical support, the UNDP brought two U.S. engineers from a networking project in Costa Rica (knowledge building). The plan called for the technical staff to “parachute drop” in, set up the project, and leave. But one of the engineers, Randy Bush, strongly disagreed with that arrangement, so the two engineers stayed for three months while they thoroughly trained the Peruvian engineers to use and understand the networking equipment (knowledge building). Bush and the trained Peruvian engineers focused on connecting subnets to the central node of the network. The Peruvian network was then linked by a dial-up line to Bush’s PSGnet.8 By January 1992, forty nodes had been established [38].

## Phase 3: Expanding the Solution (1993–94)

In 1993, OAS financed a satellite antenna (subsidy), and in February 1994 Peru's network was upgraded from UUCP to TCP/IP. In that same year, RCP began operating as an Internet network via a PanAmSat connection to an earth station in Homestead, Florida, with the sponsorship of the NSF (subsidy). These actions enabled RCP to experiment with UUCP and Internet connections (knowledge building).

## Phase 4: Enabling Changes (1994–95)

In January 1994, 140 institutions were associated with the RCP. At that time, and still counting on the financial support from OAS and NSF (subsidy), RCP initiated a plan to deploy knowledge about the network. This effort included training personnel at member institutions, holding conferences on technical topics, using the available tools to perform personalized demonstrations, developing Internet tool manuals to assist users, disseminating information about the Internet, and creating directories of existing Internet resources (knowledge deployment). By the end of the year, RCP had 420 member institutions, primarily universities and research institutions, but also nongovernmental institutions, civil associations, and government agencies.

## Phase 5: Evolving Strategy (1995–96)

Early in 1995, RCP began a project to develop a national backbone, which consisted of creating consortia in the twenty-four departments of the country, each with a 64 Kbps dedicated line to Lima (innovation directive). The objective was to establish a national infrastructure that would allow anyone to use the resources available in Lima, increase cooperation among institutions, decrease the costs of communication for the national research system, and offer training in the use of new technologies. According to RCP General Manager Jose Soriano, “the execution of the national backbone project was hindered by two factors: the lack of an appropriate telephone network in the country, and the lack of capital to construct the departmental nodes, acquire the infrastructure, and buy the telephone services.”

Another project initiated in 1995 by RCP, “La Cabana Publica,” developed public sites where the general public could access the Internet for three hours per week at a rate of \$15 per month (innovation directive). This fee included free training (knowledge deployment). The first public center opened in Lima, and RCP planned to extend centers throughout the country as the backbone was put into place. By 1996, there were 2,149 members; 73 percent of the total were businesses and individuals, while 27 percent were nonprofit institutions [57].

## Discussion

THE CASE ANALYSES SHOW THAT INTERNET ADOPTION IN THE COUNTRIES investigated differed considerably in the specific ways in which the strategy evolved, resources were marshaled, and partnerships were coordinated and nurtured. Yet, all four countries had access to equivalent technology and similar funding sources, and they all sought collaboration among government, academic institutions, private companies, and international institutions, as summarized in Table 2. Little progress can be made in this field—or in any field of study—however, if every situation is regarded as unique. IT adoption researchers are challenged to make generalizations, and to approximate summaries of data from the welter of observed and reported events. The key to these generalizations is specifying the conditions under which relationships can be expected to hold. Thus, this section examines how previously identified institutional actions may apply in light of four case studies. No attempt is made to categorize each and every event in the history of these Internet adoption initiatives; instead the major occurrences are placed into a theoretical model.

Figure 1 depicts the temporal model of institutional intervention in IT adoption in LDCs derived from the four case studies. The figure shows the adoption as a five-phase process and identifies at each phase the institutional interventions that emerged as salient from the data analysis. The model shows that institutional interventions that helped to develop trials and experiments that built knowledge contributed the most at the early phases, interventions that helped subsidize the technological solutions contributed the most at the middle phases, and institutional interventions that enabled knowledge deployment and innovation directive contributed the most at the late or ending phase of the adoption process. Standardization contributed most at the early and late phases of the process. On the other hand, institutional actions that encourage thinking in a particular way with respect to an innovation, identified by King et al. as mobilization, were not found to have contributed in these particular cases. Although the Internet theme was found very often in the popular press in the countries investigated, none of the institutions studied embarked on initiatives or devoted resources to mobilize a particular view of the Internet. Thus, we can summarize this temporal model of adoption with the following propositions:

Proposition 2. There is a temporal ordering in the institutional interventions in IT adoption in less-developed countries: knowledge-building actions contribute the most at the early phases, subsidy actions contribute the most at the middle phases, knowledge deployment and innovation directive contribute the most at the late or ending phase, and standardization actions contribute the most at the early and late phases of the adoption process.

This model and these propositions are offered as an initial formulation of the sequence of institutional intervention of IT adoption in LDCs. No claim is made that the institutional interventions presented here are exhaustive. Further IT adoption studies in LDCs should add to or modify the ideas presented here—this is how we build on each other's work.

## Knowledge Building

None of the groups leading the Internet adoption in these cases had a grand plan at the beginning of the process or a complete picture of their final destination. Each of the case studies began with the intent of enabling academic and research institutions to connect with homologous institutions throughout the world, especially in developed nations. The decision to innovate and move toward a new technological solution set a force into action. As shown in Table 2, the academic sector was the leader in helping build knowledge and bring the Internet to Chile, Costa Rica, and Peru. People in the academic and research institutions that took the leadership were aware of the Internet and had computing and networking experience. In Ecuador, the private sector was a more important driver.

During the first three phases of the adoption, the countries investigated settled for rudimentary connections that fit their resources, skills, and existing technology. Each invested time and money in developing its body of competence. The four countries experienced a common cumulative expansion in knowledge during these initial phases. These cases demonstrate that the development of technology, even with limited immediate application potential, contributes to building critical knowledge. These initial trials provided valuable insights into the costs, benefits, technological complexity, and organizational and coordination issues associated with connecting to a global network. This prepared each country to embark on a more advanced innovation in the latter phases of adoption. The first three phases, therefore, were particularly important for building a reservoir of connectivity competence.

In addition, the existing literature on IT adoption in LDCs has suggested that governments play a key supporting role in sponsoring research that helps build the base of knowledge necessary for innovative activity $[19, 35, 42, 43, 51]$ . In Costa Rica, this was the case. Governments, however, are by no means the universal or direct institutional sponsor of knowledge building. The government of Chile was, for the most part, neutral, and those of Ecuador and Peru were originally against the Internet initiative. These cases demonstrate that, in LDCs, governmental intervention to promote knowledge building is important, but not essential, for successful adoption of IT. In Ecuador, the Ecuadorian Information Corporation is an example of how private and academic institutions can cooperate in knowledge-building activities. An active academic and private sector can successfully introduce the Internet in a country. In Peru, when the RCP began this innovation process, the state-protected telephone company, ENTEL, refused to cooperate because it believed RCP would compete with its service. During the second phase of adoption, however, UNDP support was key in bringing technical expertise from Costa Rica. This is an example of an important role played by an international institution in sponsoring educational activities that helped build a necessary knowledge base for innovation.

## Subsidy

The adoption of state-of-the-art technologies (or technologies new-to-the-setting, as in this case) requires different managerial, technical, and financial resources than those needed to adopt mature technologies offered and supported by an experienced vendor base $[24]$ . This is particularly challenging for LDCs, where these types of resources are very scarce $[3, 8, 20, 41, 53]$ . Thus, the adoption leaders must move adroitly to secure or facilitate the development of these resources. Equally important are funds to build the appropriate infrastructure and the willingness of those working on the projects to invest time and effort in truly inventing and adapting new technological systems. The resulting profound transitions often demand faith on the part of the sponsors. However, as these four cases show, even a modest investment in ongoing systems experimentation can allow a country to learn how to test new technologies and appreciate their limitations and potentials, while also creating a context for learning how to use the new technology.

In phase 2 of the adoption process, as shown in Table 2, academic and research institutions in Chile, Costa Rica, and Peru provided the technical support and infrastructure for Internet adoption. Unfortunately, institutions of this type had limited budgets and were dependent on state funding. In addition, during the initial phase of adoption, a few private companies, such as IBM, occasionally donated equipment. The subsidy provided by the private sector in Ecuador during this phase was more significant.

In phases 3 and 4, all the leading adoption groups sought support from other sectors, including the academic community, the private sector, the international community, and the government. Institutions in the four nations received varying degrees of support and in different combinations, with various degrees of success, but in all four cases national Internet networks broadened in scope as more resources became available and the technology expanded the networks' capabilities. Gaining confidence from limited successes, each country supported the leading network institution and marshaled resources for repeated trials that gradually expanded the domain and functionality of the network.

International institutions played an important cooperative role in the adoption of the Internet. All four countries studied received funding and technical assistance from international institutions, such as the Organization of American States, the Inter-American Development Bank, the United Nations Development Project, the U.S. National Science Foundation (NSF), and the U.S. Agency for International Development (USAID). Such aid helped to increase local support by providing a level of certainty, giving local people more confidence in the growth prospects of the Internet projects.

Ecuador is unique among these four cases in the central role played by its private sector. Ecuador demonstrates that a country can connect to the Internet using a privately operated satellite network, thereby making the quality of the public telecommunications infrastructure inconsequential. Important additional support, in the form of equipment and financing, was also received from several private companies. The government, particularly the state-owned PTT, EMETEL, played a relatively small role. EcuaNet did not receive as much support from international institutions as did Internet projects in other Latin American countries, except for access to the NSF-Sprint link node.

## Knowledge Deployment

The most obvious form of knowledge deployment is the general provision of education to the population $[24]$ . However, in all countries investigated, the deployment of knowledge during the initial phases of adoption was nonexistent. In fact, no one outside academic and research institutions was targeted as a consumer of the service; therefore, many individuals and businesses in these countries were simply not aware that access was offered.

Starting in phase 4 of the adoption process, as shown in Table 2, the academic sectors of Chile and Costa Rica played an important role in sponsoring educational activities targeting not only individuals within academia, but the public in general, and building the base of knowledge necessary for Internet adoption. International institutions also played an important role in knowledge deployment at the end of the adoption process. In particular, OAS and UNDP provided technical support, specialized training, and technical workshops and seminars in the region to prepare technical projects, improve skills, share technical knowledge, and train network managers. $^{9}$

In Ecuador and Peru, the Information Corporation and the RCP, respectively, played important roles toward the end of the adoption process in sponsoring educational activities for the general population that helped build the base of knowledge necessary for adopting the new Internet services.

## Innovation Directive

At the end of the Internet adoption, as shown in Table 2, different institutions in all of these countries took initiatives that would increase the use of Internet service—the type of actions that King et al. [24] call “innovation directives.”

In Chile, the final phase of Internet adoption was marked by the government's supporting the development of a totally digital telephone network by the end of 1992, and engaging in the final step of deregulating the telecommunication industry in 1995. At the same time, REUNA and UNIRED, the two academic institutions that had initially coordinated the Internet connection in this country, took the initiative aggressively to market Internet services not only to academic, but also to commercial users. Their customer base has since expanded to businesses and individuals. During these phases of adoption, several of the private companies now operating in the deregulated telecommunication industry were increasingly positioning themselves to become Internet service providers.

In Costa Rica, in response to demand from other sectors, the state-owned firm in charge of data transmission and value-added services, RACSA, began to offer its own commercial Internet services, while continuing to offer service to education and research institutions. The case of Costa Rica demonstrates that the public telephone company need not always be an obstacle. In Costa Rica, one of the advantages of a government-owned PTT was that the government was able to negotiate a special rate with RACSA for the universities to connect to BITNET. In this case, the government subsidized Internet access and set the telecommunication companies' policy. In addition, the government took the initiative to support the use of the Internet by forcing all its agencies to communicate with each other by this means, and to provide information about the country to external sources using the agencies' Web pages.

In Ecuador and Peru, in addition to the Information Corporation and the RCP, respectively, several private companies began to provide Internet access as well as the design and management of Web pages to other businesses. In Peru, too, the government, which until that point had participated merely as a member of RCP, began to take a more active role. In particular, the state-owned telephone company began to provide Internet services, recognizing the high level of traffic that the service had generated; during 1995, the Peruvian telephone company generated more than \$1 million in telephone connections derived from Internet services.

## Standard Setting

As shown in Table 2, standard-setting activities in Ecuador and Peru were particularly crucial during the first phase of Internet adoption. At that time, the existing telecommunications industry regulatory process in these countries represented a formidable roadblock to their adoption of the Internet [13]. Furthermore, their governments not only did not provide support, but were in fact against the initiative. In Peru, for example, the state-protected telephone company, ENTEL, refused to cooperate because it believed the Internet project would compete with its services. In addition, both of these countries had to overcome the lack of appropriate infrastructure.

In both countries, the institutions leading the adoption process sought out agreements with other institutions to mutually develop the technologies necessary to gain access to a global network. In the case of Ecuador, the Information Corporation, a nonprofit institution, was created by private and academic institutions to regulate cooperation among various institutions to promote the adoption of the Internet. Similarly, in Peru, the RCP was formed by nongovernmental and academic institutions to accomplish this regulatory function. These initial standardization activities resulted in a new ensemble of knowledge practice, information, technology, people, structure, and a set of complementary standards that shaped the countries' possible destinies. The experiences of these two countries also demonstrate that the government does not need to be the driving force behind the setting of standards in adopting a new IT.

In the cases of Chile and Costa Rica, the governments were not directly involved in the efforts to set standards to adopt the Internet. However, they played an important role in setting standards at the national level that facilitated adoption. In Chile, the government facilitated the deregulation of the telecommunications industry, coordinated standards, and ensured vigorous competition, fair access, and basic levels of services. In Costa Rica, the government promoted the adoption of improved telecommunication services, such as X.25, Frame Relay, and ATM services.

The standard setting at the level of technology service was an emerging action in these two countries. As the Internet project grew, it was placed under the auspices of the Ministry of Science and Technology to increase the project's status and to obtain necessary external funding. These ministries played an important role in setting standards to ensure that international, state, and local regulation and policy making complemented this process, and to mobilize international resources to meet particular needs.

## Conclusions

THE CASE STUDIES PRESENTED HERE SUGGEST THAT INTERNET ADOPTION requires support from many different institutions, including the government, the academic community, the private sector, and the international community. No single institution or sector has the capability to develop, mandate, or legislate a coherent Internet service framework within which commercial competitive solutions can coexist and interact. Adoption efforts in these nations received varying degrees of support in different combinations over time. Individuals within these institutions recognized the need to connect to the Internet, experimented with new technologies in partnership with knowledgeable groups, and focused significant energies on coordinating changes and managing the dialogue among parties involved. As these ventures gained experience, they continuously adjusted national structures to better exploit their potential, and they became adept at using the technology that supported market growth and penetration.

This growth fueled competitive responses, expanding each of these countries' markets.

This study extends the work of King et al. [24] identifying crucial institutional actions involved in the adoption of technology. These authors acknowledge that not every possible institutional action is appropriate for every objective, nor is each possible action appropriate for every innovation. They warn that much depends on what the institutions involved desire to accomplish, and the range of motivations can be broad. However, beyond these broad conjectures, little empirical evidence is available to associate specific interventions with specific IT innovation. The present study demonstrates that the adoption of technology takes place gradually but progressively through a given course of institutional actions, and suggests a temporal ordering of the actions. The temporal model shows that different institutional actions tend to build over time, making it easier to adopt the technology than would be expected if only one institution were pushing the technology. Although the temporal model derived from these case studies and the propositions based on it make conceptual sense, an independent test is still required.

From the perspective of practice, the importance of this study is twofold. It could be easily argued, as some business journalists have done, that the Internet is the most powerful IT in history and we are just beginning to understand its impact as a global networked infrastructure. On those grounds alone, any investigation shedding light on such an important issue is worthwhile. The four cases studied here present powerful examples of how IT can be successfully adopted even in LDCs with poor IT infrastructure, lack of governmental support, scarce resources, and insufficient technical and managerial skills. The past literature on the adoption of IT in LDCs has tended to highlight the importance of adapting information practices and IT implementations to the core competencies (including infrastructure) and values of a nation [30]. However, a number of authors have recently begun to challenge this research focus on “fit” and “adaptation” [7, 22, 41]. This study advocates for this new trend by presenting examples of how IT can be successfully adopted even in LDCs with such contextual imperfections and scarcities. The implications discussed in the previous section on evolving institutional actions are valuable themes for debate in the adoption of national Internet connections. Further research can explore whether these implications are valid in other situations and how they can be extended.

Second, the paper should be helpful for leaders who are trying to provide nationwide IT services in LDC environments. There is little doubt that IT innovation will continue and probably accelerate throughout the world in the next decade. The rapidly developing nations of the world are building their plans for economic growth at least in part on these technologies. More broadly, the vision of the networked institution with global reach is predicated on such innovation. The appropriate role of institutions in facilitating successful IT innovation has yet to be determined. Furthermore, it is important to remember that any sustained advantage is highly context-specific—that is, there are no universal sources of advantage $[6]$ —and no two LDCs are alike $[3]$ . Additional study, therefore, is required to determine the most appropriate types of institutional interventions, not only in terms of theoretical constructs, but also in terms of demonstrated experience.
